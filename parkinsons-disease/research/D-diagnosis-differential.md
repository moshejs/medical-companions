## Facts

### MDS clinical diagnostic criteria 2015: structure and core definitions
- The MDS criteria use a stepwise process. First, parkinsonism must be present, "defined as bradykinesia, in combination with at least one between rest tremor (4-6 Hz) and rigidity". Second, absolute exclusion criteria must be absent. Third, red flags must be counterbalanced by supportive criteria. Examination of the cardinal signs follows the MDS-UPDRS [Postuma2015; Marsili2018].
- Bradykinesia (MDS wording): "slowness of movement AND decrement in amplitude or speed or progressive hesitations / halts as movements are continued." [PPMI-FEATPD; Wenning2022]
- Rigidity (MDS wording): "Judged on slow passive movement of major joints with the patient in a relaxed position. Rigidity refers to 'lead-pipe' resistance that is velocity-independent resistance to passive movement not solely reflecting failure to relax. Isolated cogwheeling without lead-pipe rigidity does not fulfill minimum requirements for rigidity." [PPMI-FEATPD]
- Rest tremor (MDS wording): "4-6 Hz tremor in the fully resting limb which is suppressed during movement initiation. Kinetic and postural tremor do not qualify for parkinsonism criteria." [PPMI-FEATPD]
- Clinically established PD requires all three of the following: no absolute exclusion criteria, at least two supportive criteria, and no red flags. The design target was that at least 90% of people meeting it truly have PD, accepting that many true PD cases will not reach this level [Postuma2015; Marsili2018].
- Clinically probable PD requires no absolute exclusion criteria. Red flags are allowed only if counterbalanced: one red flag needs at least one supportive criterion, and two red flags need at least two supportive criteria. "No more than two red flags are allowed for this category." The target was at least 80% sensitivity and 80% positive predictive value [Postuma2015; Marsili2018; Fox2025].
- Absolute exclusions were designed as features highly specific for another diagnosis that occur in fewer than 3% of true PD. Red flags were designed as features of lower or uncertain specificity. The criteria were built for research but may serve as a general guide in clinical practice, and dementia is not an exclusion [Fox2025; Marsili2018].
- Calculator logic summary (derived from the rules above): if parkinsonism is absent, PD is not diagnosed. If any of the 9 exclusions is present, PD is not diagnosed. If there are 0 red flags and 2 or more supportive criteria, the result is clinically established PD. If there are 0 to 2 red flags and the number of supportive criteria is at least the number of red flags, the result is clinically probable PD. Otherwise PD is not diagnosed [Postuma2015; Fox2025].

### MDS 2015: the 4 supportive criteria (verbatim)
- Supportive 1: "Clear and dramatic beneficial response to dopaminergic therapy. During initial treatment, patient returned to normal or near-normal level of function. In the absence of clear documentation of initial response a dramatic response can be classified as: (a) marked improvement with dose increases or marked worsening with dose decreases. Mild changes do not qualify. Document this either objectively (>30% in UPDRS III with change in treatment), or subjectively (clearly documented history of marked changes from a reliable patient or caregiver) (b) unequivocal and marked on/off fluctuations, which must have at some point included predictable end-of-dose wearing off" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Supportive 2: "Presence of levodopa-induced dyskinesia" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Supportive 3: "Rest tremor of a limb, documented on clinical examination (in past, or on current examination)" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Supportive 4: "The presence of either olfactory loss or cardiac sympathetic denervation on MIBG scintigraphy" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- An ancillary test qualified as a supportive criterion only if it had shown more than 80% specificity for separating PD from other parkinsonian conditions in at least three positive studies from different centres [Marsili2018].

### MDS 2015: the 9 absolute exclusion criteria (verbatim)
- Exclusion 1: "Unequivocal cerebellar abnormalities, such as cerebellar gait, limb ataxia, or cerebellar oculomotor abnormalities (e.g., sustained gaze evoked nystagmus, macro square wave jerks, hypermetric saccades)" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Exclusion 2: "Downward vertical supranuclear gaze palsy or selective slowing of downward vertical saccades" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Exclusion 3: "Diagnosis of probable behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia or primary progressive aphasia, defined according to consensus criteria within the first 5 years of disease" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Exclusion 4: "Parkinsonian features restricted to the lower limbs for more than 3 years" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Exclusion 5: "Treatment with a dopamine receptor blocker or a dopamine-depleting agent in a dose/time-course consistent with drug-induced parkinsonism" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Exclusion 6: "Absence of observable response to high-dose levodopa despite at least moderate severity of disease" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Exclusion 7: "Unequivocal cortical sensory loss (graphesthesia, stereognosis with intact primary sensory modalities), clear limb ideomotor apraxia, or progressive aphasia" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Exclusion 8: "Normal functional neuroimaging of the presynaptic dopaminergic system". This does not mean imaging is required, and the criterion does not apply if no imaging was done [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Exclusion 9: "Documentation of an alternative condition known to produce parkinsonism and plausibly connected to the patient's symptoms, or, the expert evaluating physician, based on the full diagnostic assessment feels that an alternative syndrome is more likely than PD" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]

### MDS 2015: the 10 red flags (verbatim)
- Red flag 1: "Rapid progression of gait impairment requiring regular use of wheelchair within 5 years of onset" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Red flag 2: "A complete absence of progression of motor symptoms or signs over 5 or more years unless stability is related to treatment" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Red flag 3: "Early bulbar dysfunction: severe dysphonia or dysarthria (speech unintelligible most of the time) or severe dysphagia (requiring soft food, NG tube, or gastrostomy) within first 5 years" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Red flag 4: "Inspiratory respiratory dysfunction: either diurnal or nocturnal inspiratory stridor or frequent inspiratory sighs" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Red flag 5: "Severe autonomic failure in the first 5 years of disease. This can include: (a) orthostatic hypotension—orthostatic decrease of blood pressure within 3 min of standing by at least 30 mmHg systolic or 15 mmHg diastolic, in the absence of dehydration, medication, or other diseases that could plausibly explain autonomic dysfunction, or (b) severe urinary retention or urinary incontinence in the first 5 years of disease (excluding long-standing or small amount stress incontinence in women), that is not simply functional incontinence. In men, urinary retention must not be attributable to prostate disease, and must be associated with erectile dysfunction" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Red flag 6: "Recurrent (>1/year) falls because of impaired balance within 3 years of onset" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Red flag 7: "Disproportionate anterocollis (dystonic) or contractures of hand or feet within the first 10 years" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Red flag 8: "Absence of any of the common non-motor features of disease despite 5 years disease duration. These include sleep dysfunction (sleep-maintenance insomnia, excessive daytime somnolence, symptoms of REM sleep behavior disorder), autonomic dysfunction (constipation, daytime urinary urgency, symptomatic orthostasis), hyposmia, or psychiatric dysfunction (depression, anxiety, or hallucinations)" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Red flag 9: "Otherwise-unexplained pyramidal tract signs, defined as pyramidal weakness or clear pathologic hyperreflexia (excluding mild reflex asymmetry and isolated extensor plantar response)" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Red flag 10: "Bilateral symmetric parkinsonism. The patient or caregiver reports bilateral symptom onset with no side predominance, and no side predominance is observed on objective examination" [Postuma2015; Marsili2018]
- Counting note: the 2025 autopsy scoping review split the multipart items and counted "6 supportive criteria; 7 absolute exclusion criteria and 11 Red Flags". The canonical totals are 4, 9 and 10 [Fox2025; Marsili2018].

### Validation of the MDS criteria and accuracy of clinical diagnosis
- Clinical validation (8 centres, 626 patients: 434 PD and 192 non-PD, measured against expert diagnosis): 94.5% of PD met clinically probable PD (5.5% false negative) and 88.5% of non-PD were correctly excluded (11.5% false positive). Overall accuracy was 92.6%. In the same study, 59.3% of PD patients but only 1.6% of non-PD patients met clinically established PD. The UK Brain Bank criteria scored lower: sensitivity 89.2%, specificity 79.2%, accuracy 86.4% [Postuma2018].
- Autopsy validation at the Queen Square Brain Bank (2009-2019; 141 PD and 126 non-PD parkinsonism): accuracy was 97.2% for movement-disorder experts, 92.5% for MDS clinically probable PD, and 90.3% for other clinicians. Within 5 years of motor onset the figures were 91.5%, 89.5% and 84.2%. At early stages the stricter "clinically established early PD" variant reached 98.4% specificity against pathology (95.4% specificity and 69.8% sensitivity in its derivation study) [Virameteekul2023; Fox2025].
- In autopsy series, co-pathology (DLB, MSA or Alzheimer pathology) was found in 37.2% of people with PD at the UK Brain Bank. This complicates any "single diagnosis" accuracy estimate [Fox2025].
- Item-level autopsy data: a normal DaT scan, cerebellar ataxia and absent levodopa response each occurred in fewer than 3% of pathologically confirmed PD. No PD cases had a normal DaT scan, but a few MSA, PSP and especially CBD cases did. In PSP, absent levodopa response was noted in 75% of pathologically proven PSP. However, 15% of PSP had a marked levodopa response and 36% had unequivocal fluctuations, so about a quarter of PSP may appear levodopa-responsive, partly because of Lewy co-pathology [Fox2025].
- Red flags that best separated PD from atypical parkinsonism (seen in under 2.5% of PD) were inspiratory stridor, severe urinary retention/incontinence, and early bulbar dysfunction. Rapid gait progression, recurrent falls and bilateral symmetric parkinsonism were less discriminating, appearing in 3.8-17.6% of PD [Fox2025].
- UK Brain Bank criteria (Gibb and Lees 1988, applied by Hughes 1992) have three steps. Step 1 is bradykinesia plus at least one of rigidity, 4-6 Hz rest tremor or postural instability. Step 2 is a list of exclusions, for example repeated strokes, encephalitis, oculogyric crises, neuroleptics at onset, more than one affected relative, supranuclear gaze palsy, cerebellar signs, early severe autonomic involvement or dementia, and a negative levodopa response. Step 3 requires three or more supportive features, for example unilateral onset, an excellent levodopa response (70-100%) and a clinical course of 10 years or more [Marsili2018].
- In 100 brains from patients diagnosed with PD by consultant neurologists, only 76 had nigral Lewy bodies. The other 24 included PSP, MSA, Alzheimer's disease and vascular disease. Retrospectively applying criteria raised accuracy to 82% [Hughes1992].
- Meta-analysis (20 studies; 11 with pathology as the gold standard) found pooled accuracy of clinical PD diagnosis of 80.6% (95% CrI 75.2-85.3). Accuracy was 73.8% for mainly non-expert diagnosis. For movement-disorder experts it was 79.6% at initial assessment and 83.9% after follow-up. UK Brain Bank criteria gave 82.7% (with higher sensitivity than expert diagnosis, 90.8% vs 81.3%, but far lower specificity, 34% vs 83.5%). Accuracy did not improve over 25 years [Rizzo2016; Marsili2018].
- Community (London population study): at least 15% of people with a PD diagnosis did not meet strict criteria. About 20% (19%) of people found to have PD had not been diagnosed with it [Schrag2002].
- Controversy: in a 2018 informal survey, 10 of 16 senior movement-disorder experts (not authors of the criteria) judged the MDS criteria "not so valuable in the clinical setting". Some objected to normal dopaminergic imaging being an absolute exclusion, given low-quality scans in early PD [Marsili2018].

### Prodromal PD: MDS research criteria (Berg 2015; Heinzel 2019 update)
- Prodromal PD is the stage when early symptoms or signs of PD neurodegeneration are present but classic motor PD cannot yet be diagnosed. The criteria are for research only. "Probable prodromal PD" means at least 80% certainty, estimated with a naive Bayesian classifier: an age-based prior probability multiplied by likelihood ratios (LRs) from risk and prodromal markers [Berg2015].
- Age-related baseline PD risk in non-carriers in the 2019 update: 50-54 y 0.4%; 55-59 y 0.75%; 60-64 y 1.25%; 65-69 y 2.0%; 70-74 y 2.5%; 75-79 y 3.5%; 80+ y 4%. These match the age bands used as priors (correspondence to the Berg 2015 prior table not checked in that full text: unverified) [Heinzel2019].
- Derived by this research pass, not stated in the source: an 80% posterior is an odds of 4. The combined LR needed is therefore 4 x (1-p)/p, which gives about 996 at age 50-54, 529 at 55-59, 316 at 60-64, 196 at 65-69, 156 at 70-74, 110 at 75-79 and 96 at 80+ [Heinzel2019; Berg2015].
- Prodromal-marker LRs (positive/negative), 2019: PSG-proven RBD 130/0.65; possible RBD by questionnaire 2.8/0.89; clearly abnormal dopaminergic PET/SPECT 43.3/0.66; subthreshold parkinsonism 9.6/0.55; abnormal quantitative motor testing 3.5/0.60; olfactory loss 6.4/0.40; constipation 2.5/0.82; excessive daytime somnolence 2.7/0.86; neurogenic orthostatic hypotension 18.5/0.88; symptomatic orthostatic hypotension 3.2/0.80; erectile dysfunction (men) 3.4/0.87; urinary dysfunction 2.0/0.90; depression with or without anxiety 1.6/0.88; global cognitive deficit 1.8/0.88. "Clearly abnormal" imaging means for example below 65% of normal or 2 SD below the mean; subthreshold parkinsonism means UPDRS-III above 3 excluding action tremor, or MDS-UPDRS-III above 6 excluding postural and action tremor [Heinzel2019].
- Risk-marker LRs, 2019: male sex 1.2 (female 0.8); regular pesticide exposure 1.5; occupational solvent exposure 1.5; non-use of caffeine 1.35/0.88; never smoker 1.2 (current smoker LR- 0.51); first-degree relative with PD 2.5; substantia nigra hyperechogenicity 3.4/0.38; type 2 diabetes 1.5/0.97; physical inactivity 1.3/0.91; low plasma urate in men 1.8/0.88; highest polygenic-risk quartile 1.57 (lowest 0.45) [Heinzel2019].
- Genetic markers in the update: the LR for intermediate-penetrance variants depends on age. For GBA carriers, PD risk is about 18% at 65 vs 2% in the general population, giving LR+ 9 at 65-69 and 20.0 at 50-54. For LRRK2 G2019S, LR+ rises from 2.5 at 50-54 to 11.6 at 70-74. The criteria are not applied to carriers of high-penetrance mutations [Heinzel2019].
- The update lists phosphorylated alpha-synuclein in skin biopsy as a promising candidate marker, sensitive (55-100%) and highly specific (above 90%), but not yet added for lack of prospective data. An MDS web calculator implements the criteria. Stated limitations: the markers may not be independent (for example, depression correlates with other self-reported markers), and LRs probably vary by age, sex and ethnicity [Heinzel2019].

### RBD conversion and the timeline before diagnosis
- In the International RBD Study Group cohort (24 centres; 1280 PSG-confirmed iRBD patients; mean age 66.3; 82.5% male), phenoconversion averaged 6.25% per year, with a median time to conversion of 8.0 years [Postuma2019RBD].
- Kaplan-Meier risk of conversion to an overt neurodegenerative syndrome was 10.6% at 2 years, 17.9% at 3 years, 31.3% at 5 years, 51.4% at 8 years, 60.2% at 10 years and 73.5% at 12 years [Postuma2019RBD].
- Of the 352 converters, 56.5% developed parkinsonism first (including 16, or 4.5%, with probable MSA) and 43.5% developed dementia first [Postuma2019RBD].
- Hazard ratios for conversion: abnormal quantitative motor testing 3.16; objective motor examination 3.03; olfactory deficit 2.62; mild cognitive impairment 1.91-2.37; erectile dysfunction 2.13; abnormal DaT scan 1.98; constipation 1.67. Sex, depression and orthostatic symptoms did not predict conversion, and even patients negative on the MDS prodromal criteria converted (13% by 5 years, 27% by 8 years) [Postuma2019RBD].
- Prospective iRBD cohort (154 patients, 55 converters), timed backwards from diagnosis: olfactory loss is predicted to start more than 20 years before conversion. Impaired colour vision, constipation and erectile dysfunction start 10-16 years before; urinary dysfunction and subtle cognitive decline 7-9 years before; motor examination changes 5-7 years before. Among cardinal signs, bradykinesia appeared about 5-6 years before diagnosis, rigidity about 3 years before and tremor about 2 years before [Fereshtehnejad2019].
- UK primary-care records (8166 people with PD vs 46,755 controls): 5 years before diagnosis, the relative risk was 13.70 for tremor, 3.23 for hypotension, 2.24 for constipation, 2.19 for balance problems, 1.99 for dizziness, 1.96 for urinary dysfunction, 1.76 for depression and 1.30 for erectile dysfunction. At 10 years before diagnosis, tremor (7.59) and constipation (2.01) were still raised [Schrag2015].
- Meta-analysis of 9 studies (741,593 participants): constipation gave a pooled OR of 2.27 for later PD. Restricting to constipation recorded more than 10 years before diagnosis still gave an OR of 2.13 [AdamsCarr2016].
- In a 400-patient multicentre Sniffin' Sticks study, 96.7% of PD patients had smell loss compared with young normosmic norms (45.0% functionally anosmic, 51.7% hyposmic). After age adjustment the figure was 74.5% [Haehner2009].

### Dopamine transporter SPECT (123I-ioflupane, DaTscan)
- The FDA first approved DaTscan (NDA 022454) on 14 January 2011 as a new molecular entity. An efficacy supplement approved on 1 November 2022 added suspected dementia with Lewy bodies. The current label indication is "an adjunct to other diagnostic evaluations for striatal dopamine transporter visualization using single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) brain imaging in adult patients with: suspected Parkinsonian syndromes (PS) or suspected dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB)." [FDA-DaTscan]
- The label states that PD, MSA and PSP all show dopaminergic neurodegeneration and that imaging "was not designed to distinguish among the conditions". Vascular, psychogenic, essential and other tremors made up the non-PS group [FDA-DaTscan].
- Label pivotal data, Study 1 (99 patients with early parkinsonism followed for 36 months): positive percent agreement was 77-79% and negative percent agreement 96%. Study 2 (185 patients with established PS or ET): positive agreement was 92-97% and negative agreement 74-96%. For DLB against autopsy, sensitivity was 80% and specificity 92% [FDA-DaTscan].
- Clinical utility meta-analysis (13 studies): DaTscan changed management in 54% (95% CI 47-61) and changed the diagnosis in 31% (22-42) of patients with suspected parkinsonism [Bega2021].
- SWEDD (scans without evidence of dopaminergic deficit): in ELLDOPA, 19 imaged patients with clinically diagnosed early PD had no dopaminergic deficit at baseline (out of 142 imaged; about 13%, a proportion computed here). In PPMI, alpha-synuclein SAA was positive in 9% of SWEDD participants vs 4% of controls. The MDS criteria do not allow SWEDD cases to count as PD, because a normal scan is an absolute exclusion [Fahn2004; Siderowf2023; Marsili2018].

### Other ancillary tests
- Cardiac 123I-MIBG meta-analysis (13 studies; 625 PD vs 220 other neurodegenerative parkinsonism): the early heart-to-mediastinum ratio gave pooled sensitivity 82.6% and specificity 89.2%. The delayed ratio gave 89.7% and 82.6%. In early PD (Hoehn and Yahr 1-2), the delayed ratio gave 94.1% sensitivity and 80.2% specificity. No clinicopathological series in the 2025 autopsy review reported MIBG, which its authors read as limited clinical availability [Orimo2012; Fox2025].
- MRI nigrosome-1: loss of the "swallow tail" sign on 3T or 7T iron-sensitive MRI separated PD from healthy adults with pooled sensitivity 0.94 and specificity 0.90 (19 studies; 903 PD and 605 controls; AUC 0.98). The comparison was with healthy controls, not with atypical parkinsonism [Chau2020].
- MRI hummingbird sign (midbrain atrophy) in 481 patients with neurodegenerative parkinsonism: specificity 99.5% and positive predictive value 96.1% for PSP, but sensitivity only 51.6%. The morning-glory sign had specificity 97.7% and sensitivity 36.8%. In early unclassifiable parkinsonism, sensitivity of both signs was 35.3% [Mueller2018].
- MRI hot-cross-bun sign (cruciform pontine T2 hyperintensity): in the 2022 MSA criteria this sign, together with atrophy of the putamen, pons, middle cerebellar peduncle or cerebellum and increased putaminal/MCP diffusivity, counts as an MRI marker. At least one MRI marker is required for clinically established MSA. Putaminal diffusivity separated MSA from PD with 90% sensitivity and 93% specificity [Wenning2022].
- Acute challenge tests (systematic review, 13 studies), sensitivity for established PD: apomorphine 0.86, acute levodopa 0.75, chronic levodopa therapy 0.91. Specificity: 0.85, 0.87 and 0.77 respectively. The authors concluded that the acute tests add nothing to chronic therapy and cause adverse events [Clarke2000].
- UPSIT: PPMI defined hyposmia as at or below the 15th percentile on the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test [Siderowf2023].

### Alpha-synuclein seed amplification assay (SAA) and skin biopsy
- CSF alpha-synuclein SAA in PPMI (1123 participants: 545 PD and 163 healthy controls): sensitivity for PD was 87.7% (95% CI 84.9-90.5) and specificity in controls 96.3% (93.4-99.2). Six of 163 controls (4%) were positive [Siderowf2023].
- Positivity by PD subgroup in PPMI: sporadic PD with typical olfactory deficit 98.6%; sporadic PD without olfactory deficit 78.3%; GBA PD 95.9%; LRRK2 PD 67.5%; LRRK2 PD with normal smell 34.7% [Siderowf2023].
- Prodromal and at-risk groups in PPMI: 44 of 51 (86%) with RBD or hyposmia were positive (16 of 18 with hyposmia; 28 of 33 with RBD), compared with 8% of non-manifesting carriers (9% LRRK2, 7% GBA) [Siderowf2023].
- The PPMI authors noted that about a third of LRRK2 PD was SAA-negative, which matches the frequency of LRRK2 PD without Lewy pathology at autopsy [Siderowf2023].
- Skin biopsy for phosphorylated alpha-synuclein (SYN-ONE study; 30 sites; 343 analysed): positive in 92.7% of PD (89 of 96), 98.2% of MSA (54 of 55), 96.0% of DLB (48 of 50), 100% of pure autonomic failure (22 of 22) and 3.3% of controls (4 of 120). The authors state that validation is still needed "in unselected clinical populations", because cases were selected by consensus criteria and reviewed by a panel [Gibbons2024].
- Regulatory status (US): the FDA has issued a Biomarker Letter of Support endorsing CSF SAA "as a susceptibility and risk biomarker for use in clinical trials" (participant enrichment). This is not a diagnostic approval. Syn-One (skin biopsy) is offered as a laboratory pathology assay that "is not approved by the US Food and Drug Administration as a diagnostic test for PD" and "can be billed through Medicare" (report of March 2024) [Stephenson2026; Hospitalist2024].
- Searches up to October 2026 found no FDA approval or clearance of any alpha-synuclein SAA or skin test as a PD diagnostic. This is a negative search result, not a confirmed statement from FDA (unverified). A 2025 autopsy review notes that access to SAA "is not widespread and mostly limited to research studies" [Stephenson2026; Hospitalist2024; Fox2025].

### Biological definitions (2024) and the disagreement
- NSD-ISS (neuronal alpha-synuclein disease integrated staging system) defines disease by in-vivo pathological neuronal alpha-synuclein (S, the first anchor, currently CSF SAA) and dopaminergic dysfunction (D, the second anchor), "regardless of the presence of any specific clinical syndrome". Stages: stage 0 is a pathogenic SNCA variant; 1A is S alone; 1B is S plus D, both without signs. Stage 2 means subtle signs without functional impairment, and stages 2B-6 require S and D with increasing functional impairment. Its authors say clinical use "is premature and inappropriate" [Simuni2024].
- SynNeurGe classifies disease on three components: S (presence or absence of pathological alpha-synuclein in tissue or CSF), N (neurodegeneration on neuroimaging) and G (pathogenic gene variants). These are linked to a clinical component C, defined either by one high-specificity feature or by several lower-specificity features. It too is for research only [Hoglinger2024].
- Key difference: SynNeurGe can classify synuclein-negative, gene-defined disease and separates PD from MSA. NSD covers all SAA-positive conditions with dopaminergic deficit (PD, DLB, RBD, even asymptomatic people) but not MSA [Cardoso2024].
- MDS statement (Cardoso 2024), on what SAA shows: SAA demonstrates abnormal alpha-synuclein by amplifying it in the laboratory. It "does not necessarily reflect any seeding or aggregation of α-syn in the brain", cannot predict "if, when, and which clinical syndrome" will follow, and is binary rather than quantitative. In LRRK2-PD and PRKN-PD, alpha-synuclein pathology is found in under 50% of cases post-mortem, and SAA is often negative in LRRK2-PD [Cardoso2024].
- MDS statement, on sequence: staging must follow an agreed definition. With no consensus on an alpha-synuclein-based biological definition, biologically based staging criteria are not yet logically possible, and Hoehn and Yahr should not be "prematurely" abandoned [Cardoso2024].
- Critique (Espay, Cardoso, Frucht, Imarisio, Lees, 2026): SAA-negative and SAA-positive PD in PPMI did not differ in later motor, non-motor, cognitive or DaT progression. In their view seeding "does not appear necessary" for the PD syndrome, and "neuronal α‑synuclein disease and PD may be overlapping syndromes, not interchangeable constructs" [Espay2026].
- How many clinical PD cases lack seeds: about 12% in PPMI CSF SAA overall (sensitivity 87.7%), about 1 in 3 LRRK2 PD, and about 7% on skin biopsy (92.7% positive) [Siderowf2023; Gibbons2024].

### Differential diagnosis: tremor and drug-induced parkinsonism
- Essential tremor (MDS 2018 definition): "isolated tremor syndrome of bilateral upper limb action tremor", of "at least 3 years' duration", "with or without tremor in other locations (e.g., head, voice, or lower limbs)" and without dystonia, ataxia or parkinsonism. ET with rest tremor is classed as "ET plus". Isolated focal head or voice tremor excludes ET; voice tremor in ET occurs only with hand tremor. Head tremor is common in ET [Bhatia2018].
- Tremor frequencies: the MDS PD criteria define rest tremor as 4-6 Hz [PPMI-FEATPD]. The tremor consensus describes parkinsonian pill-rolling rest tremor as 4-7 Hz, with higher frequencies described [Bhatia2018]. ET's core feature is "a 4 to 12 Hz simple kinetic tremor of the arms" [Louis2026].
- Alcohol: small doses may reduce ET, but the 2018 consensus judged this not consistent enough to include in the definition [Bhatia2018]. A quantitative home test showed alcohol sensitivity in 46% of 104 ET patients [Hopfner2015].
- Drug-induced parkinsonism (DIP) is the second most common cause of parkinsonism in older people after PD. Causes include typical and atypical antipsychotics, GI prokinetics (metoclopramide, levosulpiride, clebopride, itopride, domperidone), the calcium-channel blockers flunarizine and cinnarizine, valproate (parkinsonism in about 5% of long-term users) and lithium [Shin2012].
- DIP "usually resolves within weeks to months" after the drug is stopped, but "may persist or progress in 10-50% of patients". Those patients may have preclinical PD unmasked by the drug [Shin2012].
- Although DIP is classically symmetric and without rest tremor, 30-50% of DIP patients have asymmetric parkinsonism and rest tremor. DaT imaging, normal in pure DIP because the presynaptic terminals are intact, helps separate the two [Shin2012].

### Differential diagnosis: atypical and secondary parkinsonism
- Vascular parkinsonism (clinicopathological series of 17 patients): most presented with bilateral bradykinesia and rigidity and a predominantly shuffling gait, consistent with "lower-body" parkinsonism. A minority began acutely after a lacunar infarct [Zijlmans2004]. Parkinsonism restricted to the legs for more than 3 years is an MDS absolute exclusion for PD [Postuma2015].
- MSA, 2008 second consensus: probable MSA requires a sporadic, progressive adult-onset disorder with rigorously defined autonomic failure and poorly levodopa-responsive parkinsonism or cerebellar ataxia. The orthostatic criterion was a drop of at least 30/15 mmHg [Gilman2008; Wenning2022].
- MSA, MDS 2022 criteria: four levels (neuropathologically established, clinically established, clinically probable, possible prodromal). Prerequisites are onset after 30 years, a negative family history and progression. Clinically established MSA needs an autonomic core feature: post-void residual above 100 mL, urinary urge incontinence, or neurogenic OH (at least 20 mmHg systolic drop) within 3 minutes, with poorly levodopa-responsive parkinsonism (under 30% MDS-UPDRS-III improvement on up to 1000 mg/day) and/or a cerebellar syndrome, plus at least one MRI marker. In the 2008 criteria, sensitivity at first visit was only 41% for possible and 18% for probable MSA [Wenning2022].
- Levodopa response in MSA: a beneficial response occurs in 42.5-56.7% of MSA-P and 12.9-25% of MSA-C, so a good response does not rule MSA out. Stridor is distinctive for both MSA subtypes and counts as a supportive feature. Postural instability within 3 years has 88.7% specificity for MSA versus PD [Wenning2022].
- PSP (MDS 2017) requires sporadic occurrence, onset at 40 or older and gradual progression. Core features include O1 "Vertical supranuclear gaze palsy", O2 "Slow velocity of vertical saccades", P1 "Repeated unprovoked falls within 3 years" and P2 "Tendency to fall on the pull-test within 3 years". Probable PSP-Richardson syndrome is (O1 or O2) + (P1 or P2) [Hoglinger2017].
- Under the older NINDS-SPSP criteria, sensitivity at the first visit had a median of 24% (range 14-83%), and 76% of autopsy-confirmed PSP cases in one series presented as something other than Richardson syndrome [Hoglinger2017].
- CBD (Armstrong 2013) described 4 phenotypes: corticobasal syndrome, frontal behavioural-spatial syndrome, nonfluent/agrammatic PPA and a PSP syndrome. Probable CBD requires insidious onset with at least 1 year of progression, onset at 50 or older and no family history or tau mutation. CBS was the most common final clinical diagnosis in pathological CBD (37.1%). A sustained levodopa response "is rare" [Armstrong2013].
- DLB (fourth consensus, 2017): dementia is required. Core features are fluctuations, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations (up to 80%), RBD and parkinsonism (eventually over 85%). Only one cardinal parkinsonian sign is needed. Reduced DaT uptake distinguishes DLB from Alzheimer's disease with 78% sensitivity and 90% specificity. RBD occurs in 76% of autopsy-confirmed DLB vs 4% of non-DLB [McKeith2017].
- The DLB consortium "remains supportive of the 1-year rule distinguishing DLB from PD dementia", while calling the cutoff arbitrary and based on expert opinion [McKeith2017].
- Idiopathic normal-pressure hydrocephalus: gait disturbance is present in 94-100%, cognitive impairment in 78-98% and urinary dysfunction in 60-92%. The full triad is present in about 60%. Imaging shows ventriculomegaly (Evans index at or above 0.3) with DESH. Unlike PD, external cues do little for the gait. A CSF tap test supports diagnosis [Nakajima2021].
- Wilson disease (relevant to young-onset parkinsonism): symptomatic prevalence is about 1 in 30,000, with mean symptom onset at 20-30 years. Kayser-Fleischer rings occur in "almost 100%" of neurological cases, and parkinsonism in 19-62% of patients [Czlonkowska2018].
- Functional parkinsonism (systematic review; 16 studies, 120 patients): 62% were women, mean age 50.7. Onset was abrupt in half, 67.6% had a psychiatric history and 46.8% had a triggering stressor. Functional symptoms can also coexist with PD [AmbarAkkaoui2020].

### NICE NG71 (diagnosis section, recommendations 1.2.1-1.2.14)
- 1.2.1: "Suspect Parkinson's disease in people presenting with tremor, stiffness, slowness, balance problems and/or gait disorders." 1.2.2: "If Parkinson's disease is suspected, refer people quickly and untreated to a specialist with expertise in the differential diagnosis of this condition." [NICE2017]
- 1.2.3: diagnose clinically "based on the UK Parkinson's Disease Society Brain Bank Clinical Diagnostic Criteria". NG71 predates and does not adopt the MDS 2015 criteria. 1.2.5: "Review the diagnosis of Parkinson's disease regularly, and reconsider it if atypical clinical features develop", with review at intervals of 6 to 12 months. 1.2.6: consider 123I-FP-CIT SPECT "for people with tremor if essential tremor cannot be clinically differentiated from parkinsonism" [NICE2017].
- 1.2.8-1.2.14: NICE recommends against PET (except in trials), structural MRI to diagnose PD (though it "may be considered" for other parkinsonian syndromes), MR volumetry and spectroscopy, acute levodopa/apomorphine challenge tests, and objective smell testing (except in trials) in the differential diagnosis. 1.2.4 encourages discussing brain-tissue donation for diagnostic confirmation [NICE2017].
