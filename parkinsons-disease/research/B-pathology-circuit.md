# B. Pathology: alpha-synuclein, Lewy bodies, Braak staging and the nigrostriatal pathway

## Facts

### Nigrostriatal anatomy and cell numbers
- In 7 control brains counted with unbiased stereology, the substantia nigra held on average 550,000 pigmented (neuromelanin-containing) neurons and 260,000 non-pigmented neurons. In 7 people with PD, pigmented neurons were reduced by 66% and non-pigmented neurons by 24% [Pakkenberg1991].
- Across midbrain dopamine cell groups, how much a group lost in PD tracked how many of its neurons are normally neuromelanin-pigmented (r = 0.97). Within each group, non-pigmented neurons were relatively spared, so neuromelanin-pigmented dopamine neurons appear selectively vulnerable [Hirsch1988].
- Fearnley and Lees split the pars compacta into a ventral and a dorsal tier, each with 3 regions. In normal ageing (36 controls), pigmented neurons in the caudal nigra fell linearly at 4.7% per decade. The lateral ventral tier was relatively spared (2.1% per decade) compared with the medial ventral tier (5.4%) and the dorsal tier (6.9%) [Fearnley1991].
- In 20 PD cases, pigmented neuron loss was exponential, about 45% in the first decade. The regional pattern was the opposite of ageing: lateral ventral tier 91% loss on average, medial ventral tier 71%, dorsal tier 56% [Fearnley1991].
- Fearnley and Lees calculated that at symptom onset there was 68% cell loss in the lateral ventral tier and 48% in the caudal nigra as a whole. They estimated a presymptomatic phase of about 5 years from the onset of neuronal loss. In 7 incidental Lewy body cases, loss was confined to the lateral ventral tier (average 52%) [Fearnley1991].
- The motor loop runs from precentral motor cortex through the post-commissural putamen and the motor parts of GPe, GPi, SNr and STN, then to the VA/VL thalamus and back to cortex [Galvan2008].

### Dopamine loss at symptom onset: where the quoted percentages come from
- Ehringer and Hornykiewicz (1960) first reported dopamine (3-hydroxytyramine) deficiency in the striatum of people with PD and other extrapyramidal disorders. The original German paper has no abstract and was not accessed, so no figures are given here (unverified) [Ehringer1960].
- "50%" versus "30%": most reviews say motor signs appear when about 50% of SN dopamine neurons are lost (estimates up to 60-70%), but Cheng and colleagues' reanalysis puts it nearer 30%. Back-extrapolating Fearnley and Lees' regression gives a 31% age-adjusted loss of SN neurons at symptom onset. Incidental Lewy body cases showed a 27% age-adjusted loss without motor signs. Ma and colleagues' counts and Greffard and colleagues' density counts also extrapolate to about 30% [Cheng2010].
- "80% striatal dopamine": this commonly quoted figure traces to Bernheimer et al. 1973. Cheng and colleagues point out that the study biochemically analysed only 13 PD brains, did no regression analysis, and "does not provide useful information for this estimate" [Cheng2010].
- Riederer and Wuketich's caudate dopamine data back-extrapolate to a 68% loss at onset (onset age 60 plus or minus 1 years, n = 27) and an 82% loss (onset age 73 plus or minus 1 years, n = 12) [Cheng2010].
- Post-mortem VMAT2 ([3H]TBZOH) binding in the caudate extrapolated to a 49% loss at onset. Putamen TH protein was reduced by 50% in incidental Lewy body brains [Cheng2010].
- Imaging estimates of putaminal terminal loss at onset are 20-50% with [18F]-dopa PET and 50-70% with dopamine transporter and VMAT2 ligands [Cheng2010].
- Cheng and colleagues' overall estimate is that at first diagnosis only about 30% of SN dopamine neurons, but 50-60% of their axon terminals, have been lost. This is the "axons first" argument: about 70% of neurons remain, and their axonal projections, not their number, are what limits function [Cheng2010].
- In 28 PD brains taken 1-27 years after diagnosis, dorsal putamen dopaminergic markers (TH, DAT) showed modest loss at 1 year (a single case), variable moderate-to-marked loss at 3 years, and virtually complete loss from 4 years onward [Kordower2013].
- In the same series, TH-positive SNc neurons were already down 50-90% at the earliest time points, with only marginal further loss. Melanized neuron loss was about 10% at 1 year (one case), 30-60% in the first several years, and more gradual in the second decade. Melanized neuron loss lagged behind loss of dopamine markers [Kordower2013].
- Time-course numbers for an animation (dorsal putamen terminals versus SNc cell bodies): roughly 50-60% of terminals lost at diagnosis [Cheng2010], almost 100% by year 4 after diagnosis [Kordower2013]. Roughly 30% of SN neurons lost at diagnosis [Cheng2010], 30-60% of melanized neurons lost over the first several years, then slower loss [Kordower2013].

### Basal ganglia circuit (rate model)
- The rate ("box-and-arrow") model was built from MPTP-monkey firing-rate and metabolic data by Albin, Young and Penney (1989) and DeLong (1990) [Galvan2008; Albin1989; DeLong1990].
- Albin and colleagues proposed that hypokinetic disorders such as PD come from changes in striatal projection-neuron subpopulations that increase basal ganglia output. Hyperkinetic disorders come from impairment of striatal neurons projecting to the lateral globus pallidus (GPe) [Albin1989].
- Inputs: the striatum and STN receive glutamatergic (excitatory) input from cortex and thalamus. They pass information to the output nuclei, GPi and SNr [Galvan2008].
- Direct pathway: a monosynaptic striatum-to-GPi/SNr projection made by D1-receptor MSNs. Indirect pathway: D2-receptor MSNs project via GPe and STN [Galvan2008].
- Output: GPi/SNr project mainly to the VA and VL thalamic nuclei, which project back to cortex. Activating the direct pathway inhibits GPi/SNr and so disinhibits thalamocortical activity. Activating the indirect pathway does the opposite [Galvan2008].
- Transmitter signs an animation needs: cortex to striatum and cortex/thalamus to STN are glutamatergic (excitatory). In the review's circuit figure, striatal MSN, GPe and GPi/SNr outputs are drawn as inhibitory (GABAergic) [Galvan2008]. The figure's black and grey arrows mark inhibitory and excitatory connections [Galvan2008]. The STN-to-GPi/SNr excitatory link is implied by "excessive activity in targets of STN efferents, including GPi and SNr" [Galvan2008].
- Dopamine's net effect is to make corticostriatal transmission easier onto direct-pathway MSNs and harder onto indirect-pathway MSNs. Its overall action lowers GPi/SNr activity and so favours movement [Galvan2008].
- When dopamine is lost (steps in order): (1) less D2 activation at indirect MSNs leads to (2) more inhibition of GPe, which leads to (3) disinhibition of the STN, which leads to (4) excess drive to GPi/SNr. In parallel, less D1 activation of direct MSNs disinhibits GPi/SNr. The result is (5) more inhibition of the thalamus and less cortical activation [Galvan2008].
- Supporting evidence: MPTP-treated monkeys show more activity in STN and GPi and less in GPe, as the model predicts. Lesioning or inactivating the overactive STN or GPi reverses parkinsonian signs in MPTP monkeys and in people with PD [Galvan2008].
- Limits of the model (1), the "paradox of stereotaxic surgery": lesions in motor thalamus or globus pallidus improve rigidity and tremor but apparently do not worsen bradykinesia/hypokinesia, and do not regularly cause dyskinesia. Marsden and Obeso concluded the basal ganglia motor circuits are part of a distributed system that can work, imperfectly, without striato-pallido-thalamo-cortical feedback [Marsden1994].
- Marsden and Obeso proposed that it is a pause in GPi/SNr firing, by disinhibiting thalamic targets, that permits movement. Increases in pallidal firing may mainly suppress unwanted movement [Marsden1994].
- Limits of the model (2): global increases or decreases in GPi activity from local drug injections do not necessarily produce parkinsonism or involuntary movements. GPe and VA/VL lesions, which the model predicts should cause parkinsonism, do not do so consistently [Galvan2008].
- Galvan and Wichmann wrote that it seems "increasingly doubtful" that firing-rate changes or bursting are strongly pro-parkinsonian. Synchronised oscillations are the current "lead suspect" [Galvan2008].

### Beta-band oscillations
- In 4 awake patients recorded after surgery, off medication, power and coherence between GPi and STN were dominated by activity below 30 Hz. Levodopa reduced this low-frequency activity and produced a new peak at about 70 Hz [Brown2001].
- Exaggerated synchronisation in the beta band (13-30 Hz) has been associated with bradykinesia in PD [Kuhn2008].
- In 11 patients, high-frequency STN stimulation reduced STN beta power for 12 s after stimulation stopped and reduced motor cortex-STN beta coherence. Lower beta was associated with larger movement amplitude. These effects were absent for 5-12 Hz activity [Kuhn2008].
- Unresolved: it is not known whether levodopa or STN-DBS reduce akinesia because they reduce beta, or whether the reduction is only correlated with benefit [Galvan2008].

### Alpha-synuclein: gene, protein and normal role
- SNCA lies on the long arm of chromosome 4. A missense mutation (later named A53T) segregated with autosomal dominant PD in an Italian kindred and three Greek families. The protein was described as presynaptic [Polymeropoulos1997].
- The cDNA encodes a 140-amino-acid protein (then called NACP). Its most hydrophobic domain contains a 35-amino-acid peptide (NAC, "non-A-beta component" of Alzheimer amyloid) with a strong predicted tendency to form beta-structure [Ueda1993].
- Three regions for a protein diagram: an amphipathic N-terminal tract (residues 1-60) with alpha-helical tendency, an aggregation-prone central NAC region (61-95), and a negatively charged C-terminal region (96-140) that protects against aggregation [Thrush2022].
- Most post-translational modification sites lie in the C-terminal tail: phosphorylation at Y125, S129, Y133 and Y136, and truncation at D115, D119, P120, E130 and D135 [Oueslati2016].
- Normal role: alpha-synuclein binds the vesicle SNARE protein synaptobrevin-2/VAMP2 directly and promotes SNARE-complex assembly. Mice lacking all three synucleins develop age-dependent neurological impairment and die early [Burre2010].

### Misfolding, phosphorylation and Lewy pathology
- Lewy bodies are immunoreactive for alpha-synuclein (Nature 1997 letter; text not accessed, finding stated from the title) [Spillantini1997].
- Alpha-synuclein-positive Lewy neurites and Lewy bodies are essential for the neuropathological diagnosis of sporadic PD [Braak2003].
- Ser129 is "selectively and extensively" phosphorylated in synucleinopathy lesions, and Ser129 phosphorylation promoted fibril formation in vitro [Fujiwara2002].
- Proportion phosphorylated: under 4% of alpha-synuclein is phosphorylated at S129 in healthy brain, compared with over 90% within Lewy bodies (review citing Fujiwara 2002, Anderson 2006 and others) [Oueslati2016].
- Single phosphorylation at Ser129 is the main modification of alpha-synuclein in Lewy bodies. Lesser modifications are ubiquitination at K12, K21 and K23 and truncations at D115, D119, N122, Y133 and D135. Ubiquitination is found only in Lewy bodies and mostly on phosphorylated synuclein. The same pattern occurs in familial PD (A53T), sporadic disease and multiple system atrophy. This points to accumulation of normally produced pSer129 alpha-synuclein as a key step in Lewy body formation [Anderson2006].
- Correlative light and electron microscopy of human PD brain showed Lewy pathology as a crowded mix of membranes, vesicular structures and misshapen organelles, with high lipid content. Filaments were identifiable "in many but not all" inclusions [Shahmoradian2019].

### Prion-like seeding and strains
- A single injection of synthetic alpha-synuclein fibrils into the striatum of wild-type mice caused cell-to-cell transmission of pathology through connected regions. It also caused progressive loss of SNc (but not VTA) dopamine neurons, reduced dopamine and motor deficits [Luk2012].
- Two people with PD whose transplanted fetal dopamine neurons had survived 11-16 years developed alpha-synuclein-positive Lewy bodies in the grafted cells. This was the first evidence of host-to-graft propagation. Most grafted cells still appeared functional after a decade [Li2008].
- Fourteen years after transplantation, grafted nigral neurons in another person had Lewy body-like inclusions positive for alpha-synuclein and ubiquitin, and reduced DAT staining [Kordower2008].
- Structurally distinct alpha-synuclein assemblies ("strains": oligomers, ribbons, fibrils) injected into rat brain amplified in vivo and produced different pathology and behaviour. Fibrils were the most toxic. Ribbons produced combined PD and MSA features. The assemblies crossed the blood-brain barrier after intravenous injection [Peelaerts2015].

### Gut-brain and vagus hypothesis
- Dual-hit hypothesis: a neurotropic pathogen, probably viral, enters by two routes. One is nasal, spreading forward into the temporal lobe. The other is gastric, from swallowed nasal secretions, entering Meissner's plexus and travelling back up the vagus to the medulla, then to pons, midbrain and substantia nigra [Hawkes2007].
- Lewy bodies were found in Auerbach's and Meissner's plexuses in all 7 PD patients examined, most often in the Auerbach plexus of the lower oesophagus. They were also found, in small numbers, in 8 of 24 age-matched people without PD [Wakabayashi1988].
- Denmark, vagotomy 1977-1995: truncal vagotomy versus the general population gave HR 0.85 (95% CI 0.63-1.14) overall and HR 0.53 (0.28-0.99) with more than 20 years of follow-up. Superselective vagotomy gave HR 1.09 (0.84-1.43). The authors noted that statistical precision was limited [Svensson2015].
- Sweden (9,430 vagotomised people versus 377,200 matched references, 1970-2010): vagotomy overall was not associated with PD risk (HR 0.96, 0.78-1.17). Truncal vagotomy gave HR 0.78 (0.55-1.09) and HR 0.59 (0.37-0.93) when performed at least 5 years before PD diagnosis. Selective vagotomy showed no association [Liu2017].
- Controversy: a reanalysis of the same Danish population with follow-up extended to 2011 (Tysnes et al., Ann Neurol 2015 letter, PMID 26418122; reported here through Liu 2017, the letter itself not read) found HR 0.88 (0.55-1.21) for truncal vagotomy and a non-significantly raised HR 1.14 (0.23-2.05) beyond 20 years [Liu2017].
- Appendectomy, for: in Swedish registry data (over 1.6 million people, 91 million person-years), appendectomy was linked to a 19.3% lower PD risk (95% CI 10.4-27.2%), with a 25.4% lower risk in rural residents and none in urban residents. In the same paper's PPMI analysis, appendectomy at least 30 years before onset was linked to PD onset 3.6 years later. Healthy human appendix tissue contained intraneuronal alpha-synuclein aggregates [Killinger2018].
- Appendectomy, against: a meta-analysis of 6 studies (3,554,540 people) found no effect on PD risk (RR 1.02, 0.87-1.20, I-squared 83.1%) and no significant delay in onset [Lu2020].

### Contributing mechanisms
- Four people who injected an illicit meperidine-analogue preparation containing MPTP developed marked chronic parkinsonism. The authors proposed that MPTP selectively damages substantia nigra cells [Langston1983].
- Chronic systemic exposure of rats to rotenone, a lipophilic pesticide that inhibits complex I, caused selective nigrostriatal degeneration, hypokinesia and rigidity. Nigral neurons developed fibrillar inclusions containing ubiquitin and alpha-synuclein [Betarbet2000].
- Mitophagy steps for an animation: PINK1 is normally kept low on healthy, polarised mitochondria by voltage-dependent proteolysis. It builds up rapidly on damaged mitochondria, which is necessary and sufficient to recruit Parkin. Parkin then triggers removal of the mitochondrion by autophagy. Disease mutations block this at distinct steps [Narendra2010].
- Lysosomal loop: loss of glucocerebrosidase (GCase) function impairs lysosomal protein breakdown and leads to alpha-synuclein build-up. Its substrate, glucosylceramide, stabilises soluble oligomers. Alpha-synuclein in turn inhibits lysosomal GCase, closing a two-way positive feedback loop [Mazzulli2011].
- Iron: total iron in the PD substantia nigra was 31-35% higher than in controls, while globus pallidus iron was 29% lower. The authors suggested that excess nigral iron may promote toxic oxygen radicals [Dexter1989].

### Braak staging (2003)
- Braak staging is a six-stage model built from a retrospective study of incidental and clinically diagnosed cases, using unusually thick (100 micrometre) sections from many brain regions [Braak2017].
- Stage 1: lesions start in the dorsal motor nucleus of the glossopharyngeal and vagal nerves (IX/X) and the anterior olfactory nucleus [Braak2003]. In Burke's summary, region 1 is the medulla oblongata (DMN of the vagus) or the olfactory bulb [Burke2008].
- Stage 2: adds rostrally adjacent lower brainstem structures, including the caudal raphe nuclei and the locus coeruleus [Burke2008]. Braak's lower-brainstem sequence also includes the coeruleus-subcoeruleus complex and the gigantocellular reticular nucleus [Visanji2013]. Placing the gigantocellular nucleus specifically in stage 2 relies on the Braak 2003 full text, which was not accessed (unverified) [Braak2003; Visanji2013].
- Stage 3: the substantia nigra and other midbrain and forebrain nuclear grays [Braak2003; Visanji2013]. The amygdala's central subnucleus is involved in stage 3, followed by the basolateral subnuclei [Braak2017]. The pedunculopontine nucleus belongs to this midbrain step [Visanji2013; Burke2008]. Inclusion of the basal nucleus of Meynert at stage 3 is unverified [Braak2003].
- Stage 4: the cortex is first involved, starting with the anteromedial temporal mesocortex [Braak2003], specifically the transentorhinal region, after amygdala pathology [Braak2017]. Allocortical (hippocampal CA2) involvement at stage 4 is unverified. Braak 2017 notes CA2/CA3 Lewy neurites without giving a stage [Braak2017].
- Stage 5: neocortex, starting with high-order sensory association and prefrontal areas [Braak2003].
- Stage 6: first-order sensory association and premotor areas, then primary sensory and motor fields [Braak2003].
- Clinical threshold: stages 1-2 are thought presymptomatic (incidental Lewy body disease), stages 3-4 bring motor symptoms, and stages 5-6 (cortical) are often linked to cognitive impairment [Jellinger2009; Burke2008].
- Braak and Del Tredici state that PD staging does not apply to "amygdala-predominant" cases. In about 43-60% of late-stage Alzheimer cases, Lewy pathology is mostly or only in the amygdala. Such cases were not in the 2003 staging sample [Braak2017].

### Criticisms of Braak staging
- In 71 PD brains, the SN was affected in 100% and the nucleus basalis of Meynert in 98.5%. 53% fitted caudo-rostral spread and 47% did not. In 7% the DMV was spared despite SN and cortical inclusions [Kalaitzakis2008].
- Of 1,720 autopsies, 14% (248) had alpha-synuclein lesions. Of 226 assessable subjects, 83% fitted current staging systems and 17% departed from caudo-rostral spread. About 55% of those at Braak stages 5-6 had no clinical signs of dementia or extrapyramidal symptoms [Parkkinen2008].
- Across studies, 6.3-43% of cases did not follow caudo-rostral progression. 7-8.3% of clinical PD cases at stages 4-5 had spared medullary nuclei. 30-55% of elderly people with widespread pathology (stages 5-6) had no definite neuropsychiatric symptoms. There is no relationship between Braak stage and clinical PD severity [Jellinger2009].
- Burke and colleagues argue that predictive validity is doubtful. Many aged people without neurological signs have stage 4-6 synucleinopathy at death, and some people at stages 3-4 had severe PD (Hoehn and Yahr 5). They also note no relation between Braak stage and clinical severity [Burke2008].
- In Zaccai and colleagues' population cohorts (cited by Burke), 46% of 76 brains with synuclein pathology did not fit the Braak scheme, and 29% had amygdala-predominant pathology [Burke2008].
- Braak's group's reply: the staging framework has shaped research on the peripheral nervous system, defined prodromal cohorts and supported spreading models. The earliest lesions are proposed in the olfactory bulb, the DMV and possibly the enteric nervous system [Braak2017].

### Body-first vs brain-first, and Unified staging
- Body-first versus brain-first hypothesis. Body-first (PNS-first) PD: pathology starts in the enteric or peripheral autonomic nervous system and climbs the vagus, with prodromal REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD) and autonomic damage before dopaminergic involvement. Brain-first (CNS-first) PD is usually RBD-negative in the prodrome, with nigrostriatal dysfunction before autonomic involvement [Borghammer2019].
- At Hoehn and Yahr stage I, about 40-50% of PD patients still have normal cardiac sympathetic (MIBG) imaging. More than 94% of people with isolated RBD have markedly abnormal MIBG. People with isolated RBD show on average only a 20-25% reduction in putaminal dopaminergic imaging markers, compared with more than 50% in H&Y I-II PD. About 30% of H&Y I-II patients are expected to be RBD-positive [Borghammer2019].
- Imaging data (37 de novo PD: 24 RBD-negative, 13 RBD-positive; plus 22 isolated RBD): RBD-positive PD and isolated RBD showed lower cardiac MIBG and lower colonic 11C-donepezil signal than RBD-negative PD. RBD-positive PD had larger colon volume and slower transit. Two-thirds of isolated RBD patients had normal putaminal FDOPA scans. Ordered trajectories: body-first: loss of cardiac MIBG and colonic donepezil signal first, then putaminal FDOPA loss. Brain-first: putaminal FDOPA loss first, then MIBG and donepezil loss [Horsager2020].
- SOC model: brain-first PD often begins on one side, frequently in the amygdala or olfactory bulb, and spreads mostly on the same side because brain connectivity is mostly ipsilateral. This would explain motor asymmetry. Body-first pathology reaches both left and right DMV through overlapping vagal gut innervation, giving more symmetric disease, a larger burden at diagnosis and faster cognitive decline [Borghammer2021].
- Unified Staging System (417 brains, 10 regions): I olfactory bulb only, IIa brainstem predominant, IIb limbic predominant, III brainstem and limbic, IV neocortical. All subjects could be classified, whereas 42% were unclassifiable by Braak staging and 50% by the DLB Consortium system. Moving through the unified stages generally brought stepwise worsening of striatal TH, nigral pigmented-neuron loss, MMSE and UPDRS part 3 scores. Stage IIb (limbic predominant) had less nigrostriatal degeneration than IIa [Beach2009].

### Non-dopaminergic and peripheral systems
- In 19 PD cases, neuron loss was greatest in the locus coeruleus (noradrenaline; effect size 6.47), followed by the SN (2.58) and the nucleus basalis (acetylcholine; 0.85) [Zarow2003].
- Raphe/serotonin (this study also shows the putamen-heavy dopamine loss): post-mortem striatal dopamine was down 80% in the caudate and 98% in the putamen. Serotonin markers were less reduced (30-66%) and fell more in the caudate than the putamen, e.g. serotonin minus 66% versus minus 51% and SERT minus 56% versus minus 30% [Kish2008].
- Cholinergic system and gait: of 44 non-demented PD patients, the 38.6% with falls had 12.3% lower cortical acetylcholinesterase activity (non-fallers 6.6% lower) and 11.8% lower thalamic activity. Thalamic activity partly reflects PPN output. Nigrostriatal dopamine activity did not differ between fallers and non-fallers [Bohnen2009].
- Heart: 20 of 29 PD patients (all 9 with sympathetic neurocirculatory failure plus 11 without) had low cardiac 6-[18F]fluorodopamine uptake. This was unrelated to disease duration, severity or levodopa, which points to cardiac sympathetic denervation [Goldstein2000].
- Skin nerves: in a 30-site blinded study, phosphorylated alpha-synuclein was found on skin biopsy in 92.7% of PD (89/96), 96.0% of DLB, 98.2% of MSA, 100% of pure autonomic failure and 3.3% of controls [Gibbons2024].
