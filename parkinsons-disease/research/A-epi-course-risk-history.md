# A. Epidemiology, natural history, risk and protective factors, history
Scope: this file covers global, North American and Israeli burden, incidence and prevalence by age and sex, the share of parkinsonism that is Parkinson's disease (PD), Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) staging and time spent in each stage, motor subtypes, long-term cohort outcomes and mortality, risk and inverse-association factors with effect sizes, trials that tested whether those factors could be used for neuroprotection, and history from James Parkinson (1817) to deep brain stimulation, World Parkinson's Day and rasagiline. Facts come from PubMed abstracts that I fetched through E-utilities, from Europe PMC/PMC full text where it was available (Savica 2016 tables, Willis 2022 tables, Goetz 2011), from the Project Gutenberg text of Parkinson's 1817 essay, from ClinicalTrials.gov and openFDA Drugs@FDA records, and from WHO, Nobel Prize, Technion and Parkinson's NSW web pages (all opened 2026-10-20). I could not open these: the full text of Hoehn and Yahr 1967 (Neurology returned 403), Goetz 2004 (Wiley returned 403), Hirsch 2016 (Karger, abstract only), the Finberg 2020 rasagiline history (Springer paywall, and PubMed has no abstract) and the Cotzias 1967 NEJM text (PubMed has no abstract). The verbatim H&Y wording and the 1967 cohort numbers below are therefore taken from secondary reproductions and marked. The current WHO fact sheet does not contain the sentence "growing faster than any other neurological disorder". That claim is citable to Dorsey 2018 instead. The source count (49) is slightly above the 45 target because the brief asked for many specific primary papers.

## Facts
### Global burden and projections
- WHO fact sheet (dated 9 August 2023): over 8.5 million people had PD worldwide in 2019. Prevalence has doubled in the past 25 years. In 2019 PD caused 5.8 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), up 81% since 2000, and 329,000 deaths, up more than 100% since 2000. [WHO2023PD]
- GBD 2016: 6.1 million people (95% UI 5.0-7.3) had PD in 2016, compared with 2.5 million (2.0-3.0) in 1990. Age-standardised prevalence rose by 21.7% (95% UI 18.1-25.3) and crude prevalence by 74.3%, so the rise is not explained by ageing alone. [GBD2018PD]
- GBD 2016: PD caused 3.2 million DALYs (95% UI 2.6-4.0) and 211,296 deaths in 2016. The male:female ratio of age-standardised prevalence was 1.40 (1.36-1.43) in 2016 and 1.37 in 1990. [GBD2018PD]
- GBD 2016: age-standardised prevalence, DALY rates and death rates rose from 1990 to 2016 in every GBD region except southern Latin America, eastern Europe and Oceania. The authors attribute the rise mainly to more older people, with possible contributions from longer disease duration and environmental factors. [GBD2018PD]
- Dorsey and colleagues call PD "the fastest growing neurological disorder in the world". They project that the number of people with PD will double from over 6 million (2015) to over 12 million by 2040, driven mainly by ageing. Longer lifespans, falling smoking rates and industrialisation could push the figure above 17 million ("Parkinson pandemic"). [Dorsey2018]
- Su et al. (BMJ 2025, GBD 2021 modelling) project 25.2 million people with PD in 2050 (95% UI 21.7-30.1), a 112% increase from 2021. Of the growth, 89% is attributed to population ageing, 20% to population growth and 3% to changes in prevalence. [Su2025]
- Su et al. project an all-age prevalence of 267 per 100,000 in 2050 (up 76% from 2021) and an age-standardised prevalence of 216 per 100,000 (up 55%). East Asia would have the most cases (10.9 million), and western sub-Saharan Africa the largest rise (292%). [Su2025]
- Su et al. project the largest increase in case numbers among people aged 80 and over (196%). They also project the global male:female ratio of age-standardised prevalence to rise from 1.46 (2021) to 1.64 (2050). [Su2025]
- Disagreement: the projections differ in size (more than 12 million by 2040 [Dorsey2018] versus 25.2 million by 2050 [Su2025]) because they use different GBD rounds, base years and assumptions. Both identify ageing as the main driver. [Dorsey2018; Su2025]

### Prevalence and incidence by age and sex
- Worldwide meta-analysis of 47 studies, prevalence per 100,000 by age: 41 (40-49 y), 107 (50-59), 173 (55-64), 428 (60-69), 425 (65-74), 1,087 (70-79) and 1,903 (80+). These are the points for a prevalence curve. [Pringsheim2014]
- The same meta-analysis found that prevalence at ages 70-79 was 1,601 per 100,000 in North America, Europe and Australia, compared with 646 in Asia. The only significant sex difference was at ages 50-59 (41 per 100,000 in females, 134 in males). [Pringsheim2014]
- North America: prevalence among people aged 45 and over is estimated at 572 per 100,000 (95% CI 537-614). That corresponds to about 680,000 people with PD in the US in 2010, projected to reach about 930,000 in 2020 and 1,238,000 in 2030. The authors describe these as minimum estimates. [Marras2018]
- North America, 2012: age- and sex-adjusted incidence ranged from 108 to 212 per 100,000 among people aged 65 and over, and from 47 to 77 per 100,000 among people aged 45 and over, across five data sources. [Willis2022]
- North America, 2012: the male:female incidence rate ratio at ages 65 and over was 1.66 (Ontario), 1.69 (Medicare), 1.77 (Kaiser) and 2.57 (Rochester). For example, Medicare incidence was 277 per 100,000 in men and 161 in women. [Willis2022]
- Age-specific PD incidence in Olmsted County, Minnesota (1976-2005), per 100,000 person-years for men / women / both: 0-39 y, 0.2 / 0.0 / 0.1; 40-59 y, 11.7 / 7.0 / 9.2; 60-69 y, 80.8 / 32.6 / 55.1; 70-79 y, 179.2 / 77.7 / 119.2; 80-99 y, 211.4 / 101.8 / 133.9. The crude rate for all ages was 14.0 and the age-standardised rate 17.2. These are the points for an age-incidence curve. [Savica2016]
- In the same cohort the median age at onset of PD was 73 years (IQR 64-80), and 275 of 464 patients (59%) were men. In men, incidence rose over the 30 years (RR 1.24 per decade, 95% CI 1.08-1.43), mostly at ages 70 and over. The authors note that this trend might reflect changes in smoking or could be spurious. [Savica2016]
- Kaiser Permanente Northern California (1994-1995): age- and gender-adjusted incidence was 13.4 per 100,000 (95% CI 11.4-15.5). The rate in men (19.0) was 91% higher than in women (9.9). Incidence rose rapidly after age 60, and only 4% of cases began before age 50. [VanDenEeden2003]
- Kaiser Permanente by ethnicity, adjusted incidence per 100,000: Hispanic 16.6, non-Hispanic White 13.6, Asian 11.3, Black 10.2. [VanDenEeden2003]
- An incidence meta-analysis of 27 studies (2001-2014) found that incidence rises with age in both sexes and may level off at 80 and over, where heterogeneity between studies was significant. Males had higher incidence in every age group, but the difference was statistically significant only at 60-69 and 70-79. [Hirsch2016]

### Young-onset / early-onset definitions
- The MDS Task Force (2022) recommends the term "early-onset PD" (EOPD) rather than "young-onset" (YOPD), partly to avoid age-related stigma. It defines EOPD as onset after age 21 and before age 50. [Mehanna2022]
- The task force notes that published cutoffs for EOPD/YOPD have ranged from 40 to 60 years, that 56% of countries use 50 or 51, and that most international genetic studies use under 50. Onset before 21 is conventionally called juvenile parkinsonism. [Mehanna2022]

### Israel
- In a drug-tracer study of an Israeli health maintenance organisation covering about 25% of the population (1998-2008), PD incidence was stable at about 33 per 100,000 from 2000 to 2007. Prevalence rose from 170 to 256 per 100,000. Median prevalence was 1% at age 50+, 2% at 60+ and 3% at 70+. Mean age at first anti-parkinsonian drug purchase was 69 ± 13 years. [ChillagTalmor2011]
- In the same study, men aged 50 and over had higher prevalence (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.37-1.39) and incidence (RR 1.45, 1.42-1.48) than women. [ChillagTalmor2011]
- Clalit Health Services registry (2002-2021): the age-adjusted incidence of PD fell from 57 to 20.3 per 100,000, a 2.8-fold decrease. It fell faster in women (AAPC -5.3%) than in men (AAPC -4.5%). The authors suggest that better diagnosis and better health and environmental conditions could explain the decline. This trend runs against the rising trends reported elsewhere. [Balash2026; Savica2016]

### Parkinsonism versus PD
- Olmsted County (1976-1990), 364 incident cases of parkinsonism: 42% PD, 20% drug-induced parkinsonism, 17% unspecified, 14% parkinsonism in dementia and 7% other causes. [Bower1999]
- In the same study, incidence of parkinsonism at ages 50-99 was 114.7 per 100,000 person-years, rising from 0.8 (ages 0-29) to 304.8 (ages 80-99). Cumulative incidence to age 90 was 7.5%, assuming no competing causes of death. Men had higher incidence than women for every type except drug-induced parkinsonism. [Bower1999]

### Hoehn and Yahr staging
- Original H&Y stages (1967), worded as reproduced in secondary sources because the original text was not accessible: Stage I, unilateral involvement only, usually with minimal or no functional impairment. Stage II, bilateral or midline involvement, without impairment of balance. Stage III, first signs of impaired righting reflexes (unsteadiness on turning or when pushed); some restriction of activities, but patients remain physically able to lead independent lives. Stage IV, fully developed, severely disabling disease; the patient can still walk and stand unassisted but is markedly incapacitated. Stage V, confinement to bed or wheelchair unless aided. (wording unverified against the original) [Hoehn1967; Goetz2004HY]
- Modified H&Y scale, as commonly reproduced: 0 no signs of disease; 1 unilateral disease; 1.5 unilateral plus axial involvement; 2 bilateral disease without impairment of balance; 2.5 mild bilateral disease with recovery on pull test; 3 mild to moderate bilateral disease, some postural instability, physically independent; 4 severe disability, still able to walk or stand unassisted; 5 wheelchair-bound or bedridden unless aided. (secondary reproduction) [Goetz2004HY]
- MDS Task Force (2004): the modified 0.5-increment version has been widely adopted but has no clinimetric data. The task force recommends keeping the original five-point scale until the modified version is tested, using the original for describing patient groups (reported as medians and ranges, with non-parametric analysis), and that clinicians "rate what you see", including comorbidities. [Goetz2004HY]
- MDS Task Force (2004): H&Y is weighted heavily toward postural instability, mixes impairment with disability, is non-linear and gives no information on non-motor problems. Its reliability and validity are best supported for the middle stages (2-4). [Goetz2004HY]
- The 1967 cohort included 672 patients with "primary parkinsonism". 61% were severely disabled or dead after 5-9 years, and more than 80% of those followed for more than 10 years. Mortality was reported as about 2.9 times that of the general population. These figures come from secondary summaries; the original was not accessed (unverified). [Hoehn1967]
- Singapore cohort (n=695, mean age 65.2), median time to move from one H&Y stage to the next: 1→2, 20 months; 2→2.5, 62 months; 2.5→3, 25 months; 3→4, 24 months; 4→5, 26 months. Older age at diagnosis, longer disease duration and higher baseline UPDRS motor scores predicted faster transitions. Sex and ethnicity did not. [Zhao2010]

### Motor subtypes and prognosis
- In the DATATOP cohort (800 untreated early PD), 441 patients were tremor-dominant and 233 PIGD-dominant (postural instability and gait difficulty). The PIGD group reported more intellectual, motor and occupational impairment. Older age at onset with bradykinesia or PIGD was linked to more functional disability. [Jankovic1990]
- In DATATOP, patients with onset at age 40 or younger reached the same level of disability as those with onset at 70 or older more slowly (2.9 versus 1.7 years). Patients with rapid progression ("malignant PD") more often had bradykinesia and PIGD at onset. [Jankovic1990]
- In CamPaIGN, a non-tremor-dominant motor phenotype, together with age and comorbidity, predicted earlier postural instability (H&Y 3). [WilliamsGray2013]

### Long-term cohorts
- Sydney Multicentre Study at 20 years: 100 of 136 patients (74%) had died. Dementia was present in 83% of 20-year survivors, 48% were in nursing homes and only one person lived independently. [Hely2008]
- Sydney at 20 years, frequency of each problem: falls 87%, freezing 81%, moderate dysarthria 81%, hallucinations 74%, urinary incontinence 71%, excessive daytime sleepiness 70%, symptomatic postural hypotension 48%, choking 48%, fractures 35%. Most patients had dyskinesia and end-of-dose failure, but the main problems were features that do not respond to levodopa. [Hely2008]
- Sydney mortality: the death rate fell in the first 3 years of treatment and then rose relative to the general population. The standardised mortality ratio (SMR) reached 3.1 for years 15-20. [Hely2008]
- CamPaIGN at 10 years (incident population-based cohort, n=142): 55% had died, 68% had postural instability and 46% had dementia. 23% had a "good outcome", alive without dementia or postural instability. [WilliamsGray2013]
- CamPaIGN: the SMR was 1.29 (0.97-1.61). Death certificates named PD as a substantial contributor in only 20% of deaths, and pneumonia was the commonest cause of death. Baseline predictors of dementia were age, motor impairment, "posterior-cortical" cognitive deficits and MAPT genotype. [WilliamsGray2013]

### Mortality
- A meta-analysis of 88 studies found mortality ratios ranging from 0.9 to 3.8, with large heterogeneity. Inception cohorts gave a more consistent pooled ratio of about 1.5. On average, survival fell by about 5% for each year of follow-up. [Macleod2014]
- In post-mortem studies, mean disease duration until death ranged from 6.9 to 14.3 years. Older age and dementia were the factors most often linked to higher mortality. There was no robust evidence that mortality fell after levodopa was introduced. [Macleod2014]
- Disagreement: SMRs differ widely by design. Examples are 1.29 in an incident population cohort at 10 years [WilliamsGray2013], about 1.5 pooled from inception cohorts [Macleod2014] and 3.1 at 15-20 years in the Sydney cohort [Hely2008]. Mortality ratios rise with longer follow-up. [Macleod2014]
- Pneumonia was the most common cause of death in CamPaIGN. PD is under-recorded on death certificates. [WilliamsGray2013]
- No citable single figure for "median survival from diagnosis" or "years of life lost" was found in the abstracts read. The closest data are 55% dead at 10 years [WilliamsGray2013], 74% dead at 20 years [Hely2008] and mean duration to death of 6.9-14.3 years [Macleod2014].

### Risk factors (positive associations)
- Age is the strongest risk factor: incidence rises from 0.1 per 100,000 under 40 to 133.9 per 100,000 at 80-99. [Savica2016]
- Male sex: the GBD male:female prevalence ratio is 1.40, and North American incidence ratios at 65 and over are 1.66-2.57. [GBD2018PD; Willis2022]
- Family history (meta-analysis): a first-degree relative with PD gives OR 3.23 (95% CI 2.65-3.93), any relative with PD OR 4.45 (3.39-5.83) and any relative with tremor OR 2.74 (2.10-3.57). [Noyce2012]
- The same meta-analysis found constipation RR 2.34 (1.55-3.53) and significant positive associations with anxiety or depression, pesticide exposure, head injury, rural living, beta-blockers, farming and well-water drinking. Constipation, anxiety and depression may be prodromal features rather than causes. [Noyce2012]
- Pesticides (Agricultural Health Study, 110 cases and 358 controls): mitochondrial complex I inhibitors as a group OR 1.7 (1.0-2.8), rotenone OR 2.5 (1.3-4.7); pesticides causing oxidative stress as a group OR 2.0 (1.2-3.6), paraquat OR 2.5 (1.4-4.7). [Tanner2011]
- Trichloroethylene (TCE), in 99 PD-discordant twin pairs: ever exposure OR 6.1 (95% CI 1.2-33). Perchloroethylene OR 10.5 (0.97-113) and carbon tetrachloride OR 2.3 (0.9-6.1) were borderline. The wide CIs reflect the small sample. [Goldman2012]
- Camp Lejeune, where water had monthly median TCE levels more than 70 times the permissible amount, compared with Camp Pendleton (about 158,000 veterans with health data): PD prevalence was 0.33% versus 0.21%, OR 1.70 (1.39-2.07). There was no excess of other neurodegenerative parkinsonism, and prodromal features were more common at Camp Lejeune. [Goldman2023]
- Head injury: a meta-analysis of 22 studies gives a pooled OR of 1.57 (1.35-1.83) for head trauma with concussion. [Jafari2013]
- Dairy: in two US cohorts (Nurses' Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-up Study, 1,036 cases), eating at least 3 servings a day of low-fat dairy gave HR 1.34 (1.01-1.79), and skim/low-fat milk HR 1.39 (1.12-1.73). Total dairy intake was not significantly associated. [Hughes2017]
- Methamphetamine/amphetamine use disorder (Utah records): HR 2.8 (1.6-4.8) for PD compared with matched controls. Cocaine users had no excess risk. [Curtin2015]
- Melanoma: pooled OR 2.11 (1.26-3.54) for the association with PD. Melanoma was significantly more common after a PD diagnosis (OR 3.61, 1.49-8.77) than before (OR 1.07, 0.62-1.84, or 1.44 [1.06-1.96] after excluding one study). [Liu2011]
- A 2016 review lists pesticides, dairy, melanoma and traumatic brain injury as associated with higher risk. It lists smoking, caffeine, higher serum urate, physical activity and ibuprofen as associated with lower risk. [Ascherio2016]

### Inverse associations, reverse causation and confounding
- Smoking (meta-analysis of 44 case-control and 4 cohort studies), RR versus never smokers: ever smokers 0.59 (0.54-0.63), past smokers 0.80 (0.69-0.93), current smokers 0.39 (0.32-0.47). RR per 10 pack-years was 0.84 in case-control studies and 0.78 in cohort studies. [Hernan2002]
- Coffee: coffee drinkers RR 0.69 (0.59-0.80) versus non-drinkers. Per 3 cups a day, RR was 0.75 (case-control studies) and 0.68 (0.46-1.00, cohort studies). [Hernan2002]
- In a later meta-analysis, current smoking compared with never smoking gave RR 0.44 (0.39-0.50). Coffee, hypertension, NSAIDs, calcium-channel blockers and alcohol were inversely associated. [Noyce2012]
- Reverse causation in smoking: in a Danish case-control study (1,808 patients), former smokers who found quitting harder, or used nicotine substitutes, were less likely to develop PD. The authors interpret "ease of quitting" as an early sign of PD rather than proof that tobacco protects. [Ritz2014]
- Urate (men in the Health Professionals cohort): RR 0.43 (0.18-1.02) for the highest versus lowest urate quartile. Excluding cases diagnosed within about 4 years of blood draw gave RR 0.17 (0.04-0.69), which argues somewhat against reverse causation. [Weisskopf2007]
- Physical activity (8 prospective studies, 544,336 participants, 2,192 cases): highest total activity RR 0.79 (0.68-0.91), and moderate-to-vigorous activity RR 0.71 (0.58-0.87). Light activity was not associated (RR 0.86, 0.60-1.23). Each extra 10 MET-hours a week was linked to 10% lower risk (total) and 17% lower risk (moderate-to-vigorous) in men, with no linear dose-response in women. [Fang2018]
- A 2016 review concludes that the only intervention that currently seems justifiable for primary prevention of PD is promoting physical activity. [Ascherio2016]
- Interpretation caveat: observational associations may be affected by reverse causation (prodromal PD changing behaviour such as smoking, activity or diet) and by confounding. Trials of urate elevation and of isradipine, both chosen because of observational associations, showed no slowing of progression (see next section). [Ritz2014; Schwarzschild2021; PSG2020STEADY]

### Neuroprotection and prevention trials of these factors
- SURE-PD3 (inosine to raise urate; 298 people with early, untreated PD and urate below 5.8 mg/dL; 58 US sites): stopped early for futility. MDS-UPDRS I-III progression was 11.1 points a year on inosine and 9.9 on placebo (difference 1.26, 95% CI -0.59 to 3.11). Urate rose by 2.03 mg/dL (44%). Kidney stones occurred at 7.0 versus 1.4 per 100 patient-years. [Schwarzschild2021]
- STEADY-PD III (isradipine 5 mg twice daily for 36 months, 336 patients with early PD, 57 sites): change in total UPDRS was 2.99 versus 3.26 points, treatment effect -0.27 (-3.02 to 2.48), P=0.85. Secondary outcomes showed no effect. Oedema and dizziness were the commonest adverse effects. The authors note that the dose may not have engaged the target. [PSG2020STEADY]
- SPARX (phase 2, 128 people with de novo PD, H&Y 1-2): high-intensity treadmill exercise (4 days a week at 80-85% of maximum heart rate) gave a 6-month UPDRS motor change of 0.3, compared with 3.2 in wait-list controls (P=.03). Only the high-intensity arm met the non-futility threshold. [Schenkman2018]
- SPARX3 (NCT04284436, phase 3: treadmill exercise 4 times a week at 80-85% versus 60-65% of maximum heart rate): status on ClinicalTrials.gov when opened 2026-10-20 was "Active, not recruiting". It started on 30 August 2021, enrolled 370 participants, and its estimated primary completion is July 2027. Its primary outcome is change in motor symptoms. [SPARX3]
- Park-in-Shape (130 sedentary patients, H&Y 2 or lower, on medication): home-based gamified aerobic cycling, 30-45 minutes 3 times a week for 6 months, improved the off-state MDS-UPDRS motor score by 4.2 points (95% CI 1.6-6.9) compared with stretching. The pre-set clinically relevant threshold was 3.5. [vanderKolk2019]

### History
- James Parkinson's "An Essay on the Shaking Palsy" was printed in London in 1817 by Whittingham and Rowland for Sherwood, Neely, and Jones. The author is styled "Member of the Royal College of Surgeons". [Parkinson1817]
- Parkinson's definition of the disease (public-domain text): "Involuntary tremulous motion, with lessened muscular power, in parts not in action and even when supported; with a propensity to bend the trunk forwards, and to pass from a walking to a running pace: the senses and intellects being uninjured." [Parkinson1817]
- The essay presents six cases (Case I-VI). Several were people seen casually in the street. Case II was a 62-year-old man met in the street, and Case V a gentleman "only seen at a distance". [Parkinson1817; Goetz2011]
- Parkinson also described disturbed sleep, with tremor waking the patient; "torpid" bowels needing strong purgatives; drooling ("the saliva... continually draining from the mouth"); difficulty chewing and swallowing; and late loss of speech and incontinence. These are non-motor features now recognised in PD. [Parkinson1817]
- Parkinson placed the cause in the upper cervical spinal cord ("medulla spinalis"), extending to the medulla oblongata. He did not describe rigidity or bradykinesia as cardinal features. He also stated that the intellect is spared, which later cohorts showing dementia in 83% of 20-year survivors contradict. [Parkinson1817; Goetz2011; Hely2008]
- Jean-Martin Charcot, lecturing at the Salpêtrière from the 1870s, added bradykinesia and rigidity, separated PD from multiple sclerosis and other tremor disorders, and rejected the names "paralysis agitans" and "shaking palsy" in favour of naming the disease after Parkinson, noting that patients are not markedly weak. The French form "maladie de Parkinson" is conventional (not verified in the text read). [Goetz2011]
- Charcot used hyoscyamine and other belladonna alkaloids, an early anticholinergic treatment. [Goetz2011]
- Fritz Heinrich Lewy first described the eosinophilic neuronal inclusions in paralysis agitans in 1912. [Holdorff2019]
- Konstantin Tretiakoff's Paris thesis (1919) described loss of pigmented neurons in the substantia nigra and named the inclusions "corps de Lewy" (Lewy bodies). Rolf Hassler later confirmed the nigral findings (1938-1939). Discrepancy: Goetz 2011 dates Tretiakoff's work to 1921. [Holdorff2019; Goetz2011]
- Arvid Carlsson (1957-58) gave reserpine to animals, which depleted brain monoamines and stopped spontaneous movement. L-dopa reversed this and restored brain dopamine, while a serotonin precursor did not. He recognised the similarity to PD, and this work led to L-dopa treatment. He shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Paul Greengard and Eric Kandel "for their discoveries concerning signal transduction in the nervous system". [Nobel2000]
- Ehringer and Hornykiewicz (1960) found striatal dopamine depletion in brains from people with PD and postencephalitic parkinsonism. In 1961 Birkmayer and Hornykiewicz gave intravenous levodopa to patients for the first time. [Goetz2011]
- Cotzias, Van Woert and Schiffer published "Aromatic amino acids and modification of parkinsonism" in NEJM on 16 February 1967, the report that launched high-dose oral treatment. Their 1969 NEJM report described chronic L-dopa treatment. Doses were not verified because no abstract or full text was accessible. [Cotzias1967; Goetz2011]
- FDA approvals (Drugs@FDA): levodopa as Larodopa (Roche, NDA 016912) on 4 June 1970; carbidopa-levodopa as Sinemet (NDA 017555) on 2 May 1975. Carbidopa is a peripheral dopa-decarboxylase inhibitor. [FDA-DrugsFDA; Goetz2011]
- Langston and colleagues (1983) described four people who developed marked parkinsonism after injecting an illicit meperidine-analogue product found to contain MPTP. They proposed that it selectively damages substantia nigra cells. [Langston1983]
- Benabid and colleagues (1987) reported that high-frequency (100 Hz) stimulation of the thalamic VIM nucleus stopped parkinsonian tremor. They combined thalamotomy on one side with chronic VIM stimulation on the other. The stimulator was limited to 130 Hz, while the optimal frequency appeared to be about 200 Hz. This was the origin of modern deep brain stimulation. [Benabid1987]
- World Parkinson's Day is held on 11 April, James Parkinson's birthday. The red-and-white "Dr James Parkinson" tulip was bred in 1980 by J.W.S. Van der Wereld, a Dutch horticulturist who had PD. The red tulip was adopted as the symbol of PD at the 9th World Parkinson's Disease Day Conference in Luxembourg on 11 April 2005. Some secondary pages give 1981 for the tulip. [ParkinsonsNSW2026]
- Rasagiline: Technion professors Moussa Youdim and John Finberg developed rasagiline in the 1970s. Teva Pharmaceuticals then developed it as Azilect. The FDA approved Azilect (Teva, NDA 021641) on 16 May 2006. A European 2005 approval is reported in secondary sources (unverified). [Technion2016; FDA-DrugsFDA]
