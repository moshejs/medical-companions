# G. Non-motor symptoms, living, rehabilitation, caregiving and palliative care
This pass covers how common the non-motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease (PD) are: mood, apathy, psychosis, cognition and dementia, sleep, autonomic problems, pain and fatigue. It also covers how those symptoms are treated according to the 2019 MDS evidence-based review, NICE NG71, FDA labels and the key trials. Further topics are exercise and rehabilitation (physiotherapy, LSVT BIG/LOUD, cueing, occupational therapy, speech and swallowing), falls and freezing, work, driving and pregnancy, medicines timing in hospital, caregiving, palliative care, end of life and quality-of-life measurement. Facts come from PubMed/Europe PMC abstracts. I also read the full texts of Seppi 2019 (PMC6916382), McKeith 2017 (PMC5496518), Gibbons 2017 (PMC5533816), Kluger 2020 (PMC7042842) and Litvan 2012 (PMC3641655, read via pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The NICE NG71 recommendations page and openFDA (Drugs@FDA and label endpoints) were opened on 2026-10-20.

## Facts
### Overall non-motor burden
- In PRIAMO, 1,072 consecutive PD patients across 55 Italian centres were interviewed: 98.6% reported at least one non-motor symptom, with a mean of 7.8 per patient (range 0-32) [Barone2009].
- PRIAMO's most common non-motor symptoms: fatigue 58%, anxiety 56%, leg pain 38%, insomnia 37%, urgency/nocturia 35%, and drooling and difficulty concentrating 31% each. The psychiatric domain was the most frequent at 67%, and non-motor symptoms became more frequent as disease duration and severity increased [Barone2009].
- In PRIAMO, apathy was the symptom most associated with worse PDQ-39 scores. Fatigue, attention/memory and psychiatric symptoms also lowered quality of life [Barone2009].
- In an international NMSS validation study of 411 patients, the most frequent complaints were nocturia (68.4%), fatigue (65.9%) and dribbling saliva (56.7%) [Martinez-Martin2011].
- The total Non-Motor Symptoms Scale correlated more strongly with the PDQ-39 Summary Index (rS = 0.70) than the SCOPA-Motor score did (rS = 0.58). The NMSS was the best predictor of health-related quality of life, and the authors concluded that non-motor symptoms as a whole affect quality of life more than motor symptoms [Martinez-Martin2011].

### Depression, anxiety, apathy
- A systematic review of 36 articles found weighted prevalences of 17% for major depressive disorder, 22% for minor depression and 13% for dysthymia. Clinically significant depressive symptoms were present in 35% [Reijnders2008].
- Published depression rates range from 2.7% to over 90%. Major depression measured 19% with structured DSM interviews but 7% without them, and population studies reported lower rates than other settings. The authors called the true rate "substantial, but lower than generally assumed" [Reijnders2008].
- A meta-analysis of 45 quality studies put the average point prevalence of anxiety disorders at 31%. Generalized anxiety disorder was 14%, social phobia 13.8%, anxiety not otherwise specified 13.3%, specific phobia 13.0% and panic disorder 6.8%. Clinically significant anxiety symptoms on rating scales were present in 25.7% [Broen2016].
- Pooled apathy prevalence was 39.8% across 23 studies (n = 5,388). Apathy was linked to higher age (+3.3 years), lower MMSE (-1.4 points) and a 2.3-fold relative risk of comorbid depression. About half of patients with apathy had concomitant depression (57.2%). The other half had neither depression nor cognitive impairment, which supports apathy as a separate syndrome [denBrok2015].

### Psychosis and hallucinations
- In a population-based Norwegian cohort of 230 patients followed for 12 years, 60% (137) developed hallucinations or delusions. The incidence was 79.7 per 1,000 person-years [Forsaa2010].
- Independent risk factors for new psychosis were higher age at onset, higher baseline levodopa-equivalent dose, probable REM sleep behaviour disorder at baseline and follow-up time. Psychosis tracked with dementia and lower ADL function [Forsaa2010].
- Hallucinations were the strongest predictor of nursing-home admission over 4 years in a population-based study [Aarsland2000].

### Cognition: PD-MCI, dementia, PDD versus DLB
- The MDS Task Force criteria for PD mild cognitive impairment (PD-MCI) require four things: established PD, gradual cognitive decline reported by the patient or an informant or observed by a clinician, deficits on formal testing or a validated global scale, and no significant interference with functional independence [Litvan2012].
- Level I (abbreviated, "possible PD-MCI") requires impairment on a PD-validated global scale or on at least two tests of a limited battery. Level II (comprehensive) requires two tests in each of five domains: attention/working memory, executive, language, memory and visuospatial. Impairment means about 1-2 SD below norms on at least two tests [Litvan2012].
- The Task Force paper reports a mean PD-MCI prevalence of 27% (range 19-38%) in nondemented PD [Litvan2012].
- A systematic review found that 24.5% (95% CI 17.4-31.5) of people with PD had dementia, or 31.1% in the four highest-quality studies. PD dementia accounts for 3-4% of all dementia, and its estimated prevalence is 0.2-0.5% of people aged 65 and over [Aarsland2005].
- In a Norwegian cohort of 224, 26% had dementia at baseline. The 8-year prevalence was 78.2% (95% CI 71.1-84.0). Early hallucinations (OR 3.1) and akinetic-dominant or mixed motor type (OR 3.3) predicted dementia [Aarsland2003].
- In the Sydney multicenter study of newly diagnosed patients, 100 of 136 (74%) had died by 20 years. Dementia was present in 83% of the survivors. In the Sydney cohort, the standardized mortality ratio from 15 to 20 years reached 3.1 [Hely2008].
- Among Sydney 20-year survivors: excessive daytime sleepiness 70%, hallucinations 74%, falls 87%, freezing 81%, fractures 35%, symptomatic postural hypotension 48%, urinary incontinence 71%, moderate dysarthria 81% and choking 48% [Hely2008].
- In the CamPaIGN incident cohort, 46% had dementia at 10 years. 23% had a "good outcome", meaning alive without dementia or postural instability [Williams-Gray2013].
- The DLB Consortium "remains supportive of the 1-year rule distinguishing DLB from PD dementia" and calls the cutoff arbitrary but clinically useful. It may need revising as biology is clarified. Commonly paraphrased: dementia beginning before or within about 1 year of parkinsonism is labelled DLB, and dementia arising in well-established PD is labelled PDD (wording paraphrased; exact table wording not captured). Controversy: the consortium itself describes the cutoff as arbitrary and based on expert opinion, so it may not reflect distinct biology [McKeith2017].

### Sleep
- A 2023 meta-analysis pooled prevalences in PD of 46% for REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD), 35% for excessive daytime sleepiness and 44% for insomnia [Maggi2023].

### Autonomic: blood pressure, bladder, saliva, bowel
- A meta-analysis of 25 studies put the pooled point prevalence of orthostatic hypotension in PD at 30.1% (95% CI 22.9-38.4%). Heterogeneity was large, so the authors advise caution [Velseboer2011].
- Orthostatic hypotension is defined as a sustained drop of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing or head-up tilt. If heart rate rises by less than 15 bpm during the drop, a neurogenic cause is suggested [Gibbons2017].

### Treatment evidence: dementia and psychosis
- The 2019 MDS evidence-based review covered Level I studies through 31 December 2016 and added 37 new studies. No qualifying RCTs existed for anxiety disorders, RBD, excessive sweating, impaired olfaction or eye problems, and no clinically useful treatment was found for non-dementia cognitive impairment [Seppi2019].
- MDS 2019 ratings for PD dementia: rivastigmine was "efficacious" and "clinically useful". Donepezil and galantamine had insufficient evidence but were "possibly useful", and memantine was "investigational" [Seppi2019].
- EXPRESS trial: 541 patients who developed dementia at least 2 years after PD diagnosis were randomised to rivastigmine 3-12 mg/day or placebo for 24 weeks. On the 70-point ADAS-cog, rivastigmine improved scores by 2.1 points while placebo worsened by 0.7 (P<0.001) [Emre2004].
- In EXPRESS, the ADCS-CGIC showed clinically meaningful improvement in 19.8% on rivastigmine vs 14.5% on placebo, and clinically meaningful worsening in 13.0% vs 23.1% (P=0.007). Nausea, vomiting and tremor were more frequent with rivastigmine [Emre2004].
- In FDA records, the rivastigmine (Exelon capsules, NDA 020823) efficacy supplement is dated 27 June 2006. The current label lists "mild-to-moderate dementia associated with Parkinson's disease" as an indication. The link between that supplement and the PDD indication is inferred from the date [openFDA2026].
- NICE: offer a cholinesterase inhibitor for mild or moderate PD dementia, and consider one for severe PD dementia. Consider memantine only if cholinesterase inhibitors are not tolerated or are contraindicated [NICE2017].
- NICE's stepwise approach to hallucinations and delusions: ask about them at reviews and after medicine changes, do a general medical evaluation, do not treat them if they are well tolerated, and reduce any PD medicines that might have triggered them (with specialist advice) [NICE2017].
- MDS 2019 ratings for PD psychosis: clozapine "efficacious", "clinically useful", needing specialized monitoring. Pimavanserin "efficacious", "clinically useful". Quetiapine "insufficient evidence" but "possibly useful". Olanzapine "not efficacious", unacceptable risk, "not useful" [Seppi2019].
- Controversy: NICE suggests considering quetiapine for people without cognitive impairment and offering clozapine if standard treatment fails. MDS notes that no high-quality RCTs show quetiapine works, while also calling low-dose quetiapine a "pragmatic first choice" on safety grounds [NICE2017; Seppi2019].
- NICE: do not offer olanzapine. Other antipsychotics such as phenothiazines and butyrophenones (the class that includes haloperidol) can worsen motor features. Lower doses of quetiapine and clozapine are needed in PD than in other indications [NICE2017].
- Parkinson Study Group trial: 60 patients (mean age 72) with drug-induced psychosis received low-dose clozapine 6.25-50 mg/day (mean 24.7 mg) for 4 weeks with weekly blood counts. Clinical Global Impression improved by 1.6 points on clozapine vs 0.5 on placebo, without worsening parkinsonism [PSG1999].
- Pimavanserin phase 3 trial: a 2-week non-drug lead-in, then 6 weeks of 40 mg/day vs placebo in 199 patients. SAPS-PD fell by 5.79 vs 2.73 points (difference -3.06, 95% CI -4.91 to -1.20, p=0.001, Cohen's d 0.50), with no worsening of motor function [Cummings2014].
- Pimavanserin (Nuplazid, NDA 207318) was approved on 29 April 2016 "for the treatment of hallucinations and delusions associated with Parkinson's disease psychosis". It carries a boxed warning about increased mortality in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis [openFDA2026].
- MDS warns that all atypical antipsychotics need great caution in psychotic patients with dementia, because of falls, cognitive worsening, pneumonia, cardiovascular effects, stroke and death. Pimavanserin's controlled safety data do not extend beyond 6 weeks [Seppi2019].

### Treatment evidence: depression and apathy
- Antidepressant RCT: 115 patients were randomised to paroxetine (up to 40 mg), venlafaxine XR (up to 225 mg) or placebo for 12 weeks. Relative to placebo, HAM-D fell by 6.2 points with paroxetine (p=0.0007) and 4.2 points with venlafaxine XR (p=0.02), with no worsening of motor function [Richard2012].
- MDS 2019 rates venlafaxine "efficacious" and "clinically useful" for depression in PD. Some other SSRIs (paroxetine, citalopram, fluoxetine) are rated "insufficient evidence" but "possibly useful" [Seppi2019].
- CBT RCT: 80 depressed patients received 10 weeks of PD-adapted CBT or clinical monitoring. At week 10, HAM-D improved by 7.35 points with CBT vs 0.05 with monitoring, and 56% vs 8% responded. MDS rates CBT "likely efficacious" and "possibly useful" for depression. Double-blinding is impossible and adverse-event reporting is limited [Dobkin2011; Seppi2019].
- MDS rated rivastigmine "efficacious" for apathy on the basis of one small, high-quality study. Its practice implication is "possibly useful" [Seppi2019].

### Treatment evidence: blood pressure, bowel, bladder, saliva, sex, sleep
- NICE: for orthostatic hypotension, first review medicines that may cause it (antihypertensives including diuretics, dopaminergics, anticholinergics, antidepressants). Then consider midodrine with supine-hypertension monitoring, and fludrocortisone if midodrine is unsuitable (off-label in July 2017) [NICE2017].
- MDS 2019: midodrine and fludrocortisone have "insufficient evidence" but are "possibly useful". Droxidopa is "efficacious (short term)" and "possibly useful", with insufficient long-term evidence [Seppi2019].
- Droxidopa (Northera, NDA 203202) received FDA priority approval on 18 February 2014. It is indicated for symptomatic neurogenic orthostatic hypotension caused by primary autonomic failure, including PD. The label states that "effectiveness beyond 2 weeks of treatment has not been established" and has a boxed warning for supine hypertension [openFDA2026].
- Consensus non-drug measures for neurogenic orthostatic hypotension:
  - at least about 2 L of water daily;
  - rapidly drinking about 500 mL of water within 5 minutes, which can raise systolic pressure by about 30 mmHg within 5 minutes for up to an hour;
  - adding 1-2 teaspoons (2.3-4.6 g) of salt per day if appropriate;
  - raising the head of the bed by 6-9 inches (15-23 cm), not with stacked pillows;
  - compression garments of 30-40 mmHg [Gibbons2017].
- MDS 2019 ratings for constipation: probiotics and prebiotic fibre "efficacious" and "clinically useful"; macrogol (polyethylene glycol) and lubiprostone "likely efficacious" and "possibly useful". Lifestyle fibre and fluid measures should always be recommended [Seppi2019].
- MDS 2019 ratings for drooling: botulinum toxin A and B "efficacious" and "clinically useful", with specialized monitoring; glycopyrrolate "efficacious" and "possibly useful". Both parotid and submandibular glands should be injected [Seppi2019].
- NICE on drooling: consider speech and language therapy first, then glycopyrronium bromide, then referral for botulinum toxin A. Other anticholinergics should be considered only if the risk of cognitive adverse effects is minimal [NICE2017].
- MDS 2019 rates sildenafil "efficacious" and "clinically useful" for erectile dysfunction in PD [Seppi2019].
- MDS 2019 found no qualifying RCT for RBD in PD [Seppi2019]. NICE suggests considering clonazepam or melatonin after a medicines review (both off-label in 2017) [NICE2017].
- The AASM 2023 guideline makes a good-practice statement on bed safety:
  - remove bedside weapons and objects that could be thrown or wielded;
  - move or pad sharp furniture;
  - put a soft mat beside the bed;
  - with severe uncontrolled RBD, sleep separately from the partner or at least put a pillow between them [Howell2023].
- AASM 2023 makes conditional suggestions for RBD due to a medical condition: clonazepam, immediate-release melatonin, and transdermal rivastigmine in PD. It advises against deep brain stimulation for RBD [Howell2023].
- MDS 2019: modafinil has insufficient evidence but is "possibly useful" for excessive daytime sleepiness. NICE says to consider it only after a sleep history has excluded reversible causes, with review at least every 12 months [Seppi2019; NICE2017].

### Exercise and physiotherapy
- SPARX (phase 2): 128 untreated de novo patients (Hoehn and Yahr 1-2) did high-intensity treadmill exercise (4 days/week at 80-85% of maximum heart rate), moderate-intensity exercise (60-65%) or wait-list control for 6 months. UPDRS motor score changed by +0.3 in the high-intensity group vs +3.2 in controls (P=.03), and only the high-intensity arm passed the non-futility threshold [Schenkman2018].
- Park-in-Shape: 130 sedentary patients (Hoehn and Yahr ≤2) did 30-45 minutes of home aerobic cycling with exergaming, or stretching, 3 times a week for 6 months. The off-state MDS-UPDRS motor difference favoured aerobic exercise by 4.2 points (95% CI 1.6-6.9, p=0.0020), above the 3.5-point clinically relevant threshold [vanderKolk2019].
- Tai chi RCT: 195 patients (Hoehn and Yahr 1-4) did 60-minute sessions twice weekly for 24 weeks. Tai chi beat resistance training and stretching on limits of stability (maximum excursion +5.55 and +11.98 percentage points), reduced falls compared with stretching, and kept its effects at 3 months [Li2012].
- An update of the European Physiotherapy Guideline meta-analysis covered 191 RCTs (7,998 participants) in 12 categories of physiotherapy:
  - conventional physiotherapy improved motor symptoms, gait and quality of life;
  - treadmill and resistance training improved gait;
  - strategy training improved balance and gait;
  - dance, Nordic walking, balance/gait training and martial arts improved motor symptoms, balance and gait;
  - exergaming improved balance and quality of life;
  - dual-task training improved no outcome [Radder2020].
- NICE: consider early referral to a physiotherapist experienced in PD, and offer PD-specific physiotherapy for balance or motor function problems [NICE2017].
- LSVT BIG (Berlin study): 60 patients received 16 hours of one-to-one high-amplitude training over 4 weeks, Nordic walking, or unsupervised home exercise. UPDRS motor improved by 5.05 points with BIG, while it worsened by 0.58 with walking and by 1.68 with home exercise (P<0.001). Quality of life (PDQ-39) did not differ [Ebersbach2010].

### Freezing, cueing and falls
- In a survey of 6,620 patients, 47% reported regular freezing. Freezing was associated with longer disease duration, more advanced stage and male sex [Macht2007].
- Freezing of gait severity correlated with every PDQ-39 dimension except stigma and social support, independent of disease severity [Moore2007].
- RESCUE trial: 153 patients (Hoehn and Yahr II-IV) received 3 weeks of home rhythmic-cueing training. Posture and gait scores improved by 4.2% (p=0.005), and freezing severity fell by 5.5% in freezers (p=0.007). Effects had largely faded 6 weeks later [Nieuwboer2007].
- A systematic review of 22 prospective studies found that 60.5% (range 35-90%) fell at least once and 39% (18-65%) fell recurrently. Recurrent fallers averaged 20.8 falls per person per year (range 4.7-67.6). Factors associated with recurrent falls include prior falls, disease severity and duration, dopamine agonists, higher levodopa dose, cognitive impairment, fear of falling and freezing of gait [Allen2013].

### Speech, swallowing and occupational therapy
- Swallowing problems (dysphagia) were reported by 35% (95% CI 28-41) when patients were asked. On objective testing the pooled prevalence was 82% (77-87), against 23% in controls, so swallowing problems are under-reported unless asked about [Kalf2012].
- PD COMM: 388 people with dysarthria were randomised to LSVT LOUD (four 50-minute sessions a week for 4 weeks), NHS speech and language therapy, or none. At 3 months LSVT LOUD lowered Voice Handicap Index scores by 8.0 points vs no therapy (P<0.001) and by 9.6 vs NHS therapy, which did not differ from no therapy (+1.7, P=0.43). In PD COMM, LSVT LOUD had 93 adverse events, mostly vocal strain, compared with 46 for NHS therapy and none for no therapy. No serious adverse events occurred [Sackley2024].
- NICE: offer speech and language therapy for communication, swallowing or saliva problems, including expiratory muscle strength training and effort-focused therapies such as LSVT LOUD [NICE2017].
- OTiP RCT: 191 patients (124 intervention, 67 control), 94% with a participating caregiver, received home-based, individualised occupational therapy. Self-perceived performance on the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM) improved by an adjusted 1.2 points at 3 months (95% CI 0.8-1.6, p<0.0001) [Sturkenboom2014].
- NICE: offer PD-specific occupational therapy for difficulties with activities of daily living [NICE2017].

### Work, driving, pregnancy, hospital
- In two UK samples with onset before 65 (n=151 and n=308), 52% and 57% had retired early because of PD. Mean retirement age was 55.8 vs 62 years in the UK population [Schrag2006emp].
- After 5 years of PD, 46% had stopped working; after 10 years, 82% had. The median time to loss of employment was 6.0 years, ranging from 6.7 years for onset before 45 to 1.7 years for onset after 56 [Schrag2006emp].
- An evidence-based review using AAN evidence classes found that driving is impaired in PD compared with healthy drivers. Cognitive, visual and motor measures predicted driving performance only at Level B/C, so no definitive (Level A) predictors exist [Crizzle2012].
- NICE: people who drive should inform the DVLA and their insurer at diagnosis. People with daytime sleepiness or sudden onset of sleep should not drive [NICE2017].
- Evidence on pregnancy in PD comes mainly from case reports and drug registries. Levodopa has the best safety data, amantadine should be avoided in pregnancy or when trying to conceive, and there is no evidence of higher rates of birth or fetal complications [Seier2017].
- NICE: PD medicines should not be withdrawn abruptly, because of acute akinesia or neuroleptic malignant syndrome. In hospital or care homes they should be given at the appropriate times, which may mean allowing self-medication [NICE2017].

### Caregiving
- In a postal survey of 123 caregivers (66% women), over 40% said their health had suffered, almost half had raised depression scores and two-thirds said their social life had suffered. Caregiver burden rose with patient disability, depression, hallucinations, confusion and falls, and was linked to patients' quality of life [Schrag2006CG].
- In a Spanish nationwide study of 289 patient-caregiver pairs, 75% of caregivers were spouses, mostly women around 60, providing care for 6 ± 5.4 years. They had more mood disorders and worse quality of life than the general population. Caregivers' own affective state was the strongest factor in their burden and perceived health. Zarit burden scores rose with PD severity [Martinez-Martin2008].
- In a 4-year population-based study, 26.4% (47 of 178) were admitted to a nursing home. Old age, functional impairment, dementia and hallucinations were independent predictors, and hallucinations were the strongest [Aarsland2000].
- At 20 years in the Sydney cohort, 48% of survivors lived in nursing homes and only one person lived independently [Hely2008].

### Palliative care, end of life, causes of death
- Outpatient palliative care trial: 210 patients and 175 caregivers. Care came from a neurologist, social worker, chaplain and nurse, with palliative-specialist input. At 6 months quality of life was better with palliative care (treatment effect 1.87, 95% CI 0.47-3.27, P=.009) [Kluger2020].
- Caregiver burden, the co-primary outcome, did not differ at 6 months (P=.06). Palliative care did improve non-motor symptom burden, motor severity, advance-directive completion, caregiver anxiety and caregiver burden at 12 months, and benefits were larger in people with higher palliative needs [Kluger2020].
- A European Association for Palliative Care / EAN consensus for progressive neurological disease recommends:
  - early integration of palliative care (Level C);
  - multidisciplinary team involvement (Level C);
  - symptom management (Level B);
  - end-of-life care (Level C);
  - carer support and training (Level C);
  - professional education (Good Practice Point) [Oliver2016].
- NICE: offer discussions about prognosis and advance care planning (Advance Decisions to Refuse Treatment, DNACPR, Lasting Power of Attorney), and consider referral to palliative care at any stage of PD [NICE2017].
- In CamPaIGN, 55% had died at 10 years (SMR 1.29). Death certificates listed PD as a substantial contributor in only 20%, and pneumonia was the most common cause of death [Williams-Gray2013].

### Quality of life
- The PDQ-39 has 8 dimensions: mobility, activities of daily living, emotional well-being, stigma, social support, cognition, communication and bodily discomfort [Moore2007].
- In the Global Parkinson's Disease Survey across six countries, Hoehn and Yahr stage and medication explained only 17.3% of the variation in quality of life. Adding other factors raised this to 59.7%, and depression (BDI) was the strongest predictor (adjusted R² 0.582). Satisfaction with the explanation at diagnosis and optimism were also significant [GPDS2002].
