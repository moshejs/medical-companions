# H. Nursing care
This pass covers what nursing students need for the "Nursing care" module: assessment tools and how they are scored; how medicines are given and monitored, including time-critical dosing, nil-by-mouth substitution, drugs that are contraindicated or need caution, and care of DBS devices and infusions; safety topics (falls, aspiration, delirium, orthostatic hypotension, impulse-control disorders); teaching points and common misunderstandings; documentation; and evidence on Parkinson's disease nurse specialists. Every PMID and DOI was checked against Europe PMC, which mirrors PubMed (the PubMed E-utilities endpoint was rate-limiting during this session), or against Crossref. For the scales, item structure comes from the official MDS-UPDRS form (2019 update), which I read in full. All other numbers come from abstracts unless a line says otherwise. What I could not access: the full text of Brennan & Genever 2010 (BMJ, paywalled, no abstract), so it is not cited; the full text of Alty 2016 (abstract only; this paper is in *Practical Neurology*, a BMJ Publishing journal, not *The BMJ* as the brief stated); the NMSS and Epworth full texts (so per-item scoring rules for these two are not given); and the full text of the Freeman 2011 orthostatic hypotension consensus (its definition is reported here as quoted verbatim in Gibbons 2017). Two items from the brief are left out because I could not source them inside the source budget: the OPTIMAL calculator's dose discrepancies (seen only in a search snippet of a BGS abstract) and the domperidone dose limits (EMA 2014; read but dropped to stay within 45 sources). The Parkinson's UK, Parkinson's Foundation, NICE and device-centre pages were opened on 2026-10-20.

## Facts

### MDS-UPDRS: structure and scoring
- The MDS-UPDRS has four parts: I, Non-motor Experiences of Daily Living; II, Motor Experiences of Daily Living; III, Motor Examination; IV, Motor Complications. It has 65 items, compared with 55 in the original UPDRS. Validation covered 877 patients at 39 sites, with Cronbach's alpha 0.79-0.93 across parts and rho = 0.96 against the old UPDRS. The factor structure supports reporting a sum score for each part rather than one total score [Goetz2008].
- Part I has 13 questions. Part IA (6 items, rated by the clinician) covers 1.1 cognitive impairment, 1.2 hallucinations and psychosis, 1.3 depressed mood, 1.4 anxious mood, 1.5 apathy and 1.6 features of dopamine dysregulation syndrome. Part IB (7 items, completed by the patient) covers 1.7 sleep problems, 1.8 daytime sleepiness, 1.9 pain, 1.10 urinary problems, 1.11 constipation, 1.12 light-headedness on standing and 1.13 fatigue [MDS2019UPDRS].
- Part II has 13 patient-completed items: 2.1 speech, 2.2 saliva and drooling, 2.3 chewing and swallowing, 2.4 eating tasks, 2.5 dressing, 2.6 hygiene, 2.7 handwriting, 2.8 hobbies, 2.9 turning in bed, 2.10 tremor, 2.11 getting out of bed, a car or a deep chair, 2.12 walking and balance, 2.13 freezing [MDS2019UPDRS].
- Part III has 18 items (3.1 speech, 3.2 facial expression, 3.3 rigidity, 3.4 finger tapping, 3.5 hand movements, 3.6 pronation-supination, 3.7 toe tapping, 3.8 leg agility, 3.9 arising from chair, 3.10 gait, 3.11 freezing of gait, 3.12 postural stability, 3.13 posture, 3.14 global spontaneity of movement, 3.15 postural tremor, 3.16 kinetic tremor, 3.17 rest tremor amplitude, 3.18 constancy of rest tremor). Several items are rated separately for each side or body region, which makes 33 scored ratings in total. For example, rigidity is rated for neck, RUE, LUE, RLE and LLE, and rest tremor amplitude for four limbs plus lip/jaw [MDS2019UPDRS].
- Part IV has 6 items: 4.1 time with dyskinesia, 4.2 functional impact of dyskinesia, 4.3 time in OFF, 4.4 functional impact of fluctuations, 4.5 complexity (predictability) of fluctuations, 4.6 painful OFF-state dystonia. Item 4.5 bands OFF-period predictability as follows: 1 = predictable more than 75% of the time, 2 = 51-75%, 3 = 26-50%, 4 = 25% or less [MDS2019UPDRS].
- Maximum scores, derived by arithmetic from the item counts and the 0-4 scale (not stated in the sources I read): Part I 0-52, Part II 0-52, Part III 0-132 (33 x 4), Part IV 0-24, total 0-260. Every item uses the same five anchors: 0 Normal, 1 Slight, 2 Mild, 3 Moderate, 4 Severe. Part III ratings must be whole numbers ("no half points, no missing ratings"). "UR" (unable to rate) is used only when testing is impossible, for example after an amputation. Otherwise the rater is told to "rate what you see" and to take comorbidities into account [MDS2019UPDRS].- Before Part III the form records three things: whether the patient takes PD medication, their clinical state (ON = "typical functional state when patients are receiving medication and have a good response"; OFF = poor response despite medication) and minutes since the last levodopa dose. At the end it records whether dyskinesia was present and whether it interfered with the ratings, followed by the Hoehn and Yahr stage. The official Parts IA, IB and II have no separate ON and OFF ratings, but individual protocols may ask the same questions separately for each state [MDS2019UPDRS].
- Minimal clinically important difference for Part III (728 paired visits, 260 patients, anchored to clinician global impression): -3.25 points for minimal improvement and +4.63 points for minimal worsening. The thresholds are asymmetric [Horvath2015].

### Hoehn and Yahr staging
- Stages as printed on the MDS-UPDRS form: 0 asymptomatic; 1 unilateral involvement only; 2 bilateral involvement without impairment of balance; 3 mild to moderate involvement, some postural instability but physically independent, needs assistance to recover from pull test; 4 severe disability, still able to walk or stand unassisted; 5 wheelchair-bound or bedridden unless aided [MDS2019UPDRS; Hoehn1967].
- The MDS Task Force lists these weaknesses: the scale mixes impairment with disability, it is non-linear, it leans heavily on postural instability, and it says nothing about non-motor problems. Reliability and validity are best supported for stages 2-4. The "modified" Hoehn and Yahr scale with 0.5 steps is widely used but had no clinimetric data. The Task Force recommends keeping the original five-point scale, reporting medians and ranges, analysing change with nonparametric methods, and rating comorbidities as seen ("rate what you see") [Goetz2004HY].

### Quality of life and non-motor screening
- The PDQ-39 is a 39-item self-report questionnaire with eight scales. It was cut down from a 65-item pool built from patient interviews and showed satisfactory internal and test-retest reliability [Peto1995].
- The eight PDQ-39 scales are mobility, activities of daily living, emotional well-being, stigma, social support, cognitions, communication and bodily discomfort, with a one-month recall period. The PDQ-8 has 8 items, one from each scale. Summed scores give one overall health-related quality-of-life figure [OxfordPDQ].
- The PDQ-39 summary index runs from 0 to 100, with higher scores meaning worse function. In EARLYSTIM it improved by 7.8 points with neurostimulation and worsened by 0.2 points with medical therapy (difference 8.0, P = 0.002), which gives a sense of what a large change looks like [Schuepbach2013].
- The NMS Questionnaire (NMSQuest) has 30 items. It is a screening tool, not a graded rating scale, and gives no overall disability score. In the pilot (123 PD patients, 96 controls) people with PD reported a median of 9 non-motor symptoms against 5.5 in controls. Symptoms such as dribbling, apathy, low mood and taste or smell problems had often never been disclosed to health professionals [Chaudhuri2006].
- The NMS Scale (NMSS) is a 30-item rated scale covering nine domains: cardiovascular, sleep/fatigue, mood/cognition, perceptual problems, attention/memory, gastrointestinal, urinary, sexual function and miscellany. In 242 patients the mean score was 56.5 +/- 40.7 (range 0-243), and only one patient reported no non-motor symptoms [Chaudhuri2007].
- The Parkinson's Disease Sleep Scale (PDSS) is a 15-item visual analogue scale for nocturnal problems, developed in 143 patients and 137 controls. The paper notes that sleep problems may affect up to 96% of people with PD [Chaudhuri2002].
- The Epworth Sleepiness Scale asks how likely the person is to doze off in eight everyday situations. Its total score separated healthy controls from people with sleep apnoea, narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia. The per-item 0-3 scale, the 0-24 total and the normal cut-off are widely reproduced but were not verified against the primary paper (unverified) [Johns1991].

### Cognition, impulse-control, swallowing and mobility screens
- MoCA in PD (114 patients, 47 controls): a cut-off of <26/30 detected PD-MCI with 90% sensitivity, 75% specificity and 95% NPV; <21/30 detected PD dementia with 81% sensitivity, 95% specificity and 92% NPV. The MoCA performed better than the MMSE [DalrympleAlford2010].
- The QUIP has three sections: (1) four impulse-control disorders (gambling, sexual behaviour, buying, eating); (2) other compulsive behaviours (punding, hobbyism, walkabout); (3) compulsive medication use. Sensitivity for detecting any disorder was 96% for the full QUIP and 94% for the short QUIP-S. A positive screen is meant to lead to a full clinical interview [Weintraub2009].
- The Swallowing Disturbance Questionnaire (SDQ) has 15 yes/no items. A cut-off score of 11 gave 80.5% sensitivity and 81.3% specificity against clinical and endoscopic (FEES) evaluation in 57 patients. Of 24 patients who denied swallowing problems, 12 (50%) would still have been referred on the strength of the SDQ alone [Manor2007].
- The Munich Dysphagia Test-PD (MDT-PD) has 26 items (alpha 0.91). In 82 validation patients (median H&Y 3), 73% had dysphagia (44% with oropharyngeal symptoms, 29% with penetration or aspiration). The test detected dysphagia with 90% sensitivity and 86% specificity, and aspiration risk with 82% sensitivity and 71% specificity [Simons2014].
- Timed Up and Go in 2,097 patients at 16 National Parkinson Foundation centres: the model classified 74-75% of participants correctly as fallers or non-fallers, and the proposed cut-off was 11.5 seconds [Nocera2013].

### Orthostatic blood pressure measurement
- The consensus definition (Freeman 2011, as quoted by the panel) is a sustained fall in systolic BP of at least 20 mmHg, or in diastolic BP of 10 mmHg, or both, within 3 minutes of standing or of head-up tilt to at least 60 degrees [Gibbons2017].
- The recommended gold-standard method is to rest supine for at least 5 minutes, then stand for 3 minutes, measuring BP just before standing and at 1 and 3 minutes. Where that is impractical, sitting for at least 5 minutes and then standing is an acceptable alternative [Gibbons2017].
- The 30/15 mmHg criterion applies to people with supine hypertension (supine systolic of 150 mmHg or more, or diastolic of 90 mmHg or more). In them a fall of 30 mmHg systolic or 15 mmHg diastolic "may be a more appropriate criterion", because the size of the fall depends on baseline BP. This differs from the brief's "30/15 = neurogenic" framing [Gibbons2017].
- A neurogenic cause is suggested by a heart-rate rise of less than 15 bpm when OH occurs. Non-neurogenic OH typically shows a rise of more than 15 bpm within 3 minutes. The heart-rate criteria apply only when BP actually falls and no rate-limiting drug (such as a beta-blocker) is present. The panel grades severity from grade 1 (infrequent symptoms, unrestricted standing, 20-30 mmHg systolic fall) to grade 4 (standing time under 1 minute, with a fall of more than 30 mmHg or incapacitation) [Gibbons2017].
- When OH develops, NICE recommends reviewing antihypertensives (including diuretics), dopaminergics, anticholinergics and antidepressants. If midodrine is considered, supine hypertension needs monitoring [NICE2017].

### Time-critical medicines in hospital: standards
- NICE quality statement 4: adults with PD in hospital or a care home take levodopa within 30 minutes of their individually prescribed administration time [NICE2018QS]. NICE NG71 1.3.4: people with PD admitted to hospital or a care home should have medicines given at the appropriate times, "which in some cases may mean allowing self-medication", and adjusted only by, or after discussion with, a PD specialist [NICE2017].
- Parkinson's UK states that "a delay as little as 30 minutes" can mean the difference between functioning well and being unable to move, walk, talk or swallow. Its first recommendation to hospitals is that any dose more than 30 minutes late be reported as an adverse event [PUK2023].
- The two standards differ. The Parkinson's Foundation's care summary for US hospitals says to give medicines "within ±15 minutes of at-home schedule", which is tighter than the 30-minute UK/NICE standard [PF2023; NICE2018QS].

### Time-critical medicines in hospital: evidence on late and missed doses
- UK Parkinson's Audit, as summarised by Parkinson's UK: the share of people admitted to hospital who did not get their medication on time every time was 50% (2015), 58% (2017), 53% (2019) and 58% (2022). Among those affected, 40% said the delay had a negative or significantly negative effect [PUK2023].
- Parkinson's UK cites evidence that missed doses add an average of 4 days to a hospital stay. In 2021/22 the mean length of an emergency PD admission in England was 10.2 days, and about 33% of people with PD in England are admitted each year [PUK2023].
- Freedom of Information returns from English NHS trusts (May 2023): one in four trusts does not let people with PD take their own medication; only 52% require ward staff to train on time-critical medicines; 81% have e-prescribing, but only 58% use it to report on-time administration and only 37% can alert staff to time-critical medicines; 47% of trusts with an A&E have no system for on-time PD medication there [PUK2023].
- Dutch survey (684 patients): 18% had been in hospital in the past year. Of those, 21% had worse motor symptoms, 33% had at least one complication and 26% received incorrect anti-parkinsonian medication. Incorrect medication was linked to deterioration (OR 5.8, 95% CI 2.5-13.7), as were infections (OR 6.7) [Gerlach2012].
- US veterans (89 patients, 3,873 PD doses, barcode timing): 675 administrations were incorrect, made up of 322 omitted doses, 300 late by 30 minutes or more and 53 early by 30 minutes or more. Contraindicated drugs were prescribed for 19 patients. Correct administration was lower in the first 2 days of admission (74.6% vs 82.8%) and higher when a neurologist was consulted (85.5% vs 76.5%) [Hou2012].
- In 339 encounters (212 patients), people with a delayed or missed dose stayed longer: mean 8.2 days vs 3.6 days. Contraindicated dopamine blockers were given in 23% (71/339) of encounters, and those patients also stayed longer (7.5 vs 5.9 days) [MartinezRamirez2015].
- A New Zealand pre/post intervention (staff education, sticker alerts, pharmacist priority review) cut the medication error rate from 22.5% to 9.3% (P < 0.001). Complications fell from 45% to 38% and mean length of stay from 13 to 8 days, but neither change was significant. At baseline one death was directly attributed to a medication error. Like the other hospital data here, this is observational (a single-site pre/post study); no randomised trial of on-time dosing was found in this pass [Lance2021; Gerlach2012; Hou2012].

### Never stopping abruptly: parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome
- NICE 1.3.2: antiparkinsonian medicines should not be withdrawn abruptly, or allowed to fail suddenly through poor absorption (for example gastroenteritis or abdominal surgery), because of the risk of acute akinesia or neuroleptic malignant syndrome. NICE 1.3.3 advises against "drug holidays" for the same reason [NICE2017].
- PHS looks like neuroleptic malignant syndrome: rigidity, fever and reduced consciousness, sometimes with autonomic instability and a raised CK. Complications include acute renal failure, aspiration pneumonia, DVT/PE and DIC. Reported mortality is up to 4%, and about another third of patients are left with permanent sequelae [Newman2009].
- The carbidopa-levodopa label describes sporadic cases of an NMS-like syndrome after dose reduction or withdrawal and advises close observation when levodopa is cut abruptly, "especially if the patient is receiving neuroleptics" [FDAcdld2026].
- Parkinson's UK emergency guidance lists what to observe when doses are missed: tremor against baseline, temperature, respiratory rate, BP, cognition (the 4AT tool), swallowing, and creatine kinase. It also names dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome (DAWS) as a risk alongside NLMS [PUKEmergency2023].

### Nil by mouth and alternative routes
- Alty 2016 lists four alternative routes for people who cannot take oral PD medicines: dispersible preparations in thickened fluids, an enteral tube, a transdermal patch, or subcutaneous injection. The paper links omitted doses to worse disease control and to neuroleptic malignant-like syndrome [Alty2016].
- Parkinson's UK guidance before elective surgery: oral medicines may be taken with clear fluids up to 2 hours before surgery; the patient should go first on the list where possible; regional anaesthesia, compared with general, allows usual medication to continue [PUKEmergency2023].
- For tube administration Parkinson's UK prefers an NG tube for levodopa, because it is absorbed mainly in the jejunum. Dispersible co-beneldopa is used, controlled-release doses are converted to a dispersible equivalent because of reduced bioavailability, and the tube is flushed after liquid medicines [PUKEmergency2023].
- Parkinson's UK says entacapone and the other COMT and MAO-B inhibitors are not licensed for enteral tubes and can usually be omitted temporarily. Oral dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole) are not licensed for tubes either, and rotigotine patches are the substitute [PUKEmergency2023].
- Parkinson's UK rotigotine rules: round the dose to the nearest 2 mg, up to 16 mg/24 h; do not cut patches (2, 4, 6 and 8 mg strengths can be combined); rotate the site every 24 h; use 16 mg if the adjusted LEDD is above 350 mg and consult a specialist about apomorphine. The dose is raised if stiffness increases and lowered if confusion or hallucinations increase. Rotigotine "could be contraindicated in delirium", and the guidance places the patch as an option only when an NG/NJ/PEG tube is not tolerated. The patch must be removed before MRI or cardioversion because its backing contains aluminium [PUKEmergency2023].
- Parkinson's UK now points professionals to PDMedCalc, which was registered with the MHRA as a Class 1 medical device on 15 Feb 2024. The page says the OPTIMAL calculator has closed and advises professionals not to use it. It applies a correction factor to stop rotigotine doses from being too large, because large doses can cause confusion, hallucinations or delirium. Apomorphine and Duodopa/ProDuodopa need no conversion, and amantadine can be omitted if swallowing is compromised [PUKCalc2025].
- Parkinson's UK also covers devices and end of life. Apomorphine pumps continue at the prescribed rate and settings change only on specialist request. Duodopa continues if gastric emptying and the PEJ tube are working; if not, it is replaced with rotigotine. In end-of-life care, PD medication is not stopped, and patients who cannot take it orally are converted to rotigotine [PUKEmergency2023].

### Crushing, food and drug interactions
- Carbidopa-levodopa extended-release tablets "should not be chewed or crushed". A whole or half tablet is swallowed intact [FDAcdld2026]. Parkinson's UK: never crush or split modified-release products (CR, MR, XL, PR); break other tablets only if they are scored; tablets can be given one at a time on a teaspoon with soft food or thickened fluid [PUKEmergency2023].
- The levodopa label says a high-protein diet may delay levodopa absorption and reduce how much is taken up. Iron salts, including those in multivitamins, can chelate levodopa and carbidopa and lower their bioavailability [FDAcdld2026].
- Metoclopramide "may increase the bioavailability of levodopa by increasing gastric emptying" but "may also adversely affect disease control by its dopamine receptor antagonistic properties" [FDAcdld2026].

### Drugs to avoid and published safer alternatives
- NICE: other antipsychotics (phenothiazines, butyrophenones) can worsen motor features; olanzapine is not offered for PD psychosis; quetiapine is considered for people without cognitive impairment; clozapine is offered if standard treatment fails, with registration with a patient monitoring service; and lower doses of both are needed than in other indications [NICE2017].
- The Parkinson's Foundation care summary lists haloperidol and other typical antipsychotics, and atypicals other than pimavanserin, quetiapine and clozapine, as drugs to avoid. For nausea it lists prochlorperazine, metoclopramide, promethazine and droperidol to avoid [PF2023].
- Parkinson's UK emergency guidance says to avoid metoclopramide and prochlorperazine ("can worsen Parkinson's symptoms and in some cases can be fatal"), and haloperidol, chlorpromazine and other antipsychotics. It says to consider domperidone (oral or PR) if there are no contraindications but to avoid it in cardiac disease (a small rise in arrhythmia and sudden cardiac death risk). Cyclizine and ondansetron can be used after surgery but may cause confusion and altered consciousness [PUKEmergency2023].
- Sources disagree on ondansetron. The Parkinson's Foundation lists ondansetron and the other 5HT3 antagonists in its "safe" column. The apomorphine (APOKYN) label contraindicates 5HT3 antagonists (ondansetron, granisetron, dolasetron, palonosetron, alosetron) because of "profound hypotension and loss of consciousness" when apomorphine was given with ondansetron. The caution therefore applies specifically to people using apomorphine [PF2023; FDAapokyn2024].
- Rasagiline is contraindicated with meperidine (pethidine), tramadol, methadone, propoxyphene, other MAO inhibitors, St John's wort, cyclobenzaprine and dextromethorphan, because of serotonin syndrome or psychosis risk. At least 14 days should pass between stopping rasagiline and starting these drugs [FDArasagiline2026]. The Parkinson's Foundation frames these MAO-B/opioid combinations as "beware of mixing" rather than as absolute contraindications. It also warns that benzodiazepines, muscle relaxants, bladder-control drugs and other sleep or pain drugs may cause confusion, hallucinations and falls [PF2023].
- If a patient has a problematic impulse-control disorder, NICE says to seek specialist advice before changing dopaminergic therapy and to reduce any dopamine agonist gradually first, watching for agonist withdrawal [NICE2017].

### DBS and infusion devices
- One DBS centre's safety sheet states that strong electrical fields can make the DBS system carry energy "capable of causing brain injury even if the system is turned off". It says diathermy (shortwave, microwave or therapeutic ultrasound) is "NOT ALLOWED" and "may cause damage even if no heat is created" [UCDavis2025].
- The same sheet says MRI is allowed only on some DBS systems under specific protocols, which may require putting the programmer into "MRI" mode (Abbott Infinity, Boston Scientific Vercise Gevia/Genus, Medtronic Percept), or a prior eligibility check (Medtronic Activa) [UCDavis2025].
- For procedures the sheet gives these rules. Electrocautery: turn DBS off (Medtronic, Boston Scientific) or use "Surgery Mode" (Abbott), prefer bipolar cautery and place the grounding pad on the legs. Turn DBS off for ECG, EEG and cardioversion. ECT and TMS are not permitted. A physician should document DBS settings before surgery in case the device resets. Patients should always bring their patient programmer to hospital [UCDavis2025].
- DBS withdrawal syndrome. A review of 20 cases, compared with 15 patients whose stimulation stopped without problems, found the main risk factors were long disease duration and many years of DBS (p < 0.005). Patients can deteriorate quickly, may not respond to high-dose levodopa, and their condition can become life-threatening. The authors argue that a stopped battery should be replaced as an emergency [Grimaldi2023].
- Parkinson's UK: patients with DBS stay on their usual medication routine, should carry an ID card with the device model number and contact details, and need an oral drug list available in case the DBS fails [PUKEmergency2023].
- Parkinson's Foundation: patients with a Duopa device may bring their own cassettes; the device is turned off, disconnected and taken out of the room during imaging; DBS has specific requirements for MRI, EKG and EEG [PF2023].

### Safety: falls, aspiration, delirium, skin
- A systematic review of 22 prospective studies found that 60.5% of people with PD (range 35-90%) reported at least one fall and 39% (range 18-65%) recurrent falls. Recurrent fallers averaged 20.8 falls per person per year. Associated factors include previous falls, freezing of gait, dopamine agonist use, higher levodopa dose, cognitive impairment and fear of falling [Allen2013].
- In an RCT of 231 people, home exercise for balance, leg strength and freezing (40-60 minutes, 3 times a week for 6 months) did not reduce falls overall (IRR 0.73, 95% CI 0.45-1.17). Falls dropped in the milder-disease subgroup (IRR 0.31) and tended to rise in the more severe subgroup (IRR 1.61, not significant) [Canning2015].
- In a Korean national cohort (10,159 people with PD, four matched controls each), aspiration pneumonia occurred at 3.01 vs 0.59 per 1,000 person-years (HR 4.21). After a first episode, mortality in PD was 23.9% at 1 month, 65.2% at 1 year and 91.8% at 5 years, similar to controls (30.9%, 67.4%, 88.9%) [Won2021].
- The Parkinson's Foundation describes swallowing problems as "very common" and states that aspiration pneumonia is "the leading cause of death" in PD. It recommends swallow screening and speech-language therapy (SLT) input [PF2023]. This leading-cause claim was not checked against cause-of-death data in this pass (unverified). NICE 1.7.8 offers speech and language therapy for problems with communication, swallowing or saliva, including strategies such as expiratory muscle strength training to reduce aspiration risk [NICE2017].
- In a prospective study, delirium was diagnosed (DSM-5) in 66.9% of 121 hospitalised people with PD, against 38.7% of 199 older controls. In PD, delirium was linked to higher 12-month mortality (HR 3.3), institutionalisation (OR 10.7) and dementia (OR 6.1) [Gerakios2024].
- For confusion, Parkinson's UK says to screen for delirium with the 4AT, look for and treat causes such as infection, dehydration and constipation, and consider a benzodiazepine rather than antipsychotics if one is needed. Its list of complications to watch for in acute admissions is delirium, chest infection (especially aspiration pneumonia), UTIs, postural hypotension and falls (checking BP lying and sitting, then standing), constipation and NLMS [PUKEmergency2023].
- The Parkinson's Foundation lists the consequences of delayed medication as falls, skin breakdown, incontinence, eating difficulties, emotional distress and inaccurate assessment of discharge needs. It recommends mobilising as soon as it is medically safe, with PT/OT input [PF2023].
### Impulse-control disorders: screening and frequency
- In a longitudinal cohort with disease duration of 5 years or less (411 patients), the 5-year cumulative incidence of ICDs was 46.1%: 51.5% in people who had ever taken a dopamine agonist vs 12.4% in those who never had. Prevalence rose from 19.7% to 32.8%. Risk increased with agonist dose and duration, and ICDs gradually resolved after agonists were stopped [Corvol2018].
- NICE 1.4.3: when a dopamine agonist is started, give oral and written information on the increased ICD risk (including that affected people may hide it), the types of ICD, who to contact, and the possibility that the agonist will be reduced or stopped, and record that the discussion took place. NICE 1.4.4 says to discuss ICDs at review appointments, especially when therapy changes [NICE2017].
- NICE quality statement 2: adults taking dopaminergic therapy are told about ICD risk when they start and at least once a year [NICE2018QS].

### Teaching points and common misunderstandings
- The Hauser home diary records each half-hour as asleep, OFF, ON without dyskinesia, ON with non-troublesome dyskinesia, or ON with troublesome dyskinesia. Patients generally count OFF time and ON time with troublesome dyskinesia as "bad time". In 302 patients from 10 countries, 83% completed six diary days without errors. Reliability rose with more days (Cronbach's alpha 0.868 at 3 days, 0.946 at 6 days), but 76% of missing or duplicate entries came after day 3, so compliance dropped beyond 3 days [Hauser2004].
- The Parkinson's Foundation lists "levodopa stops working after five years" as a myth ("perhaps the single most pervasive myth about PD treatment") and replies that "levodopa works for decades". It also corrects the belief that the next dose should be postponed, saying levodopa works best "taken on time, just before the previous dose wears off" [PFMyths].
- What does happen over time, from the cumulative literature: after 4-6 years of levodopa, about 40% of patients have motor fluctuations (wearing-off) and just under 40% have dyskinesia. Fluctuations were "nil" in the first year. The authors note these are incidence figures, and clinically important morbidity may be much lower [Ahlskog2001].
- NICE 1.3.8: when treatment starts, give oral and written information on ICDs (higher risk with agonists), excessive sleepiness and sudden-onset sleep with agonists, and psychotic symptoms, and record that this was discussed. NICE 1.5.1: people with daytime sleepiness or sudden-onset sleep are advised not to drive and to tell the DVLA. NICE 1.1.7: people who drive should tell the DVLA and their insurer at diagnosis [NICE2017].
- NICE 1.7.2-1.7.3: consider early referral to a physiotherapist experienced in PD for advice including physical activity, and offer PD-specific physiotherapy for balance or motor problems [NICE2017].
- In a Parkinson's UK survey (cited to the UK Parkinson's Audit in its 2023 report), 48% of people with PD who are admitted to hospital wanted to manage and take their own medication [PUK2023].
- Parkinson's Foundation teaching for hospital use: write down the exact times of day doses are due so the home schedule can be followed; different strengths and formulations of the same drug are often not interchangeable; patients may need to bring their own supply if a drug is not on the formulary [PF2023].

### Documentation items
- Exact dose times. Parkinson's UK advises writing the first dose as a STAT prescription, prescribing every dose across the 24 hours, and keeping the patient's own timings even when they differ from ward rounds [PUKEmergency2023]. Hou 2012 measured timing with barcode administration records accurate to the minute [Hou2012]. Of English trusts with e-prescribing, 96% can record administration in 30-minute windows and 79% can alert staff when a dose is late [PUK2023].
- Motor state at assessment. The MDS-UPDRS records ON or OFF state, whether the patient takes levodopa, minutes since the last levodopa dose, and whether dyskinesia was present and interfered with ratings [MDS2019UPDRS].
- ICD screening. NICE asks that ICD information and discussions be recorded at the start of treatment and at reviews [NICE2017].
- DBS. Document stimulation settings before surgery, carry the patient programmer, and record the device model from the patient's ID card [UCDavis2025; PUKEmergency2023].
- Falls history. Previous falls is the factor most consistently associated with recurrent falls, which makes it a core item to record [Allen2013].

### Parkinson's disease nurse specialists
- In a 2-year RCT (1,859 patients, 438 general practices in England), community PD nurse specialists did not change mortality (17.3% died overall; HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.73-1.13), the stand-up test, the dot-in-square test, the PDQ-39 or EuroQol [Jarman2002].
- In the same trial, self-rated global health was significantly better with a nurse specialist (difference -0.23, 95% CI -0.40 to -0.06), with no PDQ-39 difference. Healthcare costs rose by about GBP 2,658 on average over 2 years, GBP 266 less in the nurse group, a difference that was not significant [Jarman2002].
- In a US RCT (328 veterans), nurse-led chronic care management (CHAPS) raised adherence to 18 PD quality-of-care indicators to 0.77 vs 0.58 with usual care (difference 0.19, 95% CI 0.16-0.22). Of 8 patient-centred secondary outcomes, only a depression screen improved [Connor2019].
- NICE 1.7.1: people with PD should have regular access to clinical monitoring and medicine adjustment, a continuing point of contact (including home visits when appropriate) and reliable information, "which may be provided by a Parkinson's disease nurse specialist". NICE 1.1.6 says the accessible point of contact with specialist services "could be provided by" a PD nurse specialist [NICE2017]. NICE quality statement 1: adults with PD have a point of contact with specialist services [NICE2018QS].
- The trial evidence is mixed. Specialist nursing improved self-rated wellbeing and process-of-care measures, but neither RCT showed better motor, mortality or PDQ-39 outcomes [Jarman2002; Connor2019].
