Part 1 · The disease

What Parkinson's is

Parkinsonism is a pattern of movement signs, not one disease. Several conditions can cause it, and Parkinson's disease is the most common of them. Press play to build the picture, then see the signs up close and how the odds change with age.

Step 1 · The syndrome

Bradykinesia plus tremor or rigidity

The MDS clinical criteria start by asking whether parkinsonism is present, "defined as bradykinesia, in combination with at least one between rest tremor (4-6 Hz) and rigidity". Only then do they ask which disease causes it. 214,289 Bradykinesia means slowness of movement and also a decrement in amplitude or speed, or hesitations, as a movement is repeated. 292 The older UK Brain Bank criteria also accepted postural instability as the second sign; the MDS definition does not. 214

In plain wordsParkinsonism is a name for a set of movement signs: slowness that gets worse as a movement is repeated, plus a shake at rest or stiffness. It describes what is happening, not why.
Exam hookParkinsonism = bradykinesia + (rest tremor and/or rigidity). Postural instability is no longer a core feature in the MDS 2015 criteria.

Core sign · always required

Bradykinesia

The MDS wording: "slowness of movement AND decrement in amplitude or speed or progressive hesitations / halts as movements are continued." Slowness alone is not enough; the movement must also shrink, slow or stall as it is repeated, as in repeated finger tapping. 292 It is the one sign every case of parkinsonism must have. 289

In plain wordsMovements become slow, and when a movement is repeated, like tapping fingers, each one gets smaller or slower, or stops for a moment.
Exam hookLook for the decrement: taps that get smaller with repetition. Slowness without decrement does not meet the definition.

Ages 70 to 79

Median onset at 73

At 70 to 79 incidence was 179.2 in men and 77.7 in women. The median age at onset was 73 (IQR 64 to 80), and 59% of the 464 patients were men. Incidence in men rose over the 30 years (RR 1.24 per decade), mostly at 70 and over, a trend the authors say might reflect changes in smoking or could be spurious. 315 Worldwide prevalence at 70 to 79 was 1,087 per 100,000, and 1,601 in North America, Europe and Australia against 646 in Asia. 293

In plain wordsHalf of people are diagnosed after their early seventies. Around one in a hundred people in their seventies has Parkinson's.
Exam hookAge is the strongest risk factor: incidence rises from 0.1 under 40 to 133.9 per 100,000 a year at 80 and over. 315

The numbers, and where sources disagree

Counts depend on the year, the method and the population. Each card names its source so the figures can be compared fairly.

Worldwide, now

Over 8.5 million

people had Parkinson's disease in 2019, according to the WHO fact sheet. Prevalence has doubled in the past 25 years. In 2019 it caused 5.8 million disability-adjusted life years, up 81% since 2000, and 329,000 deaths, up more than 100%. 378

WHO fact sheet dated 9 August 2023.

Worldwide, 1990 to 2016

2.5 → 6.1 million

The Global Burden of Disease study counted 6.1 million people with Parkinson's in 2016 (95% UI 5.0 to 7.3), against 2.5 million in 1990. Age-standardised prevalence still rose by 21.7%, so the increase is not explained by ageing alone. Parkinson's caused 211,296 deaths in 2016. 121

The authors attribute the rise mainly to more older people, with possible contributions from longer disease duration and environmental factors. 121

Forecasts

12 or 25 million?

Dorsey and colleagues call Parkinson's "the fastest growing neurological disorder in the world" and project a rise from over 6 million in 2015 to over 12 million by 2040, or above 17 million with longer lifespans, less smoking and industrialisation. 86 A 2025 GBD 2021 model projects 25.2 million in 2050 (95% UI 21.7 to 30.1), a 112% increase from 2021: 89% of the growth from ageing, 20% from population growth and 3% from changing prevalence. 348

Sources differ The projections use different GBD rounds, base years and assumptions. Both name ageing as the main driver. 86,348

North America

About 680,000

people with Parkinson's in the US in 2010, from a prevalence of 572 per 100,000 among people aged 45 and over. The model projected about 930,000 in 2020 and 1,238,000 in 2030; the authors call these minimum estimates. 212

Across five North American data sources in 2012, yearly incidence at 65 and over ranged from 108 to 212 per 100,000. 381 In Kaiser Permanente data, adjusted incidence was 16.6 per 100,000 in Hispanic, 13.6 in non-Hispanic White, 11.3 in Asian and 10.2 in Black members. 358

Israel

1%, 2%, 3%

of people aged 50, 60 and 70 and over had Parkinson's in a drug-tracer study of an Israeli health maintenance organisation covering about a quarter of the population (1998 to 2008). Incidence was stable at about 33 per 100,000 a year; prevalence rose from 170 to 256 per 100,000; the mean age at the first anti-parkinsonian prescription was 69. Men aged 50 and over had higher prevalence (RR 1.38) and incidence (RR 1.45). 61

Against the trend In the Clalit registry (2002 to 2021), age-adjusted incidence fell from 57 to 20.3 per 100,000, faster in women than in men. The authors suggest better diagnosis and better health and environmental conditions. Elsewhere, incidence in men rose over 30 years in Olmsted County. 21,315

Men and women

About 1.4 to 1

The GBD male to female ratio of age-standardised prevalence was 1.40 in 2016 and 1.37 in 1990. 121 It is projected to rise from 1.46 in 2021 to 1.64 in 2050. 348

For new cases the gap is wider. Men to women incidence rate ratios at 65 and over were 1.66 (Ontario), 1.69 (Medicare), 1.77 (Kaiser) and 2.57 (Rochester) in 2012. 381 A meta-analysis found higher incidence in men in every age group, significant at 60 to 79. 146

Early onset

21 to 50

The MDS task force recommends "early-onset" rather than "young-onset" Parkinson's, partly to avoid age-related stigma, and defines it as onset after 21 and before 50. Onset before 21 is conventionally called juvenile parkinsonism. Published cutoffs have ranged from 40 to 60. 228

Only 4% of Kaiser Permanente cases began before 50. 358 Genes matter more at younger onset: see Genes and inheritance.
