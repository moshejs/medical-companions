Nursing care

What guidelines and studies say about nursing a person with Parkinson's: the assessment scales and how they are scored, giving time-critical medicines on time, the drugs that can make Parkinson's worse, nil by mouth and surgery, safety, devices, teaching and documentation. Nursing care by topic

MDS-UPDRS: four parts, one 0 to 4 scale

The Movement Disorder Society revision of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale has 65 items in four parts, against 55 in the original. It was validated in 877 patients at 39 sites (Cronbach's alpha 0.79 to 0.93 across parts; rho 0.96 against the old UPDRS), and its factor structure supports reporting a sum for each part rather than one total. 128 Pick a part to see its items.

Every item uses the same anchors: 0 normal, 1 slight, 2 mild, 3 moderate, 4 severe. The maximum scores and the 0 to 260 total are derived by arithmetic from the item counts; the official form does not print them. 224

Part III change: is it clinically important?

Teaching tool, not a clinical record

In 728 paired visits from 260 patients, anchored to the clinician's global impression, the minimal clinically important difference for Part III was −3.25 points for improvement and +4.63 points for worsening. The thresholds are not symmetrical. 152 The form records whether the person is ON or OFF and how many minutes have passed since the last levodopa dose, so a change can be read against the motor state at each visit. 224

Hoehn and Yahr staging

Stage wording as printed on the MDS-UPDRS form. 146,224

MDS critique The MDS Task Force lists weaknesses: the scale mixes impairment with disability, it is not linear, it leans heavily on postural instability, and it says nothing about non-motor problems. Reliability and validity are best supported for stages 2 to 4. The "modified" scale with half-stages is widely used but had no clinimetric data. The Task Force recommends keeping the original five-point scale, reporting medians and ranges, and analysing change with nonparametric methods. 127

Lying and standing blood pressure

The consensus definition of orthostatic hypotension is a sustained fall in systolic pressure of at least 20 mmHg, or in diastolic pressure of at least 10 mmHg, within 3 minutes of standing or of head-up tilt to at least 60 degrees. The recommended method is to lie supine for at least 5 minutes, then stand for 3 minutes, measuring just before standing and at 1 and 3 minutes; sitting for 5 minutes then standing is an acceptable alternative. 123 Enter readings or load an example to see how the definition applies.

The 30/15 criterion

In people with supine hypertension (supine systolic 150 mmHg or more, or diastolic 90 or more), a fall of 30 mmHg systolic or 15 mmHg diastolic "may be a more appropriate criterion", because the size of the fall depends on the starting pressure. It is a threshold for supine hypertension, not a marker of a neurogenic cause. 123

Heart rate

A heart-rate rise of less than 15 bpm when orthostatic hypotension occurs suggests a neurogenic cause; non-neurogenic OH typically shows a rise of more than 15 bpm within 3 minutes. The heart-rate criteria apply only when blood pressure actually falls and no rate-limiting drug is present. 123

Severity and next steps

The panel grades severity from grade 1 (infrequent symptoms, unrestricted standing, a 20 to 30 mmHg systolic fall) to grade 4 (standing time under 1 minute, a fall of more than 30 mmHg or incapacitation). 123 When OH develops, NICE recommends reviewing antihypertensives including diuretics, dopaminergics, anticholinergics and antidepressants. 241 In a meta-analysis of 25 studies, pooled prevalence in Parkinson's was 30.1%. 360

Two cut-offs to try: MoCA and Timed Up and Go

MoCA in 114 people with Parkinson's and 47 controls: below 26 of 30 detected PD mild cognitive impairment with 90% sensitivity and 75% specificity; below 21 detected PD dementia with 81% sensitivity and 95% specificity. It performed better than the MMSE. 76 Timed Up and Go in 2,097 patients at 16 centres: the model classified 74% to 75% correctly as fallers or non-fallers, with a proposed cut-off of 11.5 seconds. 246

Two standards for "on time"

NICE quality statement 4: adults with Parkinson's in hospital or a care home take levodopa within 30 minutes of their individually prescribed time. 242 The Parkinson's Foundation's US hospital summary says "within ±15 minutes of at-home schedule". 284 Parkinson's UK asks hospitals to report any dose more than 30 minutes late as an adverse event. 298 Slide the time the dose is given.

Among those affected, 40% said the delay had a negative or significantly negative effect. Never stopped suddenly

Guideline

NICE 1.3.2 and 1.3.3

Antiparkinsonian medicines should not be withdrawn abruptly, or allowed to fail suddenly through poor absorption (for example gastroenteritis or abdominal surgery), because of the risk of acute akinesia or neuroleptic malignant syndrome. NICE advises against "drug holidays" for the same reason. 241

Parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome

It looks like neuroleptic malignant syndrome: rigidity, fever and reduced consciousness, sometimes with autonomic instability and a raised CK. Complications include acute renal failure, aspiration pneumonia, DVT and PE, and DIC. Reported mortality is up to 4%, and about another third are left with permanent sequelae. 240

Exam hookAbrupt withdrawal: rigidity + fever + reduced consciousness + raised CK. 240

The levodopa label

The carbidopa-levodopa label describes sporadic cases of an NMS-like syndrome after dose reduction or withdrawal and advises close observation when levodopa is cut abruptly, "especially if the patient is receiving neuroleptics". 102

What to observe when doses are missed

  • Tremor against the person's baseline
  • Temperature, respiratory rate, blood pressure
  • Cognition, with the 4AT
  • Swallowing
  • Creatine kinase

Parkinson's UK also names dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome as a risk alongside NMS-like syndrome. 300 In one series, 5 of 26 agonist tapers (19%) produced it, all in people with earlier agonist-related impulse-control disorders. 301

Giving the dose

Crushing

Extended-release carbidopa-levodopa tablets "should not be chewed or crushed"; a whole or half tablet is swallowed intact. 102 Parkinson's UK: never crush or split modified-release products (CR, MR, XL, PR); break other tablets only if scored; give tablets one at a time on a teaspoon with soft food or thickened fluid. 300

Protein and iron

The label says a high-protein diet may delay levodopa absorption and reduce uptake, and iron salts, including those in multivitamins, can chelate levodopa and carbidopa. 102 In 9 patients with fluctuations, meals cut peak plasma levodopa by 29% and delayed absorption by 34 minutes. 248

Self-administration

NICE: medicines at the appropriate times, "which in some cases may mean allowing self-medication", adjusted only by, or after discussion with, a Parkinson's specialist. 241 In a Parkinson's UK survey, 48% of people admitted wanted to manage and take their own medication. 298

Formulations are not interchangeable

The Parkinson's Foundation notes that different strengths and formulations of the same drug are often not interchangeable, and that people may need to bring their own supply when a drug is not on the formulary. 284

Nil by mouth is not nil Parkinson's medicine

A 2016 review lists four routes for people who cannot take their usual oral medicines: dispersible preparations in thickened fluids, an enteral tube, a skin patch, or a subcutaneous injection, and links omitted doses to worse control and to neuroleptic malignant-like syndrome. 12 Pick a situation to see what the Parkinson's UK guidance describes.

Rotigotine patch rules, as published

  • Round to the nearest 2 mg, up to 16 mg/24 h.
  • Do not cut patches; the 2, 4, 6 and 8 mg strengths can be combined.
  • Rotate the site every 24 hours.
  • 16 mg if the adjusted LEDD is above 350 mg, with specialist advice about apomorphine.
  • Raise the dose if stiffness increases; lower it if confusion or hallucinations increase.
  • "Could be contraindicated in delirium".
  • Remove before MRI or cardioversion: the backing contains aluminium.

PDMedCalc, after OPTIMAL

Parkinson's UK now points professionals to PDMedCalc, registered with the MHRA as a Class 1 medical device on 15 February 2024. Its page says the OPTIMAL calculator has closed and advises professionals not to use it. PDMedCalc applies a correction factor so rotigotine doses are not too large, because large doses can cause confusion, hallucinations or delirium. Apomorphine and Duodopa/ProDuodopa need no conversion; amantadine can be omitted if swallowing is compromised. 299

What can usually wait

Entacapone and the other COMT and MAO-B inhibitors are not licensed for enteral tubes and can usually be omitted for a short time. Oral dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole) are not licensed for tubes either; rotigotine patches are the substitute. 300

Contraindicated-drug checker

Many of the drugs to avoid block dopamine receptors; the levodopa label, for example, warns that metoclopramide's dopamine-blocking properties may worsen disease control. 102 Others interact with an MAO-B inhibitor or with apomorphine. Pick a drug to see what each source says. The sources do not always agree, and where they differ, the checker shows both.

The person uses apomorphine
Sources disagree The Parkinson's Foundation lists ondansetron and the other 5HT3 antagonists in its "safe" column. The apomorphine (APOKYN) label contraindicates 5HT3 antagonists (ondansetron, granisetron, dolasetron, palonosetron, alosetron) because of "profound hypotension and loss of consciousness" when apomorphine was given with ondansetron. The conflict matters for people using apomorphine. 101,284 Parkinson's UK says cyclizine and ondansetron can be used after surgery but may cause confusion and altered consciousness. 300

If psychosis needs a drug

NICE: do not offer olanzapine; consider quetiapine for people without cognitive impairment; offer clozapine if standard treatment fails, with registration with a patient monitoring service; both at lower doses than in other indications. Other antipsychotics (phenothiazines, butyrophenones) can worsen motor features. 241

If nausea needs a drug

Parkinson's UK: consider domperidone (oral or rectal) if there are no contraindications, but avoid it in cardiac disease, where it slightly raises the risk of arrhythmia and sudden cardiac death. 300 The Parkinson's Foundation lists prochlorperazine, metoclopramide, promethazine and droperidol to avoid. 284

If confusion needs a drug

Parkinson's UK: screen with the 4AT, look for and treat causes such as infection, dehydration and constipation, and consider a benzodiazepine rather than an antipsychotic if one is needed. 300 The Parkinson's Foundation warns that benzodiazepines, muscle relaxants, bladder-control drugs and other sleep or pain drugs may cause confusion, hallucinations and falls. 284

How often it happens

Contraindicated dopamine blockers were given in 23% of 339 hospital encounters, and those patients stayed longer. 218 In US veterans, contraindicated drugs were prescribed for 19 of 89 patients. 154

Exam hookAvoid haloperidol, metoclopramide and prochlorperazine. 284,300

Safety risks in numbers

Falls, aspiration, delirium, orthostatic hypotension and impulse-control disorders are the safety problems the sources return to most. Each panel uses its own units, so compare within a panel, not across.

Falls 11 · delirium 121 · aspiration pneumonia 381 · swallowing 167 · orthostatic hypotension 360 · impulse-control disorders 68

Falls

Across 22 prospective studies, 60.5% (range 35% to 90%) reported at least one fall and 39% recurrent falls; recurrent fallers averaged 20.8 falls a year. Associated factors: previous falls, freezing of gait, dopamine agonist use, higher levodopa dose, cognitive impairment and fear of falling. 11

In an RCT of 231 people, home exercise for balance, strength and freezing did not reduce falls overall (IRR 0.73, 95% CI 0.45 to 1.17); falls fell in milder disease (IRR 0.31) and tended to rise in more severe disease (IRR 1.61, not significant). 51

Aspiration and swallowing

In a Korean national cohort, aspiration pneumonia occurred at 3.01 against 0.59 per 1,000 person-years (HR 4.21); after a first episode, mortality was 23.9% at 1 month and 65.2% at 1 year, similar to controls. 381 Swallowing problems were reported by 35% when asked but found in 82% on testing. 167 NICE offers speech and language therapy for swallowing or saliva problems, including expiratory muscle strength training. 241

The Parkinson's Foundation calls aspiration pneumonia "the leading cause of death" in Parkinson's; that claim is unverified against cause-of-death data. 284 In the CamPaIGN cohort, pneumonia was the most common cause of death. 378

Delirium

Delirium (DSM-5) was diagnosed in 66.9% of 121 hospitalised people with Parkinson's, against 38.7% of 199 older controls. In Parkinson's it was linked to higher 12-month mortality (HR 3.3), institutionalisation (OR 10.7) and dementia (OR 6.1). 121 Parkinson's UK lists what to watch for in acute admissions: delirium, chest infection (especially aspiration pneumonia), urinary infection, postural hypotension and falls, constipation, and NMS-like syndrome. 300

Orthostatic hypotension

Pooled prevalence 30.1% (95% CI 22.9% to 38.4%), with large heterogeneity. 360 Parkinson's UK asks for blood pressure lying and sitting, then standing. 300 The measuring method and the 20/10 and 30/15 criteria are in the Assessment tab.

Impulse-control disorders

In 411 people with disease of 5 years or less, the 5-year cumulative incidence was 46.1%: 51.5% in people who had ever taken a dopamine agonist and 12.4% in those who never had; disorders gradually resolved after agonists were stopped. 68 In 3,090 patients, 13.6% had one (17.1% on an agonist vs 6.9%). 371 With a problematic disorder, NICE says to seek specialist advice and reduce any agonist gradually, watching for withdrawal. 241

When doses slip

The Parkinson's Foundation lists the consequences of delayed medication as falls, skin breakdown, incontinence, eating difficulties, emotional distress and inaccurate assessment of discharge needs, and recommends mobilising as soon as it is medically safe, with physiotherapy and occupational therapy input. 284

Deep-brain stimulation in hospital

A DBS system has leads in the brain connected to an implantable pulse generator. 103 Strong electrical fields can make the system carry energy into the brain. 354 Pick a procedure to see what one DBS centre's safety sheet says. Device rules differ by manufacturer and model; the manufacturer's labelling and the person's DBS team decide. More on how DBS works: Deep-brain stimulation.

Case series

DBS withdrawal syndrome

A review of 20 cases, compared with 15 patients whose stimulation stopped without problems, found the main risk factors were long disease duration and many years of DBS. Patients can deteriorate quickly, may not respond to high-dose levodopa, and the condition can become life-threatening. The authors argue a stopped battery should be replaced as an emergency. 134

What the person brings

People with DBS stay on their usual medication routine, carry an ID card with the device model number and contact details, and need an oral drug list in case the DBS fails. 300 They should always bring their patient programmer to hospital. 354

Apomorphine pumps

Pumps continue at the prescribed rate, and settings change only on specialist request. 300 Ondansetron and the other 5HT3 antagonists are contraindicated with apomorphine. 101

Levodopa-carbidopa intestinal gel

Duodopa continues if gastric emptying and the PEJ tube are working; if not, it is replaced with rotigotine. 300 The Parkinson's Foundation says people with a Duopa device may bring their own cassettes, and that the device is turned off, disconnected and taken out of the room during imaging. 284

Myths and what the sources say

The home diary

The Hauser diary records each half-hour as asleep, OFF, ON, ON with non-troublesome dyskinesia, or ON with troublesome dyskinesia. People generally count OFF time and troublesome dyskinesia as "bad time". 136 Pick an example day.

Fictional example days. In 302 patients, reliability rose with more days (alpha 0.868 at 3 days, 0.946 at 6), but 76% of missing or duplicate entries came after day 3. 136

What the guidelines say to teach

When treatment starts

NICE 1.3.8: give oral and written information on impulse-control disorders (higher risk with agonists), excessive sleepiness and sudden-onset sleep with agonists, and psychotic symptoms, and record that this was discussed. 241 Adults on dopaminergic therapy are told about impulse-control disorder risk when they start and at least once a year. 242

Impulse-control signs

When an agonist starts, NICE 1.4.3 asks for information on the increased risk (including that affected people may hide it), the types of disorder, who to contact, and the possibility that the agonist will be reduced or stopped; 1.4.4 asks for it to be discussed at reviews, especially when therapy changes. 241

Driving and sleep

People who drive tell the DVLA and their insurer at diagnosis (NICE 1.1.7). People with daytime sleepiness or sudden-onset sleep are advised not to drive and to tell the DVLA (1.5.1). 241

Before a hospital stay

Write down the exact times doses are due so the home schedule can be followed; know that strengths and formulations are often not interchangeable; bring one's own supply if a drug is not on the formulary. 284

Standing up safely

Consensus non-drug measures for neurogenic orthostatic hypotension include about 2 L of water a day, about 500 mL drunk within 5 minutes (raising systolic pressure by about 30 mmHg for up to an hour), extra salt if appropriate, raising the head of the bed by 15 to 23 cm (not with pillows) and 30 to 40 mmHg compression garments. 123

Constipation

The 2019 MDS review rates probiotics and prebiotic fibre "efficacious" and macrogol "likely efficacious", and says fibre and fluid measures should always be recommended. 329 How fast oral levodopa acts depends on protein intake, gastric emptying and constipation. 114

Moving and falls

NICE 1.7.2 and 1.7.3: consider early referral to a physiotherapist experienced in Parkinson's, and offer Parkinson's-specific physiotherapy for balance or motor problems. 241 Previous falls, freezing and fear of falling are among the factors linked to recurrent falls. 11

The Parkinson's disease nurse specialist

  
NICE says people with Parkinson's should have regular access to clinical monitoring and medicine adjustment, a continuing point of contact (including home visits when appropriate) and reliable information, "which may be provided by a Parkinson's disease nurse specialist". 241 Quality statement 1: adults with Parkinson's have a point of contact with specialist services. 242 Two randomised trials have tested the role.

      Mixed trial results
      
Better wellbeing and process, not motor outcomes

      
In 1,859 patients from 438 English practices over 2 years, community nurse specialists did not change mortality (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.73 to 1.13), the stand-up test, the dot-in-square test, the PDQ-39 or EuroQol, but self-rated global health was better (difference −0.23, 95% CI −0.40 to −0.06). Costs rose by about £2,658 per person over 2 years, £266 less in the nurse group, not a significant difference. 162

      
In 328 US veterans, nurse-led chronic care management raised adherence to 18 quality-of-care indicators to 0.77 against 0.58; of 8 patient-centred secondary outcomes, only a depression screen improved. 64

      
In plain wordsSpecialist nurses made care more reliable and people felt better in themselves, but the trials did not show better movement or longer life.
