# E · Urate-lowering therapy & treat-to-target

Pass date: 2026-10-20. Modules served: ult, t2t, ultsafety.

Unit note: 1 mg/dL serum urate ≈ 59.5 µmol/L. Guideline targets are written as the sources write them (ACR and EULAR use 6 mg/dL [360 µmol/L]; FAST and the EU febuxostat SmPC use 357 µmol/L).

## Findings

### ult · When to start urate-lowering therapy (ULT)
- ACR 2020 strongly recommends starting ULT for a person with gout who has ≥1 subcutaneous tophus, radiographic damage (any modality) attributable to gout, or frequent flares (≥2 per year) [[FitzGerald2020]].
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends ULT after >1 previous flare when flares are infrequent (<2 per year), and conditionally recommends against ULT after a first flare, except when the first flare comes with CKD stage ≥3, serum urate >9 mg/dL (>535 µmol/L) or urolithiasis (conditional for, very low certainty) [[FitzGerald2020]].
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends against any drug ULT for asymptomatic hyperuricaemia (SU >6.8 mg/dL [>405 µmol/L] with no prior flare or tophus). In CVD-outcome RCTs incident gout was <1% on ULT versus 5% on placebo, so about 24 people would need 3 years of ULT to prevent one first flare; in observational data only 20% of people with SU >9 mg/dL developed gout within 5 years [[FitzGerald2020]].
- EULAR 2016: ULT "should be considered from the first presentation" of gout, with SU kept at <6 mg/dL (360 µmol/L), or <5 mg/dL (300 µmol/L) in severe gout. Allopurinol is first line, with its dose adjusted to renal function [[Richette2017]].
- EULAR 2016 detail not confirmed against the full text: ULT is indicated with recurrent flares (≥2/year), tophi, urate arthropathy and/or renal stones, and should start close to diagnosis in people <40 years, with SU >8 mg/dL (480 µmol/L) or with comorbidity [[Richette2017]] (unverified).
- ACP 2017 strongly recommends against starting long-term ULT in most people after a first flare or with infrequent flares, and recommends discussing benefits, harms, costs and preferences before ULT for recurrent flares. It sets no urate target [[Qaseem2017]].
- Japan differs. The Japanese guideline (JGMHG, 3rd edition), as summarised in Circulation Journal, considers a urate-lowering agent for asymptomatic hyperuricaemia at SU ≥9 mg/dL without complications or ≥8 mg/dL with complications, with a target of ≤6 mg/dL. It defines hyperuricaemia as SU >7.0 mg/dL (ACR uses >6.8 mg/dL) [[Hisatome2021,Yamanaka2011]].
- FREED (Japan; 1070 people aged ≥65 with SU >7.0 to ≤9.0 mg/dL and cerebral, cardiovascular or renal risk; 36 months): SU at the endpoint was 4.50 ± 1.52 mg/dL on febuxostat versus 6.76 ± 1.45 mg/dL without it. The primary composite (cerebral, CV and renal events plus all deaths) fell to 23.3% from 28.7% (HR 0.750, 95% CI 0.592–0.950), driven by renal impairment (16.2% vs 20.5%; HR 0.745). Deaths from cerebral, CV or renal disease were 1.1% in both groups. Gout flares occurred in 1.1% vs 2.6% (P = 0.069) [[Kojima2019]].
- The US labels for febuxostat, pegloticase and lesinurad all state the drug is "not recommended for the treatment of asymptomatic hyperuricemia" [[FDA-Uloric,FDA-Krystexxa,FDA-Zurampic]].

### ult · Starting ULT during a flare
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends starting ULT during a flare, rather than waiting for it to settle, once ULT is indicated (moderate certainty) [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Taylor 2012 RCT (57 men with crystal-proven gout; allopurinol 300 mg vs placebo for 10 days; all received indomethacin 50 mg three times daily and colchicine 0.6 mg twice daily for 90 days): no difference in daily pain (VAS 6.72 vs 6.28 at the start; 0.18 vs 0.27 at day 10). Later flares occurred in 2 vs 3 participants. On allopurinol, SU fell from 7.8 mg/dL to 5.9 mg/dL by day 3 [[Taylor2012]].
- Hill 2015 RCT (allopurinol 100 mg/day for 14 days, then 200 mg/day, vs placebo, started within 72 h of flare treatment; 31 completers): days to resolution were 15.4 vs 13.4 (P = 0.5), so the flare was not prolonged [[Hill2015]].
- The older label wording differs. The probenecid label says not to start therapy "until an acute gouty attack has subsided" [[FDA-Probenecid]].

### ult · How the drugs work: make less (xanthine oxidase inhibitors)
- Allopurinol is a purine (hypoxanthine) analogue that inhibits xanthine oxidase, the enzyme that converts hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid. Its metabolite oxipurinol (oxypurinol, alloxanthine) also inhibits the enzyme [[FDA-Zyloprim]].
- Pharmacokinetics: allopurinol has an oral bioavailability of 79 ± 20% and a half-life of 1.2 ± 0.3 h. About 90 mg of oxypurinol forms from each 100 mg of allopurinol. Oxypurinol's half-life is 23.3 ± 6.0 h with normal kidney function, and it is cleared almost entirely in the urine (renal clearance 0.19 ± 0.06 of creatinine clearance). Most of the urate-lowering effect comes from oxypurinol [[Day2007]].
- The US label gives an allopurinol half-life of about 1–2 h and an oxipurinol half-life of about 15 h, enough for once-daily dosing. Oxipurinol is reabsorbed in the tubules much as urate is [[FDA-Zyloprim]].
- Hande 1984: oxipurinol clearance = 0.22 × creatinine clearance − 2.87. In renal insufficiency, 300 mg/day of allopurinol produced raised steady-state oxipurinol. This paper is the origin of creatinine-clearance-based dosing (78 cases of a severe toxicity syndrome) [[Hande1984]].
- Febuxostat is a non-purine selective xanthine oxidase inhibitor [[Becker2005,White2018]]. Its terminal half-life is about 5–8 h. The US starting dose is 40 mg once daily, raised to 80 mg if SU is still ≥6 mg/dL after 2 weeks, and limited to 40 mg in severe renal impairment [[FDA-Uloric]].
- EU febuxostat dosing is 80 mg once daily, with 120 mg considered if SU is >6 mg/dL (357 µmol/L) after 2–4 weeks. It acts quickly enough to retest after 2 weeks. The SmPC recommends flare prophylaxis for at least 6 months [[EMA-Adenuric]].
- Approval dates: allopurinol (Zyloprim) US 19 Aug 1966; febuxostat EU (Adenuric) 21 Apr 2008 and US (Uloric) 13 Feb 2009 [[DrugsFDA-ULT,EMA-Adenuric]].

### ult · How the drugs work: excrete more (uricosurics)
- Probenecid increases urinary uric acid excretion and lowers SU. The US label starts at 250 mg twice daily for 1 week, then 500 mg twice daily, rising by 500 mg every 4 weeks (usually not above 2000 mg/day). It "may not be effective" when GFR is ≤30 mL/min, is contraindicated with uric acid kidney stones, and salicylates antagonise its uricosuric action [[FDA-Probenecid]].
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends starting probenecid at 500 mg once or twice daily with titration. It strongly prefers allopurinol or febuxostat over probenecid in CKD stage ≥3, and conditionally recommends against checking urinary uric acid or alkalinising the urine on a uricosuric [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Benzbromarone: 100 mg/day lowered SU by 5.04 mg/dL (from 8.58 to 3.54 mg/dL), and 100% of underexcretors reached <6 mg/dL, compared with 53% on allopurinol 300 mg/day [[PerezRuiz1998]].
- Benzbromarone safety: registered in about 20 countries, then withdrawn by Sanofi-Synthélabo in 2003 after reports of hepatotoxicity, though other companies still market it. The estimated hepatotoxicity risk in Europe is about 1 in 17,000 patients (possibly higher in Japan). It is metabolised by CYP2C9 and inhibits it, so it interacts with warfarin [[Lee2008]]. Drugs@FDA lists no benzbromarone application [[DrugsFDA-ULT]].
- Head-to-head trials after allopurinol failure (normal kidney function, target ≤5 mg/dL [300 µmol/L]): benzbromarone 200 mg reached target in 92% (22/24) and probenecid 2 g/day in 65% (20/31). Mean SU fell 64% and 50% [[Reinders2009Prob]].
- Lesinurad (a URAT1 inhibitor) was approved in the US on 22 Dec 2015 as 200 mg/day with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor, never alone. Its boxed warning reads "risk of acute renal failure, more common when used without a xanthine oxidase inhibitor" [[FDA-Zurampic,DrugsFDA-ULT]].
- CLEAR 1 (n = 603; allopurinol ≥300 mg): lesinurad 200 mg plus allopurinol brought 54.2% to SU <6 mg/dL at month 6 (400 mg: 59.2%) versus 27.9% on allopurinol alone. There was no significant benefit on flares or tophus resolution [[Saag2017]].
- CLEAR 2 (n = 610): 55.4% (200 mg) and 66.5% (400 mg) versus 23.3% reached target. Renal adverse events occurred in 5.9%, 15.0% and 4.9%, and creatinine rose ≥1.5× baseline in 5.9%, 15.0% and 3.4% [[Bardin2017]].
- Lesinurad's market status: withdrawn from the US market by the manufacturer while the ACR 2020 guideline was being written (Drugs@FDA lists it as "Discontinued"). The EU authorisation (granted 18 Feb 2016) was withdrawn for commercial reasons, with notification on 31 Jul 2020 [[FitzGerald2020,DrugsFDA-ULT,EMA-Zurampic]].
- Dotinurad, a selective URAT1 inhibitor, was approved in Japan in January 2020 (URECE). It barely inhibits the secretory transporters ABCG2 and OAT1/3 [[Taniguchi2020]]. It was non-inferior to febuxostat (SU −41.82% on 2 mg vs −44.00% on 40 mg) and to benzbromarone (−45.9% on 2 mg vs −43.8% on 50 mg) [[Hosoya2020Feb,Hosoya2020Benz]]. Its efficacy is not reduced in mild to moderate renal impairment [[Ishikawa2021]].
- Mild urate-lowering by other drugs (outcomes belong to pass F):
  - Losartan 50 mg/day lowered SU from 538 to 491 µmol/L through uricosuria; irbesartan had no effect [[Wurzner2001]].
  - Fenofibrate 200 mg/day lowered SU by 19% on top of allopurinol and raised urate clearance by 36% [[Feher2003]].
  - SGLT2 inhibitors lowered SU by a weighted mean of −37.73 µmol/L across 62 RCTs (34,941 people), with no effect at eGFR <60 [[Zhao2018]].
  - ACR 2020 conditionally recommends choosing losartan as an antihypertensive when feasible, and conditionally recommends against adding or switching to fenofibrate [[FitzGerald2020]].

### ult · How the drugs work: break it down (uricase)
- Pegloticase is a PEGylated recombinant uricase. It catalyses the oxidation of uric acid to allantoin, an inert, water-soluble product cleared by the kidneys. About 24 h after the first 8 mg dose, mean plasma uric acid was 0.7 mg/dL versus 8.2 mg/dL on placebo [[FDA-Krystexxa]].
- US approval came on 14 Sep 2010. The EU authorisation (granted 8 Jan 2013) was withdrawn on 30 Jun 2016 [[DrugsFDA-ULT,EMA-Krystexxa]].
- The current US dose is 8 mg IV every 2 weeks with oral methotrexate 15 mg weekly plus folic or folinic acid, started at least 4 weeks before the first infusion. Oral ULT is stopped first, and patients are pre-medicated with an antihistamine and a corticosteroid [[FDA-Krystexxa]].
- Two replicate 6-month RCTs (n = 225; SU ≥8 mg/dL; allopurinol-refractory or intolerant): SU <6 mg/dL in months 3 and 6 was reached by 42% (36/85) on every-2-week dosing, 35% (29/84) on every-4-week dosing and 0% (0/43) on placebo [[Sundy2011]].
- Anti-drug antibodies: 41% (69/169) developed high-titre anti-pegloticase antibodies, which were linked to faster drug clearance and loss of response. Loss of the urate response came before 79% (44/56) of infusion reactions, so pre-infusion SU works as a surrogate for these antibodies [[Lipsky2014]].
- MIRROR RCT (n = 152; 2:1 randomisation; methotrexate 15 mg/week vs placebo plus pegloticase): month-6 response (SU <6 mg/dL for ≥80% of weeks 20–24) was 71.0% vs 38.5% (difference 32.3%, 95% CI 16.3–48.3). Infusion reactions fell to 4.2% (4/96) from 30.6% (15/49) [[Botson2023]]. The label gives month-12 response as 60% vs 31% [[FDA-Krystexxa]].
- ACR 2020 strongly recommends switching to pegloticase only when xanthine oxidase inhibitors, uricosurics and other measures have failed and flares remain frequent (≥2/year) or tophi persist. It strongly recommends against pegloticase as first-line therapy [[FitzGerald2020]].

### t2t · Targets, titration and monitoring
- Rationale: sustained urate lowering dissolves monosodium urate crystals, which in turn prevents flares and tophi. Allopurinol is first-line, started low and escalated gradually, and nurse-led models of care are now best practice (Established) [[Dalbeth2021,Dalbeth2019NRDP]].
- ACR 2020 strongly recommends treat-to-target dosing guided by serial SU over fixed dosing, and strongly recommends a target of <6 mg/dL (high certainty) for everyone on ULT [[FitzGerald2020]].
- EULAR 2016 sets <6 mg/dL (360 µmol/L), or <5 mg/dL (300 µmol/L) in severe gout [[Richette2017]]. EULAR also advises against SU <3 mg/dL (180 µmol/L) over the long term; this detail is not confirmed against the full text, only in secondary sources [[Richette2017]] (unverified).
- The 2016 international T2T task force issued 4 overarching principles and 9 recommendations. It found no randomised strategy trial, so these are level-D expert recommendations focused on crystal dissolution, flare prevention, education, adherence and SU monitoring [[Kiltz2017]].
- Allopurinol starting dose: ACR 2020 strongly recommends ≤100 mg/day, lower in CKD stage ≥3, and says "even lower initial allopurinol doses (e.g., ≤50 mg/day) should be considered in patients with CKD". For febuxostat it recommends ≤40 mg/day [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Titration interval: ACR 2020 says titration should take "weeks to months, not years", cites the 2012 ACR advice of every 2–5 weeks, and asks for an SU check after each dose step [[FitzGerald2020]].
  - The US label raises the dose by 100 mg each week to SU ≤6 mg/dL, with a maximum of 800 mg/day [[FDA-Zyloprim]].
  - The UK SmPC starts at 100 mg/day, gives 300–600 mg/day for moderately severe and 700–900 mg/day for severe disease, so the maximum is 900 mg [[SmPC-Allopurinol-UK]].
  - EULAR starts at 100 mg/day and adds 100 mg every 2–4 weeks [[Richette2017]] (unverified: full text not accessible).
- Real-world titration schedules:
  - Doherty 2018 (UK): 100 mg/day raised by 100 mg every 3–4 weeks to a maximum of 900 mg. Second-line options were febuxostat 80→120 mg or benzbromarone 50→200 mg [[Doherty2018]].
  - Stamp 2017 RCT: dose raised monthly [[Stamp2017RCT]].
  - STOP Gout: allopurinol started at 100 mg and febuxostat at 40 mg, titrated over 24 weeks to a maximum of 800 mg and 120 mg [[ODell2022]].
- The US label says normal SU is "usually achieved in 1 to 3 weeks" and full benefit "may be delayed for 2 to 6 weeks" [[FDA-Zyloprim]].
- Starting dose rule: in a case–control study (54 allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome [AHS] cases, 157 controls), the highest quintile of starting dose per eGFR had an odds ratio of 23.2. 91% of cases versus 36% of controls started at ≥1.5 mg per mL/min of eGFR, so the authors propose starting at ≤1.5 mg × eGFR, then escalating [[Stamp2012]].
- Escalating above the creatinine-clearance dose:
  - In 90 people (45 needing escalation), 88.8% (31/35 completers) reached SU <6 mg/dL at 12 months, with no serious adverse events, including in renal impairment [[Stamp2011]].
  - RCT (n = 183; 52% CrCl <60): 69% vs 32% at SU <6 mg/dL at month 12. SU changed by −1.5 vs −0.34 mg/dL (difference 1.2 mg/dL) [[Stamp2017RCT]].
  - Extension: when controls switched to escalation, SU fell a further 1.1 mg/dL from month 12 to 24, with similar adverse events [[Stamp2017OLE]].
- Expected response by dose:
  - Allopurinol 300 mg/day reached SU <6 mg/dL in 21% (FACT; last 3 monthly values) [[Becker2005]], 22% (APEX) [[Schumacher2008]], 42% (CONFIRMS, 300/200 mg) [[Becker2010]] and 53% (after steady state) [[PerezRuiz1998]].
  - Against the stricter ≤5 mg/dL target, 300 mg reached it in 24–26%, and escalation to 600 mg in 78% [[Reinders2009Prob,Reinders2009Allo]].
  - Allopurinol 300 mg lowered SU by 2.75 mg/dL (normal excretors) to 3.34 mg/dL (underexcretors) [[PerezRuiz1998]], a mean 35% fall [[Reinders2009Prob]]. That works out to roughly 1 mg/dL per 100 mg over the first 300 mg (derived, not stated by the sources).
  - Serbian cohort (n = 27 completers): 70.4% reached <6 mg/dL on 300 mg and 92.5% on ≤600 mg; SU went 534 → 347 → 275 µmol/L [[RadakPerovic2013]].
  - Febuxostat reached SU <6 mg/dL in 45% on 40 mg, 67% on 80 mg (CONFIRMS) [[Becker2010]], 53% on 80 mg and 62% on 120 mg (FACT) [[Becker2005]].
- Poor allopurinol response genetics: ABCG2 rs2231142 (Q141K) predicts SU ≥6 mg/dL despite >300 mg/day in adherent people (OR 2.71, 95% CI 1.70–4.48). A replication meta-analysis gave OR 2.43 [[Roberts2017,Wallace2018]]. In a GWAS of 2027 people, ABCG2 (BCRP) both transports allopurinol and determines response [[Wen2015]].
- Nurse-led T2T (Doherty 2018; 517 people in UK primary care):
  - SU <360 µmol/L at 1 year: 94.75% vs 26.22% (RR 3.59). At 2 years: 95% vs 30% (RR 3.18, 95% CI 2.42–4.18).
  - SU <300 µmol/L at 2 years: 88% vs 17%.
  - Mean allopurinol dose was 460 vs 230 mg/day, with >300 mg/day in 79–80% vs 10–11%. Adherence was 95% at 2 years, and the cost was £5066 per QALY [[Doherty2018]].
- Proof-of-concept (n = 106, nurse-led): 92% reached SU ≤360 µmol/L at 12 months and 85% reached <300, on a median allopurinol dose of 400 mg [[Rees2013]].
- Pharmacist-led automated telephone T2T (n = 1463): adherence 50% vs 37%, and SU <6 mg/dL 30% vs 15% at 1 year [[Mikuls2019]].
- STOP Gout (VA; n = 940; ≥33% with CKD stage 3; 72 weeks): 80% reached target in phase 2 on either drug. Phase-3 flares were 36.5% on allopurinol vs 43.5% on febuxostat, so allopurinol was non-inferior, with no difference in CV events [[ODell2022]].
- Adherence without support is poor. In a meta-analysis of 13 studies, only 46% (95% CI 41–51) took ≥80% of their ULT, and non-persistence ranged from 54% to 87% [[Scheepers2018]].
- In Doherty's baseline cohort only 39% were on ULT, at a mean dose of 227 mg/day, and about 20% were at target [[Doherty2018]].

### t2t · Why flares rise early, and prophylaxis
- Mechanism: the US label attributes early flares to "mobilization of urates from tissue deposits which cause fluctuations in the serum uric acid levels" and says flares become shorter and milder after several months [[FDA-Zyloprim]]. Flares are expected in the first months and do not mean the drug is failing; febuxostat need not be stopped during a flare [[FDA-Uloric]].
- In Doherty 2018, the proportion with ≥2 flares per year was higher with nurse-led T2T at 1 year (54% vs 40%, RR 1.36) and much lower at 2 years (8% vs 24%, RR 0.33). Four or more flares went 28% → 1% vs 21% → 12% [[Doherty2018]].
- Borstad 2004 RCT (n = 43; colchicine 0.6 mg twice daily vs placebo when starting allopurinol):
  - total flares 0.52 vs 2.91
  - months 0–3: 0.57 vs 1.91
  - months 3–6: 0 vs 1.05
  - flare severity VAS 3.64 vs 5.08
  - The authors support 6 months of colchicine [[Borstad2004]].
- Pooled febuxostat phase III data (n = 4101): flare rates rose sharply, up to 40%, when 8 weeks of prophylaxis stopped, but stayed at 3–5% at the end of 6 months of prophylaxis (colchicine 0.6 mg/day or naproxen 250 mg twice daily), with no rise in adverse events [[Wortmann2010]].
- FACT gave prophylaxis only in weeks 1–8. Flares in weeks 9–52 still occurred in 64–70% of every arm [[Becker2005]].
- ACR 2020 strongly recommends concomitant anti-inflammatory prophylaxis (colchicine, an NSAID or prednisone/prednisolone) for 3–6 months rather than <3 months, continuing if flares persist [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Prophylaxis duration elsewhere: EULAR advises the first 6 months, with colchicine 0.5–1 mg/day or a low-dose NSAID [[Richette2017]] (unverified: full text not accessible). The EU febuxostat SmPC says at least 6 months [[EMA-Adenuric]]. The US febuxostat label says "may be beneficial for up to six months" [[FDA-Uloric]]. The pegloticase label says at least the first 6 months [[FDA-Krystexxa]].
- The US colchicine prophylaxis dose is 0.6 mg once or twice daily, maximum 1.2 mg/day [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Slower titration lowers flares too. In FORTUNE-1 (12 weeks), flares occurred in:
  - 20.8% on febuxostat stepped up from 10 to 40 mg
  - 18.9% on fixed 40 mg plus colchicine 0.5 mg/day
  - 36.0% on fixed 40 mg alone
  - The comparisons were P = 0.047 and 0.024, not significant after multiplicity correction [[Yamanaka2018]].
- ACR 2020: "a lower starting dose of any ULT reduces the risk of flare associated with initiation" [[FitzGerald2020]].

### t2t · How long to continue
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends continuing ULT indefinitely (very low certainty). In the cited case series of withdrawal after years of control, only 13% (27/211) of those whose SU stayed <7 mg/dL off ULT were flare-free over 5 years [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Perez-Ruiz 2006 prospective withdrawal study: the longest flare-free period (>4 years) came with a mean SU <5.05 mg/dL while on ULT and <8.75 mg/dL after stopping. The authors suggest 5-year intermittent ULT rather than lifelong therapy for well-controlled people (Early evidence) [[PerezRuiz2006]].
- GO TEST Finale (≥278 people in remission >12 months, randomised to continued T2T at <0.36 mmol/L or to tapering and stopping with restart on flare) is the first randomised trial of this question. Only its protocol is published (Hypothesis-testing, results pending) [[Peeters2023]].

### ultsafety · Allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome (AHS) and HLA-B*58:01
- Incidence:
  - US Medicaid (400,401 new users): hospitalised allopurinol-associated severe cutaneous adverse reactions occurred in 1 in 1972 initiators. The mean stay was 9.6 days and 21% (43/203) died [[Keller2018]].
  - Relative risk versus white or Hispanic people: Black 3.00, Asian 3.03, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander 6.68.
  - Other risk factors: female sex 2.5-fold, age ≥60 1.7-fold, CKD 2.3-fold, starting dose >100 mg/day 1.9-fold.
  - Older women of high-risk ancestry had an RR of 12.25 [[Keller2018]].
- Literature series of 901 cases (1950–2012):
  - Onset: about 90% (489/538) within 60 days of starting.
  - HLA-B*58:01 was present in 99% (166/167) of those tested.
  - All-cause mortality was 14% (109/788).
  - Renal impairment was present in 48% and diuretic use in 45% [[Ramasamy2013]].
- EuroSCAR (379 SJS/TEN cases): allopurinol was the commonest cause (adjusted OR 18). Doses ≥200 mg/day carried an OR of 36 versus 3.0 for lower doses, and risk was confined to the first ≤8 weeks of use [[Halevy2008]].
- Hung 2005 (Han Chinese): HLA-B*58:01 was present in 100% (51/51) of allopurinol SCAR cases versus 15% (20/135) of tolerant users (OR 580.3) and 20% of healthy controls [[Hung2005PNAS]].
- Thailand: 100% (27/27) of SJS/TEN cases versus 12.96% of tolerant controls (OR 348.3). Sensitivity is 100% and specificity 87%. Assuming 0.2% prevalence, the PPV is 1.52% and the NPV 100% [[Tassaneeyakul2009]].
- CPIC: allopurinol is contraindicated in HLA-B*58:01 carriers (strong). Non-carriers follow standard dosing (strong). The 2015 update left the 2013 recommendation unchanged [[Hershfield2013,Saito2016,CPIC-HLAB-Allopurinol]].
- Allele frequency of HLA-B*58:01 (CPIC weighted averages):
  - East Asian 6.0% (range 0.4–17%)
  - Sub-Saharan African 4.4%
  - Central/South Asian 4.2%
  - African American/Afro-Caribbean 3.8%
  - Near Eastern 1.9%
  - Latino 1.4%
  - European 0.77% [[CPIC-HLAB-Allopurinol]]
- ACR 2020 quotes prevalence as 7.4% in Han Chinese, Korean and Thai people, 3.8% in African Americans and 0.7% in white and Hispanic people [[FitzGerald2020]].
- The UK SmPC gives "up to 20%" in Han Chinese, 8–15% in Thai, about 12% in Korean and 1–2% in Japanese or European people [[SmPC-Allopurinol-UK]].
- In Taiwan, 19.6% (571/2910) of Han Chinese allopurinol candidates tested positive [[Ko2015]].
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends HLA-B*58:01 testing before allopurinol for people of Southeast Asian descent (e.g. Han Chinese, Korean, Thai) and African American people, and conditionally recommends against universal testing in others. Testing was cost-effective (ICER <$109,000/QALY). Asian and African American people have about 3-fold AHS risk [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Taiwan prospective screening (Ko 2015): carriers were given alternatives, and 2339 non-carriers took allopurinol. None developed SCAR, against 7 expected from the historical 0.30% per year (P = 0.0026). Mild rash occurred in 3% [[Ko2015]].
- Allopurinol desensitisation is conditionally recommended after a prior allergic reaction when no other oral ULT is possible (very low certainty) [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Labels say to stop allopurinol at the first sign of rash. A person who has had a severe reaction must not be re-challenged [[FDA-Zyloprim,SmPC-Allopurinol-UK]].
- Benzbromarone review estimate: allopurinol life-threatening hypersensitivity syndrome occurs in about 1 in 56,000 users, and rash in about 2% [[Lee2008]].

### ultsafety · Febuxostat cardiovascular safety: CARES vs FAST
- CARES design (US-led; 6190 people with gout and major CVD; median 32 months; non-inferiority margin HR 1.3): 56.6% stopped trial drug and 45.0% stopped follow-up [[White2018]].
- CARES results:
  - Primary MACE: 10.8% vs 10.4% (HR 1.03; upper bound of the one-sided 98.5% CI 1.23; non-inferior).
  - CV death: HR 1.34 (95% CI 1.03–1.73).
  - All-cause death: HR 1.22 (1.01–1.47) [[White2018]].
- The US label adds the counts. CV deaths were 134 (1.5 per 100 PY) vs 100 (1.1 per 100 PY), sudden cardiac death 83 (2.7%) vs 56 (1.8%), and all-cause deaths 243 (7.8%; 2.6 per 100 PY) vs 199 (6.4%; 2.2 per 100 PY) [[FDA-Uloric]].
- FDA boxed warning ("Cardiovascular death") was added in February 2019 (label revision 2/2019). Febuxostat is now limited to people with an inadequate response to maximally titrated allopurinol, intolerance, or when allopurinol is not advisable [[FDA-Uloric,FDA-DSC-Febuxostat2019]].
- FAST design (UK, Denmark, Sweden; 6128 people aged ≥60 with ≥1 CV risk factor; allopurinol first optimised to <0.357 mmol/L; open label; median follow-up 1467 days) [[Mackenzie2020]].
- FAST results:
  - On-treatment primary endpoint: 1.72 vs 2.05 events per 100 patient-years (adjusted HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.70–1.03; non-inferior).
  - Deaths: 222 (7.2%) on febuxostat vs 263 (8.6%) on allopurinol.
  - Randomised drug stopped: 32.4% vs 16.5%.
  - Lost from all follow-up: only 6.2% vs 5.5% [[Mackenzie2020]].
- The EU SmPC now cites both. CARES showed more fatal CV events in people with pre-existing major CVD, and FAST did not [[EMA-Adenuric]].
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends switching people on febuxostat with a history of CVD or a new CV event to another ULT when possible [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Japan: in FEATHER and FREED, febuxostat mortality did not differ from placebo or allopurinol [[Hisatome2021,Kojima2019]].

### ultsafety · Interactions
- Azathioprine and mercaptopurine are inactivated by xanthine oxidase, so blocking the enzyme raises their levels:
  - US allopurinol label: with 300–600 mg/day of allopurinol, cut azathioprine or mercaptopurine to about one-third to one-quarter of the usual dose [[FDA-Zyloprim]].
  - UK SmPC: avoid the combination, because fatal cases have been reported [[SmPC-Allopurinol-UK]].
  - US febuxostat label: contraindicated [[FDA-Uloric]].
  - EU febuxostat SmPC: not recommended; if unavoidable, reduce to ≤20% of the previous dose [[EMA-Adenuric]].
- Didanosine is an allopurinol interaction, not a febuxostat one. Allopurinol 300 mg roughly doubles didanosine Cmax and AUC [[SmPC-Allopurinol-UK]], and the US didanosine label contraindicates co-administration [[FDA-Didanosine]]. Neither the current US nor the EU febuxostat label lists didanosine [[FDA-Uloric,EMA-Adenuric]].
- Rash with ampicillin or amoxicillin is more frequent alongside allopurinol (cause not established) [[FDA-Zyloprim]].
- Febuxostat and theophylline: use with caution [[FDA-Uloric]].
- Probenecid raises methotrexate levels and penicillin levels (2- to 4-fold); salicylates are contraindicated with it [[FDA-Probenecid]].
- Pegloticase: anti-PEG antibodies may cross-react with other PEGylated products [[FDA-Krystexxa]].
- Benzbromarone inhibits CYP2C9, which matters for warfarin [[Lee2008]].
- Lowering SU needs a G6PD screen before pegloticase in people at risk. It is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency (haemolysis, methaemoglobinaemia) [[FDA-Krystexxa]].

### ultsafety · Pegloticase safety and the stopping rule
- Boxed warning: anaphylaxis and infusion reactions can occur with any infusion, usually within 2 hours [[FDA-Krystexxa]].
- Rule: check SU before each infusion and stop if it rises above 6 mg/dL, particularly after 2 consecutive values >6 mg/dL [[FDA-Krystexxa]].
- Pre-marketing reactions (pegloticase alone, no stopping rule): anaphylaxis 6.5% (8/123) on every-2-week dosing; infusion reactions 26% vs 5% on placebo [[FDA-Krystexxa]].
- Antibodies: anti-pegloticase antibodies in 92% on every-2-week dosing. With methotrexate, anaphylaxis occurred in 1 person (1%), and a rise in antibody titre in 30% vs 51% without it [[FDA-Krystexxa]].
- Flares are frequent at the start. 74% (every 2 weeks) and 81% (every 4 weeks) vs 51% on placebo flared in the first 3 months [[FDA-Krystexxa]].

### ultsafety · Pregnancy and lactation (as labels and reviews state)
- Allopurinol:
  - The UK SmPC calls the safety evidence in pregnancy "inadequate"; use only when there is no safer alternative. Breast milk has contained 1.4 mg/L allopurinol and 53.7 mg/L oxipurinol at 300 mg/day, and use while breastfeeding "is not recommended" [[SmPC-Allopurinol-UK]].
  - The US label reports animal data and a case report of malformations, and advises caution in nursing [[FDA-Zyloprim]].
  - In 31 prospectively followed first-trimester exposures, the major malformation rate was 3.7%, with one severe case resembling a published case. The authors advise caution in the first trimester [[Hoeltzenbein2013]].
- Febuxostat: the US label says data are insufficient to inform risk, and nothing is known about human milk [[FDA-Uloric]]. The EU SmPC says it "should not be used during pregnancy" [[EMA-Adenuric]].
- Probenecid crosses the placenta and appears in cord blood [[FDA-Probenecid]].
- Pegloticase: there are no adequate studies in pregnancy, and it should not be used in breastfeeding unless the benefit is clear [[FDA-Krystexxa]].

### Exam hooks
- Strong ACR 2020 indications for ULT: ≥1 tophus, radiographic damage, or ≥2 flares/year [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Allopurinol is first line even in CKD stage ≥3. Start ≤100 mg/day (lower in CKD), titrate to SU <6 mg/dL, and give prophylaxis for 3–6 months (all strong) [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Do not stop ULT during a flare, and ULT may be started during a flare (conditional) [[FitzGerald2020,Taylor2012,Hill2015]].
- The active metabolite oxypurinol (t½ about 23 h) is cleared by the kidney, which is why CKD raises exposure [[Day2007,Hande1984]].
- HLA-B*58:01 is a CPIC contraindication to allopurinol [[Hershfield2013,CPIC-HLAB-Allopurinol]]. ACR tests before allopurinol in Han Chinese, Korean, Thai and African American people [[FitzGerald2020]].
- About 90% of AHS begins within 60 days of starting allopurinol [[Ramasamy2013]].
- In CARES, febuxostat versus allopurinol gave CV death HR 1.34 and all-cause death HR 1.22, with MACE non-inferior. FAST gave HR 0.85 and was non-inferior [[White2018,Mackenzie2020]].
- Never combine azathioprine or mercaptopurine with febuxostat (contraindicated in the US). With allopurinol, cut the thiopurine dose to one-quarter to one-third [[FDA-Uloric,FDA-Zyloprim]].
- Pegloticase: screen for G6PD, check SU before every infusion and stop if >6 mg/dL twice, and give methotrexate 15 mg/week as co-therapy [[FDA-Krystexxa,Botson2023]].
- Probenecid "may not be effective" at GFR ≤30 mL/min and is contraindicated with uric acid stones [[FDA-Probenecid]].
- Nurse-led T2T reached SU <6 mg/dL in 95% vs 30% with usual care at 2 years [[Doherty2018]].
- Febuxostat starts at 40 mg in the US (80 mg in the EU) [[FDA-Uloric,EMA-Adenuric]].

### In plain words
- There are three ways to lower uric acid: make less of it (allopurinol, febuxostat), pass more in the urine (probenecid and similar drugs), or break it down with an enzyme drip (pegloticase).
- The aim is a blood uric acid below 6 mg/dL. Below that level, the crystals in your joints slowly dissolve.
- The dose starts low and goes up a little every few weeks, with a blood test each time, until you reach the number.
- Most people need more than the "standard" 300 mg of allopurinol. Higher doses are usually safe when they are reached slowly.
- Flares often get more common in the first months, because the crystals are breaking up. That is a sign the medicine is working, not failing.
- A low daily dose of colchicine (or an anti-inflammatory) for the first 3–6 months helps prevent those early flares.
- Do not stop your uric acid medicine during a flare. Treat the flare and keep taking it.
- If you get a rash, fever or sore mouth or eyes after starting allopurinol, stop it and get medical help the same day. This is rare but serious, and almost always happens in the first two months.
- If your family comes from China, Korea or Thailand, or you are of African descent, a gene test before allopurinol is advised.
- Gout treatment usually continues for life. People who stop often get flares again, even years later.

## Open questions and controversies
- Treat to a target or treat to avoid symptoms? ACR 2020 (strong, <6 mg/dL) and EULAR 2016 treat to target [[FitzGerald2020,Richette2017]], as do G-CAN and the T2T task force [[Dalbeth2017,Kiltz2017]]. ACP 2017 sets no target and does not recommend monitoring SU [[Qaseem2017,Dalbeth2017]]. Status: nurse-led and pharmacist-led T2T trials improve SU, tophi and later flares [[Doherty2018,Mikuls2019]]. A trial of T2T strategy against treat-to-avoid-symptoms in remission is under way [[Peeters2023]].
- Is febuxostat unsafe for the heart? CARES found more CV and all-cause deaths, leading to the FDA boxed warning in 2019, but 45% were lost to follow-up [[White2018,FDA-Uloric]]. FAST was non-inferior (HR 0.85) with fewer deaths on febuxostat and about 6% lost to follow-up, though it was open label and enrolled people already tolerating allopurinol [[Mackenzie2020]]. Status: the US restricts febuxostat to second line; ACR 2020 conditionally advises switching with CVD; the EU SmPC presents both trials [[FitzGerald2020,EMA-Adenuric]].
- Treat asymptomatic hyperuricaemia? ACR 2020 conditionally against (NNT 24 over 3 years to prevent first gout) [[FitzGerald2020]]. Japan treats at ≥8–9 mg/dL [[Hisatome2021]], supported by FREED's renal-driven composite benefit [[Kojima2019]]. Status: unresolved; kidney outcomes are covered in pass F.
- Lifelong ULT, or stop once crystals clear? ACR 2020 conditionally favours indefinite therapy (very low certainty) [[FitzGerald2020]]. Perez-Ruiz proposes 5-year intermittent ULT if control was good [[PerezRuiz2006]]. Status: awaiting GO TEST Finale [[Peeters2023]].
- Start ULT during a flare? Small RCTs (n = 57 and 31 completers) show no harm [[Taylor2012,Hill2015]]. ACR moved to conditional "may start" [[FitzGerald2020]]. Status: evidence is limited to small trials.
- Dose by kidney function or escalate to target? The creatinine-clearance cap [[Hande1984]] under-treats most people. Escalation above it is effective and was well tolerated in trials [[Stamp2011,Stamp2017RCT]], but the starting dose itself is a risk factor for AHS [[Stamp2012,Keller2018]]. Status: consensus is to start low and go slow without a hard renal cap.
- Universal HLA-B*58:01 screening? Screening abolished SCAR in Taiwan [[Ko2015]], but the PPV is only about 1.5–2% [[Tassaneeyakul2009,Ko2015]]. ACR tests by ancestry only [[FitzGerald2020]].

- Corrections to the brief and dossier hand-offs:
  1. The Stamp 2017 dose-escalation RCT is in Ann Rheum Dis 2017;76(9):1522-1528 (PMID 28314755), not Arthritis Rheumatol. Its extension is Ann Rheum Dis 2017;76(12):2065-2070. At 12 months, 69% vs 32% reached target.
  2. The dossier's "Stamp2012 dose escalation above creatinine-clearance dose" mixes two papers. Escalation above the CrCl dose is Stamp2011 (and Stamp2017RCT/OLE); Stamp2012 is the starting-dose risk factor (1.5 mg per mL/min eGFR).
  3. On the ACR CKD starting dose, the dossier says "≤50 mg/day in CKD stage ≥4". ACR 2020 actually says "≤100 mg/day and lower in patients with CKD [stage ≥3]" and "even lower initial doses (e.g., ≤50 mg/day) should be considered in patients with CKD". No stage-4 threshold is stated.
  4. The ACR titration interval "every 2–5 weeks" is ACR 2020 quoting the 2012 ACR guideline. The US label itself titrates weekly by 100 mg.
  5. The brief's "didanosine with febuxostat" is not supported. The interaction is with allopurinol (UK SmPC: Cmax and AUC roughly doubled; US didanosine label: contraindicated). Neither febuxostat label lists didanosine.
  6. Lesinurad: US approval 22 Dec 2015 is confirmed. The exact US market-withdrawal date (2019) was not confirmed in a primary source; ACR 2020 states it was withdrawn by the manufacturer during guideline development. The EU withdrawal was notified 31 Jul 2020.
  7. Pegloticase EMA withdrawal (dossier "2016, to verify") is confirmed as 30/06/2016 (authorised 08/01/2013).
  8. "Botson2023, to verify" is confirmed as Arthritis Rheumatol 2023;75(2):293-304, PMID 36099211, NCT03994731.
  9. Dotinurad's Japan approval (dossier "2020") is confirmed as January 2020 [[Taniguchi2020]].
  10. The brief's slower-titration study "Yamanaka 2011" is FORTUNE-1, Yamanaka 2018 Ann Rheum Dis (PMID 29102957). Yamanaka 2011 is the Japanese guideline, 2nd edition (PMID 22132951).
  11. The dossier CARES and FAST figures (HR 1.34, 1.22 and 0.85) are confirmed. The FAST 95% CI is 0.70–1.03.
  12. The dossier Doherty figures (95% vs 30% at 2 years) are confirmed. The 1-year figures are 94.75% vs 26.22%.
