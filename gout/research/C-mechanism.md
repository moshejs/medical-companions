# C · Urate biology & crystal inflammation

Pass date: 2026-10-20. Modules served: urate, crystals, flare.

Unit rule used throughout: mg/dL first, µmol/L in brackets (× 59.48). Where a source reports µmol/L or µM only, that value is given first and the mg/dL conversion follows in brackets marked "≈".

## Findings

### urate · Scene 1–2: purines in, xanthine oxidoreductase makes urate

Ordered steps for the pathway animation (actors in bold):

1. **Purine nucleotides** (AMP, GMP, IMP) come from cell turnover, de novo synthesis and diet. De novo synthesis starts with PRPP amidotransferase, its rate-limiting step, and needs four amino acids, one PRPP, two folates and three ATP to build one IMP [[Torres2007]].
2. Nucleotides are broken down through nucleosides (inosine, guanosine) to the free bases **hypoxanthine** and **guanine**; guanine feeds into **xanthine**. Purine-degradation enzymology is reviewed in [[Maiuolo2016]] (nucleoside step: textbook biochemistry; Maiuolo2016 full text not read) (unverified).
3. **Salvage branch:** HPRT puts hypoxanthine and guanine back into IMP and GMP, using PRPP as co-substrate. This recycles bases away from urate [[Torres2007]].
4. **Xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR)** works at two consecutive steps: hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid. Each step makes reactive oxygen species (superoxide, hydrogen peroxide) [[Chen2016]]. XOR is the rate-limiting enzyme of urate production [[Furuhashi2020]].
5. XOR exists as xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) and xanthine oxidase (XO). Most liver XOR is in the XDH form, and it converts to XO by reversible sulfhydryl oxidation or irreversible proteolysis [[Chen2016,Furuhashi2020]]. XOR is also found in intestine, mammary gland, heart and skeletal muscle, and vascular endothelium [[Chen2016]].
6. The liver is the main site of urate production [[HalperinKuhns2021]].
7. At pH 7.4 and 37 °C, urate circulates mainly as the mono-deprotonated urate anion, which pairs with sodium to form monosodium urate (MSU) [[Martillo2014]].

Daily production and pool size:
- Radio-isotope studies from more than 50 years ago put the urate pool at about 1,200 mg (range 937–1,650 mg) in healthy men and about 600 mg (range 541–687 mg) in healthy women [[Ohashi2023]].
- The first new estimate of daily urate production in over 50 years came from 123 anuric haemodialysis patients. Production was 591 mg/day (men 648 mg/day; women 489 mg/day) [[Ohashi2023]].

### urate · Scene 3: why humans run high (no uricase)

- In most mammals, uricase (urate oxidase) oxidises uric acid to allantoin, which is about 100 times more water-soluble. In humans and higher primates, uric acid is the end product [[Chen2016,So2010]].
- Rats and mice normally have blood uric acid of 1–2 mg/dL (≈ 59–119 µmol/L). Human reference ranges are 2.6–5.7 mg/dL (155–339 µmol/L) in premenopausal women and 3.5–7.0 mg/dL (208–416 µmol/L) in men and postmenopausal women [[Chen2016]].
- Humans and great apes have serum uric acid of 200–500 µM (≈ 3.4–8.4 mg/dL), against 3–120 µM (≈ 0.05–2.0 mg/dL) in other mammals [[Vitart2008]]. Enomoto et al. give the same human range of 200–500 µM [[Enomoto2002]].
- **Uricase lost more than once (Established):** in human, chimpanzee and orangutan, a nonsense mutation at codon 33 silenced the gene. The gibbon lost it separately. Wu et al. reported a 13-bp deletion in gibbon and argued that two independent losses suggest an evolutionary advantage [[Wu1992]]. Oda et al. confirmed the shared CGA→TGA nonsense mutation in exon 2 for human and great apes. They found the gibbon loss came from a different nonsense or frameshift mutation, not the 13-bp deletion. They proposed two steps of decline: first the promoter, then the coding region [[Oda2002]].
- **Activity fell gradually before the gene was silenced (Established):** resurrected ancestral uricases show that activity fell steadily from the last common mammalian ancestor down the primate line, before pseudogenisation [[Kratzer2014]].
- **URAT1 and uricase co-evolved (Early evidence):** URAT1 affinity for uric acid rose during primate evolution. This happened alongside changes that greatly reduced uricase activity, 27–77 million years ago [[Tan2016]].
- Evolutionary hypotheses for losing uricase. All are labelled; none is proven.
  - Antioxidant and longer lifespan (Hypothesis): urate is "about as effective an antioxidant as ascorbate" in vitro. Human plasma urate (~300 µM, ≈ 5.0 mg/dL) is far above plasma ascorbate [[Ames1981]]. Oda et al. leave open whether uricase loss relates to oxidative protection and lifespan [[Oda2002]].
  - Making up for lost vitamin C synthesis (Hypothesis): urate rose to compensate for the lack of ascorbic acid in hominoids [[Furuhashi2020]].
  - Keeping blood pressure up on a low-salt diet (Hypothesis): in rats, mild hyperuricaemia raises blood pressure by a renin-dependent mechanism, most clearly on a low-salt diet. The proposed advantage dates to the Miocene [[Watanabe2002]].
  - Fructose-to-fat "thrifty" adaptation (Hypothesis, early experimental support): ancestral uricases expressed in HepG2 cells support the idea that losing uricase helped frugivorous apes turn fructose into fat. This would have mattered when tropical forests gave way to temperate ones [[Kratzer2014]].

### urate · Scene 4: the kidney reabsorbs most filtered urate; the gut removes the rest

- **Split of elimination:** about two thirds of urate leaves through the kidney and one third through the extra-renal route, mainly the intestine. This comes from radio-isotope studies [[Ohashi2023]]. Other sources give 70% renal [[HalperinKuhns2021,Vitart2008]] or 70% urine and 30% gastrointestinal [[Chen2016]].
- **Reabsorption:** at least 90% of filtered urate is reabsorbed, by transporters including URAT1 and GLUT9 [[Chen2016]]. Tan et al. say about 90% is reabsorbed through URAT1 [[Tan2016]].
- **Fractional excretion of urate (FEUA)** in health ranges from 5% to 15% [[HalperinKuhns2021]].
- **Tubule map, in order** (proximal tubule) [[HalperinKuhns2021]]:
  1. First, bulk reabsorption from the lumen, probably by apical **OAT4 (SLC22A11)** and **OAT10 (SLC22A13)**. Urate then exits on the basolateral side through **GLUT9 (SLC2A9)**.
  2. Secretion: urate re-enters the tubule cell from blood through basolateral **OAT1 (SLC22A6)** and **OAT3 (SLC22A8)**. It is secreted into the lumen by apical **ABCG2**, with possible extra help from apical **NPT1 (SLC17A1)** and **NPT4 (SLC17A3)**.
  3. Post-secretory reabsorption, mainly by apical **URAT1 (SLC22A12)** coupled to basolateral GLUT9.
  - ABCG2 secretion and URAT1 reabsorption can happen in the same proximal tubule cell [[HalperinKuhns2021]].
- **URAT1** was identified in 2002 as the renal urate–anion exchanger. Defects in SLC22A12 cause idiopathic renal hypouricaemia. URAT1 is the target of uricosuric and antiuricosuric drugs [[Enomoto2002]]. URAT1 exchanges urate for anions such as lactate, α-ketoglutarate and β-hydroxybutyrate [[HalperinKuhns2021]].
- **Loss-of-function anchors:** people with URAT1 mutations have plasma uric acid below 60 µM (< 1 mg/dL) and FEUA of nearly 100% [[So2010]]. People homozygous for the GLUT9 L75R mutation have serum uric acid of 0.17 ± 0.2 mg/dL (≈ 10 µmol/L) and FEUA of about 150% [[So2010]].
- **GLUT9 (SLC2A9)** has strong urate transport activity. Its variants explain 1.7–5.3% of the variance in serum uric acid and are associated with low FEUA and with gout [[Vitart2008]].
- **ABCG2** is a high-capacity urate exporter [[Matsuo2009]]. It sits in the proximal tubule brush border, where it mediates renal urate secretion [[Woodward2009]]. It is also on the apical side of intestinal enterocytes and on the liver's bile canalicular membrane [[HalperinKuhns2021]].
- **Drugs at the tubule:** diuretics compete with urate at OAT1–3. Loop diuretics also directly inhibit ABCG2 and NPT4, which reduces secretion [[HalperinKuhns2021]].
- **Gut capacity when the kidneys fail:** in anuric haemodialysis patients, extra-renal ABCG2 could excrete up to about 60% of daily urate turnover [[Ohashi2023]].

### urate · Scene 5: under-excretion vs overload

- In 100 people with primary gout and 72 healthy controls, people with gout had lower urate clearance, FEUA and urinary urate/creatinine ratio than controls. This held even after allopurinol had matched the filtered load. Even "high output" patients (> 700 mg/day urinary urate) under-excreted relative to controls. The authors call renal under-excretion "the main mechanism" of primary hyperuricaemia in gout [[PerezRuiz2002Renal]]. Most people with gout and primary hyperuricaemia under-excrete urate when fractional clearance is measured [[So2010]].
- **Reclassification (Early evidence):** in 644 hyperuricaemic men, ABCG2 export dysfunction paradoxically *raised* urinary urate excretion. ABCG2-knockout mice had lower intestinal urate excretion. Ichida et al. proposed renaming "overproduction type" as "renal overload type", with two subtypes: extra-renal under-excretion and genuine overproduction [[Ichida2012]].
- **Toggle anchors for ABCG2 Q141K (rs2231142):**
  - Q141K reduced urate transport rates by 53% compared with wild-type ABCG2 (P < 0.001). In 14,783 people, rs2231142 was strongly associated with serum urate (whites P = 10⁻³⁰, minor allele frequency 0.11; blacks P = 10⁻⁴, minor allele frequency 0.03) and with gout (adjusted OR 1.68 per risk allele). At least 10% of gout cases in whites are attributable to this variant [[Woodward2009]].
  - In a Japanese population, the non-functional Q126X variant gave a gout OR of 5.97. Of 159 people with gout, 16 (10%) had genotype combinations that cut ABCG2 function by more than 75% (OR 25.8) [[Matsuo2009]].
  - In UK Biobank, the largest per-locus OR for gout was at ABCG2 (OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.96–2.12) [[Tin2019]].
- **GWAS growth:** 28 loci in more than 140,000 people of European ancestry in 2013 [[Kottgen2013]]. In 2019 a trans-ancestry GWAS of 457,690 people found 183 loci (147 new). These explain 7.7% of serum urate variance and point to the kidney and liver as the main target organs. HNF4A drives ABCG2 transcription [[Tin2019]].
- **Heritability:** Tin et al. give serum urate heritability as 30–60%. In one pedigree study they estimated h² = 37% (95% credible interval 29–45%), and the 183 index SNPs explained 17% of it [[Tin2019]]. Halperin Kuhns & Woodward give 40–70% for gout risk and serum urate [[HalperinKuhns2021]]. The dossier's 30–60% matches Tin2019.
- **Genetic risk score:** in 334,880 UK Biobank participants, gout prevalence rose from 0.1% to 12.9% across urate genetic risk score categories. The adjusted OR ranged from 0.09 in the lowest category to 13.6 in the highest. Adding the score to age and sex gave AUC 0.84 [[Tin2019]].
- GWAS confirm that urate *excretion* (kidney, gut) dominates control of serum urate, with the liver also involved. Genes in the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway play a part in progression from hyperuricaemia to gout [[Major2018Genet]]. Altered urate transport in gut and kidney is central to hyperuricaemia [[Dalbeth2019NRDP,Dalbeth2021]].
- **Rare monogenic causes (overproduction or kidney):**
  - HPRT1 deficiency is X-linked recessive, with more than 300 mutations. Every patient overproduces uric acid, which leads to stones and gout. Severe forms are Lesch–Nyhan syndrome. Prevalence is about 1 in 380,000 live births (Canada) and 1 in 235,000 (Spain) [[Torres2007]].
  - PRPS1 superactivity (X-linked) causes uric acid overproduction, often with hearing impairment and neurological features [[deBrouwer2010]].
  - UMOD mutations cause autosomal dominant tubulointerstitial kidney disease (ADTKD-UMOD), formerly called "familial juvenile hyperuricaemic nephropathy" [[Eckardt2015]].

### crystals · Solubility: saturation, temperature, pH, sodium, tissue

- **Saturation:** plasma is saturated with urate at about 6.8 mg/dL (405 µmol/L) [[Martillo2014]]. Loeb calculated 6.8 mg/dL at body temperature and physiological sodium [[Chhana2015,Loeb1972]]. Chen et al. give the saturation point as 6.6 mg/dL (392 µmol/L) at 37 °C and pH 7.4 [[Chen2016]]. These figures differ by 0.2 mg/dL; the companion uses 6.8 mg/dL.
- **Temperature:** a drop of just 2 °C, from 37 °C to 35 °C, lowers urate solubility from 6.8 to 6.0 mg/dL (≈ 404 → 357 µmol/L) (in vitro, aqueous solutions; Loeb's data as reported by Martillo2014) [[Martillo2014,Loeb1972]]. This may partly explain why the first MTP is the most common site of a first flare: it is cooler, with lower perfusion and a high surface-to-volume ratio [[Martillo2014]].
- **pH and ions:** across 35 studies in a systematic review, solubility was consistently reduced by lower temperature, pH 7–9 and ions including sodium. Solubility was greater at pH ≤ 6 or ≥ 10 [[Chhana2015]]. Lower pH promoted MSU nucleation in vitro, partly by freeing Ca²⁺ ions [[Martillo2014]].
- **Connective tissue:** bovine cartilage homogenates and healthy human synovial fluid and serum all *enhanced* urate solubility. Nucleation was increased by sodium ions, uric-acid-binding antibodies, and synovial fluid or serum from people with gout [[Chhana2015]].
- **Three stages: solubility → nucleation → growth.** A high urate concentration drives all three. No other factor was found to promote crystal *growth* specifically [[Chhana2015]].
- **Crystal structure:** MSU crystals are triclinic. Stacked sheets of purine rings form the needles seen under the microscope [[Martillo2014]].
- **Why at night, why the big toe (Hypothesis):** water leaves a resolving joint effusion about twice as fast as urate, so the local urate concentration rises for a while and can pass saturation [[Simkin1977]]. Flares are 2.36 times as likely overnight (12:00–7:59 am) as in the daytime (OR 2.36, 95% CI 2.05–2.73; 724 people, 1,433 flares) [[Choi2015]].
- Crystals deposit preferentially at the first MTP, femoral condyle, Achilles tendon and patellar tendon [[Chhana2015]].

### crystals · Who forms crystals: serum urate vs risk over 15 years

- **15-year cumulative incidence (individual-participant data, 4 US cohorts, 18,889 people, 212,363 person-years):** 1.1% (95% CI 0.9–1.4) at baseline urate < 6 mg/dL (< 360 µmol/L), rising to 49% (31–67) at ≥ 10 mg/dL (≥ 595 µmol/L). Adjusted HRs against < 6 mg/dL: 2.7 for 6.0–6.9; 6.6 for 7.0–7.9; 15 for 8.0–8.9; 30 for 9.0–9.9; 64 for ≥ 10 mg/dL [[Dalbeth2018ARD]]. The dossier's 1.1% and 49% are confirmed.
- **Normative Aging Study (2,046 men, 14.9 years):** annual gout incidence was 4.9% at urate ≥ 9 mg/dL (≥ 535 µmol/L), 0.5% at 7.0–8.9 mg/dL and 0.1% below 7.0 mg/dL. Cumulative incidence at ≥ 9 mg/dL reached 22% after 5 years [[Campion1987]].
- About 10% of people with hyperuricaemia develop gout [[Vitart2008]]. In studies with about 5–10 years of follow-up, 2–36% did [[Martillo2014]].
- **Silent deposition before any flare (stage B):**
  - Dual-energy CT of the feet found urate deposits in 6/25 (24%) asymptomatic people with urate ≥ 540 µmol/L (≈ 9.1 mg/dL), against 11/14 (79%) with early gout (≤ 3 years) and 16/19 (84%) with late gout. Deposit volumes were lower in asymptomatic hyperuricaemia, suggesting a crystal-volume threshold for symptoms [[Dalbeth2015]].
  - On ultrasound, 25% of first MTPs in 50 asymptomatic hyperuricaemic people had a double-contour sign, against none of 52 normouricaemic controls. The figures were 17% vs 0 for femoral cartilage and 15% vs 1.9% for Achilles enthesopathy [[Pineda2011]].
  - Ultrasound MSU deposition in asymptomatic hyperuricaemia ranged from 26.0% to 68.8% depending on the definition used. People with hyperuricaemia had higher CRP, IL-1RA, IL-6, IL-18 and TGF-β than normouricaemic people [[PeralGarrido2025]].
- Disease stages: A, asymptomatic hyperuricaemia; B, asymptomatic MSU deposition; C, deposition with prior or current flares; D, advanced gout with tophi, chronic arthropathy and erosion [[Chhana2015]].

### crystals · Dissolution on urate-lowering therapy

- **Treatment target:** keep urate below 6 mg/dL (360 µmol/L), or below 5 mg/dL (300 µmol/L) in severe gout [[Richette2017]]. The ACR strongly recommends treat-to-target with a urate target below 6 mg/dL [[FitzGerald2020]]. A target below saturation (6.8 mg/dL) keeps the concentration gradient in favour of dissolution [[Martillo2014,Richette2017]].
- **Tophus shrinkage speed vs serum urate (Established):** 63 people with crystal-proven tophaceous gout were treated with allopurinol, benzbromarone or both. The speed of tophus shrinkage was linearly related to mean serum urate during therapy: the lower the urate, the faster tophi shrank. Benzbromarone, alone or combined, shrank tophi faster than allopurinol alone, and the two drugs were equally effective when the same urate level was reached [[PerezRuiz2002]]. The slope in mm/month is in the full text, which was not accessible; the animation should draw a straight line falling with urate and label it "relative".
- **Crystal disappearance from joint fluid:**
  - 18 men (11 knees, 7 first MTPs) were aspirated every 3 months after starting urate lowering. Median urate fell from 550 µmol/L (≈ 9.2 mg/dL) to 290 µmol/L (≈ 4.9 mg/dL) by 3 months.
  - Crystals disappeared from all 18 joints, after 3 to 33 months. Time to disappearance correlated with gout duration (rs = 0.71, p < 0.01).
  - Median crystal count fell from 7.5 to 3 per 400× field at 3 months.
  - In 9 of 10 joints from people with gout of < 10 years' duration, crystals had gone by 12 months; the last one cleared by 18 months [[Pascual2007]].
  - Correction to the dossier: Pascual2007 does **not** report separate times for knee (~18 months) and MTP (~33 months). The range of 3–33 months is for both joints together and follows gout duration, not joint site.
- **Some crystals persist:** 19 people kept urate ≤ 6 mg/dL for more than 12 months, and 7/16 (44%) of their asymptomatic knees still contained MSU crystals. In those still above 6 mg/dL, 14/16 knee aspirates had crystals [[LiYu2001]].
- Sustained urate lowering dissolves MSU crystals, ultimately preventing flares and tophi [[Dalbeth2021]].
- Flares become more common early after starting urate-lowering therapy. One proposed reason is crystals breaking loose from shrinking (micro)tophi ("sloughing") [[Martillo2014]] (Hypothesis).

### flare · Triggers (hour 0)

- Large crystal deposits do not by themselves cause flares: people with tophi do not necessarily have flares. MSU crystals alone had no effect on healthy blood mononuclear cells [[Joosten2010]].
- Case-crossover data from people with gout:
  - Purine intake over 2 days: OR for a flare 1.17, 1.38, 2.21 and 4.76 for the 2nd to 5th quintiles against the lowest [[Zhang2012]].
  - Alcohol in the previous 24 h: OR 1.36 for > 1–2 drinks and 1.51 for > 2–4 drinks, for any type of drink [[Neogi2014]].
  - Night-time risk: OR 2.36 [[Choi2015]].
- Possible crystal "sloughing" when urate falls (Hypothesis) [[Martillo2014]]. Concentration of urate in a resolving effusion (Hypothesis) [[Simkin1977]].

### flare · Signal 1: priming (hours 0–2, schematic)

- Without a first signal (for example PMA or LPS), monocytes and macrophages do not secrete mature IL-1β when cultured with MSU crystals [[Busso2010]].
- **Free fatty acid as signal 1 (Early evidence, human cells and mice):** the saturated fatty acid C18:0 (stearic acid) combined with MSU crystals made healthy blood mononuclear cells release large amounts of IL-1β through TLR2. Alcohol did not have this effect. The release depended on ASC and caspase-1 but, unexpectedly, not on NALP3 (NLRP3) [[Joosten2010]].
- **The role of TLRs is disputed:** TLR2- and TLR4-knockout macrophages made less IL-1β after MSU exposure. However, Chen et al. found that none of the TLR-deficient mouse strains they tested had impaired neutrophil influx, and none of TLR1–11 responded to MSU in reporter cells [[Busso2010,Chen2006]].

### flare · Signal 2: phagocytosis, lysosomal rupture, K⁺ efflux, NLRP3 (hours 1–4, schematic)

- MSU crystals (and CPPD crystals) engage the caspase-1-activating NALP3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, producing active IL-1β and IL-18. Macrophages lacking caspase-1, ASC or NALP3 are defective in crystal-induced IL-1β activation [[Martinon2006]].
- **Lysosome rupture and cathepsin B (Established for silica and alum; extended to MSU by analogy):** NALP3 activation needs crystal phagocytosis, which damages and ruptures lysosomes. Blocking phagosomal acidification or cathepsin B impairs activation [[Hornung2008]]. That study used silica and aluminium salts, not MSU.
- **K⁺ efflux is the common step:** for particulate matter and other NLRP3 activators, the only shared event was membrane permeability to K⁺ and Na⁺. A drop in cytosolic K⁺ was necessary and sufficient. In the same work, reactive oxygen species were *not* necessary [[MunozPlanillo2013]].
- **Assembly:** activated NLRP3 nucleates filaments of the ASC pyrin domain. These cluster the ASC CARD domains, which nucleate caspase-1 CARD filaments, and caspase-1 is activated by proximity [[Lu2014]]. NLRP3 assembly leads to caspase-1-dependent release of IL-1β and IL-18 and gasdermin D-mediated pyroptosis [[Swanson2019,Broz2016]].
- **Gasdermin D:** caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD. The N-terminal fragment drives pyroptosis, and IL-1β release falls in Gsdmd⁻/⁻ cells even though caspase-1 still processes it [[Shi2015]]. GSDMD-NT forms membrane pores [[Liu2016]]. Caspase-11 also cleaves GSDMD in non-canonical signalling [[Kayagaki2015]]. GSDMD pores let IL-1β out of *living* "hyperactivated" macrophages [[Evavold2018]].

### flare · Amplification: IL-1β → IL-1R1/MyD88 on tissue cells → chemokines → neutrophils (hours 4–24)

- IL-1 and the IL-1 receptor are essential for MSU-induced inflammation. Mice lacking IL-1R or MyD88 had reduced responses, and IL-1-neutralising antibodies reduced inflammation [[Chen2006]].
- IL-1R was needed on **non-bone-marrow-derived** cells, most probably endothelium, not on leukocytes [[Chen2006,Busso2010]].
- Mice lacking inflammasome components or IL-1R had impaired neutrophil influx [[Martinon2006]].
- **Chemokines and timing in animal models:**
  - In a mouse air pouch, neutrophilic inflammation 4 hours after crystal injection depended entirely on CXCR2 ligands.
  - In a rabbit model, neutralising IL-8 (CXCL8) blocked the delayed phase of neutrophil influx at 24 hours [[Busso2010]].
  - Gouty inflammation also upregulates IL-1β, IL-8, CXCL1 and G-CSF, and IL-6 and TNF rise as well [[Busso2010]].
- **Complement:** MSU activates the classical complement pathway, amplified by CRP and IgG. It also activates the alternative pathway, with C5 cleaved to C5a by a C5 convertase formed on the crystal surface [[Busso2010]].
- **Mast cells (Early evidence, mice):** mast-cell-derived IL-1β contributes to tissue swelling, mainly in the early stages of MSU arthritis [[Reber2014]].
- **IL-1 dependence in people:** in an open pilot, 10 people with gout who could not take or had failed standard therapy got anakinra 100 mg daily for 3 days.
  - All 10 responded; 4 improved within 24 h and all within 48 h.
  - 9 of 10 had complete resolution of joint signs by day 3.
  - Mean pain reduction was 79% by day 3 [[So2007]].
- Neutralising IL-1β gives rapid, sustained improvement in gout and other autoinflammatory diseases [[Dinarello2011]].
- Clinical anchor: "time to maximal pain < 24 hours" is one of the typical-episode features in the 2015 ACR/EULAR criteria [[Neogi2015]].

### flare · Peak and spontaneous resolution (days 2–14)

- **Human synovial fluid by phase** (38 people with gout; phase I 0–48 h, phase II days 3–4, phase III days 5–7) [[Scanu2012]]:
  - White cell counts were similar in phases I and II and lowest in phase III.
  - IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 and TNF were all significantly lower in phase III than in phase II.
  - TGF-β1 rose in phase II and was highest in phase III.
- **Aggregated NETs:** recruited neutrophils undergo an oxidative burst and form neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). At high neutrophil density, the NETs aggregate and degrade cytokines and chemokines through serine proteases. People with impaired NETosis had persistent inflammation. Tophi share features with aggregated NETs [[Schauer2014]].
- **Macrophage phenotype switch:**
  - Mature macrophages take up MSU crystals without secreting TNF, while less-differentiated cells secrete it [[Yagnik2000]].
  - MSU crystals make differentiated macrophages release TGF-β1, which suppresses endothelial E-selectin and neutrophil capture.
  - Cells from acute (16-hour) skin blisters secreted TNF but no TGF-β1; cells from resolving (40-hour) blisters made TGF-β1 [[Yagnik2004]].
- **Change in crystal coating:** in synovial fluid from people with gout, crystals were densely coated with IgG during acute inflammation. Apolipoprotein B coated them as inflammation subsided [[OrtizBravo1993]].
- Other resolution mechanisms include crystal coating, clearance of apoptotic cells, regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and TGF-β1 production [[Steiger2014]].
- The 2015 criteria list resolution of symptoms within 14 days as a typical-episode feature [[Neogi2015]]. Aggregated NETs are important in the resolution phase [[Dalbeth2021]].
- **Untreated course:** in 11 volunteers with podagra, 2 withdrew on day 4 because of severe pain. All remaining patients improved in pain by day 5 and in swelling by day 7, but only 3 had pain resolution within the 7-day study period [[Bellamy1987]].
- **Crystals remain:** inflammation resolves within a few days even though MSU crystals can still be found in synovial fluid and tissues [[Schauer2014]].

### flare · Why serum urate can be normal during a flare

- Serum urate was lower during the flare (7.5 ± 1.4 mg/dL [≈ 446 µmol/L]) than between flares (8.5 ± 0.9 mg/dL [≈ 506 µmol/L]; p < 0.0001). It was normal in 20 of 41 people (49%) during the flare. The fall correlated with CRP and IL-6 and with a rise in FEUA, and the change in FEUA correlated with plasma cortisol [[Urano2002]].
- Some people have a normal serum urate (≤ 6.0 mg/dL) at the time of a flare [[Martillo2014]].
- The classification criteria score serum urate ideally off urate-lowering therapy and more than 4 weeks from the start of an episode, retesting if possible [[Neogi2015]].

### flare · Chronic disease: tophus, erosion, cartilage

- **Tophus anatomy:** 16 surgically removed tophi from 12 people [[Dalbeth2010]]:
  - The corona zone holds many CD68⁺ mononuclear and multinucleated cells, very many plasma cells, and large numbers of IL-1β-expressing cells.
  - Mast cells were present in every sample; neutrophils were rare.
  - 6 of 12 patients (50%) had at least one B-cell aggregate in the fibrovascular zone.
  - CD68⁺ cell numbers correlated with IL-1β-expressing cells (r = 0.691) and TGF-β1-expressing cells (r = 0.518).
  - The authors describe a cycle of chronic inflammation, attempted resolution and remodelling.
- **Erosion comes from tophus in bone:** in 798 hand and wrist joints, 96 of 98 (98%) joints with radiographic erosion scores of 4–5 had intraosseous tophus. So did 194/237 (81.8%) of CT erosions and every erosion larger than 7.5 mm (56/56). Erosion and tophus diameters correlated at r = 0.93 [[Dalbeth2009]].
- **Osteoclasts (RANKL axis):** blood cells from people with severe erosive gout readily formed osteoclast-like cells with RANKL and M-CSF, and these patients had higher circulating RANKL and M-CSF. MSU crystals lowered osteoprotegerin in stromal cells and synovial fibroblasts without changing RANKL expression [[Dalbeth2008]].
- **Osteoblasts:** MSU crystals reduce osteoblast viability and differentiation. Bone next to a tophus has fewer osteoblasts and lining cells [[Chhana2011]].
- **Cartilage:** MSU crystals reduce chondrocyte viability. They lower type II collagen, aggrecan and versican, and raise ADAMTS4 and ADAMTS5. Joints affected by gout show empty chondrocyte lacunae [[Chhana2013]].

### Exam hooks
- Urate is the end product of purine metabolism in humans because hominoids lost uricase; xanthine oxidoreductase catalyses both hypoxanthine → xanthine and xanthine → urate [[Chen2016,Oda2002]].
- About two thirds of urate is excreted by the kidney and one third by the gut [[Ohashi2023]]. At least 90% of filtered urate is reabsorbed [[Chen2016]]. Normal FEUA is 5–15% [[HalperinKuhns2021]].
- URAT1 = SLC22A12 (apical reabsorption; uricosuric target); GLUT9 = SLC2A9 (basolateral exit; largest common-variant effect); ABCG2 = secretory exporter in kidney and gut [[Enomoto2002,Vitart2008,Woodward2009]].
- ABCG2 Q141K (rs2231142) cuts urate transport by 53%, gives a gout OR of 1.68 per allele, and accounts for ≥ 10% of gout in white people [[Woodward2009]].
- Most people with primary gout are renal under-excreters [[PerezRuiz2002Renal]].
- Urate saturation is about 6.8 mg/dL (405 µmol/L) at 37 °C; at 35 °C it is about 6.0 mg/dL [[Martillo2014]].
- At baseline urate ≥ 10 mg/dL, 15-year gout incidence is 49%; at < 6 mg/dL it is 1.1% [[Dalbeth2018ARD]].
- MSU crystals activate the NLRP3 inflammasome → caspase-1 → IL-1β [[Martinon2006]]. IL-1R/MyD88 signalling on non-haematopoietic cells is essential [[Chen2006]].
- Anakinra relieved acute gout in 10/10 people in an open pilot [[So2007]].
- Aggregated NETs degrade cytokines and help end the flare [[Schauer2014]].
- Serum urate can be normal during a flare: 49% in one series [[Urano2002]].
- Bone erosion in gout is driven by tophus infiltrating bone [[Dalbeth2009]].

### In plain words
- Your body makes urate every day when it breaks down purines, the building blocks found in every cell and in food.
- Most animals have an enzyme that breaks urate down further. Humans and great apes lost it millions of years ago, so we carry much more urate in our blood.
- The kidneys clear about two thirds of the urate and the gut clears about one third. In most people with gout, the kidneys hold on to too much of it. This is mostly inherited.
- When there is more urate than the blood and joint fluid can hold, it can form tiny needle-shaped crystals. Cooler parts of the body, like the big toe, reach that point first.
- Crystals can build up silently for years. Even among people with very high urate, only about half get gout within 15 years.
- A flare starts when immune cells swallow crystals and sound an alarm called IL-1β. That alarm calls in a flood of white blood cells, and that is the red, hot, painful joint.
- The flare usually burns out on its own within one to two weeks. The white cells form sticky nets that soak up the alarm signals.
- The crystals are still there after a flare. Keeping urate low for months to years slowly melts them away.
- Your urate level can look normal during a flare, so a normal number then does not rule out gout.

## Open questions and controversies
- **Why do only a minority of hyperuricaemic people get gout?**
  - Risk climbs steeply with urate, yet only about half of people at ≥ 10 mg/dL develop gout over 15 years. This implies that prolonged hyperuricaemia and other factors are needed [[Dalbeth2018ARD]].
  - Many hyperuricaemic people have no detectable deposits (24% DECT-positive) [[Dalbeth2015]]. Local tissue factors may decide where crystals nucleate [[Chhana2015]].
  - A metabolic second hit (fatty acids via TLR2) may be needed to turn crystals into a flare [[Joosten2010]]. Inflammasome-pathway genes affect progression [[Major2018Genet]].
  - Status: open. Deposition is necessary but not sufficient.
- **Is NLRP3 itself required?**
  - Inflammasome-deficient macrophages fail to activate IL-1β with MSU [[Martinon2006]].
  - With MSU plus free fatty acid, IL-1β release needed ASC and caspase-1 but not NALP3 [[Joosten2010]].
  - Other IL-1β-processing routes use neutrophil and mast cell proteases [[Busso2010]].
  - Status: the IL-1β/IL-1R step is firmly established [[Chen2006,So2007]]; which sensor is used upstream may depend on context.
- **Do TLRs recognise MSU?** TLR2/TLR4 and CD14 contribute in some models [[Busso2010,Joosten2010]]. Nine TLR-deficient mouse strains showed no impairment, and no TLR responded to MSU in reporter cells [[Chen2006]]. Status: unresolved.
- **ROS as the NLRP3 trigger?** ROS are often drawn as part of signal 2, but K⁺ efflux was the only necessary common step, and ROS were not required [[MunozPlanillo2013]]. Status: draw K⁺ efflux as the essential step and ROS as optional.
- **Overproduction vs under-excretion:** under-excretion is the main mechanism even in "high output" patients [[PerezRuiz2002Renal]]. Ichida et al. argue that many apparent overproducers are gut (ABCG2) under-excreters, a "renal overload" type [[Ichida2012]]. Status: the reclassification is used in Japanese practice but is not a universal standard.
- **Urate: antioxidant or harmful?** Urate is a major plasma antioxidant [[Ames1981]]. Inside cells it may act as a pro-oxidant [[Sautin2008]]. Mendelian randomisation does not support urate as causal for the metabolic comorbidities of gout [[Major2018Genet]]. Status: open. Pass F covers the kidney and cardiovascular trials.
- **Why did hominoids lose uricase?** Several independent losses suggest selection [[Wu1992,Oda2002]]. Proposed advantages include antioxidant defence and longer lifespan [[Ames1981]], vitamin C compensation [[Furuhashi2020]], blood pressure on low salt [[Watanabe2002]], and fructose-to-fat storage [[Kratzer2014]]. URAT1 affinity rose in parallel [[Tan2016]]. Status: all hypotheses; none is proven.
- **The exact saturation value:** 6.8 mg/dL [[Martillo2014]] vs 6.6 mg/dL [[Chen2016]]. Status: both cite older in vitro work; the companion uses 6.8 mg/dL and the target of 6 mg/dL sits below either.
- **How common is silent deposition?** It depends on the method and definition: 24% on DECT [[Dalbeth2015]], 25% double contour at the first MTP [[Pineda2011]], 26.0–68.8% across ultrasound definitions [[PeralGarrido2025]]. Status: no agreed imaging definition of stage B.

## Provenance
- **Validation route.** PubMed E-utilities returned HTTP 429 (rate limit) almost at once, because 8 parallel passes share one IP. Every record was therefore validated against Europe PMC's MEDLINE-sourced records instead (`/search?query=EXT_ID:<PMID>` with `resultType=core`), checking authors, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, PMID, PMCID and DOI. The 64 DOIs of the 66 newly entered works were then cross-checked against `api.crossref.org/works/<doi>` (title, volume, pages, year). All resolved to the matching work. Two works have no registered DOI (LiYu2001, Urano2002) and were validated on the Europe PMC record only. Martillo2014 and Steiger2014 show 2013 in Crossref (online-first) and 2014 as the issue year; the issue year is used. PeralGarrido2025 is 2024 online and 2025 in issue.
- **FMF reuse.** Broz2016, Swanson2019, Lu2014, Shi2015, Kayagaki2015, Liu2016, Evavold2018 and Dinarello2011 keep their key and text verbatim from `fmf/src/sources.json`, with a validation note added. Each was re-checked against Europe PMC (PMID, DOI, journal, pages) and its abstract was read.
- **Shared canonical keys** cited here and checked against Europe PMC and Crossref: Martinon2006, Dalbeth2021, Dalbeth2019NRDP, Neogi2015, FitzGerald2020, Richette2017. Their identifiers match the dossier's shared-key list.
- **Full texts read** (via PMC efetch or PMC HTML): HalperinKuhns2021, Martillo2014, Chhana2015, Ohashi2023, Tin2019, Pascual2007, So2007, Busso2010, Neogi2015 (criteria table), Torres2007, Chen2016, and So2010 (from the JCI site). All other works were read as abstracts only.
- **Not accessed:**
  - Loeb1972 has no abstract, and the full text (Wiley) was not reachable. The 6.8 → 6.0 mg/dL (37 → 35 °C) figures are quoted as Martillo2014 reports them (its ref. 28 is Loeb 1972), and the 6.8 mg/dL figure as Chhana2015 reports it.
  - PerezRuiz2002 full text (Wiley) was not reachable. Only the abstract's linear, inverse relation is used; no mm/month slope is quoted.
  - Maiuolo2016 was read as abstract only. The nucleoside (inosine/guanosine) step of purine catabolism is therefore marked (unverified) in Findings, although it is standard biochemistry.
  - Schauer2014, Dalbeth2010, Dalbeth2008, Dalbeth2009, Scanu2012, Joosten2010 and Steiger2014 were read as abstracts only.
  - A later attempt to read PMC HTML pages hit a reCAPTCHA wall, so no further PMC HTML was used.
- **Corrections to dossier hand-offs:**
  - Pascual2007: the dossier's "knee ~18 months vs first MTP ~33 months" is not in the paper. The paper reports 3–33 months for 11 knees and 7 first MTPs together, correlated with gout duration (rs = 0.71). In gout of < 10 years' duration, 9/10 joints were crystal-free at 12 months and all by 18 months.
  - Dalbeth2018ARD: the 1.1% (< 6 mg/dL) and 49% (≥ 10 mg/dL) figures are confirmed. The full HR ladder is added.
  - Loeb1972: 6.8 mg/dL at 37 °C is confirmed via secondary sources. The figure at 35 °C is 6.0 mg/dL. A third source, Chen2016, gives 6.6 mg/dL (392 µmol/L).
  - Heritability: the dossier's "30–60%" is the range Tin2019 cites. HalperinKuhns2021 says 40–70%. Tin2019's own pedigree estimate is h² = 37%.
  - Joosten2010: the dossier says "TLR2/4". The paper shows TLR2 engagement by C18:0 fatty acid, with ASC/caspase-1-dependent but NALP3-independent IL-1β release in its model. This is recorded as a controversy.
  - Signal 2 "ROS": MunozPlanillo2013 found ROS were not necessary for NLRP3 activation, while K⁺ efflux was. The animation should draw K⁺ efflux as essential.
  - Hornung2008 (cathepsin B, lysosomal rupture) used silica and alum, not MSU. It is cited as the general crystal mechanism and labelled that way.
  - The alcohol case-crossover study for flare triggers is Neogi2014 (Am J Med 2014), not a 2012 paper. Its key differs from Neogi2015 (classification criteria).
  - The TGF-β1 resolution papers are Yagnik2000 (non-inflammatory phagocytosis) and Yagnik2004 (TGF-β1 release).
- **Two Perez-Ruiz 2002 papers** appear in the same journal and year. PerezRuiz2002 keeps the dossier's key for the tophus-velocity study; the renal under-excretion study is PerezRuiz2002Renal.
