# A · Epidemiology, risk factors & history

Pass date: 2026-10-20. Modules served: what, living (risk factors), history, home.

Unit note: urate conversions in brackets use 1 mg/dL = 59.48 µmol/L, rounded. Where a source gives its own µmol/L value (for example 6 mg/dL [360 µmol/L]), the source's value is used.

## Findings

### what · How common gout is (global, modelled)
- In 2020, 55.8 million people worldwide (95% UI 44.4–69.8 million) had gout, an age-standardised prevalence of 659.3 per 100,000 (525.4–822.3), which is 22.5% higher than in 1990 (all ages, GBD 2021 modelled estimate) [[GBD2024Gout]].
- The number of prevalent cases rose 150.6% from 1990 to 2020; most of that rise is population growth and ageing, not a higher age-standardised rate [[GBD2024Gout]].
- Males: 41.7 million cases and 1030.8 per 100,000; females: 316.4 per 100,000; the male:female prevalence ratio was 3.26 (3.11–3.39). Prevalence rises with age in both sexes [[GBD2024Gout]].
- Projection: 95.8 million people (81.1–116 million) with gout in 2050, a rise of more than 70%. Age-standardised prevalence barely moves (667 per 100,000 in 2050), so the growth is driven by population growth and ageing [[GBD2024Gout]].
- Regions in 2020 (age-standardised, per 100,000): high-income North America 1719.8, Australasia 1424.4, southern Latin America 926.0 (highest); Tropical Latin America 254.7, Caribbean 245.7, central Latin America 188.4 (lowest). The largest rise from 1990 to 2020 was in high-income North America (+76.6%) [[GBD2024Gout]].
- Disability: 1.73 million years lived with disability (YLDs) in 2020, an age-standardised 20.5 YLDs per 100,000. High BMI accounted for 34.3% (27.7–40.6) of gout YLDs and kidney dysfunction for 11.8% (9.3–14.2) [[GBD2024Gout]].
- The previous GBD round (GBD 2017) reported about 41.2 million prevalent cases, 7.4 million incident cases per year, an age-standardised prevalence of 510.6 and incidence of 91.8 per 100,000 in 2017; high BMI and impaired kidney function accounted for 32.4% and 15.3% of YLDs. New Zealand (1394.0), Australia (1171.4) and the USA (996.0 per 100,000) had the highest age-standardised prevalence [[Safiri2020]].
- GBD's own modelling inputs for a person with acute gout: an average of 5.66 flares a year (95% UI 5.14–6.18), each lasting 6.1 days (5.4–6.8), so about 9.4% of the year is spent in a flare (modelling assumption, not a cohort observation) [[GBD2024Gout]].
- Gout distribution is uneven worldwide; prevalence is highest in Pacific countries, and higher in developed than in developing countries (narrative review) [[Kuo2015NRR]].

### what · How common gout is (surveys and records, by place)
- USA, NHANES 2015–2016 (5,467 adults; self-reported doctor-diagnosed gout): 3.9% of US adults (9.2 million), 5.2% of men (5.9 million) and 2.7% of women (3.3 million). The dossier hand-off figure (3.9%, about 9.2 million) is confirmed [[ChenXu2019]].
- Same survey: mean serum urate 6.0 mg/dL [357 µmol/L] in men and 4.8 mg/dL [285 µmol/L] in women; hyperuricaemia (>7.0 mg/dL [416 µmol/L] in men, >5.7 mg/dL [339 µmol/L] in women) in 20.2% of men and 20.0% of women. Gout and hyperuricaemia prevalence did not change from 2007 to 2016 (P for trend >0.05), and only 33% of people with gout were taking urate-lowering therapy (2007–2014) [[ChenXu2019]].
- UK, Clinical Practice Research Datalink: gout prevalence 2.49% (95% CI 2.48–2.51%) and incidence 1.77 per 1,000 person-years in 2012. Prevalence rose 63.9% and incidence 29.6% from 1997 to 2012; the North East and Wales had the highest rates. The dossier hand-off figure (2.49%, 2012) is confirmed [[Kuo2015ARD]].
- UK, same data: only 18.6% of people with newly diagnosed gout received urate-lowering therapy within 6 months and 27.3% within 12 months [[Kuo2015ARD]].
- USA, Olmsted County (Rochester Epidemiology Project, record review): age- and sex-adjusted incidence doubled from 66.6 per 100,000 (1989–1992) to 136.7 per 100,000 (2009–2010), incidence rate ratio 2.62 (95% CI 1.80–3.83) [[Elfishawi2018]].
- Same cohort: at the first flare in 2009–2010 versus 1989–1992, more people already had hypertension (69% vs 54%), diabetes (25% vs 6%), kidney disease (28% vs 11%), hyperlipidaemia (61% vs 21%) and BMI ≥35 kg/m² (29% vs 10%) [[Elfishawi2018]].
- Aotearoa New Zealand, 2019 national data: gout prevalence was 8.5% in Māori, 14.8% in Pasifika peoples and 4.7% in other New Zealanders (mainly NZ European); more than one-third of older Māori men and almost half of older Pasifika men were estimated to have gout [[TeKaru2021]].
- In the Genetics of Gout in Aotearoa study, mean age at gout onset was 39 years in Māori, 34 in Pasifika and 46 in NZ European participants [[TeKaru2021]].

### what · Israel
- No population-based Israeli gout prevalence study was found in PubMed or Europe PMC (searched 2026-10-20). The national GBD estimate for Israel sits in the GBD appendix, which was not accessed (unverified).
- Clalit Health Services case-control study: among 19,214 age- and sex-matched controls (matched to people with ankylosing spondylitis, so not a population sample), 0.56% had a gout diagnosis [[Gonen2020]].
- Maccabi Healthcare Services (about 2 million members): 7,644 adults were diagnosed with gout in 2002–2008. Among men, the rate the authors call incidence rose with age from 0.5 per 1,000 under age 45 to more than 36 per 1,000 at age 85 or older. Only 17% were adherent to allopurinol [[ZandmanGoddard2013]].
- Clalit nationwide cohort (2007–2022): of 301,385 adults with at least two serum urate values above 6.8 mg/dL [≈405 µmol/L] and no prior gout, 15,055 (5%) were later diagnosed with gout. In the highest urate quartile (mean second urate 8.62 mg/dL [≈513 µmol/L]) 13% developed gout over 10 years, a steady 1.3% a year [[Brikman2025]].
- Single Israeli centre: among 771 people with CKD stages 3–5 and asymptomatic hyperuricaemia (≥7.0 mg/dL [416 µmol/L]), 18.2% developed gout over a median 47 months (41.9 per 1,000 person-years); higher urate (HR 1.29 per mg/dL) and diuretic use (HR 1.86) predicted gout [[Beberashvili2026]].
- Israeli screening-centre cohort (12,036 adults, 69.8% male): hyperuricaemia was present in 19.1–20.0% of people with metabolic syndrome, depending on the definition used [[Cohen2012]].

### what · Who gets it: sex, age, menopause
- Women develop gout later. In US managed-care claims, women with gout were older than men (mean 70 vs 58 years) and used diuretics more often (77% vs 40%) [[Harrold2006]]. In a US rheumatology cohort the figures were 71 vs 61 years [[Harrold2017]].
- A systematic review found that, compared with men, women with gout have a later onset, more hypertension and kidney insufficiency, more diuretic use, less alcohol use, less podagra with more involvement of other joints, and fewer recurrent flares [[DirkenHeukensfeldt2010]].
- Nurses' Health Study (92,535 women, 16 years): incidence rose from 0.6 per 1,000 person-years under 45 to 2.5 at 75 or older. Postmenopausal women had a higher risk than premenopausal women (RR 1.26; 95% CI 1.03–1.55); menopause before 45 carried RR 1.62 versus menopause at 50–54; postmenopausal hormone use carried RR 0.82 [[Hak2010]].
- Framingham women: gout incidence per 1,000 person-years was 0.8, 2.5, 4.2, 13.1 and 27.3 for serum urate <5.0, 5.0–5.9, 6.0–6.9, 7.0–7.9 and ≥8.0 mg/dL; the gradient was less steep than in men [[Bhole2010]].
- Mean age at gout onset in Olmsted County (1989–2010) was 59.7 years (SD 17.3), and 73% were men [[Elfishawi2020]].
- Hippocratic aphorism VI-29 already held that a woman does not get gout unless her menses have stopped [[Nuki2006]].

### what · Hyperuricaemia is not gout: how many progress
- Pooled data from four US cohorts (18,889 people, mean follow-up 11.2 years): 15-year cumulative incidence of gout was 1.1% at baseline urate <6 mg/dL [360 µmol/L] and 49% (31–67%) at ≥10 mg/dL [≈595 µmol/L]. Compared with <6 mg/dL, adjusted HRs were 2.7 (6.0–6.9), 6.6 (7.0–7.9), 15 (8.0–8.9), 30 (9.0–9.9) and 64 (≥10 mg/dL). The dossier hand-off figures (1.1% and 49%) are confirmed [[Dalbeth2018ARD]].
- So only about half of people with urate ≥10 mg/dL develop clinically evident gout over 15 years [[Dalbeth2018ARD]].
- Normative Aging Study (2,046 men, 14.9 years): annual gout incidence was 4.9% at urate ≥9 mg/dL [≈535 µmol/L], 0.5% at 7.0–8.9 mg/dL and 0.1% below 7.0 mg/dL; cumulative incidence reached 22% after 5 years at ≥9 mg/dL [[Campion1987]].
- The ACR 2020 guideline conditionally recommends against starting urate-lowering therapy for asymptomatic hyperuricaemia (≥6.8 mg/dL with no prior flare or tophus). In cardiovascular trials incident gout over 3 years was <1% with urate-lowering therapy versus 5% with placebo, about 24 people treated for 3 years to prevent one first flare [[FitzGerald2020]].

### what · Clinical picture and staging
- Proposed staging (Dalbeth & Stamp 2014): Stage A, hyperuricaemia without urate crystal deposition or symptoms; Stage B, crystal deposition shown by microscopy or advanced imaging but no symptoms; Stage C, crystal deposition with prior or current flares; Stage D, advanced gout needing specialist interventions (tophi, chronic arthritis, erosions) [[Dalbeth2014]]. Correction to the dossier: the module hook says "Dalbeth/Gaffo staging"; the 2014 proposal is by Dalbeth and Stamp.
- Gout is a chronic disease of monosodium urate crystal deposition, even though it shows itself as intermittent flares [[Dalbeth2021,Dalbeth2019NRDP]].
- Gout typically presents as an acute, self-limiting monoarthritis of a lower-limb joint [[Dalbeth2019NRDP]].
- First metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra) at presentation: in Olmsted County, classic podagra at the first presentation fell from 74% (1989–1992) to 59% (2009–2010) [[Elfishawi2020]]. In the SUGAR study of crystal-proven gout in rheumatology clinics in 16 countries, first-MTP involvement was the most common joint pattern (39.4% of gout cases) [[Taylor2015]]. Correction to the dossier: its "about half of first flares" is not a sourced figure; the sourced figures are 59–74% at first presentation (population) and 39.4% as the dominant pattern (clinic).
- In SUGAR (983 patients, 509 with crystal-proven gout), the features that best separated gout from other arthritis included difficulty walking (OR 7.34), tophus (OR 7.29), ultrasound double contour sign (OR 7.23), resolution within 2 weeks (OR 3.58), urate >6 mg/dL [0.36 mmol/L] (OR 3.35) and first-MTP involvement ever (OR 2.30) [[Taylor2015]].
- The 2015 ACR/EULAR criteria score first-MTP involvement 2 points and ankle or midfoot 1 point; a typical flare has at least 2 of these: maximal pain in <24 hours, resolution in ≤14 days, complete resolution between flares; ≥8 of 23 points classifies gout; sensitivity 92% and specificity 89% [[Neogi2015]].
- Why the foot: lower temperature, lower pH, minor trauma and osteoarthritis of the first MTP all favour urate crystal formation there (narrative review, Hypothesis) [[Roddy2011]].
- Other joints: tarsal and metatarsal joints, ankles, knees, wrists, MCPs and finger joints; hip and shoulder rarely [[Roddy2011,Neogi2015]].
- Flare recurrence after a first flare: in Olmsted County the cumulative incidence of a further flare was 62% (1989–1992) and 60% (2009–2010) by 5 years; hyperuricaemia (HR 1.59) and kidney disease (HR 1.34) predicted flares [[Elfishawi2020]]. In UK primary-care records (23,857 people with incident gout, mean follow-up 3.8 years), 36.9% had at least one recorded flare after diagnosis; kidney failure carried HR 1.33 and allopurinol at diagnosis HR 0.80 [[Rothenbacher2011]]. The gap between the two figures reflects how flares were counted (chart review versus GP coding).
- Tophi: at the Mayo Clinic, tophaceous gout at new diagnosis fell from 14% to 3% between 1949 and 1972 [[ODuffy1975]]. No validated modern figure was found for the share of untreated people who go on to develop tophi (open gap; see Provenance).

### living · Alcohol (incident gout and flares)
- Health Professionals Follow-up Study (47,150 men, 12 years, 730 cases), versus no alcohol: RR 1.32 for 10.0–14.9 g/day, 1.49 for 15.0–29.9, 1.96 for 30.0–49.9 and 2.53 for ≥50 g/day. Per daily serving: beer RR 1.49 (1.32–1.70) per 12-oz serving, spirits 1.15 (1.04–1.28) per drink, wine 1.04 (0.88–1.22, not significant) per 4-oz serving. Dossier hand-off values confirmed [[Choi2004Lancet]].
- People who already have gout (724 participants, internet case-crossover): >1–2 drinks in 24 hours raised flare risk 1.36-fold (1.00–1.88) and >2–4 drinks 1.51-fold (1.09–2.09); wine, beer and liquor were each associated with flares [[Neogi2014]].
- ACR 2020: limiting alcohol is conditionally recommended for all people with gout; people who limited or stopped alcohol had serum urate 1.6 mg/dL [≈95 µmol/L] lower, and one unit of beer raised urate by 0.16 mg/dL [≈10 µmol/L] [[FitzGerald2020]].

### living · Foods and drinks
- Meat (highest vs lowest fifth of intake): RR 1.41 (1.07–1.86); seafood RR 1.51 (1.17–1.95); dairy RR 0.56 (0.42–0.74). Purine-rich vegetables and total protein were not associated with gout [[Choi2004NEJM]].
- In people with gout, purine intake over 2 days raised flare odds stepwise across quintiles: OR 1.17, 1.38, 2.21 and 4.76 versus the lowest quintile; for animal-source purines the ORs were 1.42, 1.34, 1.77 and 2.41 [[Zhang2012Purine]].
- Sugar-sweetened soft drinks in men (46,393 men, 755 cases): RR 1.29 for 5–6 servings a week, 1.45 for one a day, 1.85 (1.08–3.16) for ≥2 a day; top fifth of fructose intake RR 2.02; diet soft drinks not associated. Dossier hand-off value 1.85 confirmed [[Choi2008BMJ]].
- In women (Nurses' Health Study, 78,906 women, 22 years): sugar-sweetened soda RR 1.74 for 1 a day and 2.39 for ≥2 a day; orange juice RR 1.41 and 2.42; absolute excess 36 and 68 cases per 100,000 person-years for soda [[Choi2010JAMA]].
- ACR 2020: limiting high-fructose corn syrup is conditionally recommended; 1 g of fructose per kg body weight raises serum urate by 1–2 mg/dL [≈60–120 µmol/L] within 2 hours [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Diet patterns (44,444 men, 26 years, 1,731 cases): highest vs lowest fifth of DASH score RR 0.68 (0.57–0.80); Western diet RR 1.42 (1.16–1.74). Dossier "to find" value is now filled [[Rai2017BMJ]].
- Coffee (45,869 men): RR 0.97, 0.92, 0.60 and 0.41 for <1, 1–3, 4–5 and ≥6 cups a day versus none; decaffeinated coffee 1–3 cups RR 0.67; tea and total caffeine not associated (Early evidence, observational) [[Choi2007Coffee]].
- Cherries (633 people with gout, case-crossover): cherry intake over 2 days, OR 0.65 (0.50–0.85) for a flare; cherry extract OR 0.55 (0.30–0.98); cherries plus allopurinol OR 0.25 (0.15–0.42). Early evidence: observational, no randomised trial; ACR 2020 judged the evidence too weak for a recommendation [[Zhang2012Cherry,FitzGerald2020]].
- Vitamin C: ACR 2020 conditionally recommends against vitamin C supplements for people with gout (two small RCTs, clinically insignificant urate change) [[FitzGerald2020]]. A post hoc analysis of a randomised trial in 14,641 male physicians found 500 mg/day lowered new gout diagnoses by 12% (HR 0.88; 8.0 vs 9.1 per 1,000 person-years) (Early evidence: prevention, not treatment) [[Juraschek2022]].

### living · Body weight, blood pressure, kidneys and medicines
- BMI versus 21–22.9 kg/m² (men): RR 1.95 at 25–29.9, 2.33 at 30–34.9, 2.97 at ≥35. Weight gain ≥30 lb (≈14 kg) since age 21: RR 1.99; weight loss ≥10 lb (≈4.5 kg) since study baseline: RR 0.61. Hypertension RR 2.31; diuretic use RR 1.77 [[Choi2005]].
- Women (Framingham, 52 years): obesity RR 2.74, alcohol ≥7 oz pure alcohol a week RR 3.10, hypertension RR 1.82, diuretic use RR 2.39, per 5 years of age RR 1.24 [[Bhole2010]].
- Population-attributable risk (HPFS, 44,654 men, 26 years): BMI ≥25, any alcohol, low DASH score and diuretic use together accounted for 77% (95% CI 56–88%) of incident gout. In normal-weight and overweight men, diet, alcohol and diuretics together accounted for 69% and 59%, but in men with obesity those three factors accounted for only 5% (not significant); the authors conclude weight must be addressed first [[McCormick2020]].
- Weight loss in people with gout (systematic review, 10 studies, mostly observational): urate change from −168 to +30 µmol/L [≈−2.8 to +0.5 mg/dL]; 6 of 8 studies showed fewer flares; flares and urate can rise briefly after bariatric surgery. Evidence quality low to moderate [[Nielsen2017]].
- Chronic kidney disease (Framingham, 54 years): gout incidence 6.82 vs 2.43 per 1,000 person-years with vs without CKD; adjusted HR 1.88 in men and 2.31 in women [[Wang2015]].
- Antihypertensives (UK nested case-control, 24,768 cases): RR of incident gout 2.36 for diuretics, 1.48 for beta-blockers, 1.24 for ACE inhibitors, 1.29 for non-losartan ARBs; 0.87 for calcium channel blockers and 0.81 for losartan [[Choi2012BMJ]].
- Gene-by-diuretic interaction (ARIC, 3,524 people with hypertension): thiazide or loop diuretics were associated with OR 2.13 for gout in people with a high genetic urate score and OR 0.40 (not significant) in those with a low score; interaction p=0.006 [[McAdamsDeMarco2013]].
- Low-dose aspirin (≤325 mg/day on two consecutive days) was associated with OR 1.81 (1.30–2.51) for a flare, 1.91 for ≤100 mg; taking allopurinol cancelled the effect [[Zhang2014Aspirin]]. ACR 2020 still conditionally recommends against stopping low-dose aspirin taken for a valid reason [[FitzGerald2020]].
- ACR 2020 conditional recommendations: switch hydrochlorothiazide to another antihypertensive where feasible; prefer losartan; do not add or switch to fenofibrate for its urate effect; weight loss programme if overweight [[FitzGerald2020]].

### living · Genes versus diet
- In 16,760 US adults of European ancestry, each diet score explained ≤0.3% of the variance in serum urate, whereas common genome-wide variation explained 23.9% (Established for urate variance) [[Major2018]].
- A GWAS of 2.6 million people (120,295 with gout) found 377 loci and 410 independent signals (149 new), including genes for NLRP3 inflammasome regulation, and Mendelian randomisation evidence that clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential is causal in gout (Early evidence for the inflammatory genes) [[Major2024]].
- ACR 2020: the panel asked clinicians to be mindful when asking about diet, given the strong genetic contribution to hyperuricaemia and gout [[FitzGerald2020]].

### history · Names and antiquity
- Podagra, gout of the first MTP joint, was described by the Egyptians in 2640 BC and by Hippocrates in the 5th century BC, who called it "the unwalkable disease" [[Nuki2006]].
- Five Hippocratic aphorisms on gout: eunuchs do not get gout (VI-28); a woman not unless her menses have stopped (VI-29); a youth not before sexual intercourse (VI-30); inflammation subsides within 40 days (VI-40); gout is active in spring and autumn [[Nuki2006]].
- Hippocrates called podagra an "arthritis of the rich"; Galen first described tophi and noted a hereditary trait [[Nuki2006]].
- The word "gout" was first used by Randolphus of Bocking (1197–1258), from Latin gutta, a drop, after the medieval idea of a humour dropping into a joint [[Nuki2006]].
- Chiragra (gout of the hand) and gonagra (gout of the knee) are older regional names (unverified).
- The phrase "disease of kings" comes from gout's link with rich food and drink that only the affluent could afford; in some eras it was even seen as socially desirable [[Nuki2006]].

### history · Physicians, crystals and chemistry
- Thomas Sydenham, himself disabled by gout, described the night-time great-toe flare in his Tractatus de Podagra et Hydrope (1683) [[Nuki2006]].
- Antonie van Leeuwenhoek described needle-shaped crystals from a tophus in 1679, without knowing what they were made of [[McCarty1970,Nuki2006]].
- Scheele identified uric acid in a kidney stone in 1776; Wollaston showed urate in a tophus from his own ear in 1797 [[Nuki2006]].
- Alfred Baring Garrod measured raised uric acid in the blood of people with gout (1848) and devised the "thread test", called the first clinical chemistry test; in 1859 he proposed that deposited sodium urate is the cause, not the effect, of gouty inflammation [[Garrod1848,Nuki2006]].
- Freudweiler (1899) caused gout-like arthritis by injecting urate microcrystals; this was overlooked until McCarty and Hollander (1961) used compensated polarised light microscopy to identify monosodium urate crystals in gouty synovial fluid [[McCarty1961,Nuki2006]].
- In 1967 Seegmiller, Rosenbloom and Kelley described the first purine enzyme defect (HPRT deficiency) causing a rare inherited gout [[Seegmiller1967,Nuki2006]].
- In 2006 Martinon and Tschopp showed that urate crystals activate the NALP3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, releasing active IL-1β and IL-18 [[Martinon2006]].

### history · Treatments
- Colchicum (autumn crocus) was a purgative in ancient Greece; its first use as a specific gout treatment is credited to Alexander of Tralles (6th century AD). Sydenham's rejection of purgatives kept it out of use for about 150 years, until von Störck revived it in Vienna in 1763 [[Nuki2006]].
- Pelletier and Caventou isolated the active substance from Colchicum autumnale in 1819; Geiger named it colchicine in 1833; Houdé produced pure crystalline colchicine granules in 1884 [[Karamanou2018]]. Correction to the dossier: the source gives 1819, not 1820, for the isolation.
- Uricosurics: high-dose salicylate (See, 1877) was the first, later replaced by probenecid (Benemid; clinical report 1951), sulfinpyrazone and benzbromarone [[Talbott1951,Nuki2006]].
- Uricase was first given to humans in 1957 (London and Hudson) [[London1957,Nuki2006]].
- Allopurinol, the first xanthine oxidase inhibitor, came from Hitchings and Elion's purine research; they shared the 1988 Nobel Prize [[Elion1989,Nuki2006]]. An early clinical report of allopurinol in gout appeared in 1965 [[Klinenberg1965]]; Zyloprim was approved by the FDA on 19 August 1966 [[DrugsFDA-Zyloprim]]. The first 1963 report by Rundles and colleagues (Trans Assoc Am Physicians 1963;76:126–40) is not indexed in PubMed (unverified).
- Febuxostat: in the FACT trial (762 people), 53% (80 mg) and 62% (120 mg) reached serum urate <6.0 mg/dL [360 µmol/L] versus 21% on allopurinol 300 mg [[Becker2005]]; FDA approval as Uloric on 13 February 2009 [[DrugsFDA-Uloric]].
- Colchicine was first approved as a single-ingredient FDA product (Colcrys) on 29 July 2009 [[DrugsFDA-Colcrys]].
- Pegloticase: FDA approval as Krystexxa on 14 September 2010 [[DrugsFDA-Krystexxa]]; in two pooled RCTs, 42% (every 2 weeks) and 35% (monthly) versus 0% on placebo met the urate response end point [[Sundy2011]].

### history · Criteria and guidelines
- 2015 ACR/EULAR classification criteria: entry criterion of at least one episode of peripheral joint or bursa swelling, pain or tenderness; urate crystals in a symptomatic joint or tophus are sufficient on their own [[Neogi2015]].
- 2016 EULAR recommendations: keep serum urate <6 mg/dL [360 µmol/L], or <5 mg/dL [300 µmol/L] in severe gout; allopurinol first line [[Richette2017]].
- 2020 ACR guideline: 42 recommendations (16 strong), including treat-to-target with serum urate <6 mg/dL and allopurinol first line [[FitzGerald2020]].

### In plain words
- Gout is common. About 56 million people in the world have it, and the number keeps rising as people live longer.
- Men get gout about three times as often as women. Women usually get it later, after menopause.
- Having high uric acid in the blood is not the same as having gout. Many people with high uric acid never get a flare.
- Gout often starts in the big toe, but it can hit the foot, ankle, knee, wrist or fingers.
- Beer and spirits raise the risk more than wine. Sugary drinks, a lot of meat and seafood, and extra body weight raise it too.
- Low-fat dairy, coffee and a DASH-style diet are linked with less gout, but food changes alone rarely bring uric acid down enough.
- Your genes set much of your uric acid level. Gout is not a punishment for how you eat.
- Some medicines, like water pills, can raise uric acid. Do not stop a heart aspirin on your own; talk to your doctor.
- People have known about gout for more than 2,500 years. Today it is one of the best-understood types of arthritis.

## Open questions and controversies
- Modelled versus survey prevalence: GBD 2021 puts age-standardised prevalence in high-income North America at 1719.8 per 100,000 (about 1.7%, all ages, adjusted to physician-diagnosed ACR-based gout) [[GBD2024Gout]], whereas NHANES 2015–2016 reports 3.9% of US adults by self-reported doctor diagnosis [[ChenXu2019]]. The measures differ (age-standardised all-ages model vs crude adult survey) and self-report overcalls gout: "self-report of gout or urate-lowering therapy" had 82% sensitivity but only 72% specificity against crystal proof [[Dalbeth2016]]. Status: both are correct for their measure; never place them on the same axis without a label.
- Is gout still rising? GBD shows a 22.5% rise in age-standardised prevalence from 1990 to 2020 [[GBD2024Gout]]; the UK showed +63.9% prevalence from 1997 to 2012 [[Kuo2015ARD]]; Olmsted County incidence doubled [[Elfishawi2018]]; but US NHANES prevalence was flat from 2007 to 2016 [[ChenXu2019]]. GBD 2017 (510.6 per 100,000 in 2017) [[Safiri2020]] and GBD 2021 (659.3 in 2020) [[GBD2024Gout]] use different inputs, so the gap between rounds is not a trend. Status: rising over decades in most high-income settings; recent US plateau.
- Diet versus genes: diet explains ≤0.3% of serum urate variance versus 23.9% for common genetic variants [[Major2018]], yet weight, alcohol, diet and diuretics together account for 77% of incident gout in US men [[McCormick2020]]. These answer different questions (urate variance in people without gout, mostly excluding diuretic users, vs gout incidence including BMI and diuretics). Status: both hold; body weight and medicines matter more than single foods.
- Wine: no rise in incident gout per daily glass in men (RR 1.04, not significant) [[Choi2004Lancet]], but in people who already have gout, wine, beer and liquor each raised short-term flare risk [[Neogi2014]]. Status: advise limiting all alcohol once gout is established, as ACR 2020 does [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Cherries and vitamin C: cherry intake OR 0.65 for flares is case-crossover only (Early evidence) [[Zhang2012Cherry]]; vitamin C lowered new gout diagnoses by 12% in one post hoc trial analysis [[Juraschek2022]], but ACR 2020 recommends against vitamin C for people with gout and made no cherry recommendation [[FitzGerald2020]]. Status: unresolved; no trial in people with gout shows fewer flares.
- Treat asymptomatic hyperuricaemia? Only 5% of Israelis with repeated urate >6.8 mg/dL were diagnosed with gout during follow-up [[Brikman2025]], and about half at ≥10 mg/dL developed gout in 15 years [[Dalbeth2018ARD]]; ACR 2020 conditionally recommends against urate-lowering therapy (about 24 treated for 3 years to prevent one first flare) [[FitzGerald2020]]. Status: not recommended outside trials in ACR/EULAR guidance (pass E/F cover Japan and FREED).
- "Disease of kings" and stigma: the historical image of gout as a disease of excess [[Nuki2006]] persists. Rheumatologists shown gout vignettes with stereotyped lifestyle profiles judged the patients more responsible and less likely to adhere to or benefit from drug therapy than identical non-gout cases [[Vitriol2025]]. Calling gout "urate crystal arthritis" made the public see it as less under personal control and better suited to long-term medical treatment [[Coleshill2021]]. Status: active research area (Early evidence); the companion should avoid blame-based framing.
- Is podagra becoming less typical? Classic podagra at first presentation fell from 74% to 59% over 20 years in Olmsted County, alongside more comorbidity at onset [[Elfishawi2020,Elfishawi2018]]; women have less podagra and more involvement of other joints [[DirkenHeukensfeldt2010]]. Status: atypical starts are common enough that "no big toe" should not rule gout out.
