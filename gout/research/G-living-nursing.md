# G · Living with gout & nursing care

Pass date: 2026-10-20. Modules served: living, nursing.

Unit note: 1 mg/dL of urate = 59.48 µmol/L. Where a source reports only µmol/L, the mg/dL figure in brackets is our conversion and is marked "(converted)".

## Findings

### living · What the guidelines say about food, drink and weight
- ACR 2020 gives four conditional lifestyle recommendations "regardless of disease activity": limit alcohol (certainty of evidence: low), limit purine intake (low), limit high-fructose corn syrup (very low), and a weight-loss programme for people who are overweight or obese, with no specific programme endorsed (very low). It conditionally recommends against adding vitamin C supplements (low). Gout flare was the only critical outcome for the lifestyle questions. [[FitzGerald2020]]
- ACR 2020 evidence summary: serum urate (SU) was 1.6 mg/dL [95 µmol/L, converted] lower in people who limited or stopped alcohol than in those who did not; one unit of beer raised SU by 0.16 mg/dL [9.5 µmol/L, converted]; ingesting 1 g fructose per kg body weight raises SU by 1–2 mg/dL [59–119 µmol/L] within 2 hours. [[FitzGerald2020]]
- ACR 2020 on weight: in 11 obese patients a mean 5 kg weight loss lowered SU by 1.1 mg/dL [65 µmol/L, converted]; in 12 patients after bariatric surgery (mean 34.3 kg lost over 12 months) SU fell by 2.0 mg/dL [119 µmol/L, converted]; a BMI rise of more than 5% carried 60% higher odds of a recurrent flare and a fall of more than 5% carried 40% lower odds; the first month after bariatric surgery may bring a transient rise in flare risk. [[FitzGerald2020]]
- ACR 2020 reviewed cherries and dairy protein and found the evidence (mainly observational) low or very low, so it made no recommendation on either. The vitamin C recommendation rests on two small RCTs (n = 29 and n = 40) showing clinically insignificant SU changes. [[FitzGerald2020]]
- ACR 2020 says diet, lifestyle and other-medicine choices are areas for shared decision-making, and that the panel aimed to give guidance "without implying any patient-blaming" given gout's strong genetic determinants. [[FitzGerald2020]]
- EULAR 2016 makes education and lifestyle advice part of its overarching principles. People with gout should be told about the nature of the disease and its treatments. Lifestyle advice covers weight loss where appropriate; avoiding alcohol (especially beer and spirits), sugar-sweetened drinks, heavy meals and excessive meat and seafood; encouraging low-fat dairy; and regular exercise. EULAR also asks for flares to be treated early and for people to be taught to self-medicate at the first warning symptoms. [[Richette2017]] (Wording checked against the abstract and a secondary tabulation; full text not opened.)
- The 2012 ACR guideline grouped foods into "avoid", "limit" and "encourage" categories, saying it did so because prospective randomised evidence linking amounts eaten to SU or gout outcomes was generally lacking. It made patient education on diet, lifestyle, treatment objectives and comorbidities a core measure. [[Khanna2012P1]]

### living · How much diet actually moves urate (effect sizes)
- In a diet-wide meta-analysis of 16,760 US adults of European ancestry (5 cohorts), 7 foods raised SU and 8 lowered it. Each diet score explained ≤0.3% of the variance in SU, against 23.9% explained by common genome-wide variation (Established). [[Major2018]]
- Per extra weekly serving: beer +1.38 µmol/L, liquor +1.38, wine +1.07, potato +0.87, poultry +0.70, soft drinks +0.68, beef/pork/lamb +0.63; eggs −1.10, peanuts −0.88, cold cereal −0.72, skim milk −0.63, cheese −0.62, brown bread −0.60 µmol/L. One beer a day (7 servings a week) is therefore about +9.7 µmol/L [+0.16 mg/dL, converted], which matches the ACR 2020 figure. [[Major2018,FitzGerald2020]]
- DASH feeding trial (103 adults with prehypertension or stage 1 hypertension, 30-day crossover periods, weight held constant; baseline SU 5.0 mg/dL [297 µmol/L]): DASH lowered SU by 0.35 mg/dL [21 µmol/L] (95% CI −0.65 to −0.05). In the 8 participants with baseline SU ≥7 mg/dL [416 µmol/L] it fell by 1.29 mg/dL [77 µmol/L] (95% CI −2.50 to −0.08). Higher sodium intake lowered SU. [[Juraschek2016]]
- The only DASH trial in people with gout (43 participants not on ULT; baseline SU 8.1 mg/dL [482 µmol/L]; 4-week crossover with no washout) found dietitian-directed DASH groceries lowered SU by 0.55 mg/dL [33 µmol/L] in period 1. The direction reversed after crossover (period interaction P = 0.11), so the result is inconclusive (Early evidence). [[Juraschek2021]]
- In 44,444 men followed for 26 years, the highest versus lowest fifth of DASH score had RR 0.68 (95% CI 0.57–0.80) for incident gout, and a Western diet pattern had RR 1.42 (1.16–1.74). [[Rai2017BMJ]]
- Weight loss systematic review (10 longitudinal studies, one randomised; mean weight loss 3–34 kg): SU changes ranged from −168 to +30 µmol/L [−2.8 to +0.5 mg/dL, converted], 0–60% reached SU <360 µmol/L [6 mg/dL], and 6 of 8 studies (75%) showed benefit on flares. Certainty was low (SU), moderate (reaching target) and low (flares). Temporary rises in SU and flares tended to follow bariatric surgery. [[Nielsen2017]]
- In the DIRECT weight-loss trial (low-fat, Mediterranean or low-carbohydrate diets, no purine focus), SU fell on average by 48 µmol/L [0.81 mg/dL, converted] at 6 months and 18 µmol/L [0.30 mg/dL] at 24 months, with no difference between diets. Among people with hyperuricaemia the falls at 6 months were 113, 119 and 143 µmol/L [1.9, 2.0 and 2.4 mg/dL, converted]. [[Yokose2020]]
- Vitamin C RCT in 40 people with gout (SU >6 mg/dL [360 µmol/L]): 500 mg/day for 8 weeks lowered SU by 0.23 mg/dL [0.014 mmol/L], compared with 1.9 mg/dL [0.118 mmol/L] for starting or increasing allopurinol. [[Stamp2013]]
- Vitamin C meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (556 participants, mostly without gout; median 500 mg/day for a median 30 days): SU −0.35 mg/dL [−20.8 µmol/L] (95% CI −0.66 to −0.03), with high heterogeneity (I² 77%). [[Juraschek2011]]
- In treated gout (30 people on stable ULT), comprehensive dietary education raised knowledge scores (8/13 to 12/13) but did not change SU at 6 months (0.27 vs 0.30 mmol/L [4.5 vs 5.0 mg/dL]). [[Holland2015,FitzGerald2020]]
- Coffee (Early evidence for SU; observational): in NHANES III, 4–5 and ≥6 cups a day were associated with SU 0.26 and 0.43 mg/dL [15 and 26 µmol/L] lower than no coffee; tea and total caffeine were not associated with SU. In 45,869 men, ≥6 cups a day had RR 0.41 (95% CI 0.19–0.88) for incident gout. [[Choi2007NHANES,Choi2007Incident]]
- Milk: in a crossover trial, intact milk lowered SU acutely by about 10% while a soy control raised it by about 10%. A 3-month RCT of skim milk powder enriched with glycomacropeptide and G600 reported fewer flares than lactose control (P = 0.044) (Early evidence). [[Dalbeth2010Milk,Dalbeth2012]]
- Sugar-sweetened soft drinks in 46,393 men: compared with fewer than one a month, RR 1.45 for one a day and 1.85 (95% CI 1.08–3.16) for two or more a day. Diet soft drinks showed no association. [[Choi2008BMJ]]

### living · Triggers of recurrent flares (case-crossover studies in people with gout)
- Purines (633 participants): compared with the lowest fifth of 2-day purine intake, the ORs for a flare across the next four fifths were 1.17, 1.38, 2.21 and 4.76 (top fifth: median 3.48 g over 2 days). Animal sources had a top-fifth OR of 2.41 and plant sources 1.39. [[Zhang2012Purine]]
- Alcohol (724 participants): >1–2 drinks in the prior 24 hours gave OR 1.36 (1.00–1.88) and >2–4 drinks OR 1.51 (1.09–2.09). Wine, beer and liquor each raised the risk; >1–2 servings of wine gave OR 2.38 (1.57–3.62). [[Neogi2014]]
- Cherries (633 participants; Early evidence, observational): cherry intake over 2 days gave OR 0.65 (0.50–0.85), with the benefit plateauing at about 3 servings over 2 days. Cherry extract gave OR 0.55 (0.30–0.98), and cherries plus allopurinol OR 0.25 (0.15–0.42). [[Zhang2012Cherry]]
- Low-dose aspirin (724 participants): aspirin ≤325 mg/day on two consecutive days gave OR 1.81 (1.30–2.51), and ≤100 mg/day OR 1.91 (1.32–2.85). Taking allopurinol at the same time removed the effect. [[Zhang2014]]
- Hydration: no published trial in people with gout was found. The only published fluid instruction is the allopurinol label's urine output of at least 2 litres a day, which is there to prevent stones. [[FDA-Allopurinol]]

### living · Other medicines that raise or lower urate
- ACR 2020, all conditional and very-low certainty: switch hydrochlorothiazide to another antihypertensive when feasible; choose losartan preferentially when feasible; do **not** stop low-dose aspirin taken for an appropriate indication; do **not** add or switch cholesterol-lowering drugs to fenofibrate. Any switch should happen only when the urate and gout benefits outweigh the risks of changing. [[FitzGerald2020]]
- Antihypertensives and incident gout (UK case-control, 29,138 people with hypertension): diuretics RR 2.36, β-blockers 1.48, non-losartan ARBs 1.29, ACE inhibitors 1.24, calcium-channel blockers 0.87 and losartan 0.81. The losartan effect deepened with duration (0.71 at ≥2 years). [[Choi2012BMJ]]
- SGLT2 inhibitors: SU −37.73 µmol/L [−0.63 mg/dL, converted] across 62 RCTs (34,941 people with type 2 diabetes), with the effect abolished at eGFR <60 [[Zhao2018]]. Incident gout HR 0.64 (0.57–0.72) compared with GLP-1 agonists (4.9 vs 7.8 per 1000 person-years) [[Fralick2020]] (Early evidence for gout outcomes).
- Fenofibrate 200 mg (FIELD, 9795 people with type 2 diabetes): uric acid fell 20.2% (about 1 mg/dL [0.06 mmol/L]) and first gout events had HR 0.54 (0.41–0.70) over 5 years. ACR still advises against switching to it for gout. [[Waldman2018,FitzGerald2020]]
- Allopurinol label: thiazide diuretics, ampicillin/amoxicillin and bendamustine may increase the risk of severe skin rash, and kidney impairment adds to the thiazide risk. [[FDA-Allopurinol]]

### living · A flare plan
- ACR 2020 strongly recommends colchicine, an NSAID or a glucocorticoid as first-line flare treatment, and low-dose over high-dose colchicine. It conditionally recommends topical ice as an add-on. The Patient Panel asked for an at-home "medication-in-pocket" strategy for people who recognise early flare signs. [[FitzGerald2020]]
- Colchicine label for a flare: 1.2 mg at the first sign, then 0.6 mg one hour later, maximum 1.8 mg in one hour. Someone already on prophylaxis can take the same flare dose, wait 12 hours, then restart the prophylactic dose. [[FDA-Colcrys]]
- Keep ULT going during a flare. The allopurinol label tells patients to stay on allopurinol if a flare happens, and the pegloticase label says it need not be stopped for a flare. ACR 2020 allows ULT to be started during a flare (conditional). [[FDA-Allopurinol,FDA-Krystexxa,FitzGerald2020]]
- ACR 2020 strongly recommends anti-inflammatory prophylaxis when ULT starts, continued for 3–6 months rather than <3 months and extended if flares continue. [[FitzGerald2020]]

### living · Monitoring
- The ACR 2012 guideline recommended checking SU every 2–5 weeks during ULT titration and every 6 months once target is reached, partly as a check on adherence. [[Khanna2012P1]]
- ACR 2020 strongly recommends treat-to-target with SU <6 mg/dL [360 µmol/L] for everyone on ULT. It repeats the 2012 titration interval of every 2–5 weeks, says SU should be checked after each dose change, and says titration should take "weeks to months, not years". [[FitzGerald2020]]
- EULAR 2016 targets SU <6 mg/dL [360 µmol/L], or <5 mg/dL [300 µmol/L] in severe gout. [[Richette2017]]
- Allopurinol label baseline tests before starting: SU, full blood count, chemistry panel, liver tests and kidney function (creatinine, eGFR). It suggests titrating by 100 mg weekly to SU ≤6 mg/dL, maximum 800 mg/day. [[FDA-Allopurinol]]
- Febuxostat label: baseline liver panel. Start at 40 mg; if SU is not below 6 mg/dL after 2 weeks, go to 80 mg. Flare prophylaxis is recommended at the start. [[FDA-Uloric]]
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends continuing ULT indefinitely. In one withdrawal series, only 13% (27/211) of people whose SU stayed <7 mg/dL off ULT were flare-free over 5 years. [[FitzGerald2020]]

### living · Work, footwear and physical activity
- Among about 300,000 US employees, people with gout had 4.56 more health-related absence days a year than those without (annual prevalence 4.7 per 1000 employees). [[Kleinman2007]]
- HAQ-DI scores in gout correlated with days of sick leave (r² = 0.44). [[Taylor2008]]
- Footwear RCT (94 people): adding an athletic shoe to podiatry plus education did not reduce foot pain at 6 months (−6.7, 95% CI −16.4 to 2.9). Overall pain (−13.2) and foot disability (−4.7) improved at 2 months only, and fit, ease and weight scores improved throughout. [[Frecklington2019ART]]
- Interviews with 11 people with gout found four footwear themes: comfort first, knowing what to buy, knowing what to wear, and different environments (formal settings, workplace). Tophi and flares limited shoe choice. [[Frecklington2019JFAR]]
- Interviews with 25 people about physical activity found stigma, fear of judgement and uncertainty about whether exercise could trigger flares or damage joints, "particularly in the absence of gout-specific advice". [[Stewart2026]]

### living · Surgery and hospital stays
- In a population cohort of 429 people with gout, the flare rate rose about tenfold during hospitalisation (RR 10.2, 95% CI 6.8–14.5), and an in-hospital flare lengthened the stay by 1.8 days. [[Zleik2018]]
- The GOUT-36 rule counts four pre-admission items: no flare prophylaxis, no ULT, tophus, and SU >0.36 mmol/L [6 mg/dL] in the past year. It had 75% sensitivity and 67% specificity for inpatient flares. In a validation cohort with 34% inpatient flares, 80% of very-high-risk patients flared against 11% of low-risk patients. [[Jatuworapruk2022Rheum]]
- Among 36,047 New Zealand admissions of people with comorbid gout, regular pre-admission ULT was linked to a 10% shorter stay, the strongest of the variables studied (observational). [[Jatuworapruk2022IJRD]]
- Postsurgical flares: 31 of 70 (44.3%) people with known gout flared after surgery, a mean 3.7 days after the operation; 61.3% of flares were monoarticular, 83.9% in the lower limb, and the first MTP was involved in only 13%. Preoperative SU ≥9 mg/dL [535 µmol/L] had OR 3.77 and taking allopurinol OR 0.15. [[Jeong2019]]
- In an older surgical series, 52 of 302 people with gout flared 1–17 days (mean 4.2) after admission. Almost all were febrile, and many went through tests for an unexplained fever before gout was recognised. [[Craig1995]]

### living · Pregnancy and breastfeeding
- Gout is uncommon in younger women. In 92,535 women, incidence was 0.6 per 1000 person-years under age 45 and 2.5 at ≥75 years. Postmenopausal women had RR 1.26 compared with premenopausal women. [[Hak2010]]
- BSR 2023: colchicine "may be considered during pregnancy" (GRADE 1B) and "may be used in breastfeeding" (GRADE 2C). This is based on a meta-analysis of 550 exposed pregnancies (mostly FMF, 1–2 mg/day) against 1263 unexposed, with no rise in malformations or miscarriage. [[Schreiber2023,Indraratna2018]]
- BSR 2023 on non-selective NSAIDs in pregnancy: use them only intermittently in the first trimester, intermittently rather than regularly throughout, wean from 26 weeks and stop by 30 weeks. They are compatible with breastfeeding, ibuprofen especially. The naproxen label says to limit use between about 20 and 30 weeks and avoid it from about 30 weeks. [[Schreiber2023,FDA-Naproxen]]
- Allopurinol label: animal data suggest possible fetal harm. Among about 50 published human pregnancies, 2 infants had major malformations. It advises against breastfeeding during treatment and for 1 week after the last dose. [[FDA-Allopurinol]]
- Febuxostat label: human pregnancy data are insufficient, and there are no data on human milk. [[FDA-Uloric]]

### nursing · Assessment
- The OMERACT 9 core domains for acute gout are pain, joint swelling, joint tenderness, patient global assessment and activity limitation. For chronic gout they are SU, flares, tophus burden, health-related quality of life, activity limitation, pain and patient global assessment. [[Schumacher2009]]
- Pain on a 0–4 Likert scale, a 0–10 NRS or a 0–100 mm VAS, and swelling and tenderness on a 4-point Likert scale, meet the OMERACT filter for acute gout. HAQ showed within-group but not between-group discrimination. [[Taylor2014]]
- A self-report flare definition needs at least 3 of 4 criteria: the person says it is a flare, pain at rest >3 on a 0–10 NRS, at least one swollen joint, and at least one warm joint. It was 85% sensitive and 95% specific (accuracy 92%) in 509 patients at 17 sites. [[Gaffo2018]]
- The Gout Impact Scale has 5 scales scored 0–100 (higher means worse), with 2-week test–retest reliability of 0.77–0.89 in 308 patients [[Hirsch2008]]. Its minimally important difference is 5–8 points [[Khanna2011]].
- HAQ-DI is valid for physical function in gout. A 3-option rescoring fits the Rasch model better, and there are floor effects. [[Taylor2008]]
- Tophus size: Vernier caliper measurement of the longest diameter differed by a mean of 0.45 mm between observers and correlated with CT volume (r = 0.91) [[Dalbeth2007]]. A systematic review found 8 tophus methods, of which Vernier calipers and ultrasound met most of the OMERACT filter [[Dalbeth2011ARD]]. Doherty 2018 recorded tophus number, sites and the maximum diameter of the largest tophus by Vernier caliper [[Doherty2018]].
- A normal SU does not rule out a flare. In 339 people with acute gout, 14% had SU ≤6 mg/dL [360 µmol/L] and 32% ≤8 mg/dL [476 µmol/L]. [[Schlesinger2009]]
- Septic red flags: in 30 cases of combined septic and gouty arthritis, one-third were afebrile, 30% had a normal blood white count and 10% had synovial fluid white count <6000/mm³. Ruptured or infected tophi were the commonest route of infection, and the authors advise culturing aspirated fluid in a flare. [[Yu2003]]
- Drug-interaction screen: in pharmacokinetic studies, CYP3A4/P-glycoprotein inhibitors other than azithromycin raised colchicine Cmax and AUC by >125%. Recommended dose cuts are 33–66% for flare treatment and 50–75% for prophylaxis. [[Terkeltaub2011]] Febuxostat is contraindicated with azathioprine or mercaptopurine. With allopurinol their doses must be reduced. [[FDA-Uloric,FDA-Allopurinol]]

### nursing · Giving the medicines (from the labels)
- Colchicine: in the flare trial, gastrointestinal adverse reactions occurred in 26% at the recommended 1.8 mg over 1 hour, against 77% at the high 4.8 mg regimen and 20% on placebo. Diarrhoea (23%) was the commonest adverse reaction. Acute toxicity typically begins within 24 hours with abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea, and multi-organ failure follows at 24–72 hours. [[FDA-Colcrys]]
- NSAIDs (naproxen label): boxed warning for cardiovascular thrombotic events and GI bleeding, ulceration and perforation, with the elderly and people with prior ulcer or bleeding at greater GI risk. Contraindicated around CABG surgery. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest time. Monitor kidney function in renal or hepatic impairment, heart failure or dehydration. The acute gout dose is 750 mg, then 250 mg every 8 hours until the flare subsides. [[FDA-Naproxen]]
- Allopurinol: better tolerated after meals; doses above 300 mg are given divided; do not double a missed dose. Stop it at the first appearance of skin rash or other signs of hypersensitivity, and tell the patient to stop and seek care promptly. Serious reactions occur in about 5 per 10,000 (0.05%). Monitor kidney function often early on. [[FDA-Allopurinol]]
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends HLA-B*58:01 testing before allopurinol for people of Southeast Asian descent (Han Chinese, Korean, Thai; carriage 7.4%) and African American people (3.8%), and not universal testing (0.7% in white and Hispanic people). [[FitzGerald2020]]
- Febuxostat: boxed warning that people with established cardiovascular disease had higher CV death than on allopurinol in a CV outcomes study. It is indicated only after inadequate response to, intolerance of, or unsuitability for allopurinol. Interrupt it for ALT or AST >3× ULN, and stop it if serious skin reactions are suspected. [[FDA-Uloric]]
- Pegloticase: 8 mg IV every 2 weeks over no less than 120 minutes, with weekly oral methotrexate 15 mg (plus folic or folinic acid) started at least 4 weeks before. Pre-medicate with antihistamines and corticosteroids, give it in a setting ready for anaphylaxis, and observe afterwards. Stop oral ULT before starting it. [[FDA-Krystexxa]]
- Pegloticase urate check: measure SU before every infusion and stop treatment if levels rise above 6 mg/dL, "particularly when 2 consecutive levels above 6 mg/dL are observed". Loss of response carries a higher risk of anaphylaxis and infusion reactions. Screen at-risk patients (African, Mediterranean, Southern Asian ancestry) for G6PD deficiency first, because it is a contraindication (haemolysis, methaemoglobinaemia). [[FDA-Krystexxa]]
- Pegloticase alone (pre-marketing trials, no stopping rule): anaphylaxis in 6.5% (every 2 weeks) and infusion reactions in 26%. Flares in the first 3 months: 74% (every 2 weeks) against 51% on placebo. With methotrexate, flares were 66% in months 1–3 and 8–9% by months 9–12. [[FDA-Krystexxa]]

### nursing · Teaching and adherence
- Adherence to ULT is poor. A meta-analysis of 13 studies pooled 46% adherent (95% CI 41–51); non-persistence (a gap of ≥30 days) ranged from 54% to 87% in 6 studies. Older age, more comorbidities, diabetes and hypertension were linked to better adherence. [[Scheepers2018SAR]]
- Among 48,280 UK people starting allopurinol, non-persistence (a first 90-day gap) was 38.5% at 1 year and 56.9% at 5 years, and 61% were non-adherent overall. After a gap, 43.3% restarted within a year, and only 52.3% of those persisted for a year. [[Scheepers2018Rheum]]
- Earlier reviews and cohorts agree. In prescription-record studies 10–46% were adherent [[DeVera2014]]. Among 4,166 US starters, 56% were non-adherent (median MPR 0.68), and age <45 gave OR 2.43 for poor adherence [[Harrold2009]].
- Knowledge gaps (240 US respondents): seafood, beef, pork and beer were recognised as triggers by only 23%, 22%, 7% and 43%. Only 12% of those on ULT knew flares can worsen when it starts. [[Harrold2012]]
- Illness perceptions (142 people with gout <10 years): most believed diet caused their gout. Adherence rose with perceived understanding and fell with perceived severity, and negative perceptions predicted worse HAQ-II disability at 1 year. [[Dalbeth2011ACR]] Consequence beliefs predicted all-cause mortality (HR 1.18, 1.02–1.37) in 242 people. [[Serlachius2017]]
- Poor health literacy (9.4% of 551 primary-care patients) was associated with having ≥2 flares in the past year (adjusted OR 4.10, 2.04–8.19). [[Watson2024]]
- EULAR points to consider on non-adherence: discuss adherence regularly, assess it without blame, build trust and tailor solutions. Non-adherence in rheumatic diseases runs at 30–80%. [[Ritschl2021]] EULAR's patient-education recommendations (eight, four with strength-A evidence) cover when, by whom and how education is given. [[Zangi2015]]
- What is taught in the effective model: Doherty 2018 nurses gave a holistic assessment, discussed illness perceptions, explained the nature, causes, associations, consequences and treatment options of gout, shared decisions and handed out a written booklet. [[Doherty2018]]

### nursing · Common misunderstandings documented in studies
- "Gout is self-inflicted or part of ageing" and "treat the attacks, not the cause": interviews with 20 patients and 18 professionals found these beliefs widespread. All participants linked gout to Victorian cartoon stereotypes, and professionals were reluctant to offer ULT as a curative long-term strategy. [[Spencer2012]]
- "It isn't serious": Doherty 2018 names the beliefs that gout "is not a serious condition" and "is self-induced by lifestyle" as barriers to care, and notes some physicians share them. [[Doherty2018]]
- "Flares after starting ULT mean it isn't working": only 12% of ULT users knew about initiation flares [[Harrold2012]]. Flares are expected early. On pegloticase, flares fell from 74% in months 1–3 to 41% in months 4–6 [[FDA-Krystexxa]], and in Doherty 2018 the nurse-led group had more flares in year 1 but far fewer in year 2 [[Doherty2018]].
- "I can stop when the pain goes": ACR 2020 favours indefinite ULT. In the withdrawal series it cites, only 13% (27/211) of people in remission whose SU stayed <7 mg/dL off ULT remained flare-free over 5 years [[FitzGerald2020]]. Qualitative syntheses describe denial of illness, minimising symptoms and uncertainty about medicines as themes behind stopping [[Tvedten2025,Rai2018]].
- "Gout is all about diet": diet explains ≤0.3% of SU variance against 23.9% for common genetic variation [[Major2018]], and dietary education alone did not move SU in treated gout [[Holland2015]].
- "A normal uric acid during a flare means it isn't gout": 14% of people in acute flare had SU ≤6 mg/dL [[Schlesinger2009]].
- Shame and stigma: men with chronic gout described shame, embarrassment, isolation and work disability, which led them to play down severity and expect little from treatment [[Lindsay2011]]. A synthesis of 45 qualitative studies (1203 patients) found five themes: denying illness, negotiating uncertainty, juggling competing priorities, minimising symptoms and lifestyle restrictions, and developing strategies and empowerment [[Tvedten2025]].

### nursing · Nurse-led and pharmacist-led care
- Doherty 2018 (Nottingham, 56 practices, 517 adults with a flare in the past 12 months; 255 nurse-led, 262 GP-led usual care; 2 years): SU <360 µmol/L [6 mg/dL] at 1 year 94.75% vs 26.22% (RR 3.59, 2.72–4.75), and at 2 years 94.88% vs 29.71% (RR 3.18, 2.42–4.18). [[Doherty2018]]
- SU <300 µmol/L [5 mg/dL]: 87.42% vs 13.75% at 1 year and 88.05% vs 17.46% at 2 years. Mean SU fell from 443 to 251 µmol/L [7.45 to 4.22 mg/dL, converted] with nurse-led care against 439 to 421 µmol/L [7.38 to 7.08 mg/dL] with usual care. There was a mean fall of 183 µmol/L [3.1 mg/dL, converted] in the first 3 months. [[Doherty2018]]
- Taking ULT: 96.70% vs 46.83% at 1 year and 96.10% vs 56.13% at 2 years. The mean allopurinol dose at 2 years was 460 vs 230 mg/day, and 79% were on >300 mg/day vs 10%. Febuxostat was used by 14% vs 3%, and only 3 nurse-led patients chose colchicine prophylaxis. [[Doherty2018]]
- Flares: ≥2 flares in a year went from 79.92% at baseline to 53.99% (year 1) and 8.00% (year 2) with nurse-led care, against 79.77%, 39.82% and 24.29% with usual care (year 2 RR 0.33, 0.19–0.57). ≥4 flares in year 2: 1.15% vs 12.39% (RR 0.09). [[Doherty2018]]
- Tophi: present in 13.73%, 7.06% and 2.85% (baseline, 1 and 2 years) with nurse-led care vs 8.78%, 10.15% and 11.29% (year 2 RR 0.21, 0.08–0.52). The largest tophus shrank from 16.89 to 3.29 mm vs 20.09 to 13.61 mm. [[Doherty2018]]
- Quality of life: the SF-36 physical component went from 35.64 to 41.01 vs 35.48 to 37.43 (difference 3.48 at 2 years), with no change in the mental component. Gout Impact Scale "gout concern overall" went from 71.56 to 37.54 vs 68.51 to 53.62 (difference 17.54). [[Doherty2018]]
- Cost: £5066 per QALY gained at 2 years, modelled at £286 at 3 years and cost-saving from 5 years, well under the NICE threshold of £20,000. [[Doherty2018]]
- What the package contained: trained research nurses; individualised information and shared decision-making; allopurinol from 100 mg titrated by 100 mg every 3–4 weeks to a maximum of 900 mg; then febuxostat 80→120 mg or benzbromarone 50→200 mg; SU checked "as often as required"; phone contact allowed in place of visits. On average there were 9.3 visits and 8.3 phone calls over 2 years, mostly in the first 6 months, against 0.6 gout-specific GP visits under usual care. 99% chose ULT once fully informed. 24 (10%) stopped a ULT, 12 for side-effects, and all were on another regimen by the end of year 1. [[Doherty2018]]
- Rees 2013 pilot (106 people, mainly nurse-delivered education, lifestyle advice and ULT): all agreed to joint aspiration and all chose ULT. At 12 months 92% had SU ≤360 µmol/L and 85% <300 µmol/L, on a median allopurinol dose of 400 mg. [[Rees2013]]
- Abhishek 2017: of 75 pilot patients who replied at 5 years, after GP takeover, 90.7% were still on ULT, 85.3% took it ≥6 days a week, and mean SU was 292.8 µmol/L [4.9 mg/dL, converted]. [[Abhishek2017]]
- Fuller 2020 (Doherty 2018 follow-up, 82% response, ≥1 year after the trial): former nurse-led patients reported more satisfaction, better knowledge, more ULT use (aRR 1.19) and fewer flares (median 0 vs 1). 41–63% preferred a nurse and 5–20% a GP. [[Fuller2020]]
- Pharmacist-led care: a 1463-patient site-randomised trial using mostly automated telephone contact improved adherence (50% vs 37%) and SU <6 mg/dL (30% vs 15%) at 1 year. The adherence gain faded in year 2. [[Mikuls2019]] A 77-patient pharmacist telephone RCT reached SU ≤6 mg/dL in 35% vs 13% at 26 weeks, with SU change −1.5 vs +0.1 mg/dL. [[Goldfien2016]]
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends an augmented, non-physician protocol (education, shared decisions, treat-to-target) for everyone on ULT. It adds that "the key is for the treating provider (who could be the treating physician)" to educate and treat to target where such staff are not available. [[FitzGerald2020]]

### nursing · Documentation
- Doherty 2018 documented flare diaries; SU at every titration contact; ULT doses and changes; side-effects and reasons for stopping; tophus number, sites and largest diameter; SF-36 and Gout Impact Scale; and every contact (visit or call). This is a ready template for a nurse-led record. [[Doherty2018]]
- Measures with published validity for the record: the 3-of-4 flare definition [[Gaffo2018]], pain NRS or VAS [[Taylor2014]], Gout Impact Scale [[Hirsch2008]], HAQ-DI [[Taylor2008]] and caliper tophus diameter [[Dalbeth2007]].
- BSR 2023 asks that the risks and benefits of drug treatment in pregnancy be discussed and clearly documented by every professional involved (GRADE 1A). [[Schreiber2023]]

### Exam hooks
- ACR 2020 lifestyle advice is all conditional: limit alcohol, purines and HFCS; lose weight if overweight; no vitamin C. [[FitzGerald2020]]
- Do not stop low-dose aspirin for gout. Switch hydrochlorothiazide if feasible. Losartan is the preferred antihypertensive. Do not switch to fenofibrate. [[FitzGerald2020]]
- Diet explains ≤0.3% of SU variance and common genetic variation 23.9%. [[Major2018]]
- DASH lowers SU by 0.35 mg/dL overall and 1.29 mg/dL when baseline SU is ≥7. [[Juraschek2016]]
- The top fifth of purine intake gives about a fivefold flare risk (OR 4.76). [[Zhang2012Purine]]
- Hospitalisation multiplies flare risk about tenfold (RR 10.2). [[Zleik2018]]
- Nurse-led T2T: 95% vs 30% at SU <6 mg/dL at 2 years; £5066 per QALY. [[Doherty2018]]
- Pegloticase: check SU before each infusion and stop above 6 mg/dL; screen for G6PD; pre-medicate. [[FDA-Krystexxa]]
- Allopurinol: stop at the first rash. [[FDA-Allopurinol]]
- A normal SU does not exclude a flare (14% ≤6 mg/dL). [[Schlesinger2009]]
- Monitor SU every 2–5 weeks while titrating and every 6 months at target (ACR 2012). [[Khanna2012P1]]
- A flare is 3 of 4: patient says flare, pain at rest >3/10, a swollen joint, a warm joint. [[Gaffo2018]]

### In plain words
- Food and drink matter a little. The medicine that lowers uric acid matters most.
- Beer, spirits, wine, sugary drinks and big meals of meat or seafood can set off a flare within a day or two.
- Losing weight helps, but slowly. Five kilos might lower uric acid by about 1 point.
- Vitamin C pills do not lower uric acid enough to help.
- Cherries might help a little, but the proof is weak.
- Keep a flare kit at home and take it at the very first twinge. Ice helps too.
- Do not stop your daily uric acid pill during a flare.
- Flares in the first months on the pill are normal. They mean the crystals are dissolving, not that the pill has failed.
- If you get a rash on allopurinol, stop it and get help the same day.
- Being in hospital or having an operation makes a flare much more likely. Tell the team you have gout and keep taking your pill.
- Gout is not your fault. Much of it comes from the genes and kidneys you were born with.
- A nurse who explains gout, checks your blood every few weeks and raises the dose can get almost everyone to target.

## Open questions and controversies
- Dietary blame or genetics? Position A: lifestyle advice is part of every guideline, and purine and alcohol intake raise short-term flare risk [[Richette2017,FitzGerald2020,Zhang2012Purine,Neogi2014]]. Position B: diet explains ≤0.3% of SU variance against 23.9% for genetics [[Major2018]], dietary education did not lower SU in treated gout [[Holland2015]], and ACR explicitly avoids "patient-blaming" [[FitzGerald2020]]. Status: settled that diet has small effects on SU and that ULT is the main lever. Diet's role as a flare trigger remains supported by case-crossover data.
- Who should deliver treat-to-target? Position A: trained nurses (Doherty2018) or pharmacists [[Mikuls2019,Goldfien2016]] get far more people to target than usual care. Position B: ACR 2020 says the key is that the treating provider, who could be the physician, educates and titrates where such staff are not available [[FitzGerald2020]]. Doherty 2018 used research nurses, not practice nurses, and its authors call for an implementation study [[Doherty2018]]. Status: efficacy established; real-world implementation untested.
- Treat to target or treat to avoid symptoms? ACP 2017 recommends discussing benefits, harms, costs and preferences before ULT and advises against ULT after a first or infrequent flare [[Qaseem2017]]. Doherty 2018 describes ACP as advising against treat-to-target and presents its results as refuting that [[Doherty2018]]. ACR 2020 and EULAR 2016 recommend a target of <6 mg/dL [[FitzGerald2020,Richette2017]]. Status: rheumatology guidelines favour targets.
- Is prophylaxis needed if titration is slow? Both ACR 2020 and the labels recommend it (strong recommendation, 3–6 months) [[FitzGerald2020,FDA-Uloric,FDA-Krystexxa]]. Only 3 of 255 nurse-led patients took it. Flares were higher in year 1 but adherence held, and the authors suggest prophylaxis "might not be needed if up titration is done slowly" [[Doherty2018]]. Status: unresolved.
- Titration interval: the allopurinol label says 100 mg weekly [[FDA-Allopurinol]], ACR says every 2–5 weeks [[Khanna2012P1,FitzGerald2020]] and Doherty used every 3–4 weeks [[Doherty2018]]. No head-to-head data exist.
- Cherries and dairy: case-crossover and small RCT signals (Early evidence) [[Zhang2012Cherry,Dalbeth2012]] against ACR's decision not to recommend either [[FitzGerald2020]]. A tart cherry juice RCT protocol has been registered (not reviewed here).
- Keeping ULT in hospital: pre-admission ULT is linked to fewer inpatient flares and shorter stays [[Jatuworapruk2022Rheum,Jatuworapruk2022IJRD,Jeong2019]], but no trial tests continuing or starting ULT during an admission. Status: observational only.
- Hydration: no trial in people with gout was found. Advice to drink more rests on the allopurinol label's stone-prevention instruction [[FDA-Allopurinol]]. Status: evidence gap.
- Footwear: people with gout rate footwear as important [[Frecklington2019JFAR]], but adding a shoe to podiatry did not reduce foot pain at 6 months [[Frecklington2019ART]].

## Provenance
- **Validation method.** Every journal source was found through Europe PMC (core records, abstracts read). All 69 PMIDs were then cross-checked in one batch against PubMed E-utilities esummary on 2026-10-20 for authors, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, PMCID and DOI. Every record matched and none was missing. Labels were downloaded as SPL XML from the DailyMed v2 API and searched for the cited sections. Nothing in this file is marked (unverified).
- **Full texts read.** These were read through Europe PMC fullTextXML or the PMC OAI service: Doherty2018 (PMC6196879, Tables 1–3 and methods), FitzGerald2020 (PMC10563586), Khanna2012P1 (PMC3683400), Major2018 (PMC6174725), Zhang2012Cherry, Zhang2012Purine, Neogi2014 and Schreiber2023 (PMC10070063). Everything else rests on the abstract.
- **Access gaps.** Richette2017 full text returned HTTP 403 at ard.bmj.com and the Elsevier mirror. The EULAR lifestyle and concomitant-medication statements were checked against the abstract and a side-by-side table of ACP/EULAR/ACR recommendations in an open-access 2021 review ("Contentious Issues in Gout Management: The Story so Far", PMID 34012303, PMC8126966), which is not cited as a source. Qaseem2017 was read as abstract only, and its position on treat-to-target is given as characterised by Doherty2018. Logan 1997 (serum urate in acute gout, PMID 9462177) has no abstract and was dropped in favour of Schlesinger2009. Sammaritano 2020 (ACR reproductive health guideline) full text was not opened, so pregnancy drug guidance uses BSR 2023 plus the labels.
- **Reused FMF entries.** FDA-Colcrys, Zangi2015, Ritschl2021, Indraratna2018 and Terkeltaub2011 keep their fmf/src/sources.json key and text verbatim, with a "Validated" tag appended. Each was re-checked against PubMed, and the Colcrys SPL was re-downloaded from the same setid.
- **Dossier corrections.**
  - (1) living_and_caregiving "monitoring: SU every 2–5 weeks during titration, then every 6 months (ACR)". The every-6-months interval is from the ACR 2012 guideline (Khanna2012P1). ACR 2020 repeats the 2–5-week titration interval, says to check SU after each dose change, and names no maintenance interval.
  - (2) nursing.administration "pegloticase … stop if > 6 mg/dL on two checks". The label says to monitor before each infusion and discontinue if SU rises above 6 mg/dL, "particularly when 2 consecutive levels above 6 mg/dL are observed". A single rise is already a stop signal. Current dosing pairs it with methotrexate 15 mg weekly, started ≥4 weeks before.
  - (3) The "monthly urate" in the brief for the Doherty2018 package is not in the paper. Allopurinol was titrated every 3–4 weeks and SU was measured "as often as required by the nurse".
  - (4) prevention_and_risk: cherry OR 0.65 confirmed (Zhang2012Cherry; the dossier's "Zhang2012" is this paper, keyed with a suffix because a second Zhang 2012 paper is cited). Soft drinks ≥2/day RR 1.85 confirmed (Choi2008BMJ). DASH "to find" filled in as RR 0.68 for extreme fifths (Rai2017BMJ).
  - (5) living_and_caregiving "no fenofibrate added for gout". ACR 2020 says do not add or switch to fenofibrate, even though fenofibrate lowered uric acid by about 20% (≈1 mg/dL) and halved first gout events in FIELD (Waldman2018).
- **Conversions.** Every bracketed figure labelled "converted" uses 1 mg/dL = 59.48 µmol/L. Source figures are kept as published.
- **Not found.** No published study was found of hydration and flares in gout (the only candidate is a conference abstract, not cited), of a travel "flare kit", or of gout-specific nursing documentation standards. The flare-kit concept is given only as ACR 2020's "medication-in-pocket" strategy. No study was found that defines a gout-specific nursing documentation template, so the documentation list is derived from what Doherty2018 recorded.
