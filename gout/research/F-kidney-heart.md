# F · Kidney & cardiovascular links

Pass date: 2026-10-20. Modules served: links.

## Findings

### links · Comorbidity burden: how often gout travels with other conditions
- US adults with gout (NHANES 2007–2008, n = 5707 adults aged ≥ 20, self-reported physician diagnosis): 74% had hypertension (6.1 million), 71% chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage ≥ 2 (5.5 million), 53% obesity (4.3 million), 26% diabetes (2.1 million), 24% nephrolithiasis (2.0 million), 14% myocardial infarction (1.2 million), 11% heart failure (0.9 million) and 10% stroke (0.9 million); all proportions were substantially higher than in people without gout (Established) [[Zhu2012]].
- The same survey showed a gradient with serum urate: at ≥ 10 mg/dL [≥ 595 µmol/L], 86% had CKD stage ≥ 2, 66% hypertension, 65% obesity, 33% heart failure, 33% diabetes, 23% myocardial infarction and 12% stroke, 3 to 33 times the prevalence in the lowest category (< 4 mg/dL [< 238 µmol/L]) [[Zhu2012]].
- Metabolic syndrome (revised NCEP/ATP III, NHANES III 1988–1994, n = 8807): prevalence 62.8% (95% CI 51.9–73.6) in people with gout vs 25.4% (23.5–27.3) without gout; age- and sex-adjusted OR 3.05 (2.01–4.61); about 3.5 million US adults with gout have metabolic syndrome (Established) [[Choi2007AR]].
- UK primary care (CPRD, 39 111 incident gout cases vs 39 111 matched controls): gout was associated with a Charlson comorbidity index ≥ 5 at diagnosis (adjusted OR 2.51, 2.19–2.86); median time to a first new comorbidity was 43 months in cases vs 111 months in controls; after adjustment for comorbidities at diagnosis, all-cause mortality HR 1.13 (1.08–1.18) [[Kuo2016]].
- Obesity and weight: in 47 150 men followed 12 years, gout risk rose with BMI (RR 1.95 for BMI 25–29.9, 2.33 for 30–34.9, 2.97 for ≥ 35, vs 21–22.9); gaining ≥ 30 lb since age 21 gave RR 1.99, while losing ≥ 10 lb gave RR 0.61 [[Choi2005]]. A dose-response meta-analysis of 10 prospective studies (215 739 participants, 27 944 cases) found RR 1.55 (1.44–1.66) per 5 BMI units; RR 1.78, 2.67, 3.62 and 4.64 at BMI 25, 30, 35 and 40 vs BMI 20 [[Aune2014]].
- Type 2 diabetes: in MRFIT men (n = 11 351, 6 years), gout at baseline carried RR 1.34 (1.09–1.64) for incident type 2 diabetes, 1.26 (1.02–1.54) after also adjusting for serum urate (Early evidence) [[Choi2008Rheum]].

### links · Gout and the kidney: CKD
- Meta-analysis of 17 observational studies: pooled prevalence of CKD stage ≥ 3 in people with gout 24% (95% CI 19–28%); gout was associated with CKD (pooled adjusted OR 2.41, 1.86–3.11) (Established association) [[Roughley2015]].
- Gout as a risk factor for new CKD: in CPRD, 41 446 people with incident gout vs matched controls developed CKD stage ≥ 3 at 28.6 vs 15.8 per 10 000 person-years; adjusted HR 1.78 (1.70–1.85). After adjustment, urate-lowering therapy (ULT) use was not associated with CKD risk (non-significant in all analyses except the 3-year analysis in women, HR 1.31, 1.09–1.59) [[Roughley2018]].
- Advanced CKD: in 68 897 people with gout vs 554 964 without (CPRD), advanced CKD occurred at 8.54 vs 4.08 per 1000 patient-years; adjusted HR 1.29 (1.23–1.35); strongest for end-stage kidney disease (HR 2.13, 1.73–2.61) [[Stack2019]].
- Gout is common in people with CKD: in the German CKD (GCKD) cohort (n = 5085), self-reported gout prevalence was 24.3%, rising from 16.0% at eGFR ≥ 60 to 35.6% at eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m² (prevalence ratio 1.46, 1.21–1.77). 30.7% of those with gout took no gout medicine, and 47.2% of those on ULT were still hyperuricaemic [[Jing2015]].
- Direction of causation: reduced kidney function raises serum urate, so CKD drives hyperuricaemia and gout. Whether urate itself injures the kidney is unsettled. Cell and animal studies and epidemiology support it, but Mendelian randomisation (MR) generally does not, and small ULT trials disagree (Hypothesis) [[Johnson2018]].
- MR for kidney function: genetically raised urate had no causal effect on eGFR or CKD (all P > 0.05; > 99% power), even though the same cohorts showed an observational eGFR change of −1.99 mL/min/1.73 m² and an OR for CKD of 1.48 (1.32–1.65) per 1 mg/dL [59.48 µmol/L] higher urate. The positive control worked: OR for gout 3.41 to 6.04 per 1 mg/dL [[Jordan2019]].
- Allopurinol in people with gout does not appear to harm the kidney: in a propensity-matched UK cohort (4760 vs 4760), starting allopurinol at ≥ 300 mg/day was associated with lower risk of CKD stage ≥ 3 (HR 0.87, 0.77–0.97); starting below 300 mg/day had HR 1.00 (0.91–1.09) (Early evidence) [[VargasSantos2018]].

### links · Does lowering urate protect the kidney? Trials
- CKD-FIX (stage 3–4 CKD without gout, high risk of progression, n = 363 analysed; mean eGFR 31.7 mL/min/1.73 m²; mean urate 8.2 mg/dL [≈ 488 µmol/L]): allopurinol 100–300 mg/day vs placebo for 104 weeks. eGFR change −3.33 vs −3.23 mL/min/1.73 m² per year; difference −0.10 (−1.18 to 0.97); P = 0.85. Serious adverse events 46% vs 44%. Enrolment stopped early (369 of 620 planned) [[Badve2020]].
- PERL (type 1 diabetes with early-to-moderate diabetic kidney disease; 267 allopurinol vs 263 placebo; 3 years plus 2-month washout): urate fell from 6.1 to 3.9 mg/dL [≈ 363 to 232 µmol/L] on allopurinol. Between-group difference in iohexol-measured GFR after washout 0.001 mL/min/1.73 m² (−1.9 to 1.9; P = 0.99); GFR decline −3.0 vs −2.5 mL/min/1.73 m² per year; albumin excretion 40% higher with allopurinol [[Doria2020]].
- FEATHER (Japan; stage 3 CKD with asymptomatic hyperuricaemia; febuxostat n = 219 vs placebo n = 222; 108 weeks): eGFR slope 0.23 ± 5.26 vs −0.47 ± 4.48 mL/min/1.73 m² per year; difference 0.70 (−0.21 to 1.62; P = 0.1). Benefit appeared in subgroups without proteinuria (P = 0.005). Gouty arthritis 0.91% vs 5.86% (P = 0.007) [[Kimura2018]].
- FREED (Japan; 1070 older people with urate > 7.0 to ≤ 9.0 mg/dL [> 416 to ≤ 535 µmol/L] plus hypertension, type 2 diabetes, renal disease or prior cerebral/cardiovascular disease; open label): end-of-study urate 4.50 vs 6.76 mg/dL [≈ 268 vs 402 µmol/L]. The composite cerebral/cardiovascular/renal/death endpoint had HR 0.750 (0.592–0.950), driven mainly by "renal impairment" (16.2% vs 20.5%; HR 0.745, 0.562–0.987) (Early evidence; open label, composite driven by a laboratory endpoint) [[Kojima2019]].
- Meta-analysis of 28 RCTs (6458 participants, ≥ 6 months): ULT did not reduce kidney failure (RR 0.97, 0.61–1.54), MACE (RR 0.93, 0.74–1.18) or all-cause mortality (RR 1.04, 0.78–1.39). It did slow the fall in GFR slope (+1.18 mL/min/1.73 m² per year, 0.44–1.91) and lowered BP (systolic −3.45 mm Hg, diastolic −2.02 mm Hg). The authors concluded the evidence is insufficient to support urate lowering to protect the kidneys or heart [[Chen2020]].
- KDIGO 2024: people with CKD and symptomatic hyperuricaemia (gout) should be offered urate-lowering intervention (1C). Consider ULT after a first gout flare in CKD, particularly when urate is > 9 mg/dL [535 µmol/L; the guideline text prints "mmol/l"]. Prefer xanthine oxidase inhibitors over uricosurics in CKD. KDIGO suggests not lowering urate in CKD with asymptomatic hyperuricaemia to delay CKD progression (2D) [[KDIGO2024]].

### links · Gout and kidney stones
- Pooled prevalence of self-reported lifetime nephrolithiasis in gout 14% (12–17%); gout associated with nephrolithiasis, pooled adjusted OR 1.77 (1.43–2.19) [[Roughley2015]].
- Health Professionals Follow-Up Study (51 529 men): stone history 15% in men with gout vs 8% without (age/BMI-adjusted OR 1.88, 1.68–2.11). Confirmed gout raised the risk of incident stones (multivariate RR 2.12, 1.22–3.68). A history of stones did not raise the risk of gout (RR 1.05, 0.54–2.07) [[Kramer2003]].
- Uric acid stones make up around 10% of all stones. At urinary pH < 5.5, almost all uric acid is undissociated and urine becomes supersaturated. Persistently low urine pH, hyperuricosuria and low urine volume are the key drivers, and primary gout, insulin resistance and high cell turnover are listed causes [[AbouElela2017]].
- Urine pH falls as body weight rises in people with stones (Dallas sextiles 6.09 → 5.91; Chicago 6.18 → 5.88; P for trend < 0.0001). This links obesity and insulin resistance to uric acid stones [[Maalouf2004]].
- Gout is also linked to calcium stones. Allopurinol (100 mg three times daily) prevented recurrent calcium oxalate stones in people with hyperuricosuria and normal urine calcium: 0.12 vs 0.26 stone events per patient-year, with a longer time to recurrence (P < 0.02) [[Ettinger1986]].
- Of 136 outcomes studied for serum urate, an umbrella review of observational meta-analyses, RCT meta-analyses and MR studies found convincing evidence of a clear role for urate only in gout and nephrolithiasis (Established) [[Li2017]].
- SGLT2 inhibitors and recurrent stones (people with nephrolithiasis and type 2 diabetes, target-trial emulation): recurrence 105.3 vs 156.4 per 1000 person-years with SGLT2 inhibitors vs GLP-1 receptor agonists, rate ratio 0.67 (0.57–0.79). In those who also had gout, rate ratio 0.67 (0.57–0.79) vs GLP-1 receptor agonists and 0.63 (0.55–0.72) vs DPP-4 inhibitors (Early evidence) [[McCormick2024]].
- Acute kidney injury from tumour lysis syndrome is a recognised, direct urate-related kidney injury (Established) [[Johnson2018]].
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends starting ULT at a first flare when CKD stage ≥ 3, serum urate > 9 mg/dL [535 µmol/L] or urolithiasis is present. Allopurinol and febuxostat lower 24-hour urinary uric acid more than placebo [[FitzGerald2020]].

### links · Gout, urate and blood pressure
- Hypertension raises gout risk: multivariate RR 2.31 (1.96–2.72) for incident gout; diuretic use RR 1.77 (1.42–2.20) [[Choi2005]].
- Hyperuricaemia predicts hypertension: 18 prospective cohorts (55 607 participants), adjusted RR 1.41 (1.23–1.58); RR 1.13 (1.06–1.20) per 1 mg/dL [59.48 µmol/L]; the effect was larger in younger people (P = 0.02) (Established association) [[Grayson2011]].
- Small trial signal: in 30 adolescents with new stage 1 hypertension and urate ≥ 6 mg/dL [≥ 357 µmol/L], allopurinol 200 mg twice daily for 4 weeks (crossover) lowered casual systolic BP by 6.9 vs 2.0 mm Hg on placebo, and 24-hour ambulatory systolic BP by 6.3 mm Hg vs a 0.8 mm Hg rise. BP normalised in 20 of 30 on allopurinol vs 1 of 30 on placebo. The authors called the findings preliminary (Hypothesis) [[Feig2008]].
- MR in two Danish cohorts (58 072 and 10 602 people) found no causal link between urate and blood pressure or ischaemic heart disease. BMI did raise urate causally (+0.03 mmol/L per 4 BMI units; +7.5% hyperuricaemia risk), which points to BMI as a confounder of observational links (urate-to-BP causation: not established) [[Palmer2013]].
- Across 28 RCTs, ULT lowered systolic BP by 3.45 mm Hg (−6.10 to −0.80) [[Chen2020]].

### links · Gout and cardiovascular disease
- Meta-analysis (6 studies, 223 448 people free of CVD at entry): gout raised CVD mortality (adjusted HR 1.29, 1.14–1.44) and coronary heart disease (CHD) mortality (adjusted HR 1.42, 1.22–1.63); none of the studies showed a significant association with myocardial infarction (MI) mortality [[Clarson2015]].
- Health Professionals Follow-Up Study (51 297 men, 12 years; no CHD at baseline): gout RR 1.28 (1.15–1.41) total mortality, 1.38 (1.15–1.66) CVD death, 1.55 (1.24–1.93) fatal CHD; nonfatal MI RR 1.59 (1.04–2.41) [[Choi2007Circ]].
- MRFIT (12 866 men, 6.5 years): acute MI 10.5% with gout vs 8.43% without; adjusted OR 1.26 (1.14–1.40). The association held without diuretics, alcohol, metabolic syndrome, diabetes or obesity [[Krishnan2006]].
- Flares and short-term CV risk (CPRD, England, 1997–2020; 62 574 people with gout). In the nested case-control analysis, people with a CV event (MI or stroke) had higher odds of a gout flare in the prior 0–60 days (adjusted OR 1.93, 1.57–2.38) and 61–120 days (OR 1.57, 1.26–1.96), but not 121–180 days (OR 1.06, 0.84–1.34). In the self-controlled case series (n = 1421), CV event rates per 1000 person-days were 2.49 (days 0–60), 2.16 (61–120) and 1.70 (121–180), vs 1.32 in reference periods. Adjusted IRRs were 1.89, 1.64 and 1.29 (Early evidence; observational, consistent across two designs) [[Cipolletta2022]].
- Colchicine flare prophylaxis when starting ULT (CPRD Aurum, 99 800 people starting ULT; 16.1% given colchicine ≥ 21 days): MI or stroke within 180 days, 28.8 vs 35.3 per 1000 person-years; weighted HR 0.82 (0.69–0.94); rate difference −6.5 per 1000 person-years (Early evidence) [[Cipolletta2025]].
- Is urate itself causal for CVD? In MR with 14 urate-specific variants, genetically higher urate was not associated with type 2 diabetes, CHD, ischaemic stroke or heart failure, but strongly predicted gout (OR 5.84 per SD, 4.56–7.49) [[Keenan2016]]. In a second MR, conventional estimates suggested causation (OR 1.18 per SD urate for CHD), but the pleiotropy-robust Egger estimate was null (OR 1.05, 0.92–1.20); observational OR 1.07 (1.04–1.10) [[White2016]]. Status: urate as a cause of CVD is not established (Hypothesis) [[Li2017,Johnson2018]].
- ALL-HEART (5721 people aged ≥ 60 with ischaemic heart disease and no gout; allopurinol up to 600 mg/day vs usual care; mean 4.8 years): primary MACE 11.0% (2.47 per 100 patient-years) vs 11.3% (2.37), HR 1.04 (0.89–1.21), P = 0.65; all-cause death 10.1% vs 10.6%, HR 1.02 (0.87–1.20) [[Mackenzie2022]].
- CARES (6190 people with gout and CVD, median 32 months): febuxostat was noninferior to allopurinol for MACE (10.8% vs 10.4%; HR 1.03). All-cause death was higher on febuxostat (HR 1.22, 1.01–1.47), as was CV death (HR 1.34, 1.03–1.73). Trial regimen was stopped by 56.6% and follow-up by 45.0% [[White2018]].
- FAST (6128 people with gout aged ≥ 60 with ≥ 1 CV risk factor; UK, Denmark, Sweden; median follow-up 1467 days): on-treatment MACE 1.72 vs 2.05 per 100 patient-years, adjusted HR 0.85 (0.70–1.03), noninferior. Deaths: 7.2% on febuxostat vs 8.6% on allopurinol [[Mackenzie2020]].
- US label: ULORIC (febuxostat) carries a boxed warning (CV death higher than allopurinol in people with gout and established CVD) and is restricted to people with an inadequate response to, intolerance of, or unsuitability for allopurinol [[FDA-Uloric]]. ACR 2020 conditionally recommends switching from febuxostat to another ULT in people with a history of CVD or a new CV event [[FitzGerald2020]].

### links · Colchicine and the heart
- COLCOT (4745 people within 30 days of MI; colchicine 0.5 mg daily vs placebo; median 22.6 months): primary endpoint 5.5% vs 7.1%, HR 0.77 (0.61–0.96). Stroke HR 0.26 (0.10–0.70); urgent angina hospitalisation with revascularisation HR 0.50 (0.31–0.81). Pneumonia (serious) 0.9% vs 0.4% [[Tardif2019]].
- LoDoCo2 (5522 people with chronic coronary disease; 0.5 mg daily; median 28.6 months): primary endpoint 6.8% vs 9.6% (2.5 vs 3.6 per 100 person-years), HR 0.69 (0.57–0.83), P < 0.001. Key secondary HR 0.72 (0.57–0.92). Non-CV death 0.7 vs 0.5 per 100 person-years, HR 1.51 (0.99–2.31) [[Nidorf2020]].
- CLEAR SYNERGY (7062 people after MI, 104 centres, 14 countries; colchicine started soon after MI; median 3 years): primary outcome 9.1% vs 9.3%, HR 0.99 (0.85–1.16), P = 0.93. CRP fell by 1.28 mg/L at 3 months. Diarrhoea 10.2% vs 6.6% [[Jolly2025]].
- Pre-CLEAR meta-analysis (5 trials, 11 816 patients): low-dose colchicine reduced MACE (RR 0.75, 0.61–0.92), MI (RR 0.78), stroke (RR 0.54) and revascularisation (RR 0.77), with no difference in all-cause mortality. Fewer CV deaths were offset by more non-CV deaths [[Fiolet2021]].
- FDA approved LODOCO (colchicine 0.5 mg once daily; NDA 215727, approval 16 June 2023) to reduce the risk of MI, stroke, coronary revascularisation and CV death in adults with established atherosclerotic disease or multiple CV risk factors. Its efficacy evidence is LoDoCo2. It is contraindicated with strong CYP3A4 or P-gp inhibitors and in renal failure (CrCl < 15 mL/min) or severe hepatic impairment. Myalgia occurred in 21.2% vs 18.5% in LoDoCo2 [[FDA-Lodoco]].
- The evidence is mixed: two positive trials (COLCOT, LoDoCo2) and one large neutral post-MI trial (CLEAR SYNERGY). The benefit is not established for every setting (Early evidence) [[Tardif2019,Nidorf2020,Jolly2025]].
- People with gout, observational data. In US Medicare-linked records (501 new colchicine users vs 501 matched non-users), CV events were 35.6 vs 81.8 per 1000 person-years, adjusted HR 0.51 (0.30–0.88); all-cause mortality HR 0.55 (0.35–0.85) [[Solomon2016]]. In a VA cross-sectional study (n = 1288), MI prevalence was 1.2% with colchicine vs 2.6% without (P = 0.03) [[Crittenden2012]]. In VA men with gout and no known coronary artery disease (CAD), the reduction in incident CAD was not significant (IPW HR 0.49, 0.23–1.05), while the composite of CAD and MI had HR 0.37 (0.16–0.83) [[Shah2020]]. (Early evidence; confounding by indication possible.)
- The IL-1β pathway links both diseases. In CANTOS (10 059 people after MI with high hsCRP), canakinumab did not change serum urate but cut gout flares at every baseline urate level (HR 0.40, 0.48 and 0.45 for ≤ 404.5, 404.6–535.3 and ≥ 535.4 µmol/L [≈ ≤ 6.8, 6.8–9.0, ≥ 9.0 mg/dL]). Flare rates on placebo were 0.28, 1.36 and 5.94 per 100 person-years [[Solomon2018]].

### links · Drugs that help both, and drugs that raise urate
- SGLT2 inhibitors lower urate: a meta-analysis of 62 RCTs (34 941 people with type 2 diabetes) found a weighted mean difference of −37.73 µmol/L [≈ −0.63 mg/dL] (−40.51 to −34.95), with empagliflozin −45.83 µmol/L. The effect was lost in CKD subgroups [[Zhao2018]]. In DAPA-HF, dapagliflozin lowered urate by 0.84 mg/dL [≈ 50 µmol/L] over 12 months vs placebo [[McDowell2022]]. In EMPEROR-Reduced, 53% of patients were hyperuricaemic; empagliflozin caused a rapid, sustained fall in urate and fewer hyperuricaemia-related events [[Doehner2022]].
- SGLT2 inhibitors and incident gout: in 295 907 adults with type 2 diabetes (US claims), gout occurred at 4.9 vs 7.8 per 1000 person-years with SGLT2 inhibitors vs GLP-1 agonists, HR 0.64 (0.57–0.72) [[Fralick2020]]. In the CANVAS Program post hoc analysis (n = 10 142), canagliflozin lowered urate by 23.3 µmol/L (6.7%), and the HR for a gout flare or starting gout treatment was 0.53 (0.40–0.71) [[Li2019]]. A meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (29 776 people, 1052 gout events) gave HR 0.55 (0.45–0.67) [[Banerjee2023]].
- SGLT2 inhibitors in people who already have gout (with type 2 diabetes, vs DPP-4 inhibitors): recurrent flares 52.4 vs 79.7 per 1000 person-years, RR 0.66 (0.57–0.75); gout emergency visits or admissions RR 0.52 (0.32–0.84); MI HR 0.69 (0.54–0.88); stroke HR 0.81 (0.62–1.05) (Early evidence) [[McCormick2023]].
- Diuretics raise gout risk (UK case-control, 91 530 cases). Adjusted OR for current vs past use: loop 2.64 (2.47–2.83), thiazide 1.70 (1.62–1.79), thiazide-like 2.30 (1.95–2.70), potassium-sparing 1.06 (0.91–1.23); loop plus thiazide 4.65 (3.51–6.16) [[Bruderer2014]].
- Antihypertensives in people with hypertension (UK nested case-control, 24 768 gout cases): RR for incident gout 0.87 (0.82–0.93) with calcium channel blockers, 0.81 (0.70–0.94) with losartan, 2.36 (2.21–2.52) with diuretics, 1.48 (1.40–1.57) with β-blockers, 1.24 (1.17–1.32) with ACE inhibitors and 1.29 (1.16–1.43) with other ARBs [[Choi2012BMJ]].
- Losartan is uricosuric: in 13 hypertensive people with gout, losartan 50 mg once daily lowered urate from 538 ± 26 to 491 ± 20 µmol/L [≈ 9.0 to 8.3 mg/dL]; irbesartan did not, and losartan 50 mg twice daily gave no extra fall [[Wurzner2001]].
- ACR 2020 (all conditional, very-low-certainty evidence): switch hydrochlorothiazide to another antihypertensive when feasible; choose losartan preferentially when feasible; do not stop low-dose aspirin taken for an appropriate reason; do not add or switch to fenofibrate. The guideline states it does not directly address the effect of gout or hyperuricaemia on CVD, hypertension, urolithiasis or CKD [[FitzGerald2020]].
- ACR 2020 recommends against ULT for asymptomatic hyperuricaemia (serum urate > 6.8 mg/dL [> 404 µmol/L] with no prior flares or tophi), including in people with CKD, CVD, urolithiasis or hypertension. Among people with asymptomatic urate > 9 mg/dL, only 20% developed gout within 5 years [[FitzGerald2020]].
- EULAR 2016: allopurinol is first-line and its dose should be adjusted to kidney function; target urate < 6 mg/dL [360 µmol/L] [[Richette2017]]. EULAR's overarching principle is that every person with gout should be systematically screened for comorbidities and CV risk factors (renal impairment, CHD, heart failure, stroke, peripheral arterial disease, obesity, hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, diabetes, smoking). EULAR also advises replacing a loop or thiazide diuretic if possible, and considering losartan or calcium channel blockers for hypertension (unverified: EULAR full text blocked, 403; wording from the published recommendation not re-read) [[Richette2017]]. The Hong Kong consensus states the same screening principle and prefers calcium channel blockers and losartan over loop/thiazide diuretics, β-blockers, ACE inhibitors and non-losartan ARBs [[Yip2023]].

### Exam hooks
- Gout comorbidity prevalence in NHANES 2007–2008: hypertension 74%, CKD ≥ 2 71%, obesity 53%, diabetes 26%, stones 24%, MI 14% [[Zhu2012]].
- Metabolic syndrome in gout 62.8% vs 25.4% without gout [[Choi2007AR]].
- About 1 in 4 people with gout has CKD stage ≥ 3 (24%), and about 1 in 4 people with CKD has gout (24.3%) [[Roughley2015,Jing2015]].
- CKD-FIX and PERL: allopurinol did not slow eGFR decline, in CKD stage 3–4 or in type 1 diabetic kidney disease [[Badve2020,Doria2020]].
- Gout flare → CV event odds roughly double in the next 60 days (OR 1.93) and return to baseline by 121–180 days [[Cipolletta2022]].
- CARES: febuxostat noninferior for MACE, but higher CV death (HR 1.34) → FDA boxed warning. FAST: no excess death [[White2018,Mackenzie2020,FDA-Uloric]].
- Colchicine 0.5 mg daily: COLCOT HR 0.77, LoDoCo2 HR 0.69, CLEAR SYNERGY HR 0.99; FDA-approved (LODOCO) for CV risk reduction in 2023 [[Tardif2019,Nidorf2020,Jolly2025,FDA-Lodoco]].
- Losartan and calcium channel blockers lower gout risk; diuretics, β-blockers, ACE inhibitors and non-losartan ARBs raise it [[Choi2012BMJ]].
- SGLT2 inhibitors lower urate (≈ −38 µmol/L) and gout risk (HR ≈ 0.55–0.64) [[Zhao2018,Fralick2020,Banerjee2023]].
- Uric acid stones form in persistently acidic urine (pH < 5.5) [[AbouElela2017]].
- MR studies do not support urate as a cause of CKD, CHD or hypertension; the one convincing causal links are gout and kidney stones [[Jordan2019,Keenan2016,Palmer2013,Li2017]].

### In plain words
- Many people with gout also have high blood pressure, kidney disease, extra weight or diabetes. Checking for these is part of good gout care.
- Weak kidneys cannot clear urate well, so kidney disease and gout often go together.
- Gout makes kidney stones more likely. Drinking plenty of fluid helps.
- In the two months after a bad flare, the chance of a heart attack or stroke goes up for a short time. It then settles back down.
- Doctors know gout and heart disease are linked, but they do not know if high urate itself harms the heart or kidneys.
- Two large studies found that allopurinol did not protect the kidneys in people who did not have gout. In people with gout, allopurinol is still the main treatment, and it does not seem to harm the kidneys.
- A low daily dose of colchicine may lower heart attack and stroke risk in some people with heart disease, but not every study agrees.
- Some blood pressure pills, such as water pills, can raise urate. Others, like losartan, lower it a little. Ask before you change any medicine.
- Some diabetes medicines called SGLT2 inhibitors also lower urate and seem to cut gout flares.

## Open questions and controversies
- Is urate a cause of CKD, or only a marker? Position A: urate predicts CKD and its progression in cohorts (observational OR 1.48 per 1 mg/dL), and gout predicts incident and advanced CKD [[Jordan2019,Roughley2018,Stack2019]]; animal and cell data and small trials suggest harm [[Johnson2018]]. Position B: MR shows no causal effect [[Jordan2019]], CKD-FIX and PERL were null [[Badve2020,Doria2020]], FEATHER missed its primary endpoint [[Kimura2018]], and a 28-trial meta-analysis found no effect on kidney failure [[Chen2020]]. FREED's positive composite was mostly a renal-laboratory endpoint in an open-label trial [[Kojima2019]]. Status: not established. KDIGO 2024 suggests against treating asymptomatic hyperuricaemia in CKD (2D) [[KDIGO2024]], and ACR 2020 recommends against ULT for asymptomatic hyperuricaemia [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Is urate a cause of cardiovascular disease and hypertension? Position A: consistent observational links [[Grayson2011,Clarson2015]], conventional MR OR 1.18 for CHD [[White2016]], and BP falls with allopurinol in adolescents [[Feig2008]] and modestly across trials [[Chen2020]]. Position B: pleiotropy-robust and urate-specific MR are null [[White2016,Keenan2016,Palmer2013]], and ALL-HEART found no benefit of allopurinol in ischaemic heart disease (HR 1.04) [[Mackenzie2022]]. Status: hypothesis; BMI likely confounds the observational links [[Palmer2013]]. The umbrella review finds convincing evidence only for gout and stones [[Li2017]].
- Is the gout–CVD link driven by inflammation rather than urate? For: CV risk rises transiently after flares [[Cipolletta2022]]; colchicine prophylaxis at ULT start was associated with fewer CV events [[Cipolletta2025]]; IL-1β blockade cut flares without changing urate [[Solomon2018]]. Against a simple story: anti-inflammatory colchicine trials disagree (COLCOT and LoDoCo2 positive, CLEAR SYNERGY neutral) [[Tardif2019,Nidorf2020,Jolly2025]]. Status: early evidence; no RCT has tested colchicine for CV outcomes specifically in people with gout.
- Is febuxostat cardiovascularly unsafe? Position A: CARES showed higher CV death (HR 1.34) and all-cause death (HR 1.22), with high dropout (45.0% stopped follow-up) [[White2018]]; FDA boxed warning [[FDA-Uloric]]. Position B: FAST showed noninferiority (HR 0.85) and no excess death [[Mackenzie2020]]. Status: ACR 2020 conditionally recommends switching away from febuxostat after CVD or a new CV event [[FitzGerald2020]]; US label restricts it to second line [[FDA-Uloric]].
- Should comorbidity drugs be chosen with gout in mind? Losartan, calcium channel blockers and SGLT2 inhibitors are associated with lower urate or gout risk, and diuretics with higher risk [[Choi2012BMJ,Bruderer2014,Fralick2020,McCormick2023]]. Guidelines favour losartan and switching away from hydrochlorothiazide only "when feasible" (conditional, very low certainty) [[FitzGerald2020]]. The SGLT2 data in gout are observational apart from post hoc RCT analyses [[Li2019,Banerjee2023]].

## Provenance
- Method. PubMed E-utilities was rate-limited (HTTP 429) because several passes were querying it in parallel. After the first record (Zhu2012, confirmed by PubMed esummary), every journal source was validated against Europe PMC MED records, which mirror PubMed: authors, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, PMID, PMCID, DOI and the abstract. Twelve DOIs were cross-checked in Crossref (Badve2020, Cipolletta2022, Cipolletta2025, Fralick2020, Jolly2025, Kojima2019, Li2019, Mackenzie2022, McCormick2023, Nidorf2020, Tardif2019, Yip2023), and all matched. Six trial registrations were confirmed on ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02551094 COLCOT, NCT03048825 CLEAR SYNERGY, NCT02017171 PERL, NCT01101035 CARES, NCT00288184 (Feig), NCT01984749 FREED. Labels were read on DailyMed (LODOCO, ULORIC). The LODOCO approval date (NDA 215727, 16 June 2023) comes from Drugs@FDA via openFDA. Numbers were taken from abstracts, except: ACR 2020 recommendation wording (PMC full text), KDIGO 2024 recommendations 3.14.1–3.14.2 (KDIGO PDF), Abou-Elela uric acid stone figures (Europe PMC full text) and the Yip2023 consensus statements (Europe PMC full text).
- Blocked or not opened. The EULAR 2016 full text (ARD, ScienceDirect and the open-access PDF all returned 403) was not opened. Only the abstract statements on allopurinol renal dose adjustment and the < 6 mg/dL target are verified. The overarching comorbidity-screening principle and the diuretic/losartan recommendation are marked (unverified), and Yip2023 independently confirms the same principle.
