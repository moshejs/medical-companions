# D · Treating a flare & colchicine

Pass date: 2026-10-20. Modules served: colin, flarerx, coldose.

## Findings

### colin · Colchicine from pill to blood
- One 0.6 mg Colcrys tablet gives a mean Cmax of 2.5 ng/mL (range 1.1–4.4) 1–2 h after a fasting dose; absolute bioavailability is about 45%, and secondary plasma peaks appear 3–36 h after a dose (39–155% of the first peak), attributed to intestinal secretion and reabsorption or biliary recirculation [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- After a 1 mg oral tablet, Cmax was 5.5 ng/mL at 62 min in healthy men and 12 ng/mL at 87 min in elderly women; absolute bioavailability was 44% and 45%, volume of distribution 6.7 and 6.3 L/kg, and plasma elimination half-life 30 and 34 h [[Rochdi1994]].
- After an intravenous dose the terminal half-life was 57.8 ± 10.7 h; oral solution bioavailability was 47 ± 14%, and most volunteers had a second plasma peak within 6 h [[Ferron1996]].
- With 0.6 mg twice daily for 10 days, the mean half-life was 26.6 h (label range across studies 26.6–31.2 h) and 40–65% of a 1 mg oral dose was recovered unchanged in urine [[FDA-Colcrys]]. Published half-lives disagree (about 27–58 h across these sources), so the animation should show half-life as a range, not one number [[FDA-Colcrys,Ferron1996,Rochdi1994]].
- The low-dose gout regimen (1.8 mg over 1 h) gave a mean Cmax of 6.2 ng/mL at a median 1.81 h; the old high-dose regimen (4.8 mg over 6 h) gave 6.8 ng/mL at 4.47 h. The extra 3 mg added almost nothing to peak level [[FDA-Colcrys]]. AGREE chose these two regimens because they produced comparable maximum plasma concentrations in healthy volunteers [[Terkeltaub2010]].

### colin · Colchicine inside the white cell
- After a single 1 mg dose, plasma colchicine peaked at 1 h but leukocyte colchicine peaked 47 h later; on repeated dosing granulocytes held about twice the concentration of mononuclear cells (20–53 vs 9–24 ng/10⁹ cells), and the leukocyte half-life was 41–46 h [[Chappey1993]].
- In 30 people taking 0.5–2 mg/day (FMF), plasma and leukocyte levels varied widely and did not correlate with the dose taken; uptake into leukocytes was saturable [[Chappey1994]].
- Granulocytes showed a moderate MDR1 mRNA level but no surface P-glycoprotein and no P-glycoprotein pump function [[Klimecki1994]]. That neutrophils trap colchicine because they lack this efflux pump is a proposed explanation for the drug's selectivity (Hypothesis) [[BenChetrit1998MH]].
- The primary mechanism is tubulin disruption, which then dampens several inflammatory pathways, including the NALP3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and P2X7-activated pore formation in macrophages (Established) [[Leung2015]]. The Colcrys label says colchicine inhibits β-tubulin polymerisation into microtubules and so prevents activation, degranulation and migration of neutrophils "thought to mediate some gout symptoms" [[FDA-Colcrys]].

### colin · How it acts on crystal-driven inflammation (gout-specific)
- MSU crystals engage the caspase-1-activating NALP3 inflammasome, producing active IL-1β and IL-18; mice lacking caspase-1, ASC or NALP3, or the IL-1 receptor, had impaired neutrophil influx in crystal-induced peritonitis (Established) [[Martinon2006]].
- NLRP3 inflammasome assembly needs microtubules: inducers lower NAD⁺, inactivate the α-tubulin deacetylase sirtuin 2, and acetylated α-tubulin drives dynein-dependent transport of mitochondria so that ASC meets NLRP3 (Established in cells) [[Misawa2013]]. This is the step the gout animation uses in place of the FMF pyrin scene; that colchicine's microtubule block dampens it is stated in reviews [[Leung2015,Dalbeth2014ClinTher]].
- A systematic review of colchicine in gout concludes that by preventing microtubule assembly it disrupts inflammasome activation, microtubule-based chemotaxis of inflammatory cells, leukotriene and cytokine generation, and phagocytosis (Established) [[Dalbeth2014ClinTher]].
- In human blood neutrophils, colchicine dose-dependently blocked the tyrosine phosphorylation triggered by MSU and CPPD crystals but not that triggered by fMLP, C5a, leukotriene B4 or zymosan; inactive lumicolchicine, indomethacin and phenylbutazone had no such effect (Established in vitro) [[Roberge1993]].
- At very low, prophylactic-range concentrations (IC50 about 3 nM) colchicine changed the distribution, not the number, of E-selectin on endothelial cells and abolished their extra stickiness for neutrophils; at higher, therapeutic-range concentrations (IC50 about 300 nM) neutrophils lost L-selectin [[Cronstein1995]].
- In a mouse MSU peritonitis model, oral colchicine blocked crystal-induced neutrophil superoxide production at doses 100 times lower than those needed to block neutrophil infiltration, without acute liver or kidney toxicity; the authors offer this as a rationale for low-dose regimens (Early evidence) [[Chia2008]].

### flarerx · Natural course and why early treatment is advised
- The ACR/EULAR classification criteria define a "typical symptomatic episode" by at least 2 of: time to maximal pain < 24 h, resolution of symptoms in ≤ 14 days, and complete resolution between episodes, irrespective of treatment [[Neogi2015]].
- In 11 volunteers with untreated podagra, 2 withdrew on day 4 because of severe persistent pain; all 9 who stayed had some improvement in pain by day 5 and in swelling by day 7, yet only 3 reported their pain had resolved within the 7-day study [[Bellamy1987]].
- In the first placebo-controlled colchicine trial, two-thirds of colchicine-treated patients improved at 48 h vs one-third on placebo, with separation from placebo after 18–30 h [[Ahern1987]].
- ACR 2012 advised starting treatment preferably within 24 h of flare onset (Evidence C, consensus that early treatment gives better patient-reported outcomes) and grouped flares as early (< 12 h), well established (12–36 h) and late (> 36 h) [[Khanna2012P2]].
- EULAR 2016 recommends colchicine within 12 h of flare onset and advises educating fully informed patients to self-medicate at the first warning symptoms [[Richette2017]]; the Hong Kong 2023 consensus restates "as early as possible, preferably within 12 h" (level 1B) [[Yip2023]]. ACR 2020 notes efficacy "particularly when administered early" and records the Patient Panel's preference for a "medication-in-pocket" strategy [[FitzGerald2020]].
- No randomised trial has compared the same drug started at different times after onset; the time windows rest on trial entry criteria (AGREE enrolled within 12 h) and consensus [[Terkeltaub2010,Khanna2012P2,Richette2017]].

### flarerx · Colchicine: low dose vs high dose (AGREE)
- AGREE randomised 184 people (intention to treat) within 12 h of flare onset to low-dose colchicine (1.2 mg then 0.6 mg 1 h later, 1.8 mg total), high-dose colchicine (1.2 mg then 0.6 mg hourly for 6 h, 4.8 mg total) or placebo [[Terkeltaub2010,FDA-Colcrys]].
- Primary end point (≥ 50% pain reduction at 24 h without rescue medication): 28/74 (37.8%) low dose, 17/52 (32.7%) high dose, 9/58 (15.5%) placebo (P = 0.005 and P = 0.034 vs placebo) [[Terkeltaub2010]]. The label gives differences from placebo of 22 (95% CI 8 to 37) and 17 (1 to 33) percentage points [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Rescue medication within 24 h: 31.1% low dose, 34.6% high dose, 50.0% placebo [[Terkeltaub2010]].
- Diarrhoea: 76.9% high dose (severe in 19.2%), 23.0% low dose (none severe), vs 8/59 (14%) on placebo; vomiting 17.3% high dose vs 0% low dose and placebo [[Terkeltaub2010,FDA-Colcrys]]. Any gastrointestinal adverse reaction: 77% high dose, 26% low dose, 20% placebo [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Low dose had an adverse-event profile similar to placebo (OR 1.5, 95% CI 0.7–3.2) [[Terkeltaub2010]].
- In the 1987 high-dose trial, every colchicine-treated patient developed diarrhoea, after a median of 24 h (mean dose 6.7 mg), usually before pain relief [[Ahern1987]].
- Cochrane 2021 (4 trials, 803 people): low-dose colchicine vs placebo gave treatment success at 32–36 h in 418 vs 172 per 1000 (RR 2.43, 95% CI 1.05–5.64; low-quality evidence), with total adverse events 364 vs 276 per 1000 (RR 1.32, 0.68–2.56). High-dose vs placebo: 518 vs 240 per 1000 success (RR 2.16), but adverse events 829 vs 260 per 1000 (RR 3.21). No trial compared colchicine with glucocorticoids [[McKenzie2021]].
- Cochrane 2014: high dose vs low dose gave no extra benefit (19/52 vs 31/74 success; RR 0.87, 0.56–1.36) but more adverse events (40/52 vs 19/74; RR 3.00, 1.98–4.54; number needed to harm 2) [[vanEchteld2014]].
- ACR 2020 strongly recommends low-dose over high-dose colchicine when colchicine is chosen (high certainty), and to follow the FDA dosing of 1.2 mg then 0.6 mg 1 h later, with ongoing anti-inflammatory therapy until the flare resolves [[FitzGerald2020]].

### flarerx · Colchicine vs naproxen (CONTACT)
- CONTACT (100 UK general practices, 399 adults, open label) compared naproxen 750 mg then 250 mg every 8 h for 7 days with colchicine 0.5 mg three times daily for 4 days [[Roddy2020]].
- Baseline worst pain was 7.1 (naproxen) and 6.9 (colchicine) on a 0–10 scale; over days 1–7 the mean difference in pain change was −0.18 (95% CI −0.53 to 0.17; P = 0.32), with a small difference favouring naproxen on day 2 only [[Roddy2020]].
- Complete pain resolution by day 7: 67.3% naproxen vs 67.1% colchicine; median time to resolution 5 vs 6 days [[Roddy2020]].
- Days 1–7 side effects: diarrhoea 45.9% colchicine vs 20.0% naproxen (OR 3.31), headache 20.5% vs 10.7%, constipation 4.8% vs 19.3% [[Roddy2020]]. The authors concluded naproxen should be first-line in primary care when there are no contraindications [[Roddy2020]].

### flarerx · NSAIDs
- Cochrane 2021 (28 trials, 3406 people): one placebo-controlled trial (30 people, tenoxicam) found ≥ 50% pain reduction at 24 h in 11/15 on NSAID vs 4/15 on placebo (RR 2.7, 95% CI 1.1–6.7; low certainty) [[vanDurme2021]].
- Non-selective NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors gave similar pain relief (moderate certainty), but non-selective NSAIDs increased withdrawals for adverse events (RR 2.3) and total, mainly gastrointestinal, adverse events (RR 1.9) [[vanDurme2021]]. The 2014 version put total adverse events at 38% with COX-2 inhibitors vs 60% with NSAIDs [[vanDurme2014]].
- NSAIDs vs systemic glucocorticoids (5 trials, 712 people): little or no difference in pain, function or treatment success; total adverse events were higher with NSAIDs (RR 1.6, 95% CI 1.0–2.5) [[vanDurme2021]].
- ACR 2012 recommended full anti-inflammatory doses; FDA-approved NSAIDs for gout were naproxen, indomethacin and sulindac, while other NSAIDs at analgesic doses "may be as effective" [[Khanna2012P2]]. NICE suggests adding a proton pump inhibitor when an NSAID is used for a flare [[NICE-NG219]].

### flarerx · Glucocorticoids and ACTH
- Prednisolone 35 mg once daily vs naproxen 500 mg twice daily for 5 days (120 people with crystal-proven monoarticular gout, primary care): pain fell by 44.7 mm and 46.0 mm on a 100 mm scale after 90 h (difference 1.3 mm, 95% CI −9.8 to 7.1), meeting the equivalence margin; adverse effects were minor and similar [[Janssens2008]].
- Oral prednisolone vs indomethacin in 416 emergency-department patients (Hong Kong): equivalent pain reductions of about 10 mm at rest and 20 mm on activity in the ED, and about 25 mm and 45 mm over days 1–14; minor adverse events in the ED 19% indomethacin vs 6% prednisolone (P < 0.001); 37% in each group over days 1–14 [[Rainer2016]].
- Meta-analysis of 6 RCTs (817 people): no difference in pain at < 7 days (SMD −0.09, 95% CI −0.26 to 0.08); corticosteroids caused less indigestion (RR 0.50), nausea (RR 0.25) and vomiting (RR 0.11) than NSAIDs [[Billy2018]].
- Intramuscular triamcinolone acetonide 60 mg vs indomethacin 50 mg three times daily: all symptoms resolved at an average of 7 vs 8 days (27 people) [[Alloway1993]]. IM ACTH 40 IU vs IM triamcinolone 60 mg: resolution at an average of 8 days in both, but 11 vs 5 reinjections and 2 ACTH rebound flares (31 people) [[Siegel1994]]. In 38 hospitalised people, single IM ACTH and IM betamethasone gave similar pain falls at 24 h (4.48 vs 4.67 points) and 48 h [[Daoussis2022]].
- ACR 2012 regimens: oral prednisone or prednisolone at least 0.5 mg/kg/day for 5–10 days then stop (Evidence A), or 2–5 days at full dose then taper over 7–10 days (Evidence C); intra-articular steroid for 1–2 large joints (Evidence B); IM triamcinolone 60 mg as an alternative; for people who cannot take anything by mouth, IV or IM methylprednisolone 0.5–2.0 mg/kg (Evidence B) or subcutaneous ACTH 25–40 IU (Evidence A) [[Khanna2012P2]].
- ACR 2020 strongly recommends glucocorticoids (IM, IV or intra-articular) over IL-1 inhibitors or ACTH for people who cannot take oral medication (high certainty) [[FitzGerald2020]]. EULAR 2016 lists oral corticosteroid or joint aspiration with intra-articular corticosteroid as first-line options [[Richette2017]]; its quoted dose of 30–35 mg/day prednisolone equivalent for 3–5 days could not be checked against the full text (unverified).

### flarerx · IL-1 blockade
- Canakinumab phase II (200 people for whom NSAIDs and/or colchicine were unsuitable): 150 mg subcutaneously reduced pain more than IM triamcinolone 40 mg at 24, 48 and 72 h (differences −11.5, −18.2 and −19.2 mm); relative risk reduction for recurrent flares 94%; adverse events 41% vs 42% [[So2010Cana]].
- β-RELIEVED and β-RELIEVED-II (230 people on canakinumab, 226 on triamcinolone; frequent flares; 82.6% had comorbidities): 72-h pain 25.0 mm with canakinumab 150 mg vs 35.7 mm with triamcinolone 40 mg (difference −10.7 mm, 95% CI −15.4 to −6.0); new-flare risk cut by 62% at 12 weeks (HR 0.38) and 56% over 24 weeks (HR 0.44); adverse events 66.2% vs 52.8% and serious adverse events 8.0% vs 3.5%, with more infections and low neutrophil and platelet counts on canakinumab [[Schlesinger2012]].
- The EMA public summary of the same trials gives pain falling from 74 to 25 (canakinumab) vs 74 to 35 (triamcinolone) on a 0–100 scale, and new flares in 17% vs 37% [[EMA-IlarisGout2013]].
- EU: indication added by variation II/0010 (CHMP opinion 17 Jan 2013, Commission decision 18 Feb 2013) for adults with frequent flares (≥ 3 in the previous 12 months) in whom NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated, not tolerated or inadequate, and repeated corticosteroid courses are not appropriate; a single on-demand injection, given by a healthcare professional [[EMA-IlarisGout2013]].
- US: approved 25 Aug 2023 (BLA 125319, supplement 107) for gout flares in adults with the same NSAID, colchicine and corticosteroid conditions; dose 150 mg subcutaneously, with at least 12 weeks before re-treatment; avoid during an active infection requiring medical intervention [[FDA-IlarisGout2023,IlarisPIG]]. In the label's trials over 85% had at least one comorbidity and 25% had CKD stage ≥ 3 [[IlarisPIG]].
- Anakinra is not FDA-approved for gout; its label indications are rheumatoid arthritis, NOMID (CAPS) and DIRA [[KineretPI]]. Open pilot: 10 of 10 people unable to take standard therapy responded rapidly to 100 mg daily for 3 days [[So2007]].
- anaGO (88 people, crystal-proven flare): anakinra was non-inferior to treatment as usual (colchicine, naproxen or prednisone) for pain change on days 2–4 (ITT mean difference −0.18, 95% CI −0.44 to 0.08, on a 5-point scale) [[Janssen2019]].
- Phase II in 165 people for whom NSAIDs and colchicine were unsuitable: mean pain decline from baseline to 24–72 h was −41.2 with anakinra (100 or 200 mg/day for 5 days) vs −39.4 with triamcinolone 40 mg (P = 0.688), so anakinra was not superior on the primary end point but was favoured on most secondary end points [[Saag2021]].
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends IL-1 inhibition (moderate certainty) when other anti-inflammatory therapies are ineffective, poorly tolerated or contraindicated, and notes concern that the trial comparator (triamcinolone 40 mg) was weak and cost favours other agents [[FitzGerald2020]]. EULAR 2016 adds that current infection is a contraindication to IL-1 blockers [[Richette2017]]. NICE advises against IL-1 inhibitors unless NSAIDs, colchicine and corticosteroids are contraindicated, not tolerated or ineffective [[NICE-NG219]].

### flarerx · Ice and other measures
- 19 people on prednisone plus colchicine: adding topical ice gave a mean pain reduction of 7.75 cm vs 4.42 cm without ice on a 10 cm scale over 1 week (P = 0.021) [[Schlesinger2002]].
- ACR 2020 conditionally recommends topical ice as an add-on (low certainty) [[FitzGerald2020]]; ACR 2012 rated it Evidence B and voted cherry juice, willow bark, ginger and other oral complementary agents inappropriate for treating a flare [[Khanna2012P2]]; NICE advises ice packs alongside medicine [[NICE-NG219]].

### flarerx · Guideline summary and keeping urate-lowering therapy going
- ACR 2020: colchicine, NSAIDs or glucocorticoids (oral, intra-articular or intramuscular) are strongly recommended as first-line over IL-1 inhibitors or ACTH (high certainty), with the choice driven by patient factors; the network meta-analysis showed similar efficacy, so no first-line agent is ranked above another [[FitzGerald2020]].
- NICE 2022: offer an NSAID, colchicine or a short course of oral corticosteroid first-line; consider an intra-articular or intramuscular corticosteroid if NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated, not tolerated or ineffective [[NICE-NG219]].
- ACR 2012 judged combination therapy appropriate for severe or polyarticular flares (Evidence C) and defined inadequate response as < 20% pain improvement within 24 h or < 50% at ≥ 24 h [[Khanna2012P2]]. The Hong Kong consensus does not recommend combining an NSAID with a systemic glucocorticoid because of additive toxicity [[Yip2023]].
- ACR 2012: continue established urate-lowering therapy without interruption during a flare (Evidence C) [[Khanna2012P2]]. ACR 2020 conditionally recommends that ULT may be started during a flare when it is indicated (moderate certainty) [[FitzGerald2020]], whereas NICE advises starting ULT 2–4 weeks after a flare settles, or during a flare if flares are frequent [[NICE-NG219]].

### flarerx · Choosing by comorbidity
- ACR 2012 examples of comorbidity-driven limits: moderate or severe CKD (NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors, colchicine), congestive heart failure (NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors), peptic ulcer disease (NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors, corticosteroids), anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy (NSAIDs), diabetes (corticosteroids), ongoing infection or high infection risk (corticosteroids), and hepatic disease (NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors, colchicine) [[Khanna2012P2]].
- EULAR 2016: avoid colchicine and NSAIDs in severe renal impairment, and do not give colchicine with strong P-glycoprotein and/or CYP3A4 inhibitors such as cyclosporin or clarithromycin [[Richette2017]].
- In 575 people with gout, hypertension prevalence was 0.89 and more than 90% had at least 1 contraindication to NSAIDs; colchicine was prescribed despite at least 1 strong contraindication in 30–39.6% depending on diagnostic certainty [[Keenan2011]].
- In the canakinumab registration trials, over 85% had at least one comorbidity (hypertension 60%, obesity 53%, diabetes 15%, ischaemic heart disease 12%) [[IlarisPIG]].

### coldose · Labelled gout doses
- Colcrys flare dose: 1.2 mg at the first sign of a flare, then 0.6 mg 1 h later; maximum 1.8 mg over 1 h; "higher doses have not been found to be more effective" [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Colcrys prophylaxis: 0.6 mg once or twice daily (adults and adolescents over 16), maximum 1.2 mg/day; prophylaxis "may be beneficial for at least the first six months" of urate-lowering therapy [[FDA-Colcrys]]. Flare during prophylaxis: up to 1.2 mg then 0.6 mg 1 h later, wait 12 h, then resume the prophylactic dose [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Repeat flare courses: the label states the safety and efficacy of repeat treatment for gout flares have not been evaluated. A "no earlier than 3 days" interval appears only in the interaction dose table; a "no more than once every two weeks" limit applies in severe renal or severe hepatic impairment and on dialysis [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Mitigare (0.6 mg capsules) is indicated only for prophylaxis of gout flares in adults, 0.6 mg once or twice daily, maximum 1.2 mg/day; its safety and effectiveness for treating flares during prophylaxis have not been studied [[FDA-Mitigare]].
- ACR 2012 (US tablets): after 1.2 + 0.6 mg, continue 0.6 mg once or twice daily starting 12 h later until the flare resolves; where 1 mg and 0.5 mg tablets are used, 1 mg then 0.5 mg 1 h later, then after 12 h up to 0.5 mg three times daily; colchicine only if onset was ≤ 36 h earlier (Evidence C) [[Khanna2012P2]].
- EULAR 2016 regimen: 1 mg then 0.5 mg 1 h later on day 1, within 12 h of onset; prophylaxis colchicine 0.5–1 mg/day during the first 6 months of ULT, reduced in renal impairment [[Richette2017]]. Hong Kong 2023: prophylaxis 0.5 mg once or twice daily for 3–6 months [[Yip2023]].
- ACR 2020: prophylaxis (colchicine, NSAIDs, prednisone or prednisolone) when starting ULT is strongly recommended, for 3–6 months rather than < 3 months, continued if flares persist (moderate certainty) [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Prophylaxis RCT: colchicine 0.6 mg twice daily vs placebo while starting allopurinol (43 people): total flares 0.52 vs 2.91 (P = 0.008), flares in months 3–6 0 vs 1.05, and lower flare severity (VAS 3.64 vs 5.08) [[Borstad2004]].
- Approvals: Colcrys NDAs 022351, 022352 and 022353 were approved between 29 Jul and 16 Oct 2009; Mitigare NDA 204820 on 26 Sep 2014 [[FDA-ColcrysApproval,FDA-Mitigare]]. The Colcrys label lists initial US approval as 1961 [[FDA-Colcrys]].

### coldose · Kidneys and liver
- Prophylaxis: no dose change for CrCl 50–80 or 30–50 mL/min (monitor closely); severe impairment, start 0.3 mg/day; dialysis, start 0.3 mg twice a week [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Flare treatment: no dose change in mild to moderate impairment; in severe impairment the same dose but no more than one course every 2 weeks; on dialysis a single 0.6 mg dose, no more than once every 2 weeks; flare treatment with Colcrys is not recommended in people with renal impairment who already take it for prophylaxis [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Hepatic: no change in mild to moderate impairment (monitor); in severe impairment consider a lower prophylaxis dose, and repeat flare courses no more than once every 2 weeks [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- In people with FMF and end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis, colchicine clearance was 75% lower (0.17 vs 0.73 L/h/kg) and half-life longer (18.8 vs 4.4 h) than with normal kidneys; dialysis does not remove colchicine [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Colchicine myopathy affects people with gout taking customary doses when reduced kidney function raises drug levels; it presents with proximal weakness and always a raised CK, and remits within 3–4 weeks of stopping [[Kuncl1987]].

### coldose · Interactions (CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein)
- Single 0.6 mg dose AUC increases in the label studies: ritonavir +296%, clarithromycin +282% (Cmax +227%), cyclosporine +259%, ketoconazole +212%, verapamil +103%, diltiazem +93%, azithromycin +57%; grapefruit juice −2% [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Label dose table (if no alternative; also within 14 days of stopping the inhibitor). Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g. clarithromycin, ketoconazole, itraconazole, ritonavir and most protease inhibitors): flare 0.6 mg then 0.3 mg 1 h later, not repeated within 3 days; prophylaxis 0.3 mg once daily (if 0.6 mg twice daily was intended) or 0.3 mg every other day (if 0.6 mg once daily) [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors (diltiazem, verapamil, erythromycin, fluconazole, aprepitant, amprenavir/fosamprenavir, grapefruit juice): flare 1.2 mg once, not repeated within 3 days; prophylaxis 0.3 mg twice daily or 0.6 mg once daily (from 0.6 mg twice daily) or 0.3 mg once daily (from 0.6 mg once daily) [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- P-gp inhibitors (cyclosporine, ranolazine): flare 0.6 mg once, not repeated within 3 days; prophylaxis 0.3 mg once daily or every other day [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Contraindication: no P-gp or strong CYP3A4 inhibitor with Colcrys in renal or hepatic impairment, because fatal toxicity has occurred at therapeutic doses [[FDA-Colcrys]]. The Mitigare label words this differently: no drugs that inhibit both P-gp and CYP3A4 in renal or hepatic impairment, and no Mitigare at all in combined renal and hepatic impairment [[FDA-Mitigare]].
- Pharmacokinetic studies support dose reductions of 33–66% for treating flares and 50–75% for prophylaxis with most of these inhibitors, and no adjustment with azithromycin [[Terkeltaub2011]].
- Of 88 inpatients given clarithromycin and colchicine together, 9 (10.2%) died vs 1 of 28 (3.6%) given them sequentially; longer overlap (RR 2.16), baseline renal impairment (RR 9.1) and pancytopenia (RR 23.4) predicted death [[Hung2005]].
- Statins, fibrates and cyclosporine can potentiate colchicine myopathy and rhabdomyolysis; older people and people with renal dysfunction are at higher risk [[FDA-Colcrys]].

### coldose · Toxicity and overdose
- Colchicine has a narrow therapeutic index with no clear line between non-toxic, toxic and lethal doses; the lowest reported lethal oral doses are 7–26 mg, and fatality is high after ingestions above 0.5 mg/kg [[Finkelstein2010]].
- In a review of 150 overdoses, people who took < 0.5 mg/kg survived with mostly gastrointestinal effects, 0.5–0.8 mg/kg caused severe reactions such as myelosuppression, and > 0.8 mg/kg was 100% fatal [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Myelosuppression, leukopenia, granulocytopenia, thrombocytopenia and aplastic anaemia have been reported; gastrointestinal symptoms are often the first sign of toxicity [[FDA-Colcrys,FDA-Mitigare]].
- Colchicine is approved for gout and FMF, and gout is its best-established rheumatic use [[Slobodnick2018]].

### Exam hooks
- Low-dose colchicine (1.2 mg + 0.6 mg at 1 h = 1.8 mg) works as well as 4.8 mg over 6 h (37.8% vs 32.7% responders vs 15.5% placebo) with far less diarrhoea (23% vs 77%) [[Terkeltaub2010]].
- Peak plasma level is nearly the same for both regimens (6.2 vs 6.8 ng/mL), which is why the extra dose adds toxicity, not benefit [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- First-line flare options: colchicine, NSAID or glucocorticoid, chosen by comorbidity; none is ranked above another [[FitzGerald2020,NICE-NG219]].
- Prednisolone 35 mg/day for 5 days is equivalent to naproxen 500 mg twice daily [[Janssens2008]]; prednisolone matches indomethacin with fewer early minor adverse events [[Rainer2016]].
- IL-1 blockade (canakinumab 150 mg SC, re-treatment ≥ 12 weeks apart) is reserved for people who cannot use NSAIDs and colchicine and for whom repeated steroid courses are not appropriate; avoid in active infection [[IlarisPIG,Richette2017]].
- Do not stop urate-lowering therapy during a flare [[Khanna2012P2]].
- Strong CYP3A4 or P-gp inhibitors with colchicine are contraindicated in renal or hepatic impairment; clarithromycin is the classic fatal interaction [[FDA-Colcrys,Hung2005]].
- On dialysis: flare dose a single 0.6 mg, no more than once every 2 weeks; prophylaxis 0.3 mg twice a week [[FDA-Colcrys]].
- Mitigare capsules are labelled for prophylaxis only; Colcrys tablets for prophylaxis and flare treatment [[FDA-Mitigare,FDA-Colcrys]].
- Ice is a conditionally recommended add-on [[FitzGerald2020,Schlesinger2002]].

### In plain words
- A flare usually hits its worst within a day, then slowly settles over one to two weeks, even without treatment.
- Medicine works best if you take it at the first sign of a flare. Many people keep a supply at home for this.
- Colchicine, an anti-inflammatory pill such as naproxen, or a short course of a steroid like prednisolone all work about equally well.
- Your doctor picks the one that is safest for your kidneys, heart, stomach and other medicines.
- With colchicine, the small dose (three tablets over one hour) works as well as the old big dose and causes much less diarrhoea.
- Colchicine builds up in white blood cells and slows them down, so fewer of them pour into the joint.
- Some medicines, such as the antibiotic clarithromycin, stop the body clearing colchicine. Tell every doctor and pharmacist that you take it.
- If you already take a urate-lowering medicine such as allopurinol, keep taking it during a flare.
- An ice pack on the joint can ease the pain on top of your medicine.
- If none of the usual medicines are safe for you, a specialist may offer an injection that blocks the inflammation signal IL-1.

## Open questions and controversies
- Low vs high colchicine dose: the first placebo-controlled trial gave 0.5 mg every 2 h until relief or toxicity [[vanEchteld2014]], and every colchicine-treated patient developed diarrhoea [[Ahern1987]]; AGREE and Cochrane show the low dose is as effective with an adverse-event rate close to placebo [[Terkeltaub2010,McKenzie2021]]. Status: settled; ACR 2020 makes it a strong recommendation [[FitzGerald2020]].
- Exact colchicine regimen across regions: US 1.2 + 0.6 mg then 0.6 mg once or twice daily from 12 h [[FDA-Colcrys,Khanna2012P2]]; EULAR 1 + 0.5 mg on day 1 [[Richette2017]]; CONTACT used 0.5 mg three times daily for 4 days without a loading dose [[Roddy2020]]. Status: no head-to-head trial of these schedules.
- NSAID or colchicine first in primary care: CONTACT found equal pain relief but more diarrhoea and headache with colchicine and favoured naproxen [[Roddy2020]]; ACR 2020 and NICE do not rank the options [[FitzGerald2020,NICE-NG219]]. Status: patient-specific.
- NSAID vs steroid in people with cardiovascular or kidney disease: trials show equal efficacy and fewer gastrointestinal effects with steroids [[Janssens2008,Rainer2016,Billy2018]]; guideline lists flag NSAIDs in CKD and heart failure and steroids in diabetes and infection [[Khanna2012P2]]. Status: trials excluded many high-risk people, so the choice rests on comorbidity lists, not head-to-head outcome data.
- How much does timing matter: EULAR says within 12 h, ACR 2012 within 24 h overall and ≤ 36 h for colchicine [[Richette2017,Khanna2012P2]]; no RCT has randomised start time. Status: consensus, not trial evidence.
- IL-1 blockers: canakinumab beat triamcinolone on pain and new flares [[Schlesinger2012]] and has EU (2013) and US (2023) approval [[EMA-IlarisGout2013,FDA-IlarisGout2023]], but ACR 2020 flags the low-dose triamcinolone comparator and cost [[FitzGerald2020]]; anakinra matched usual care and triamcinolone but is off-label [[Janssen2019,Saag2021,KineretPI]]. Status: second line.
- Starting ULT during a flare: ACR 2020 conditionally yes [[FitzGerald2020]]; NICE prefers 2–4 weeks after the flare settles unless flares are frequent [[NICE-NG219]]. Status: guidelines differ (pass E covers the trials).
- Grapefruit juice: the Colcrys label lists it as a moderate CYP3A4 inhibitor in the dose table, yet its own study found colchicine AUC changed by −2% [[FDA-Colcrys]]. Status: unresolved within the label.
- Interaction wording differs between products: Colcrys contraindicates P-gp or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors in renal or hepatic impairment; Mitigare contraindicates dual P-gp and CYP3A4 inhibitors and use in combined renal and hepatic impairment [[FDA-Colcrys,FDA-Mitigare]].
