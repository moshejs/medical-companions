Treating a flare

Colchicine, an NSAID or a steroid all treat a gout flare about equally well, so the choice turns on the person's other conditions. Slide the start time to see why guidelines say start early, then switch on conditions and watch which options get flagged.

Schematic. No trial has randomised people to different start times, so these curves only illustrate the published reasoning: a flare peaks within a day and settles within two weeks even untreated, and guidelines advise treating early (within 12 h in EULAR 2016, within 24 h in ACR 2012, colchicine only within 36 h). What the trials measured

Trial

Choosing by the person's other conditions

ACR 2020 strongly recommends colchicine, an NSAID or a glucocorticoid (oral, intra-articular or intramuscular) as first line, chosen by patient factors; no first-line agent is ranked above another. NICE 2022 says the same. [[FitzGerald2020,NICE-NG219]]

Switch on a condition to see which options the published guidance flags. This mirrors guideline lists; it is not a prescribing tool.

Flags follow ACR 2012's comorbidity examples, EULAR 2016, the COLCRYS label and the canakinumab label. In 575 people with gout, more than 90% had at least one contraindication to NSAIDs, and colchicine was prescribed despite a strong contraindication in 30–39.6%. [[Khanna2012P2,Richette2017,FDA-Colcrys,IlarisPIG,Keenan2011]]

Keep urate-lowering therapy going

ACR 2012: do not interrupt established urate-lowering therapy during a flare (Evidence C). Starting it is a separate question: ACR 2020 conditionally allows starting during a flare when it is indicated, while NICE prefers 2–4 weeks after the flare settles unless flares are frequent. [[Khanna2012P2,FitzGerald2020,NICE-NG219]]

When one drug is not enough

ACR 2012 judged combination therapy appropriate for severe or polyarticular flares (Evidence C), and defined an inadequate response as under 20% pain improvement within 24 hours or under 50% at 24 hours or later. The Hong Kong 2023 consensus does not recommend combining an NSAID with a systemic glucocorticoid because of additive toxicity. [[Khanna2012P2,Yip2023]]

NSAID vs steroid, pooled

Across 6 trials (817 people), pain at under 7 days did not differ, but corticosteroids caused less indigestion (RR 0.50), nausea (RR 0.25) and vomiting (RR 0.11). A Cochrane review of 5 trials (712 people) found total adverse events higher with NSAIDs (RR 1.6). [[Billy2018,vanDurme2021]]

Established

IL-1 is the flare's key signal

In an open pilot, all 10 people unable to take standard therapy responded to anakinra 100 mg daily for 3 days. In anaGO (88 people), anakinra was non-inferior to usual care; in a phase II trial (165 people) it was not superior to triamcinolone 40 mg on the primary end point (−41.2 vs −39.4). [[So2007,Janssen2019,Saag2021]]

Debated

Was the canakinumab comparator fair?

ACR 2020 makes IL-1 inhibition a conditional recommendation (moderate certainty) and notes that the comparator, triamcinolone 40 mg, was weak and that cost favours other agents. Canakinumab carries EU (2013) and US (2023) approval for selected people with gout. [[FitzGerald2020,EMA-IlarisGout2013,FDA-IlarisGout2023]]

Ice, and what does not help

ACR 2020 conditionally recommends topical ice as an add-on, and NICE advises ice packs alongside medicine. ACR 2012 voted cherry juice, willow bark, ginger and other oral complementary agents inappropriate for treating a flare. [[FitzGerald2020,NICE-NG219,Khanna2012P2]]

In plain wordsSeveral medicines work about equally well. The best one for you depends on your kidneys, heart, stomach and other medicines. Take it at the first sign of a flare, and keep taking any urate-lowering medicine you already use.
