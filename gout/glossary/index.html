Glossary

A searchable glossary of gout terms, from allantoin and ABCG2 to tophus and xanthine oxidase. Type to filter; the arrow on each entry jumps to the module where it matters most.

88 of 88 terms

ABCG2A high-capacity urate exporter in the kidney's proximal tubule, the gut wall and the liver. Common variants that weaken it (such as Q141K) raise urate and gout risk.
ACR/EULAR 2015 criteriaGout classification criteria: an entry criterion, MSU crystals as a sufficient criterion, otherwise a score of ≥8 of 23 points across clinical, laboratory and imaging domains.
AdherenceTaking a medicine as prescribed. Often measured as taking ≥80% of doses; low for urate-lowering therapy.
AGREE trialThe randomised trial showing low-dose colchicine (1.8 mg over 1 hour) works as well as high-dose (4.8 mg over 6 hours) with far less diarrhoea.
AllantoinThe product of urate breakdown by uricase; about 100 times more water-soluble than uric acid.
AllopurinolA purine-analogue xanthine oxidase inhibitor; first-line urate-lowering therapy, started low and titrated to target.
Allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome (AHS)A rare, severe reaction to allopurinol with rash, fever and organ involvement; about 90% begin within 60 days.
AnakinraAn IL-1 receptor antagonist injected daily; used off-label for flares when standard treatments are unsuitable.
Anti-drug antibodiesAntibodies the body makes against a biologic drug, such as pegloticase; they speed clearance and cause loss of response and infusion reactions.
Asymptomatic hyperuricaemiaHigh serum urate with no flare or tophus. ACR 2020 advises against urate-lowering drugs for it.
BenzbromaroneA potent uricosuric that blocks URAT1; withdrawn by its original maker in 2003 over liver toxicity, still marketed in some countries.
CanakinumabA monoclonal antibody against IL-1β; given as a single 150 mg injection for flares when other treatments are unsuitable.
CARES trialA trial of febuxostat vs allopurinol in people with gout and cardiovascular disease; MACE was non-inferior but CV death was higher on febuxostat.
Caspase-1The enzyme activated by the inflammasome that cuts pro-IL-1β into its active form.
ChondrocalcinosisCalcification of cartilage seen on imaging; a hallmark of CPP crystal deposition.
Chronic gouty arthritisPersistent joint inflammation caused by MSU crystals. G-CAN advises against the vaguer term "chronic gout".
CKDChronic kidney disease: lasting loss of kidney function, graded in stages 1–5 by eGFR. It raises urate and travels with gout.
Clinical remissionFor gout (G-CAN): 12 months with no flares, no subcutaneous tophi and serum urate <6 mg/dL.
ColchicineAn anti-inflammatory drug from the autumn crocus that blocks microtubule assembly; used to treat and prevent flares.
Colchicine myopathyMuscle weakness with a raised CK from colchicine build-up, usually when kidney function is reduced or with interacting drugs.
Colchicum autumnaleThe autumn crocus, the plant source of colchicine.
Compensated polarised light microscopyMicroscopy with a red retardation plate that shows crystal birefringence and colour; the reference test for gout.
CPP crystalsCalcium pyrophosphate crystals: rhomboid or rod-like and weakly positively birefringent; they cause CPPD.
CPPDCalcium pyrophosphate deposition disease, which can cause acute arthritis (formerly pseudogout), often in the knee or wrist of older people.
Crowned dens syndromeCPP deposition around the top of the spine (the dens) causing acute neck pain; a sufficient criterion for CPPD.
CYP3A4A liver and gut enzyme that breaks down colchicine and many other drugs; strong inhibitors raise colchicine levels sharply.
DASH dietDietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension: fruit, vegetables, low-fat dairy, less red meat and sugar. Linked with lower urate and less gout.
DECTDual-energy CT, which colour-codes urate deposits by their X-ray behaviour at two energies; can miss very early gout.
DotinuradA selective URAT1 inhibitor approved in Japan in 2020 and in trials elsewhere.
Double-contour signOn ultrasound, a bright irregular line over the cartilage surface from urate crystals, independent of probe angle.
FAST trialA European trial of febuxostat vs allopurinol in people aged 60 or more with CV risk; febuxostat was non-inferior with no excess deaths.
FebuxostatA non-purine selective xanthine oxidase inhibitor; in the US now used after allopurinol fails or is unsuitable.
FEUAFractional excretion of urate: the share of filtered urate that ends up in urine, normally 5–15%.
G-CANThe Gout, Hyperuricemia and Crystal-Associated Disease Network, which sets shared definitions such as flare and remission.
G6PD deficiencyAn inherited red-cell enzyme deficiency; pegloticase can cause haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia in it, so screening comes first.
Gasdermin DA protein cut by caspase-1 whose fragment forms membrane pores, letting IL-1β out and driving pyroptosis.
GlucocorticoidA steroid medicine such as prednisolone; given by mouth, into a joint or by injection to treat flares.
GLUT9 (SLC2A9)A urate transporter that carries reabsorbed urate out of kidney tubule cells into the blood; common variants affect serum urate.
GoutA chronic disease of monosodium urate crystal deposition that shows itself as flares, tophi and joint damage.
Gout flareA clinically evident episode of acute inflammation induced by MSU crystals.
Gout Impact ScaleA gout-specific questionnaire with five 0–100 scales (higher is worse) for patient-reported impact.
HAQ-DIHealth Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index, a measure of physical function used in gout studies.
High-fructose corn syrupA sweetener in many soft drinks; fructose raises serum urate within hours.
HLA-B*58:01A tissue-type gene variant that strongly predicts severe allergic reactions to allopurinol; commoner in some Asian and African populations.
HPRT deficiencyLoss of the purine-recycling enzyme hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase; causes urate overproduction, and in its severe form Lesch–Nyhan syndrome.
HyperuricaemiaSerum urate above the saturation point (about 6.8 mg/dL [405 µmol/L]); necessary for gout but not enough on its own.
IL-1βInterleukin-1 beta, the main inflammatory signal of a gout flare, released after inflammasome activation.
InflammasomeA protein complex inside immune cells that senses danger and activates caspase-1; NLRP3 is the one triggered by urate crystals.
Initiation flareA flare in the first months of urate-lowering therapy, as falling urate mobilises crystals; prevented by prophylaxis.
Intercritical goutThe symptom-free period between gout flares; crystals are often still present in the joint.
Janssens ruleA primary-care score (also called the Nijmegen rule) using seven items to estimate the chance that a monoarthritis is gout.
LesinuradA URAT1 inhibitor approved in 2015 for use with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor; later withdrawn from the market.
LosartanAn angiotensin-receptor blocker that also increases urate excretion; preferred for blood pressure in gout when feasible.
MACEMajor adverse cardiovascular events: a combined trial end point that usually includes CV death, MI and stroke.
Medication-in-pocketKeeping flare treatment at home to take at the first warning signs, as the ACR 2020 Patient Panel preferred.
Mendelian randomisationA genetic method that uses inherited variants to test whether a risk factor causes an outcome; it does not support urate causing CKD or heart disease.
Metabolic syndromeA cluster of central obesity, high blood pressure, high glucose and abnormal lipids; present in 62.8% of US adults with gout in one survey.
Monosodium urate (MSU)The salt urate forms with sodium; it crystallises as the needle-shaped crystals of gout.
NephrolithiasisKidney stones. Gout raises the risk of both uric acid and calcium stones.
NETsNeutrophil extracellular traps: webs of DNA and proteins released by neutrophils; when aggregated they degrade cytokines and help end a flare.
NeutrophilThe white blood cell that floods a gouty joint; colchicine dampens its migration.
NLRP3The inflammasome sensor (also called NALP3) activated by urate crystals.
NSAIDNon-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, such as naproxen; first-line for flares when no contraindication applies.
NucleationThe first step of crystal formation, when dissolved urate begins to form a solid seed.
Nurse-led careCare in which trained nurses educate, share decisions and titrate urate-lowering therapy to target.
OxypurinolAllopurinol's active metabolite (also oxipurinol); long-lived and cleared by the kidney.
P-glycoprotein (P-gp)A pump that pushes drugs such as colchicine out of cells; drugs that block it raise colchicine levels.
PegloticaseA PEGylated recombinant uricase given by IV infusion; converts urate to allantoin in refractory gout.
PodagraA gout flare at the first metatarsophalangeal (big-toe) joint.
ProbenecidAn older uricosuric drug that increases urate excretion in the urine.
ProphylaxisLow-dose anti-inflammatory treatment (colchicine, an NSAID or prednisone) given to prevent flares when starting urate-lowering therapy.
PurinesBuilding blocks of DNA, RNA and energy molecules, from cell turnover and food; their breakdown makes urate.
SaturationThe concentration above which urate can no longer stay dissolved: about 6.8 mg/dL (405 µmol/L) in plasma at 37 °C.
Septic arthritisA bacterial joint infection that can mimic, or coexist with, a gout flare; joint aspiration and culture are key.
SGLT2 inhibitorsDiabetes and heart-failure drugs that increase urinary urate loss; linked with lower urate and fewer gout flares.
SJS/TENStevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis, severe skin reactions; allopurinol is a leading cause.
Synovial fluidThe fluid inside a joint; aspirated to look for crystals, count white cells and culture for infection.
Thiazide diureticA water pill such as hydrochlorothiazide that raises urate; ACR 2020 suggests switching it when feasible.
Thread testGarrod's 1848 test in which uric acid crystallised on a thread in acidified serum; called the first clinical chemistry test.
TophusA deposit of MSU crystals with surrounding inflammatory tissue, felt as a firm, chalky lump under the skin or in bone.
Treat to targetRaising the urate-lowering dose step by step, with blood tests, until serum urate is below the target.
TubulinThe protein building block of microtubules; colchicine binds it and stops microtubule assembly.
URAT1 (SLC22A12)The main apical transporter that reabsorbs urate from the kidney tubule; the target of uricosurics.
Urate-lowering therapy (ULT)Long-term medicine that lowers serum urate so crystals dissolve: xanthine oxidase inhibitors, uricosurics or uricase.
UricaseUrate oxidase, the enzyme most mammals use to break urate down to allantoin; humans lack a working copy.
UricosuricA drug that increases urate excretion in the urine, usually by blocking URAT1.
Xanthine oxidase inhibitorA drug, such as allopurinol or febuxostat, that blocks urate production.
Xanthine oxidoreductaseThe enzyme that converts hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid; xanthine oxidase is one of its forms.
