The routes, and how strong the evidence is

Each finding below carries a label. Established marks a firm finding: a peer-reviewed trial result, a large cohort, a guideline, or a settled fact such as a trial missing its endpoint or a regulator's decision. Early evidence marks small, early-phase, uncontrolled or not yet peer-reviewed efficacy results. Hypothesis marks laboratory, animal or single-case work. Rejected marks an idea that later trials did not support. Findings that rest on a company press release say so. Failed trials are shown as plainly as successful ones.

One drug, two regulators

Regulators in the United States and the European Union reached different decisions on tolebrutinib for non-relapsing secondary progressive MS. Both statements below are given as the sources word them.

United States · FDA

Complete response letter

The FDA issued a complete response letter for tolebrutinib in non-relapsing SPMS (Sanofi release, 24 December 2025), calling it a change from earlier agency feedback. The drug is not approved in the US. [[Sanofi2025CRL]]

Source: company press release. The FDA letter itself was not accessed.

European Union · EMA

Positive opinion, then authorisation

The EMA's CHMP gave a positive opinion on 23 April 2026 for tolebrutinib (Cenrifki) "for the treatment of adult patients with SPMS without relapses in the last 2 years". It cites a 31% reduction in 6-month confirmed disability progression and 38% fewer new or enlarging T2 lesions, and calls drug-induced liver injury the "most worrisome side effect". The EMA page lists the medicine as authorised on 19 June 2026, under additional monitoring, with mandatory liver testing. [[EMA2026Cenrifki]]

Source: EMA summary of opinion and EPAR page.

Why they may differ. HERCULES met its primary endpoint (HR 0.69) [[Fox2025]], but tolebrutinib showed no meaningful benefit in primary progressive MS (PERSEUS) [[Maxwell2026,Sanofi2025PERSEUS]], and liver injury (six Hy's Law cases, one death after a liver transplant) shapes the benefit-risk debate [[Maxwell2026]]. As of October 2026 the US and EU positions differ. This page reports those decisions; it is not advice about any person's treatment.

Where the experts disagree

Where this leaves us. Drugs that keep immune cells out of the brain are established. The other three jobs are not done: the International Progressive MS Alliance describes neurodegeneration and compartmentalised inflammation as "virtually untargeted" and funds experimental-medicine trials to speed things up [[Dangond2021]], and the National MS Society's refined Pathways to Cures roadmap calls for global data sharing and sustained, coordinated funding [[Bebo2024]]. Biomarkers such as serum neurofilament light, GFAP and paramagnetic rim lesions may make trials shorter, but none is yet validated as a trial endpoint [[Kapoor2020,Bagnato2024]].

In plain wordsNo treatment repairs MS damage yet. Today's drugs are good at stopping new waves of immune attack. Researchers are now testing ways to stop the slow, hidden inflammation inside the brain and to rebuild myelin, and some of the biggest trials have failed. Nothing on this page is advice about anyone's treatment.
