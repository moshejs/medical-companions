# H. Nursing care in multiple sclerosis
Scope: what drug labels, guidelines and the nursing and rehabilitation literature report about nursing care in MS, for nursing students. It covers the MS nurse role and the care team; assessment tools, how they are scored and what change is treated as meaningful; administering and monitoring disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) and high-dose methylprednisolone; topics the literature and labels identify for patient teaching; common misunderstandings; documentation; and care in advanced or inpatient MS. Everything is reported as what a source says, not as instructions. Drug details come from current US labels retrieved through openFDA on 2026-10-20. Tysabri itself is not indexed in openFDA, so natalizumab details come from the biosimilar TYRUKO label, which carries the same natalizumab class wording. NICE NG220 (last updated 26 Aug 2026) and CG148 were read directly on nice.org.uk. PubMed E-utilities returned HTTP 429 (rate-limited), so bibliographic details were confirmed through Europe PMC, which indexes PubMed records with their PMIDs, and through Crossref. Full texts of Fowler 2009 (bladder consensus) and Larson 2013 (MFIS) could not be retrieved; numbers from them are marked. The NICE pages and two UK nursing-workforce web documents have no PMID and are marked "(unverified)" with the reason. All shared keys used here (Kurtzke1983, Hauser2017, Polman2006, RaeGrant2018, Confavreux1998) were checked against Europe PMC and match the details in the format file. A specific nursing documentation standard for MS was not found. The documentation section is therefore built from the NICE comprehensive-review content list and from label monitoring requirements, and says so.

## Facts

### The MS nurse and the MS care team
- NICE NG220 recommends offering people with MS a single point of contact who knows MS services, and care through a coordinated multidisciplinary team. The team listed includes MS nurses, consultant neurologists, physiotherapists and occupational therapists with MS expertise, speech and language therapists, psychologists, dietitians, social care, continence specialists, specialist neuropharmacists or MS pharmacists, rehabilitation medicine consultants and the primary healthcare team [NICE2022].
- NICE NG220 recommends a face-to-face follow-up with an MS-experienced professional within 6 weeks of diagnosis, and a comprehensive review of all aspects of care at least once a year. People with MS are to be told they can ask for the review if it has not happened [NICE2022].
- An international review of the MS nurse role describes MS nurses helping patients understand the disease and treatment options, supporting treatment initiation and management, and encouraging adherence. It predicted that oral therapies would add therapy-specific monitoring to the role [Burke2011].
- A proposal for multidisciplinary "MS Care Units" puts MS neurologists and nurses at the core, alongside neuropsychologists, clinical psychologists, physiotherapists, occupational therapists and secretaries, working to formal protocols for starting and following DMTs [SoelbergSorensen2019].
- **Early evidence**: a UK workforce-modelling study of 163 MS specialist nurses found that 83.4% regularly worked unpaid overtime. Work most often left undone was psychological interventions, physical assessments, social and benefits support, and recommending or prescribing medicines. The authors judged the then-recommended caseload of 358 people with MS per full-time nurse to be too high [Punshon2021].
- The MS Trust's 2021 UK mapping survey counted 360 MS specialist nurses (298 whole-time equivalents). The mean caseload was 472 people with MS per whole-time nurse, against a sustainable figure of 315. It found that 79% of people with MS lived in areas above that figure, with an estimated shortfall of 149 whole-time nurses [MSTrust2021].
- **Early evidence**: a 2026 scoping review identified 12 studies (2015–2025) of nurse-led digital or telehealth interventions in MS. It reported associations with better self-management, adherence, self-efficacy, fatigue, sleep, balance and quality of life. These were mapped associations, not pooled effect estimates [Azzellino2026].
- MS Nurse PRO is a free, CME-accredited e-learning curriculum for MS nurses in Europe. It was started in 2012 by the European MS Platform with the International Organization of MS Nurses (IOMSN) and Rehabilitation in MS (RIMS) [EMSP_MSNursePRO].
- **Established** (as a gap): no randomised trial or systematic review with pooled outcomes isolating the effect of MS specialist nurses on hard outcomes (relapse, admissions, disability) was found in this search. The case for the role rests mainly on workforce surveys, service evaluations and descriptive reviews [Punshon2021; MSTrust2021; Azzellino2026].

### Disability and performance measures
- The Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) runs from 0 (normal) to 10 (death due to MS). Each step from 1 to 9 is split into half-points (1.0, 1.5 … 9.5). The lower range is defined by grades in 8 Functional Systems: pyramidal, cerebellar, brainstem, sensory, bowel and bladder, visual, cerebral, and other [Kurtzke1983].
- The EDSS is traditionally scored by trained neurologists using the standardised Neurostatus method. In a Swiss randomised crossover study of 100 people with MS, specially trained non-neurologist healthcare professionals agreed with trained neurologists at a concordance rate of 0.87 (95% CI 0.815–0.925) [Mallucci2025].
- In two phase 3 trials (10,789 EDSS assessments), 40.1% had internal inconsistencies at first entry. Electronic real-time checking reduced this to 22.1%, and the checking changed the final EDSS in 14.8% of assessments [DSouza2020].
- The Multiple Sclerosis Functional Composite (MSFC) combines leg function (Timed 25-Foot Walk), arm and hand function (9-Hole Peg Test) and cognition. Each component is converted to a z-score and the z-scores are averaged into one score [Fischer1999]. In OPERA II, the change in MSFC favoured ocrelizumab over interferon beta-1a (0.28 vs 0.17, P=0.004); in OPERA I the difference was not significant (0.21 vs 0.17, P=0.33) [Hauser2017].
- Timed 25-Foot Walk (T25FW): in one cohort, minimally affected patients often walked 25 feet in 3–5 seconds. Walk times of clinically stable people varied by less than 20% over 12 months or less. The authors proposed that an increase of more than 20% may indicate a significant change in gait [Kaufman2000].
- The MS Outcome Assessments Consortium review describes the T25FW as reliable and responsive. It notes that whether a 20% change is clinically meaningful across the whole disability spectrum still needs validation [Motl2017T25FW].
- 9-Hole Peg Test (9HPT): described as the gold-standard measure of manual dexterity in MS. A 20% change in score is the threshold commonly used for clinically meaningful worsening, a definition the authors say needs further validation at all disease stages [Feys2017].
- Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT): research supports a responder definition of change of about 4 points or 10% [Benedict2017]. The US National MS Society cognitive-care recommendations repeat the 4-point or 10% threshold, or a change of 0.5 SD [Kalb2018].

### Symptom and impact scales
- Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS): a unidimensional fatigue-severity scale, developed and validated in 25 people with MS, 29 with lupus and 20 healthy adults. Fatigue scores were largely independent of self-reported depressive symptoms [Krupp1989]. A mean FSS score of 4 or more is used as the criterion for substantial fatigue; 77% of an 82-person MS sample met it [Learmonth2013]. The common description of 9 items each rated 1–7 was not confirmed in the abstracts read (unverified).
- Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS): a multidimensional scale with physical, cognitive and psychosocial subscales. Six-month reliability was ICC 0.863, against 0.751 for the FSS [Learmonth2013]. A four-country study found internal consistency of 0.92 overall but only 0.65 for the psychosocial subscale, and advised caution in interpreting that subscale [Kos2005]. The widely quoted structure of 21 items and a 0–84 total comes from the full text of Larson 2013, which could not be retrieved (unverified) [Larson2013].
- MSIS-29 is a patient-reported impact scale with 20 physical and 9 psychological items. It was developed from 129 candidate items in a postal survey of 1,530 UK MS Society members. Item test-retest reliability was r=0.65–0.90 and Cronbach's alpha up to 0.91 [Hobart2001].
- Modified Ashworth Scale: in the original reliability study, two raters grading elbow-flexor spasticity in 30 patients agreed on 86.7% of ratings (Kendall's tau 0.847) [Bohannon1987]. The grade set 0, 1, 1+, 2, 3, 4 (with 1+ added to Ashworth's original scale) is standard teaching but was not confirmed in the abstract read (unverified).
- **Established**: NICE NG220 lists signs that should raise suspicion of spasticity: spasms, stiffness, pain or restriction with movement, or a change in mobility or upper-limb function. It recommends looking for aggravating factors such as pressure ulcers, bladder and bowel dysfunction, infection, poor posture or positioning, and pain. It notes that some people use their spasticity to stand, walk or transfer [NICE2022].

### Bladder, bowel and skin
- A UK consensus (neurologists, urologists, primary care, MS nurses and nursing groups) based routine bladder management on a simple algorithm: reagent-strip testing for urinary infection and measuring the post-micturition residual volume. This contrasts with other countries' guidelines, which recommend cystometry. Antimuscarinics, with clean intermittent self-catheterisation if needed, are the mainstay [Fowler2009].
- The post-void residual threshold of more than 100 mL for starting intermittent self-catheterisation is widely attributed to the UK consensus. It could not be confirmed from the full text in this search (unverified) [Fowler2009].
- NICE CG148 recommends measuring post-void residual volume by ultrasound, preferably with a portable scanner, possibly on several occasions. It also recommends monitoring residual volume after starting antimuscarinics or botulinum toxin A in people not using a catheter [NICE2012CG148].
- NICE CG148 says indwelling urethral catheters may carry higher risks of renal complications (stones, scarring) than other forms of bladder management such as intermittent self-catheterisation. It does not recommend routine antibiotic prophylaxis when long-term catheters are changed [NICE2012CG148].
- In 1,100 Italian MS outpatients assessed with the Neurogenic Bowel Dysfunction score, 14% had moderate or severe bowel dysfunction, rising to 26–32% among the most disabled. Female sex, impaired walking and bladder symptoms predicted severity [Bisecco2022].
- A nursing review describes neurogenic bowel dysfunction as one of the most frequent problems in MS, present from onset onwards. Its management ranges from diet and fluid intake and drugs for constipation or incontinence to biofeedback, abdominal massage, transanal irrigation and tibial nerve stimulation [Gulick2022].
- The Braden Scale for pressure-sore risk has 6 subscales: sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, friction and shear, and nutrition. At a cut-off of 16, sensitivity was 100% in two prospective studies and specificity 64–90%. Inter-rater reliability reached r=0.99 when used by registered nurses [Bergstrom1987].
- NICE NG220 recommends checking people with MS and severely reduced mobility for areas at risk of pressure ulcers at every contact. It also recommends regular assessment for contracture risk and bone health [NICE2022].
- In Missouri long-term care data, residents with MS had a higher prevalence of pressure ulcers on admission than residents without MS (P=.004). An MS diagnosis was associated with developing pressure ulcers by 180 days (P=.02) [Newland2005].

### Falls, swallowing, mood and cognition
- An individual-data meta-analysis of 537 people with MS from four countries, followed prospectively for 3 months, found that 56% fell at least once and 37% fell two or more times (1,721 falls). 65% of falls happened indoors. Fall risk peaked at EDSS 4.0 and 6.0 (odds ratios about 5.1–5.3) [Nilsagard2015].
- A handbook review reports that 50–80% of people with MS have balance and gait dysfunction and that over 50% fall at least once a year [Cameron2018].
- A 2023 meta-analysis of 54 studies (11,266 people) put the pooled prevalence of dysphagia in MS at 44.8% (95% CI 40.4–49.2%), with very high heterogeneity [Mirmosayyeb2023].
- The DYMUS questionnaire, built for MS, was abnormal in 35% of 226 consecutive clinic patients (mean EDSS 3.1). It splits into solid and liquid subscales and is intended to select patients for instrumental swallowing assessment [Bergamaschi2008].
- **Early evidence**: against endoscopic swallowing evaluation in 108 people with MS, DYMUS scores of 2 or more flagged pharyngeal residue or penetration, and 3 or more flagged aspiration [Erensoy2025].
- A systematic review of 118 studies put population-based prevalence in MS at 23.7% for depression (95% CI 17.4–30.0%) and 21.9% for anxiety [Marrie2015Psych].
- In 253 people with MS assessed against a structured diagnostic interview, PHQ-9 had the highest sensitivity for major depression (84%). The HADS depression subscale at a cut-point of 11 had the highest specificity (95%). Areas under the curve did not differ between instruments [Marrie2018].
- Depending on sample and criteria, cognitive impairment affects 34–65% of adults with MS and about 33% of under-18s. The National MS Society recommends a baseline SDMT, or a similar validated test, when the person is clinically stable, then annual re-assessment with the same instrument [Kalb2018].
- NICE NG220 recommends assessing cognition as part of the comprehensive review. It notes that people may not link cognitive problems to their MS, and that anxiety, depression, poor sleep, fatigue and medicines can affect cognition [NICE2022].
- In a large UK cohort (TONiC, n=6,356), the prevalence of moderate or severe fatigue was 565.4 per 1,000 (95% CI 553.0–577.8). It was higher in progressive MS and peaked at ages 50–64; only 4.9% followed a low, stable fatigue trajectory [Young2026].
- In a Swiss registry survey (n=760), 73.3% of people with MS reported temperature sensitivity, most often to heat. Of those aware of cooling methods and heat-sensitive, only 44.3% used them [Iaquinto2026].

### Self-injected DMTs: technique, sites and reactions
- Glatiramer acetate (COPAXONE) is for subcutaneous use only. The label lists self-injection areas as the arms, abdomen, hips and thighs, and says to let the syringe reach room temperature for about 20 minutes before injecting. The 20 mg/mL (daily) and 40 mg/mL (three times weekly, at least 48 hours apart) products are not interchangeable [CopaxoneLabel2026].
- With COPAXONE 20 mg/mL in placebo-controlled trials, injection-site erythema occurred in 43% (placebo 10%), injection-site pain in 40% (20%) and injection-site mass in 26% (6%). With 40 mg/mL, injection-site erythema occurred in 22% (placebo 2%) [CopaxoneLabel2026].
- An immediate post-injection reaction is defined as at least two of flushing, chest pain, palpitations, tachycardia, anxiety, dyspnoea, throat constriction or urticaria. It occurred in about 16% on COPAXONE 20 mg/mL (placebo 4%) and about 2% on 40 mg/mL (placebo 0%). It usually starts several months into treatment, occurs within seconds to minutes of injection, and its symptoms may overlap with anaphylaxis [CopaxoneLabel2026].
- The COPAXONE label reports lipoatrophy in about 2% (20 mg/mL) and 0.5% (40 mg/mL), describes it as probably permanent with no known treatment, and lists correct injection technique and site rotation as measures that may reduce it. Skin necrosis has been seen only after marketing [CopaxoneLabel2026].
- Interferon beta-1a SC (REBIF) is titrated from 20% of the target dose over 4 weeks. The label suggests dosing at the same time, preferably late afternoon or evening, on the same three days at least 48 hours apart, and says analgesics or antipyretics on dosing days may ease flu-like symptoms [RebifLabel2025].
- In the REBIF pivotal trial, injection-site reactions occurred in 92% at 44 mcg (placebo 39%) and injection-site necrosis in 3%. Influenza-like symptoms occurred in 59% (placebo 51%). The label advises against injecting into an affected area until it has healed, and lists depression and suicidal ideation as warnings [RebifLabel2025].
- Ofatumumab (KESIMPTA) is self-injected subcutaneously into the abdomen, thigh or outer upper arm. The label excludes moles, scars, stretch marks and tender, bruised, red, scaly or hard skin, and states that the first injection should be done under healthcare-professional guidance [KesimptaLabel2026].
- With KESIMPTA, systemic injection-related reactions occurred in 21% (vs 15% with teriflunomide plus placebo injections) and local injection-site reactions in 11% (vs 6%). Systemic reactions were most frequent after the first injection (14.4%), falling to 4.4% after the second and under 3% after the third; 99.8% were mild to moderate [KesimptaLabel2026].
- **Early evidence**: in a 101-patient cohort starting weekly IM interferon beta-1a, pre-treatment injection self-efficacy predicted 6-month adherence, an effect mediated by the ability to self-inject. Injection anxiety was related to adherence [Mohr2001].
- **Early evidence**: in a small randomised trial (n=30) of patients unable to self-inject because of anxiety or phobia, 8 of those receiving a six-session nurse-administered self-injection anxiety programme self-injected at 6 weeks, against 3 controls. The difference was significant among completers (p=0.022) but only a trend by intention to treat (p=0.058) [Mohr2005].

### Infused DMTs: premedication, rates and observation
- Ocrelizumab IV (OCREVUS): premedication before every infusion is IV methylprednisolone 100 mg (or 2 mg/kg if under 40 kg), finished about 30 minutes before, plus an antihistamine 30–60 minutes before. An antipyretic 30–60 minutes before may be considered [OcrevusLabel2026].
- The OCREVUS label calls for checking for active infection before every infusion and delaying the infusion if one is present. It requires HBV screening, quantitative immunoglobulins and liver tests before the first dose. Live or live-attenuated vaccines are to be given at least 4 weeks before starting, and non-live vaccines where possible at least 2 weeks before [OcrevusLabel2026].
- OCREVUS infusion rates: the 300 mg initial infusions start at 30 mL/h, rising by 30 mL/h every 30 minutes to a maximum of 180 mL/h (2.5 hours or longer). The 600 mg doses use either a 3.5-hour schedule (40 mL/h rising to 200 mL/h) or, if there has been no prior serious infusion reaction, a 2-hour schedule (100→200→250→300 mL/h) [OcrevusLabel2026].
- In a randomised substudy, the proportion with infusion reactions within 24 hours of the first randomised infusion was similar with 2-hour and 3.5-hour OCREVUS infusions (24.4% vs 23.3%) [OcrevusLabel2026].
- In OPERA I and II, infusion-related reactions occurred in 34.3% of ocrelizumab-treated patients [Hauser2017]. The label table gives 34% with OCREVUS vs 10% with interferon plus placebo infusion in relapsing MS, and 40% vs 26% with placebo in primary progressive MS. Serious infusion reactions occurred in 0.3% of adults, with none fatal [OcrevusLabel2026].
- The current OCREVUS label calls for observation during and for at least 1 hour after each infusion, and for telling patients that reactions can occur up to 24 hours later. For mild to moderate reactions it halves the rate for at least 30 minutes. For severe reactions it interrupts the infusion and restarts at half the rate once symptoms resolve. Life-threatening reactions mean permanent discontinuation [OcrevusLabel2026].
- Ublituximab (BRIUMVI) has the same corticosteroid-plus-antihistamine premedication. Infusion reactions occurred in 48% of patients. The label requires at least 1 hour of observation after the first two infusions only, leaving later monitoring to the physician unless a reaction has occurred [BriumviLabel2026].
- Natalizumab is given as 300 mg IV over about 1 hour every 4 weeks, never as a push or bolus. The label calls for observation during all infusions and for 1 hour afterwards for the first 12 infusions, then according to clinical judgment if there has been no hypersensitivity [TyrukoLabel2025].
- In MS trials, infusion-related reactions (any event within 2 hours of starting the infusion) occurred in about 24% on natalizumab vs 18% on placebo. Serious systemic hypersensitivity occurred in under 1%, usually within 2 hours of starting, and was linked to anti-natalizumab antibodies [TyrukoLabel2025]. In AFFIRM, hypersensitivity reactions of any kind occurred in 4% and serious hypersensitivity in 1% [Polman2006].
- Natalizumab's boxed warning names three risk factors for PML: anti-JCV antibody positivity, longer treatment (especially beyond 2 years) and prior immunosuppressant use. Estimated US PML incidence is below 1/1,000 in the first 24 months for antibody-positive patients without prior immunosuppressants, about 2–4/1,000 thereafter, and about 1/10,000 for antibody-negative patients [TyrukoLabel2025].
- The natalizumab label describes PML symptoms as progressing over days to weeks: one-sided weakness or limb clumsiness, visual disturbance, and changes in thinking, memory and orientation leading to confusion and personality change. Dosing is to be withheld at the first suggestive sign [TyrukoLabel2025].
- The natalizumab label reports anti-JCV seroconversion of 3–8% a year in MS and asks for antibody-negative patients to be "retested periodically" without giving an interval. It advises waiting at least 2 weeks after plasma exchange and at least 6 months after IVIg before testing [TyrukoLabel2025]. A UK expert group reports that the EMA's CHMP recommends 6-monthly testing, and suggests MRI at least 6-monthly (index ≤1.5) or every 3–4 months (index >1.5) after 18 months of treatment in antibody-positive patients [McGuigan2016].
- Alemtuzumab (LEMTRADA) premedication is 1,000 mg methylprednisolone (or equivalent) immediately before infusion on the first 3 days of each course. Herpes antiviral prophylaxis starts on day 1 and continues for at least 2 months, or until CD4+ count is at least 200 cells/µL, whichever is later. Each infusion runs over 4 hours, with a baseline ECG and vital signs before and periodically during it [LemtradaLabel2025].
- In LEMTRADA trials, 92% had infusion reactions and 3% serious reactions. Some reactions occurred more than 24 hours after infusion. The boxed warning requires 2 hours of monitoring after each infusion and reports stroke and cervicocephalic arterial dissection within 3 days of dosing; patients are to be taught stroke symptoms [LemtradaLabel2025].
- LEMTRADA monitoring runs until 48 months after the last dose: CBC with differential, serum creatinine and urinalysis with cell counts monthly; thyroid function every 3 months; liver tests periodically; and skin examinations at baseline and yearly. Reported autoimmune conditions include thyroid disorders (36.8%) and immune thrombocytopenia (2%), with ITP diagnosed more than 3 years after the last dose in some patients [LemtradaLabel2025].

### Oral DMTs: first-dose monitoring and laboratory schedules
- Fingolimod (GILENYA) first-dose monitoring applies at the first dose, on restarting after a break of more than 14 days, and on paediatric dose increases. It includes an ECG before dosing and at the end of observation, and observation for at least 6 hours with hourly pulse and blood pressure. Monitoring is extended if the adult heart rate is below 45 bpm at 6 hours, if there is AV block, or if the lowest heart rate falls at the end of the period [GilenyaLabel2026].
- After the first fingolimod dose, heart rate starts falling within an hour, reaches its lowest point within 6 hours and has a second dip within 24 hours. Symptomatic bradycardia occurred in 0.6% (placebo 0.1%), and heart rate returns to baseline within a month [GilenyaLabel2026].
- The GILENYA label requires VZV antibody testing in people without a confirmed chickenpox history or full vaccination, with vaccination of seronegative people followed by a 1-month delay before starting. It also requires a fundus examination including the macula near the start and 3–4 months after starting. Macular oedema occurred in 0.5% on 0.5 mg (placebo 0.4%), mostly in the first 3–4 months [GilenyaLabel2026].
- Dimethyl fumarate (TECFIDERA) starts at 120 mg twice daily for 7 days, then 240 mg twice daily. Capsules are swallowed whole. Taking it with food, or non-enteric-coated aspirin (up to 325 mg) 30 minutes before, may reduce flushing [TecfideraLabel2025].
- On TECFIDERA, flushing occurred in 40% vs 6% on placebo; 3% stopped because of flushing. Abdominal pain occurred in 18% (10%), diarrhoea 14% (11%), nausea 12% (9%) and vomiting 9% (5%). GI events were most frequent in month 1, and 4% stopped for GI events [TecfideraLabel2025].
- The TECFIDERA label requires a CBC with lymphocyte count before starting, at 6 months and every 6–12 months thereafter. It advises considering interruption if lymphocytes stay below 0.5×10⁹/L for more than 6 months, and continuing counts until recovery after stopping [TecfideraLabel2025].
- Cladribine tablets (MAVENCLAD) require lymphocytes to be within normal limits before the first course and at least 800 cells/µL before the second; the second course may be delayed up to 6 months for recovery. CBC is checked at 2 and 6 months after the start of each course, monthly if the month-2 count is below 200 cells/µL, with herpes prophylaxis below 200 cells/µL [MavencladLabel2026].
- The MAVENCLAD boxed warning requires effective contraception during dosing and for 6 months after the last dose of each course for women, and 14 weeks for men. The label treats it as a cytotoxic drug: tablets are handled with dry hands, swallowed immediately, and taken at least 3 hours apart from other oral drugs [MavencladLabel2026].
- Teriflunomide (AUBAGIO) requires transaminases and bilirubin within 6 months before starting, ALT at least monthly for 6 months, and blood pressure checks. Without accelerated elimination, plasma levels take on average 8 months, and up to 2 years, to fall below 0.02 mg/L. Cholestyramine 8 g every 8 hours or activated charcoal 50 g every 12 hours for 11 days lowers levels by more than 98% [AubagioLabel2026].
- The most common AUBAGIO adverse reactions (≥10% and ≥2% above placebo) were headache, diarrhoea, nausea, alopecia and raised ALT. Accelerated elimination may allow disease activity to return [AubagioLabel2026].

### High-dose methylprednisolone for relapse
- NICE NG220 recommends oral methylprednisolone 0.5 g daily for 5 days, with IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days as an alternative when oral steroids have failed or are not tolerated, or when admission is needed for a severe relapse or for monitoring conditions such as diabetes or depression. Treatment is to be offered as early as possible and within 14 days of onset [NICE2022].
- NICE NG220 asks that people be told about temporary mental-health effects of high-dose steroids (insomnia, depression, confusion, agitation) and worse glucose control in diabetes, and be given written information. It recommends against supplying steroids for self-treatment of future relapses [NICE2022].
- The SOLU-MEDROL label reports arrhythmias and cardiac arrest after rapid IV administration of large doses (more than 0.5 g over less than 10 minutes), and bradycardia unrelated to infusion speed. It describes psychiatric effects from euphoria and insomnia to severe depression and psychosis. It reports rare toxic hepatitis, sometimes weeks later, after pulsed 1 g/day courses for MS relapse, and raised blood glucose [SoluMedrolLabel2026].
- A Cochrane review of 6 placebo-controlled trials (377 participants) found methylprednisolone or ACTH protective against worsening within 5 weeks (OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.24–0.57). Data were insufficient to show any effect on later relapses or long-term disability. High-dose oral methylprednisolone caused more GI and psychiatric effects than placebo [Filippini2000].
- A Cochrane review of 5 trials (215 patients) found no significant difference between oral and IV steroids in relapse recovery at 4 weeks [Burton2012]. In the COPOUSEP non-inferiority trial (methylprednisolone 1,000 mg/day for 3 days), 81% (oral) vs 80% (IV) improved by day 28. Insomnia was more frequent with oral treatment (77% vs 64%) [LePage2015].

### Relapse, pseudo-relapse and infection
- NICE NG220 defines a relapse as new or worsening symptoms lasting more than 24 hours in the absence of infection or another cause, after at least 1 month of stability. It recommends ruling out infection, particularly urinary and respiratory, and distinguishing relapse from fluctuation or progression, before diagnosing a relapse [NICE2022].
- NICE NG220 recommends explaining that symptom changes can come from another illness such as infection, a relapse or a change in disease course. It also recommends a management plan saying whom to contact when symptoms change significantly, and that MS-related fatigue may be brought on by heat or by biological, physical or emotional stress [NICE2022].
- **Established**: in a prospective study of 73 people with relapsing-remitting MS, the risk of exacerbation was raised (rate ratio 2.1) from 2 weeks before to 5 weeks after a systemic infection, mostly upper airway infections. Exacerbations in that window more often left sustained deficit (rate ratio 3.8) [Buljevac2002].

### Teaching topics reported by guidelines and labels
- NICE NG220 recommends encouraging exercise and telling people that regular exercise may benefit their MS and does not harm it. It recommends advising against smoking because it increases disability progression [NICE2022].
- For fatigue, NICE NG220 recommends a personalised self-management discussion (goals and priorities, energy conservation, lifestyle, stress management and CBT techniques) and aerobic, resistance and balance exercise. It recommends against vitamin B12 injections and hyperbaric oxygen for fatigue [NICE2022].
- **Early evidence**: a meta-analysis of 2 RCTs (n=350) found energy-conservation management better than no treatment on Fatigue Impact Scale subscales (physical MD −2.99; psychosocial MD −6.05) in the short term, with no long-term data [Blikman2013].
- The AAN vaccination guideline recommends that people with MS follow local vaccine standards and receive influenza vaccine every year. It recommends vaccinating at least 4–6 weeks before starting immunosuppressive or immunomodulating therapy, avoiding live-attenuated vaccines during such therapy, and delaying vaccination during a relapse [Farez2019].
- In a European cohort of 269 pregnancies, the relapse rate fell from 0.7 per woman-year before pregnancy to 0.2 in the third trimester. It rose to 1.2 in the first 3 months after delivery, then returned to the pre-pregnancy rate. Breastfeeding and epidural analgesia did not affect relapses or disability [Confavreux1998].
- The 2026 UK consensus update on pregnancy in MS stresses family planning in treatment choice. It describes monoclonal-antibody transfer into breast milk as generally low, so treatment during breastfeeding can be considered, and vaccines in pregnancy as generally safe [Mullan2026].
- Labels set specific contraception windows: 2 months after stopping fingolimod [GilenyaLabel2026], 6 months after stopping ofatumumab [KesimptaLabel2026], 6 months (women) and 14 weeks (men) after each cladribine course [MavencladLabel2026], and, for teriflunomide, contraception during treatment and during accelerated elimination [AubagioLabel2026].
- The GILENYA label reports severe increases in disability, with multiple new MRI lesions, after stopping fingolimod. They usually occurred within 12 weeks but up to 24 weeks after stopping, and most patients did not return to their previous level of function [GilenyaLabel2026].
- The AAN DMT guideline's 30 recommendations include engaging patients, monitoring adherence, assessing comorbidity, and counselling about PML risk with natalizumab, fingolimod, rituximab, ocrelizumab and dimethyl fumarate [RaeGrant2018].
- A narrative review of 24 studies (2001–2011) found adherence to DMTs ranging from 41% to 88% [Menzin2013]. In US claims data, adherence (MPR ≥0.8) at 12 months did not differ between new users of self-injectable (54.1%) and oral (53.0%) DMTs (adjusted OR 1.062, 95% CI 0.937–1.202). Depression was associated with lower adherence (OR 0.618) [Munsell2017].
- In a meta-analysis of real-world use of once- or twice-daily oral DMTs (31 studies, 16,398 patients), pooled 1-year adherence was 78.5% by MPR ≥80% and 71.8% by PDC ≥80%, and pooled 1-year discontinuation was 25.4% [Nicholas2020].

### Common misunderstandings (each set against what a source states)
- "Every new symptom is a relapse." NICE requires symptoms lasting more than 24 hours with no infection or other cause, after at least 1 month of stability, and asks for infection to be ruled out first [NICE2022]. How common this belief is among patients has no citable source.
- "Steroids change the long-term course." The Cochrane review found a short-term benefit within 5 weeks but insufficient data on later relapses or long-term disability [Filippini2000].
- "IV steroids work better than oral." Pooled trials and COPOUSEP found no difference in recovery at about 4 weeks [Burton2012; LePage2015].
- "Fatigue is always the MS." NICE lists other causes to look for: sleep problems, pain, spasticity, bladder dysfunction, medicines, infection, anaemia, thyroid dysfunction, anxiety and depression [NICE2022].
- "Exercise will make MS worse." NICE states regular exercise does not harm MS [NICE2022].
- "A negative JCV test means no PML risk." The label states antibody-negative patients remain at risk, from new infection or a false-negative result (assay false-negative rate 3%) [TyrukoLabel2025].
- "Infusion reactions only happen in the chair." Labels state reactions can occur up to 24 hours after ocrelizumab or ublituximab, and after the 2-hour monitoring period with alemtuzumab [OcrevusLabel2026; BriumviLabel2026; LemtradaLabel2025].
- "Stopping a DMT when stable is harmless." The fingolimod label reports severe rebound disability after stopping [GilenyaLabel2026]. Accelerated elimination of teriflunomide may allow disease activity to return [AubagioLabel2026].
- "Pills are easier, so people take them more reliably." Claims data showed similar adherence for oral and self-injectable DMTs [Munsell2017].
- "Pregnancy makes MS worse." Relapse rates fall during pregnancy, especially in the third trimester, and rise only in the first 3 months after delivery [Confavreux1998]. NICE says pregnancy does not increase the risk of progression [NICE2022].

### Documentation items reported for MS clinic or ward contacts
- NICE NG220's comprehensive-review content is the most explicit published checklist found. It covers mobility, balance and falls; aids and wheelchair needs; arm and hand use; spasms and stiffness; tremor; bladder, bowel and sexual function; sensory symptoms and pain; speech and swallowing; vision; cognition; fatigue; depression and anxiety; sleep; respiratory function; disease course (progression, activity, relapses in the past year, DMT eligibility); the documented nature and extent of disability; weight, smoking, alcohol and drugs, exercise, screening and contraception; social participation, driving, employment; and personal and social care and carers [NICE2022].
- Label-driven items to record include: infection screening before each anti-CD20 infusion; premedication timing and infusion rate changes; post-infusion observation time (ocrelizumab ≥1 h each infusion; natalizumab 1 h for the first 12; alemtuzumab ≥2 h) [OcrevusLabel2026; TyrukoLabel2025; LemtradaLabel2025]; hourly pulse and blood pressure and both ECGs for first-dose fingolimod [GilenyaLabel2026]; lymphocyte counts against label thresholds [TecfideraLabel2025; MavencladLabel2026]; and anti-JCV status and index [TyrukoLabel2025; McGuigan2016].
- NICE NG220 asks that the MS multidisciplinary team be told about every relapse, because relapse frequency may influence DMT choice [NICE2022].
- No MS-specific nursing documentation standard (for example from IOMSN or a nursing body) could be found and validated in this search. The items above are taken from guideline and label requirements, not from a nursing documentation guideline.

### Advanced and inpatient MS
- The EAN palliative-care guideline for severe progressive MS (EDSS above 6.0) made 34 recommendations (33 weak, 1 strong) and 7 good-practice statements. It recommends home-based palliative care with weak strength and notes a lack of evidence on advance care planning, on managing fatigue and mood, and on interventions for carers and professionals [Solari2020].
- NICE NG220 recommends referral to palliative care for symptom control and end-of-life care when appropriate, and discussion of carers' roles and their right to a carer's assessment [NICE2022].
- In Veneto (Italy) death records from 2010–2021, influenza or pneumonia appeared in 18.4% of MS-related deaths vs 11.0% of other deaths (OR 2.72). Aspiration pneumonia, pressure ulcers or skin infections, sepsis and urinary tract infection (OR 8.16 in men) were all significantly associated with MS-related deaths [Fedeli2023].
- NICE NG220 recommends regular assessment for contracture risk in people with severely reduced mobility [NICE2022]. No MS-specific trial of nursing contracture-prevention methods (positioning, stretching programmes) was found in this search.

## Controversies
- Do energy-conservation programmes reduce MS fatigue? Benefit: a meta-analysis of 2 RCTs (n=350) found short-term benefit on Fatigue Impact Scale subscales [Blikman2013]. No benefit: an RCT of 86 severely fatigued people found individual energy-conservation management no better than information from an MS nurse on fatigue (difference −0.81, 95% CI −3.71 to 2.11) [Blikman2017]. Status: NICE still lists energy conservation within a personalised self-management discussion [NICE2022]; the evidence is mixed and short-term.
- Is routine depression screening useful? For: PHQ-9 sensitivity 84% and HADS-D specificity 95% support valid screening [Marrie2018]. Against: a Canadian analysis found about 30% screened positive at every visit, with persistent rather than new symptoms, and argued that resources may do more good in long-term management [Patten2010]. Status: validated tools exist, but whether screening improves outcomes is unsettled.
- How often should anti-JCV antibodies be retested? The US label says only "periodically" [TyrukoLabel2025]; the EMA's CHMP recommends every 6 months [McGuigan2016]. Status: practice follows regional regulation.
- Oral vs IV methylprednisolone: trials show equivalent short-term recovery [Burton2012; LePage2015], and NICE makes oral the default with IV as an alternative [NICE2022]. Insomnia was more frequent with oral dosing in COPOUSEP [LePage2015], and some services still default to IV.
- How long to observe after an infusion: ocrelizumab requires at least 1 hour after every infusion [OcrevusLabel2026]; ublituximab only after the first two [BriumviLabel2026]; natalizumab for the first 12 [TyrukoLabel2025]; alemtuzumab 2 hours after each [LemtradaLabel2025]. Status: the rules differ by product, not by any class principle.
- Who can score the EDSS? Traditionally trained neurologists; trained non-neurologist professionals reached 0.87 concordance [Mallucci2025], and electronic checking changed 14.8% of trial scores [DSouza2020]. Status: delegated scoring is being evaluated for decentralised trials.
- Are 20% T25FW and 9HPT changes meaningful at all disability levels? They are widely used [Kaufman2000; Feys2017], but the consortium reviews say validation across the disability spectrum is still needed [Motl2017T25FW; Feys2017].
- What is a sustainable MS-nurse caseload? Punshon et al. judged the 358 figure too high [Punshon2021]; the MS Trust now uses 315, while the observed UK mean was 472 [MSTrust2021].
- Do self-injectable or oral DMTs have better adherence? Independent claims analysis found no difference [Munsell2017]. Industry-funded analyses report higher adherence with monthly ofatumumab than with oral or platform injectables (for example 73% vs 51% at 18 months) [Tai2026]. Status: these depend on database, adherence metric and funding.
- Bladder assessment: the UK consensus rests routine management on dipstick testing and post-void residual measurement, whereas other countries' guidelines recommend cystometry [Fowler2009].
