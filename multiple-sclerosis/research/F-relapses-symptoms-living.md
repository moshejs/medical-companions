# F. Relapses, symptoms, rehabilitation & living with MS
Scope: what relapses are and how they are treated, the commoner MS symptoms (with prevalence and the trials or guidelines on managing them), rehabilitation and exercise, pregnancy and family planning, infections and vaccination, menopause and ageing, comorbidity, work, driving, diet, smoking and alcohol, life expectancy and palliative care. Every source was checked through Europe PMC (core record with abstract), PubMed E-utilities, openFDA or the regulator's own page. Most numbers come from abstracts; the NICE NG220 recommendations were read in full on the NICE site. Notes on the brief and shared keys: (1) the "Langer-Gould 2020" postpartum cohort is in *Neurology* 2020;94:e1939 (PMID 32284359), not *JAMA Neurol*; the 2020 *JAMA Neurol* paper on breastfeeding is Krysko et al. (PMID 31816024). Both are cited. (2) The "Dalgas 2019" exercise review is in *Curr Neurol Neurosci Rep* 2019;19:88 (PMID 31720862), not *Nat Rev Neurol*. (3) The WAVES diet trial (Wahls 2021) is confirmed in *Mult Scler J Exp Transl Clin*. (4) The shared keys Confavreux1998 (PMID 9682040) and Thompson2018Lancet (PMID 29576504) were checked and are correct. Thompson2018, Reich2018 and Montalban2018 were also checked (PMIDs correct) but are not cited, because I could not read their full texts to take numbers from them. I took the relapse definition from Polman 2011 (full text read) and NICE NG220 instead. (5) The 30-day gap between attacks is NICE's wording: "a stable period of at least 1 month". (6) I found no citable prevalence figure for Uhthoff's phenomenon itself; a scoping review of exercise-induced heat sensitivity is used instead. (7) The NHS England fampridine commissioning policy and the NICE cannabis guideline (NG144) were not read. ## Facts
### Relapses: definition and pseudo-relapse
- The 2010 McDonald panel defined an attack (relapse, exacerbation) as patient-reported symptoms or objectively observed signs typical of an acute inflammatory demyelinating CNS event, lasting at least 24 hours, in the absence of fever or infection. Paroxysmal symptoms count only as multiple episodes over not less than 24 hours [Polman2011].
- NICE NG220 (2022) says to diagnose a relapse when new or worsening symptoms last more than 24 hours, with no infection or other cause, after a stable period of at least 1 month. Before diagnosing one, clinicians should rule out infection (particularly urinary tract and respiratory infection) and tell a relapse apart from fluctuation or progression. A relapse should not routinely be diagnosed when symptoms have been present for more than 3 months [NICE2022].
- NICE NG220 notes that MS-related fatigue may be brought on by heat or by biological, physical and emotional stress. This is the background to "pseudo-relapse", a temporary worsening of old symptoms without new inflammation [NICE2022].
- Screening for urinary tract infection (UTI) at a suspected relapse (Johns Hopkins): 30.4% (48/158) of inpatients and 20.0% (10/50) of ambulatory patients had a possible UTI. The number needed to screen to find one possible UTI was 3 (95% CI 2–6) in hospital and 5 (3–11) in clinic. Female sex was associated with UTI (OR 3.90, 95% CI 1.59–9.61) [Fitzgerald2019].
- **Established:** In a prospective study of 73 people with relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) (6466 patient-weeks; 167 infections, 145 exacerbations), the risk of exacerbation was 2.1 times higher from 2 weeks before to 5 weeks after a systemic infection, mostly upper-airway infections. Exacerbations in that window more often left a sustained deficit (rate ratio 3.8). Gadolinium-enhancing lesions did not increase after infection [Buljevac2002].

### Relapses: time course, MRI and recovery
- A new lesion's gadolinium enhancement is short-lived. In 26 people with RRMS scanned weekly, enhancement lasted 3.07 weeks on average (median 2 weeks), shorter than the roughly 1 month that trial designs had assumed [Cotton2003].
- In CombiRx (240 first on-trial relapses), median time to relapse recovery was 111 days. Of 202 people who recovered, 80% did so by 180 days and 94% by 365 days. Relapse severity was the only factor associated with speed of recovery [Koch2023].
- In pooled placebo-arm trial data, 42% of patients had a residual deficit of at least 0.5 EDSS points, and 28% of at least 1.0 point, an average of 64 days after an exacerbation [Lublin2003].
- In a Welsh series of 279 relapses (182 patients), mean EDSS was 3.73 before, 5.18 during and 4.23 after relapse (assessed a mean 127 days later). 49.4% had a residual increase of at least 0.5 points and 32.7% of at least 1 point [Hirst2008].

### Relapses: treatment
- Cochrane review (6 trials, 377 participants): methylprednisolone or ACTH protected against worsening or no improvement within 5 weeks (OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.24–0.57). Courses of 5 and 15 days did not differ. There were too few data to judge effects on future relapses or long-term disability [Filippini2000].
- NICE NG220 recommends oral methylprednisolone 0.5 g daily for 5 days for relapses that affect usual tasks, treated as early as possible and within 14 days of onset. It suggests IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days as an alternative, for example for severe relapses needing hospital admission. Lower doses should not be used, and people should not be given steroids to keep at home for future relapses [NICE2022].
- COPOUSEP (13 French centres, 199 patients with RRMS): oral methylprednisolone 1000 mg/day for 3 days was non-inferior to IV. At day 28, 81% (66/82) on oral and 80% (72/90) on IV had improved (difference 0.5%, 90% CI −9.5 to 10.4; margin 15%). Insomnia was more common with oral treatment (77% vs 64%) [LePage2015].
- Cochrane review of oral versus IV steroids (5 trials, 215 patients): no significant difference in EDSS recovery at 4 weeks, and no difference in gadolinium enhancement [Burton2012].
- Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial (457 patients randomised to oral prednisone 1 mg/kg for 14 days, IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3 days then oral prednisone, or placebo): the IV group recovered faster. At 6 months the IV group had slightly better visual fields, contrast and colour vision but no better visual acuity (P = 0.66). Oral prednisone alone did no better than placebo and raised the risk of new optic neuritis (RR 1.79, 95% CI 1.08–2.95) [Beck1992].
- At 15 years in the ONTT (294 patients), 72% of affected eyes had visual acuity of 20/20 or better. The authors judged long-term visual outcome favourable for most patients, including those with MS [ONSG2008Vision].
- Plasma exchange (sham-controlled crossover; severe demyelinating attacks that had not recovered with IV steroids): moderate or greater improvement occurred in 8 of 19 (42.1%) active courses vs 1 of 17 (5.9%) sham courses [Weinshenker1999].

### Fatigue
- Meta-analysis of 69 studies from 27 countries: the global prevalence of MS fatigue was 59.1%. It has fallen each decade since 2000, and it varied most with the measuring instrument used [Yi2024].
- In a 16-country European survey of 16,808 people with MS, 95% reported fatigue and 71% cognitive difficulties; both independently lowered health utility [Kobelt2017].
- TRIUMPHANT-MS (crossover RCT, 141 enrolled, 136 analysed): Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS) scores were 51.3 at baseline, 40.6 on placebo, 41.3 on amantadine, 39.0 on modafinil and 38.6 on methylphenidate (overall P = 0.20). None of the three drugs was superior to placebo, and adverse events were more frequent (39–40% vs 31%) [Nourbakhsh2021].
- Cochrane review of exercise for MS fatigue (45 trials, 2250 participants): in 26 trials with a non-exercise control, exercise reduced fatigue (SMD −0.53, 95% CI −0.73 to −0.33), with heterogeneity. Relapses were similar with and without exercise (25 vs 26). The authors concluded exercise can be prescribed without harm [Heine2015].
- Meta-analysis of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for MS fatigue (4 RCTs, 403 patients): there was a short-term benefit (SMD −0.47, 95% CI −0.88 to −0.06) and a smaller long-term one (SMD −0.30, −0.51 to −0.08), and the effect waned after treatment stopped [vandenAkker2016].
- NICE NG220 suggests aerobic, resistive and balance exercise (including yoga and pilates), plus self-management that uses mindfulness and cognitive behavioural techniques. For people with EDSS ≥4 it suggests supervised aerobic and progressive resistance activity combined with cognitive behavioural techniques. It says there is no evidence that any specific diet improves fatigue. It advises against vitamin B12 injections for fatigue, and lists amantadine, modafinil or an SSRI as off-label options after specialist discussion [NICE2022].

### Spasticity
- In the NARCOMS registry, 16% reported no spasticity, 31% minimal, 19% mild, 17% moderate, 13% severe and 4% total. About a third changed or gave up daily activities because of it. Intrathecal baclofen users reported less spasticity, pain and spasms than oral-drug users [Rizzo2004].
- NICE NG220: oral baclofen is the first-line option for spasticity with specific treatment goals. Gabapentin (off-label) is second-line or can be combined with baclofen, with caution about respiratory depression. Clinicians should first look for aggravating factors (pressure ulcers, bladder and bowel problems, infection, posture, pain). Refractory cases go to a specialist multidisciplinary team, and NICE's cannabis guideline covers THC:CBD spray. Tizanidine and dantrolene are not named first-line in the recommendations read [NICE2022].
- Nabiximols (THC:CBD oromucosal spray) enriched-design phase 3 trial: of 572 people with refractory spasticity, 272 improved by at least 20% in a 4-week single-blind phase and 241 were randomised. Nabiximols beat placebo on the spasticity numeric rating scale over 12 weeks (P = 0.0002) [Novotna2011].

### Bladder, bowel and sexual function
- Meta-analysis (12 studies): the pooled prevalence of lower urinary tract symptoms was 68.4% by self-report and 64.0% on urodynamics. Frequency (73.5%) and urgency (63.9%) were the commonest symptoms, detrusor overactivity was found in 42.9% and detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia in 35.4% [AlDandan2020].
- The UK bladder consensus algorithm uses a urine dipstick for infection and measures post-micturition residual volume, rather than routine cystometry. Antimuscarinics, with clean intermittent self-catheterisation if needed, are the mainstay; botulinum toxin A detrusor injections were also reviewed [Fowler2009].
- Phase 3 trial of onabotulinumtoxinA for neurogenic detrusor overactivity (416 patients, 227 with MS): incontinence episodes at week 6 fell by 21 (200 U) and 23 (300 U) per week vs 9 on placebo. Median time to re-treatment request was about 256 days vs 92. Of those not catheterising at baseline, 35% (200 U) and 42% (300 U) started catheterisation for retention, vs 10% on placebo [Ginsberg2012].
- In 1100 Italian outpatients, 14% had moderate or severe bowel dysfunction (NBD score >10), rising to 26–32% at high disability. It was more frequent with progressive MS, older age and longer disease [Bisecco2022].
- A review of neurogenic bowel dysfunction describes constipation and faecal incontinence, which can coexist, and stepwise management: lifestyle and a bowel regime first, then biofeedback and transanal irrigation; a stoma or antegrade enemas in selected cases [Preziosi2018].
- Meta-analyses put the pooled prevalence of sexual dysfunction at 62.5% (95% CI 53.9–70.5) in women with MS (14 studies) and 62.9% (53–72.7) in men (20 studies) [Salari2023; Dastoorpoor2021].

### Pain and trigeminal neuralgia
- Meta-analysis (17 studies, 5319 subjects): overall pain prevalence was 63% (95% CI 55–70%). Headache 43%, neuropathic extremity pain 26%, back pain 20%, Lhermitte sign 16%, painful spasms 15%, trigeminal neuralgia 3.8% [Foley2013].
- A meta-analysis of 19 studies (30,348 patients) estimated trigeminal neuralgia prevalence in MS at 3.4% (95% CI 1.5–5.9%) [Houshi2022].
- NICE NG220 asks for the cause of pain to be identified and treated. Neuropathic pain follows NICE's neuropathic pain guideline. Musculoskeletal pain is usually secondary to immobility, spasticity and posture [NICE2022].

### Walking and fampridine (dalfampridine)
- MS-F203 phase 3 (301 patients): 35% (78/224) on fampridine 10 mg twice daily were timed-walk responders vs 8% (6/72) on placebo (p<0.0001). Responders' walking speed improved by 25.2% (95% CI 21.5–28.8%) [Goodman2009].
- MS-F204 (239 patients): 42.9% (51/119) were responders on dalfampridine vs 9.3% (11/118) on placebo. Responders' walking speed improved by 24.7% (95% CI 21.0–28.4%) [Goodman2010].
- The US label says dalfampridine is indicated to improve walking, as shown by an increase in walking speed. It is contraindicated with a history of seizure, moderate or severe renal impairment (CrCl ≤50 mL/min) or hypersensitivity to 4-aminopyridine, and seizure risk rises with dose [FDAdalfampridine].
- In England, NICE withdrew its fampridine recommendation; access now follows an NHS England commissioning policy [NICE2022].

### Tremor, falls, heat
- Meta-analysis (14 studies, 17,458 people): pooled tremor frequency was 33.3% (95% CI 23.5–44.9%). It was lower in studies of more than 200 people (22.5%) [Vaheb2025].
- NICE reviewed drug treatment of ataxia and tremor in MS and made only a research recommendation; it refers to separate guidance on deep brain stimulation [NICE2022].
- Individual-data meta-analysis (537 people, 3 months of prospective follow-up): 56% fell at least once and 37% fell repeatedly. 65% of falls were indoors. Fall risk peaked at EDSS 4.0 and 6.0 (OR about 5), and primary progressive MS doubled the odds (OR 2.02) [Nilsagard2015].
- A scoping review found features of exercise-induced heat sensitivity reported in 29–80% of people with MS. Cooling before or during exercise can reduce it [Silarova2024].
- Cooling-garment RCT (84 heat-sensitive patients, EDSS <6.0): an hour of high-dose cooling gave a small MS Functional Composite improvement (p = 0.007). Patients reported less fatigue during a month of daily use; benefits were objectively modest [Schwid2003].

### Cognition
- Meta-analysis of 50 studies (5859 adults with RRMS): pooled prevalence of cognitive impairment was 32.5% (95% CI 29.3–36.0%), higher with longer disease, older age and longer test batteries [Wu2025].
- The often-quoted 40–70% range could not be confirmed from an abstract read for this pass (unverified). Reviews name processing speed and episodic memory as the domains most often affected [Benedict2020; Chiaravalloti2008].
- The Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) is brief, reliable and sensitive enough to monitor cognition, including during acute disease activity [Benedict2020].
- Cochrane review of neuropsychological rehabilitation (20 studies, 986 participants): low-level evidence of benefit. Cognitive training improved memory span (SMD 0.54) and working memory (SMD 0.33); there was no effect on emotional functions [RostiOtajarvi2014].
- NICE NG220 asks for cognition to be assessed at comprehensive review, for anxiety, depression, sleep, fatigue and medicines that affect cognition to be checked, and for referral to occupational therapy or neuropsychology when impairment persists. A relapse may affect cognition in the short term [NICE2022].

### Depression, anxiety, suicide
- Structured interviews of 221 people with MS gave a lifetime risk of depression of 50.3% [Sadovnick1996].
- In population-based studies, prevalence was 23.7% (95% CI 17.4–30.0%) for depression, 21.9% for anxiety, 14.8% for alcohol abuse and 5.83% for bipolar disorder [Marrie2015Psych].
- A meta-analysis (58 studies, 87,756 patients) gave pooled prevalence of 30.5% for depression and 22.1% for anxiety [Boeschoten2017].
- In 44,452 Canadians with MS vs 220,849 matched controls, the incidence and prevalence of depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia were all higher in MS. In 2005, depression incidence was 979 per 100,000 [Marrie2015Canada].
- Meta-analysis (16 studies): the suicide rate ratio in MS was 1.72 (95% CI 1.48–1.99), and 2.12 around the time of diagnosis [Shen2019].

### Rehabilitation and exercise
- Cochrane review of multidisciplinary rehabilitation (8 trials, 747 participants): strong evidence that inpatient programmes improve activity and participation in the short term without changing impairment. Low-intensity programmes over longer periods brought longer-term quality-of-life gains [Khan2007].
- *Lancet Neurology* review: exercise can help manage symptoms, restore function and improve quality of life, yet people with MS remain much less active than the general population, unchanged over 25 years [Motl2017].
- **Early evidence:** A 2019 review proposes treating exercise as "medicine" prescribed early alongside drugs. It summarises symptomatic benefit and possible disease-modifying and preventive effects; the last two are still being tested [Dalgas2019].
- NICE NG220 tells clinicians to encourage exercise and to say that it has no harmful effects on MS. It also suggests vestibular rehabilitation for limited standing balance and supervised resistance plus aerobic programmes for mobility problems [NICE2022].
- A *Lancet* seminar recommends a comprehensive management programme that promotes wellness, deals with aggravating factors and manages comorbidities [Thompson2018Lancet].

### Pregnancy and family planning
- PRIMS (254 women, 269 pregnancies, 12 European countries): annualised relapse rate was 0.7 in the year before pregnancy, 0.5 in the first trimester, 0.6 in the second and 0.2 in the third (P<0.001). It rose to 1.2 in the first 3 months postpartum, then returned to the pre-pregnancy rate [Confavreux1998].
- In PRIMS, neither epidural analgesia nor breastfeeding adversely affected relapse rate or disability. Mean EDSS worsened 0.7 point over 33 months, with no faster worsening after delivery [Confavreux1998].
- PRIMS 2-year follow-up: 72% of women had no relapse in the 3 months after delivery. Higher relapse rates before and during pregnancy, and a higher disability score at the start of pregnancy, predicted postpartum relapse; epidural and breastfeeding did not [Vukusic2004].
- MSBase (893 pregnancies): annualised relapse rate was 0.32 before pregnancy, 0.13 in the third trimester and 0.61 in the first 3 months postpartum. DMT before conception and a low pre-conception relapse rate were independently protective [Hughes2014].
- Kaiser Permanente cohort (466 pregnancies, 2008–2016): relapse rate fell from 0.37 before pregnancy to 0.14–0.07 during pregnancy. It was 0.27 in the first 3 months postpartum, with no rebound above baseline, and 26.4% relapsed in the postpartum year. Exclusive breastfeeding lowered the risk of early postpartum relapse (aHR 0.37) [LangerGould2020].
- Meta-analysis of breastfeeding (24 studies, 2974 women): pooled OR 0.63 (95% CI 0.45–0.88) for postpartum relapse with breastfeeding, with a stronger association for exclusive breastfeeding. Included studies had moderate to serious risk of bias [Krysko2020].
- NICE NG220 tells clinicians to explain that MS should not stop people planning a family, that pregnancy does not increase the risk of progression, and that relapses may fall during pregnancy and rise in the 3–6 months after birth. It mentions vitamin D and folic acid before and during pregnancy [NICE2022].
- Assisted reproductive technology (ART): the Boston cohort (22 cycles) showed no increase in relapse rate. A meta-analysis of all published case series (220 cycles) showed a higher relapse rate in the 3 months after ART (mean difference 0.92, 95% CI 0.33–1.51) [Bove2020].
- The Association of British Neurologists consensus (modified Delphi) covers pre-pregnancy counselling, management in pregnancy, delivery and anaesthesia, postpartum advice and individual DMTs. It recommends a UK-wide MS pregnancy register; drug-specific registries are pass E's subject [Dobson2019].

### Infections, vaccination and COVID-19
- AAN 2019 guideline: follow local vaccine schedules and have annual influenza vaccination. Vaccinate at least 4–6 weeks before starting immunosuppressive or immunomodulating therapy, and avoid live-attenuated vaccines while on it. Screen for latent infection before starting, and delay vaccination during a relapse [Farez2019].
- Italian COVID-19 cohort (844 people with MS): 1.54% died, most of them with progressive MS. Anti-CD20 therapy (ocrelizumab or rituximab) was associated with severe COVID-19 (OR 2.37, 95% CI 1.18–4.74), and so was methylprednisolone within the past month (OR 5.24) [Sormani2021].

### Menopause, ageing and comorbidity
- MSBase Australian cohort (987 women with relapse-onset MS): after adjustment, menopause was not associated with confirmed disability progression (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.70–1.29) or conversion to secondary progressive MS (HR 1.00). Median age at menopause was 48.5 years [Bridge2025].
- A *Nature Reviews Neurology* review describes people with MS getting older. It covers more age-related comorbidity, a shift from inflammatory to neurodegenerative disease (with immunosenescence as one proposed reason), DMTs working less well against age-related disability, and when stopping a DMT might be appropriate [Vaughn2019].
- A systematic review of comorbidity in MS (249 articles) found prevalence estimates that varied widely, high heterogeneity and few data from Central and South America, Asia and Africa [Marrie2015Comorbidity].
- In a Manitoba population cohort (5797 people with MS, 28,807 controls), median survival from birth was 75.9 vs 83.4 years and the adjusted mortality hazard was 2.40. Diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, depression, anxiety and chronic lung disease each raised the risk of death [Marrie2015Mortality].

### Life expectancy
- 60-year Norwegian cohort (1388 patients): median life expectancy was 74.7 years with MS vs 81.8 in the general population (77.8 for RRMS, 71.4 for PPMS). The overall standardised mortality ratio was 2.7, falling from 3.1 for onset in 1953–1974 to 0.7 for onset in 1997–2012. MS itself was the cause in 56.4% of deaths [Lunde2017].

### Work, driving, quality of life
- In a meta-analysis of 153 studies (188,436 people, 29 countries), 35.6% (95% CI 32.8–38.4) were unemployed and 17.2% had retired early. Unemployment has fallen slightly over time [Vitturi2022].
- In the European burden survey, work capacity fell from 82% at mild disability to 8% at severe disability, and utility fell from population norms to below zero. Mean annual costs were €22,800 (mild), €37,100 (moderate) and €57,500 (severe) [Kobelt2017].
- UK DVLA: drivers with chronic neurological disorders, MS included, must notify the DVLA. Car drivers may keep driving while they can control the vehicle safely, possibly on a 1–5-year licence or with adapted controls. Bus and lorry licences are refused or revoked if the condition is progressive or disabling. Rules differ between countries [DVLA2025].

### Diet, smoking, alcohol, vitamin D
- Cochrane review of dietary interventions (30 trials): PUFA supplements may not differ from alternatives for relapses or disability, and evidence on antioxidants and other dietary interventions was insufficient. Every trial had unknown or high risk of bias [Parks2020].
- NICE NG220 tells clinicians to explain that no specific diet is known to improve MS fatigue, while a healthy diet benefits general health [NICE2022].
- WAVES (87 people with RRMS randomised to the Swank low-saturated-fat diet or the Wahls modified Paleolithic elimination diet): both groups improved fatigue and quality of life at 12 weeks compared with their own baseline, for example MFIS −9.87 with Swank and −14.41 with Wahls. There was no non-diet control group, and 6-minute walk distance did not change [Wahls2021].
- In a UK GP-records cohort of relapsing-onset MS (179 cases), ever-smokers had a hazard ratio of 3.6 (95% CI 1.3–9.9) for conversion to secondary progression [Hernan2005].
- Swedish GEMS study (728 people who smoked at diagnosis): each additional year of smoking after diagnosis brought conversion to secondary progressive MS forward by 4.7% (acceleration factor 1.047). People who kept smoking reached it at about 48 years of age, and those who quit at about 56 [Ramanujam2015].
- NICE NG220 tells clinicians to advise people with MS not to smoke, because smoking increases progression of disability [NICE2022].
- **Hypothesis:** In the CLIMB cohort (923 patients), more than 4 drinks a week was cross-sectionally associated with lower EDSS (OR 0.41) compared with abstinence. Red wine at 1–3 glasses a week was associated with faster growth in T2 lesion volume. The design cannot show cause and effect [DiazCruz2017].
- **Early evidence:** SOLAR (229 patients with RRMS on interferon beta-1a): vitamin D3 14,007 IU/day did not improve NEDA-3 at 48 weeks (36.3% vs 35.3%), though MRI lesion outcomes favoured vitamin D [Hupperts2019].
- **Early evidence:** D-Lay MS (316 patients with CIS): cholecalciferol 100,000 IU every 2 weeks reduced disease activity over 24 months (60.3% vs 74.1%; HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.50–0.87), driven by MRI outcomes. Relapse did not differ significantly (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.42–1.16). Pass B covers vitamin D in detail [Thouvenot2025].

### Palliative care and advance care planning
- EAN guideline on palliative care for severe progressive MS (EDSS >6.0): 34 recommendations (33 weak, 1 strong) and 7 good-practice statements. Home-based palliative care (general or specialist) is recommended with weak strength. There is a lack of evidence on advance care planning, fatigue and mood management, and support for caregivers [Solari2020].
- NICE NG220 says to refer people with MS to palliative care services for symptom control and end-of-life care when appropriate [NICE2022].

## Controversies
- Drugs for MS fatigue: in TRIUMPHANT-MS, amantadine, modafinil and methylphenidate did no better than placebo and caused more adverse events [Nourbakhsh2021]. NICE NG220 (2022) still lists amantadine, modafinil or an SSRI as off-label options after specialist shared decision-making [NICE2022]. Current status: non-drug approaches (exercise, CBT) have the more consistent evidence [Heine2015; vandenAkker2016].
- Oral vs IV steroids for relapse: COPOUSEP and Cochrane found oral high-dose methylprednisolone no worse than IV [LePage2015; Burton2012]. The ONTT, however, found oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg (a low dose) worse than placebo for optic neuritis recurrence [Beck1992]. Current status: the difference is dose, not route; NICE uses oral 0.5 g/day for 5 days and forbids lower doses [NICE2022].
- Postpartum rebound: PRIMS found a clear rise in relapses in the first 3 months postpartum (1.2 vs 0.7 per year) [Confavreux1998]. Kaiser Permanente found no rebound above pre-pregnancy rates in women diagnosed by current criteria [LangerGould2020]. MSBase showed a peak that pre-conception DMT reduced [Hughes2014]. Current status: postpartum risk depends on prior disease activity and treatment.
- Breastfeeding and relapse: the meta-analysis suggests protection [Krysko2020] and the Kaiser cohort found exclusive breastfeeding protective [LangerGould2020]. PRIMS found no effect either way [Confavreux1998; Vukusic2004]. Current status: observational data at moderate or serious risk of bias, with confounding by disease activity possible.
- ART and relapse: pooled case series show more relapses after ART [Bove2020]; the authors' own prospective Boston cohort did not [Bove2020]. Current status: unresolved; the effect may depend on protocol.
- Menopause and progression: a 2026 *Mult Scler* debate argues both "Yes" [Campagna2026] and "No" [Jokubaitis2026]. The largest adjusted cohort found no independent effect of menopause [Bridge2025].
- Diet: the Swank vs Wahls trial reported within-group improvements in fatigue and QoL with no non-diet control [Wahls2021]. The Cochrane review finds no diet proven to change MS outcomes [Parks2020].
- Vitamin D as therapy: SOLAR missed its primary endpoint [Hupperts2019]; D-Lay MS reduced MRI-driven disease activity in CIS without a significant effect on relapse [Thouvenot2025]. Current status: under study.
- Prevalence of cognitive impairment: the common "40–70%" range (unverified here) is higher than the 32.5% pooled from standardised definitions in RRMS [Wu2025]. Estimates depend on test battery, age and disease duration.
