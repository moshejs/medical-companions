# A. Epidemiology, clinical course & history

This file covers how common multiple sclerosis (MS) is worldwide, in the US and in Israel, and the latitude gradient. It also covers sex ratio, age at onset, paediatric onset, progressive onset and survival. It then covers the clinical course definitions (Lublin 1996/2013, CIS, RIS, PIRA, "smouldering" MS and the 2026 roadmap toward biologically informed descriptors) and natural-history numbers. These include conversion from CIS to MS and RRMS to SPMS, residual deficit after relapses, time to EDSS milestones, falling placebo relapse rates and the "benign MS" debate. The disability scales are EDSS, MSFC, T25FW, 9HPT and SDMT, plus the trial definitions of confirmed disability progression. The history runs from Augustus d'Esté to the McDonald 2024 criteria. Every source below was checked through PubMed E-utilities, Europe PMC, Crossref or openFDA (Drugs@FDA). Numbers come from abstracts. Where an open-access full text existed it was read too: Atlas of MS 2020, Lublin 2014, Lublin 2022, Kappos 2020, Tur 2023, Wallin 2019, Hurani 2024, Meyer-Moock 2014, Das 2019 and Zalc 2018. Some sources were available only as abstracts: King 2026 (the MSIF 2024 Atlas update), Tintore 2015, Kuhle 2015, Montalban 2025 and Thompson 2026. A few classic papers have no abstract in PubMed: Schumacher 1965, Poser 1983 and Teitelbaum 1971. For these, only the bibliographic facts are used. The Atlas of MS 3rd-edition paper does not give the share of people with PPMS at diagnosis. The progressive-onset share therefore comes from cohort studies. The Atlas website (atlasofms.org) was not used. Sylvia Lawry's founding of the US National MS Society rests only on society and encyclopaedia pages. The NMSS founder page returned 404, so that fact is marked unverified. All shared-key PMIDs given in the format were confirmed correct. [Montalban2025] is Lancet Neurol 2025;24(10):850-865, PMID 40975101.

## Facts

### Global prevalence and incidence
- The Atlas of MS 3rd edition (country survey Sept 2019–Mar 2020; 115 countries, 87% of world population) estimated 2.8 million people living with MS worldwide, a prevalence of 35.9 per 100,000 [Walton2020].
- Pooled MS incidence across 75 reporting countries was 2.1 per 100,000 persons/year (95% CI 2.09–2.12); the Atlas authors translate this as someone being diagnosed with MS every 5 minutes [Walton2020].
- Mean age at diagnosis in the 2020 Atlas was 32 years (range of country means 20–50), versus 30 (20–44) in 2013 [Walton2020].
- Globally, women are about twice as likely as men to have MS. In some countries the female:male ratio reaches 4:1, and in others it has doubled since 2013 [Walton2020].
- Prevalence rose in every WHO region between 2013 and 2020. Among 81 countries reporting in both years, prevalence went from 29.3 to 44.0 per 100,000, a 50% increase [Walton2020].
- In 2020, prevalence by WHO region (countries reporting both years) was: Europe 142.8 per 100,000, Americas 117.5, Eastern Mediterranean 33.0, Africa 8.8, South-East Asia 8.6 and Western Pacific 4.8 [Walton2020].
- Reported incidence was highest in Europe (6.8 per 100,000/yr), then the Americas (4.8), and lowest in South-East Asia and Africa (0.4). Africa had survey coverage of only 56% of its population [Walton2020].
- MSIF's 2024 Atlas update covered 133 countries (93% of the world population; 118 gave prevalence data). It estimated 3.1 million people living with MS and a global prevalence of 38.1 per 100,000 in 2024, up from 24.8 in 2013 (recalculated with the new method) [King2026].
- The 2024 update estimates about 120,000 new MS diagnoses per year (incidence data from 91 countries). It finds higher prevalence in higher-income than lower-income countries [King2026].
- The Global Burden of Disease 2016 study estimated 2,221,188 prevalent cases worldwide (95% UI 2.03–2.44 million). Age-standardised prevalence rose 10.4% from 1990 to 2016 [GBD2019].
- GBD 2016 found the highest age-standardised prevalence in high-income North America (164.6 per 100,000), western Europe (127.0) and Australasia (91.1). The lowest was in eastern sub-Saharan Africa (3.3), central sub-Saharan Africa (2.8) and Oceania (2.0) [GBD2019].
- GBD 2016 counted 18,932 deaths due to MS and 1,151,478 DALYs in 2016. Age-standardised MS death rates fell 11.5% from 1990 to 2016 [GBD2019].

### Latitude gradient
- **Established**: A 2011 meta-analysis found MS prevalence rises significantly with latitude [Simpson2011].
- **Established**: The 2019 update of that meta-analysis pooled 880 prevalence points. Time-corrected prevalence rose by 5.27 per 100,000 per degree of latitude (4.34 after age standardisation). The gradient had grown stronger since 2011 [Simpson2019].
- The 2019 meta-analysis noted that some regions changed their gradient over time, such as Scandinavia and Atlantic Coast/Central Europe [Simpson2019].
- In the US, MS prevalence rose by 16.3 cases per 100,000 for each degree of latitude (95% CI 12.7–19.8). This held after adjusting for race, ethnicity, age and sex [Hittle2023].

### United States
- Using a validated claims algorithm, the estimated 2010 US adult MS prevalence (cumulated over 10 years) was 309.2 per 100,000 (95% CI 308.1–310.1). This represents 727,344 cases [Wallin2019].
- US prevalence in 2010 was 450.1 per 100,000 in women and 159.7 in men (female:male ratio 2.8). It peaked at ages 55–64, and decreased from north to south [Wallin2019].
- Extrapolated to 2017 and cumulated over 17 years, US prevalence was estimated at 337.9–362.6 per 100,000, or 851,749–913,925 people. The authors advise caution with these figures [Wallin2019].
- By race and ethnicity, US 2010 prevalence per 100,000 adults was 374.8 in White, 298.4 in Black, 197.7 in other non-Hispanic and 161.2 in Hispanic individuals. Among 744,781 adult cases, the female:male ratio was 2.9 [Hittle2023].

### Israel
- Using a validated case definition in the Clalit Health Services database (PPV 87%, sensitivity 92%, specificity 90%), MS prevalence in Israel was 68 per 100,000 at the end of 2011. It was 82 in 2016 and 95 in 2021 [Hurani2024].
- Israel has no national MS registry. The Clalit-based estimates are the first validated population-level prevalence figures [Hurani2024].
- From 2010 to 2021 in the Clalit population, MS prevalence in Israel rose from 65 to 95 per 100,000. Annual incidence rose from 3.6 to 5.2 per 100,000 [Spierer2025].
- Prevalence stayed higher among Israeli Jews than Israeli Arabs throughout 2010–2021. Over the same period, MS incidence among Israeli Arab women rose steeply, reaching a rate comparable to Israeli Jewish women [Spierer2025].
- A 1962 study already examined MS prevalence in Israel among immigrants and native-born inhabitants. This made Israel an early setting for migrant studies of MS risk [Alter1962].
- In 149 Arab people with MS (Muslim, Christian and Druze) at three Israeli MS centres, mean age at onset was 30–31 years. About 50% had incomplete recovery after the first relapse, and primary progressive MS was rare (≤1%) [Siegel2012].

### Paediatric onset
- In the 2020 Atlas, 47 countries reported ≥30,000 cases of MS diagnosed before age 18. In 2013, 34 countries had reported 7,000 cases [Walton2020].
- The 2024 Atlas update counted more than 40,000 reported paediatric MS cases [King2026].
- Using Atlas 2020–2022 data from 53 of 219 countries (31,420 paediatric cases), estimated global paediatric MS prevalence was 2.53 per 100,000 crude. After adjustment for income distribution it was 1.48 per 100,000 [Gombolay2025].
- A review states that 3–5% of all people with MS have their first attack in childhood [Bigi2012].
- In a French cohort, childhood-onset MS (onset ≤16 years; n=394) was compared with adult onset (n=1775). Childhood-onset patients were more often female (F:M 2.8 vs 1.8) and more often had a relapsing initial course (98% vs 84%). They took about 10 years longer to reach secondary progression and irreversible disability, but reached these at an age about 10 years younger [Renoux2007].
- In childhood-onset MS, median time from onset to secondary progression was 28 years, at a median age of 41 [Renoux2007].

### Progressive onset (PPMS share)
- In the Lyon cohort of 1844 patients classified by the 1996 definitions, 58% had RRMS, 27% SPMS, 6% progressive-relapsing and 9% PPMS. Overall, 282 (15%) had a progressive course from onset [Confavreux2006].
- In the London, Ontario natural-history cohort (1,099 patients, 1972–1984), the course was progressive from onset in 33% overall. In the geographically based Middlesex County subgroup it was 28% [Weinshenker1989].
- In adult-onset MS in the French cohort, 84% had a relapsing initial course, so about 16% were progressive at onset [Renoux2007].
- Median age at onset of the progressive phase was similar in SPMS and in progressive-from-onset MS: 39.1 and 40.1 years [Confavreux2006].

### Survival and mortality
- In a 60-year population cohort in western Norway (n=1388, onset 1953–2012), median life expectancy was 74.7 years with MS versus 81.8 in the general population. That is a gap of about 7 years [Lunde2017].
- In the same Norwegian cohort, the overall standardised mortality ratio was 2.7 (2.4 in RRMS, 3.9 in PPMS). Life expectancy was 77.8 years in RRMS and 71.4 in PPMS, and 56.4% of deaths were due to MS [Lunde2017].
- The Norwegian SMR fell over time: 3.1 for onset in 1953–1974, 2.6 for 1975–1996 and 0.7 for 1997–2012 [Lunde2017].
- In Manitoba, Canada (5,797 people with MS vs 28,807 matched controls), median survival from birth was 75.9 years with MS versus 83.4 years for controls (adjusted HR for death 2.40, 95% CI 2.24–2.58) [Marrie2015].
- In the London, Ontario natural-history cohort, 87% of patients followed up to 40 years were still alive. Ascertainment at this duration was incomplete [Weinshenker1989].

### Clinical course definitions (phenotypes)
- In 1996, an international survey of clinicians produced the first standardised definitions of four courses: relapsing-remitting (RR), secondary progressive (SP), primary progressive (PP) and progressive relapsing (PR) [Lublin1996; Lublin2014].
- The 2013 revision (published 2014) kept RR, SP and PP as the core phenotypes. It added clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) as the first clinical presentation of possible MS that does not yet meet dissemination in time [Lublin2014].
- The 2013 revision added two modifiers. "Activity" means clinical relapses and/or MRI activity (gadolinium-enhancing or new/unequivocally enlarging T2 lesions), assessed at least annually. "Progression" means worsening shown on clinical evaluation, assessed at least annually in progressive disease. When assessments are missing, both are "indeterminate" [Lublin2014].
- Because "activity" became a modifier, the PRMS category was eliminated. A person with PPMS who has an acute attack is now classified as "PP–active" [Lublin2014].
- The 2013 revision did not count radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS) as an MS phenotype. Its reasons were that clinical evidence of demyelinating disease is lacking and that MRI findings alone may be nonspecific [Lublin2014].
- In the 2013 revision, SPMS is usually diagnosed in hindsight. No clinical, imaging, immunologic or pathologic criteria mark the point of transition from RRMS, which is usually gradual [Lublin2014].
- The 2013 revision states that "benign" and "malignant" are severity labels, not phenotypes. It says "benign" should only ever be applied in hindsight and should be used with caution [Lublin2014].
- **Established**: The 2024 McDonald criteria allow RIS, in certain cases, to fulfil a diagnosis of MS. They also add the optic nerve as a fifth anatomical location for dissemination in space [Montalban2025].
- **Early evidence**: A 2023 International Advisory Committee paper argues that the clinical course is better seen as a continuum. In this view, the apparent move to a progressive course reflects a partial shift from focal acute injury to widespread inflammation and neurodegeneration, together with failing compensation (neuroplasticity, remyelination) and the effects of ageing [Kuhlmann2023].
- **Early evidence**: In 2026, an International Advisory Committee workshop published a roadmap for moving from the RR/SP/PP descriptors to a "dynamic, biologically informed" description of the MS disease course [Thompson2026].

### Radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS)
- The original 2009 RIS cohort had 44 people (41 women; median age 38.5) with incidental MS-like MRI findings. Radiologic progression occurred in 59%, and 10 developed CIS or MS. Gadolinium enhancement at baseline predicted new lesions over time (HR 3.4) [Okuda2009].
- In a multinational cohort of 451 people with RIS, 34% had a first clinical event within 5 years. Of those who developed symptoms, 9.6% met criteria for PPMS. Age under 37, male sex (HR 1.93) and spinal cord lesions (HR 3.08) predicted conversion [Okuda2014].
- In 10-year follow-up, the cumulative probability of a first clinical event was 51.2%. Younger age, CSF oligoclonal bands, infratentorial lesions and spinal cord lesions were independent predictors [LebrunFrenay2020].
- The 2023 revised RIS criteria study followed 747 people with RIS. At 5 years, the risk of a clinical event was 38.7% for those meeting the 2009 RIS criteria and 29.0% for those with fewer lesions. Spinal cord lesions plus CSF oligoclonal bands raised the lower-lesion group's risk to 38% [LebrunFrenay2023].
- **Established**: In the ARISE randomised trial in RIS (n=87), dimethyl fumarate reduced the risk of a first clinical demyelinating event over 96 weeks compared with placebo (HR 0.18, 95% CI 0.05–0.63) [Okuda2023].

### First presentation and conversion from CIS to MS
- In about 85% of young adults with MS, the disease begins as a subacute clinically isolated syndrome of the optic nerve, brainstem or spinal cord. Only 30–70% of people with CIS go on to develop MS [Miller2005].
- In the Barcelona first-attack cohort (n=1128, mean age 32.1, 69.2% female), the first attack involved the optic nerve in 34.1%, spinal cord in 29.5%, brainstem in 25.1% and other sites in 10.7% [Tur2023].
- In the Barcelona CIS cohort (1015 patients, mean follow-up 81 months, F:M 2.1), having ≥10 brain lesions on baseline MRI raised the risk of clinically definite MS (adjusted HR 11.3, 95% CI 6.7–19.3). Oligoclonal bands also raised it (adjusted HR 1.3) [Tintore2015].
- In the Barcelona CIS cohort, optic neuritis presentations had a lower crude risk of clinically definite MS (HR 0.6) and of disability. Treatment before a second attack was associated with lower risk of McDonald MS (adjusted HR 0.6) and of reaching EDSS 3.0 (adjusted HR 0.5) [Tintore2015].
- In a 33-centre study of 1047 people with CIS, 623 converted to clinically definite MS at a median of 4.31 years. Predictors were oligoclonal bands (HR 2.18), number of T2 lesions (>9 vs 0–1 lesions: HR 2.74) and younger age [Kuhle2015].
- In the Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial (n=389, 15-year follow-up), the cumulative risk of MS after optic neuritis was 50% (95% CI 44–56%). It was 25% with no brain lesions on baseline MRI and 72% with ≥1 lesion [ONSG2008].
- In a 20-year follow-up of 107 people with CIS, 63% developed clinically definite MS: 82% of those with an abnormal baseline MRI and 21% of those with a normal one. Of those with MS, 42% had become secondary progressive [Fisniku2008].

### Natural history: relapses, residual deficit and disability milestones
- In placebo-arm patients pooled from clinical trials, 42% had a residual deficit of ≥0.5 EDSS points and 28% of ≥1.0 point, an average of 64 days after a relapse [Lublin2003].
- In the London, Ontario cohort, median time from onset to DSS 6 (needing help to walk) was 14.97 years in the whole population and 9.42 years in those seen from onset. Disability had a bimodal distribution, peaking at DSS 1 and DSS 6 [Weinshenker1989].
- In the London, Ontario cohort, 30–40% of people with relapsing onset and 6–10 years of disease had developed progressive MS [Weinshenker1989].
- In the Lyon cohort (n=1844), median times from onset to irreversible DSS 4, 6 and 7 were 11.4, 23.1 and 33.1 years for relapsing onset. For progressive onset they were 0.0, 7.1 and 13.4 years. Once DSS 4 was reached, the time to DSS 6 was similar in both groups (5.7 vs 5.4 years) [Confavreux2000].
- In the London, Ontario cohort, having 1 versus ≥3 attacks in the first 2 years made a difference of 7.6, 12.8 and 20.3 years in time to DSS 6, 8 and 10. Relapses after year 2 did not predict these hard endpoints [Scalfari2010].
- In the same cohort (806 people with relapsing onset), the odds of entering SPMS rose with each additional year of disease (OR 1.07/yr). Male sex, older age at onset and ≥3 early attacks predicted earlier SPMS [Scalfari2014].
- In the NO.MS pooled dataset (>27,000 patients, about 200,000 EDSS transitions), the hazard of disability worsening was 31–48% higher after a year with relapses. Pre-existing disability and older age were the main risk factors for incomplete recovery from relapses [Lublin2022].
- In NO.MS, placebo-treated patients starting at EDSS 1 took 8.95 years to reach EDSS 4 and 18.48 years to reach EDSS 6. Disease-modifying therapy delayed these milestones by 3.51 and 3.09 years [Lublin2022].

### RRMS to SPMS: older versus treatment-era cohorts
- A 2019 JAMA paper cites the figure that within two decades of onset, 80% of untreated people with RRMS convert to SPMS [Brown2019].
- In the UCSF EPIC cohort of 517 actively treated patients (>91% retained, up to 10 years), 18.1% (95% CI 13.5–22.5%) had evolved to SPMS and 10.7% had reached EDSS ≥6 at a median of 16.8 years after onset. EDSS was stable or improved in 41% [Cree2016].
- In MSBase (1555 propensity-matched patients), initial interferon beta or glatiramer acetate was associated with lower SPMS conversion than no treatment (HR 0.71). The 5-year absolute risk was 12% versus 27% [Brown2019].
- In 15,717 MSBase patients, the risk of SPMS rose with older age, longer disease duration, higher EDSS and faster disability trajectory. Exposure to disease-modifying therapy was associated with lower risk (HR 0.71) [Fambiatos2020].
- In a 20-year UK CIS cohort, 39% of those with MS had a "benign" course (EDSS ≤3) and 42% were secondary progressive [Fisniku2008].

### Continuum view: PIRA, silent progression, smouldering MS
- **Established**: In pooled data from OPERA I/II (relapsing MS, 96 weeks), progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA) explained most 12-week confirmed disability accumulation. This was 78.0% of events on interferon beta-1a and 88.0% on ocrelizumab, while relapse-associated worsening explained 17.7% [Kappos2020].
- **Established**: In NO.MS, PIRA began early, occurred in all phenotypes and became the main driver of disability in the progressive phase. Relapse-associated worsening was the dominant driver only in paediatric-onset MS [Lublin2022].
- **Early evidence**: In the UCSF cohort, relapses were linked to transient disability increases but not to confirmed progression (p=0.551). The authors proposed the term "silent progression" for worsening in people who otherwise meet RRMS criteria [Cree2019].
- **Established**: A 2023 systematic review of 48 studies found PIRA occurs in about 5% of people with RRMS per year and causes at least 50% of disability accrual in typical RRMS. The review proposed a harmonised definition [Muller2023].
- **Early evidence**: In the Barcelona first-attack cohort (n=1128), 25% had ≥1 PIRA event (median 7.2 years to first PIRA). PIRA carried an 8-fold higher risk of reaching EDSS 6.0 (HR 7.93), and early PIRA (within 5 years) a 26-fold higher risk [Tur2023].
- **Early evidence**: In the Italian MS register (30,203 patients, 11.3 years of follow-up), the proportion with PIRA ranged from 38.8% to 74.1% depending on which of 18 definitions was used. Roving baseline + no relapse 90 days before or 30 days after + 24-week confirmation predicted EDSS 6.0 best [Portaccio2025].
- **Hypothesis**: "Smouldering MS" is described as a diffuse, compartmentalised pathological process present from early disease. Some authors argue it is the "real MS" and that targeting no evident inflammatory activity alone cannot prevent disability [Giovannoni2022; Calabrese2024].

### Falling relapse rates in trial placebo arms
- Across 26 placebo-controlled RRMS trials, the placebo-arm annualised relapse rate fell by 6.2% per year (95% CI 4.2–8.1%) [Nicholas2011].
- A meta-regression of 56 trials found that, over time, participants got older and had longer disease, while pre-trial relapse rates fell. After adjusting for these, the time trend in placebo relapse rates was no longer significant [Steinvorth2013].
- In the 1993 pivotal interferon beta-1b trial, the placebo annual exacerbation rate was 1.27 [IFNB1993]. In the 1995 glatiramer acetate trial, the annualised placebo rate was 0.84 [Johnson1995].

### "Benign MS"
- In British Columbia, 169 people with "benign" MS (EDSS ≤3 at 10 years) were assessed at 20 years. 52.1% were still benign, 21.3% had reached EDSS ≥6, and 23% of those with relapsing onset had converted to SPMS [Sayao2007].
- A 2012 review reports cognitive decline, fatigue, pain and depression in "benign" MS despite preserved motor function. It concludes the current definition, which rests mainly on EDSS, overestimates true benign MS [Correale2012].

### EDSS and other outcome measures
- The EDSS (1983) expanded Kurtzke's earlier 0–10 Disability Status Scale by splitting each step into half-steps (1.0, 1.5 … 9.5). It is built on eight Functional Systems: pyramidal, cerebellar, brainstem, sensory, bowel & bladder, visual, cerebral, and other [Kurtzke1983].
- On the EDSS, 0 is a normal examination. Scores 1.0–3.5 reflect increasing signs or disability in the Functional Systems while the person remains fully ambulatory [Kurtzke1983; Das2019].
- EDSS walking anchors from 4.0 to 6.5 are:
  - 4.0: walks ≥500 m without aid or rest.
  - 4.5: walks ≥300 m.
  - 5.0: walks ≥200 m.
  - 5.5: walks ≥100 m.
  - 6.0: walks ≥100 m with unilateral assistance.
  - 6.5: walks ≥20 m with bilateral assistance.
  [Das2019; Confavreux2000]
- EDSS anchors from 7.0 to 10 are:
  - 7.0: walks ≤5 m with aid; essentially restricted to a wheelchair, wheels self and transfers alone.
  - 7.5: a few steps only; may need help to transfer.
  - 8.0: essentially restricted to bed or chair, keeps effective use of the arms.
  - 8.5: restricted to bed much of the day.
  - 9.0: helpless in bed but can communicate and eat.
  - 9.5: totally dependent; cannot communicate effectively or eat/swallow.
  - 10: death due to MS.
  [Das2019]
- The lower EDSS range rests on the neurological examination. EDSS 4–6 depends heavily on walking ability, and the upper range (>6) measures handicap [MeyerMoock2014].
- Reported inter-rater reliability of the EDSS has kappa values of 0.32–0.76, and 0.23–0.58 for individual Functional Systems. Variability is greater at EDSS 1.0–3.5 [MeyerMoock2014].
- In trials, confirmed disability accumulation is typically an EDSS increase of ≥1.0 point if baseline EDSS is ≤5.5, or ≥0.5 if baseline is >5.5. It must be confirmed after ≥12 or ≥24 weeks. Composite definitions add a ≥20% worsening on the timed 25-foot walk or the 9-hole peg test [Kappos2020].
- PIRA definitions in trials re-baseline disability ≥30 days after each relapse. They also require no relapse within 30 days around the initial increase and its confirmation; relapse-associated worsening is an increase within 90 days of a relapse [Kappos2020; Lublin2022].
- In MSBase (16,636 patients), concordance among 48 progression definitions was only 17.3%. 11–34% of progression events regressed within 5 years [Kalincik2015].
- In MSBase, 70%, 74%, 80% and 89% of events confirmed at 3, 6, 12 and 24 months were still present 5 years later. The authors conclude that 3–6-month confirmation overestimates permanent disability by up to 30% [Kalincik2015].
- The MS Functional Composite (MSFC, 1999) has three parts: leg function (Timed 25-Foot Walk), arm function (9-Hole Peg Test) and cognition (PASAT). Its result is expressed as a z-score [Cutter1999; MeyerMoock2014].
- Change in the MSFC over one year predicted later change in EDSS, which suggests the MSFC is more sensitive to change [Cutter1999]. Its main limitations are learning effects and z-score scoring [MeyerMoock2014].
- The Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) measures processing speed. A change of about 4 points or 10% has been proposed as clinically meaningful [Benedict2017].

### History
- The diary of Sir Augustus d'Esté (1794–1848), a grandson of King George III, is regarded as perhaps the first personal record of MS. It describes onset in 1822 with bilateral optic neuritis, later relapses, and eventual paraparesis and sphincter problems [Landtblom2010].
- Robert Carswell's pathological atlas, completed by 1838, depicts MS lesions (plate 4, fig. 1). Jean Cruveilhier's MS depiction cannot have appeared earlier than 1841 [Compston1988].
- In 1868, Jean-Martin Charcot (1825–93) lectured on "sclérose en plaques" at La Salpêtrière and established it as a distinct disease. He worked with Alfred Vulpian, who published their joint cases in 1866 [Zalc2018].
- Charcot's 1868 clinical description included the "classic triad" of nystagmus, dysarthria and ataxia (with intention tremor). It also covered cognitive features and spinal signs [Zalc2018].
- Others had come close before Charcot, including Frerichs and Valentiner, who reported 15 cases in 1856 [Zalc2018; Murray2009]. A 1935 review counted 158 treatments then used in MS [Murray2009].
- James W. Dawson published "The histology of disseminated sclerosis" in 1916 (Trans R Soc Edinb, 50:517–740) [Dawson1916].
- Rivers and colleagues in 1933, and Rivers and Schwentker in 1935, showed that repeated injection of normal rabbit brain tissue caused encephalomyelitis with myelin destruction in monkeys. In 1935 this occurred in 7 of 8 monkeys. This is the origin of experimental (allergic) autoimmune encephalomyelitis, EAE [Rivers1933; Rivers1935].
- The US society now called the National MS Society was founded by Sylvia Lawry in New York on 11 March 1946, as the Association for Advancement of Research on Multiple Sclerosis (unverified; society and encyclopaedia pages only) [NMSS-Lawry].
- In 1965, a panel led by G.A. Schumacher published criteria for trials of MS therapy (the "Schumacher criteria") [Schumacher1965].
- The Poser criteria (1983) followed as guidelines for research protocols [Poser1983]. The McDonald 2001 criteria later dropped the terms "clinically definite" and "probable MS" [McDonald2001].
- The first MRI ("NMR") study of MS (Lancet 1981, 10 patients) found 112 lesions in addition to the 19 seen on CT. MRI showed abnormalities "on a scale not previously seen except at necropsy" [Young1981].
- In 1971, Teitelbaum, Meshorer, Hirshfeld, Arnon and Sela reported that a synthetic polypeptide (copolymer 1, later glatiramer acetate) suppressed EAE [Teitelbaum1971]. The authors' Weizmann Institute affiliation could not be checked against a validated record (unverified).
- In the pivotal interferon beta-1b trial (n=372), 8 MIU every other day reduced the annual exacerbation rate from 1.27 on placebo to 0.84 [IFNB1993]. The FDA approved Betaseron (BLA 103471) on 23 July 1993 [FDA-Betaseron].
- In the phase III copolymer 1 trial (n=251), the 2-year relapse rate fell by 29% (1.19 vs 1.68; p=0.007) [Johnson1995]. The FDA approved Copaxone (NDA 020622) on 20 December 1996 [FDA-Copaxone].
- The McDonald criteria (2001) brought MRI into the diagnosis of MS. Possible outcomes were "MS", "possible MS" or "not MS" [McDonald2001].
- The 2005 revision clarified dissemination in time, the use of spinal cord lesions, and the diagnosis of PPMS [Polman2005].
- The 2010 revision allowed dissemination in space and time to be shown on a single scan in some circumstances [Polman2011].
- The 2017 revision allowed CSF-specific oligoclonal bands to substitute for dissemination in time. It also allowed symptomatic and cortical lesions to count toward dissemination [Thompson2018].
- The 2024 McDonald revision (published 2025) is a unified approach for relapsing and progressive courses at all ages. It adds the optic nerve as a fifth location and allows the central vein sign, paramagnetic rim lesions and CSF kappa free light chains as supportive evidence. In certain cases, RIS or nonspecific symptoms can fulfil a diagnosis [Montalban2025].

## Controversies
- One disease on a continuum, or distinct phenotypes? Confavreux & Vukusic found that disability milestones follow an age-dependent schedule whatever the initial course, and treat MS as one disease [Confavreux2006]. Kuhlmann et al. and the 2026 roadmap call for a mechanism-based framework [Kuhlmann2023; Thompson2026]. The 2013 phenotypes kept PPMS separate because there are no exacerbations before progression [Lublin2014]. Current status: the RR/SP/PP labels remain in regulatory and clinical use while a biologically informed description is being developed.
- Do relapses drive long-term disability? In natural-history data, relapses after year 2 and superimposed relapses did not change time to hard disability endpoints [Confavreux2000; Scalfari2010]. Placebo-arm and pooled-trial data show residual deficit after relapses and a 31–48% higher hazard of worsening [Lublin2003; Lublin2022]. Current status: relapses matter mostly early in the disease; PIRA dominates later [Lublin2022; Kappos2020].
- How often does RRMS become SPMS today? Older untreated cohorts give 30–40% by 6–10 years and about 80% within two decades [Weinshenker1989; Brown2019]. A treated modern cohort found 18.1% at about 17 years [Cree2016]. Part of the gap may reflect earlier diagnosis and milder cases being included under newer criteria, and SPMS definitions differ between studies [Lublin2014; Fambiatos2020]. Current status: treatment is associated with lower conversion in observational data [Brown2019; Fambiatos2020], but no randomised long-term proof exists.
- What counts as PIRA? Definitions vary in baseline (fixed vs roving), confirmation period and relapse windows. Depending on the definition, the share of patients with PIRA ranges from 38.8% to 74.1% [Portaccio2025; Muller2023]. Short confirmation periods overestimate permanent worsening [Kalincik2015].
- Is the fall in placebo relapse rates real? It may be a true change in MS [Nicholas2011]. Steinvorth et al. attribute it to changes in trial populations and eligibility (lower pre-trial relapse rates, older patients) [Steinvorth2013].
- Does "benign MS" exist? About half of "benign" patients at 10 years are no longer benign at 20 years [Sayao2007]. Non-motor and cognitive problems are missed by EDSS-based definitions [Correale2012]. The 2013 phenotype revision advises using the term only in hindsight and with caution [Lublin2014].
- Is RIS part of MS? The 2013 phenotypes said RIS is not an MS subtype [Lublin2014]. The 2024 McDonald criteria allow RIS to meet an MS diagnosis in certain cases [Montalban2025], and a randomised trial shows treatment delays a first event [Okuda2023]. Current status: under the newest criteria, part of RIS is classed as MS.
- Is MS truly becoming more common? Atlas estimates rose from 2.8 million (2020) to 3.1 million (2024), with prevalence going from 24.8 per 100,000 (2013, recalculated) to 38.1 (2024) [Walton2020; King2026]. The Atlas authors stress changes in methods and gaps in data from low-income countries. GBD found only a 10.4% rise in age-standardised prevalence from 1990 to 2016 [GBD2019]. Longer survival and better case-finding also inflate prevalence [Lunde2017; Wallin2019].
- Who first depicted MS lesions? Compston dates Carswell (1838) before Cruveilhier (≥1841) [Compston1988]. Zalc agrees about one Cruveilhier plate but says doubt remains over the other [Zalc2018].
- Is the latitude gradient weakening? A 2019 meta-analysis found the gradient strengthening overall, with regional changes (e.g. Scandinavia) [Simpson2019]. Israel shows rising incidence among Arab women, a group with historically low prevalence, which suggests environmental and lifestyle change [Spierer2025].
