# B. Genetics & risk factors
This file covers inherited susceptibility to MS: HLA alleles, the IMSGC genome-wide maps, the 2023 severity locus, twin and family recurrence, and polygenic scores. It also covers Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and molecular mimicry; vitamin D, sunlight, latitude and month of birth; smoking (active and passive), adolescent obesity, shift work, alcohol, coffee and the gut microbiome; gene-environment interaction; sex ratio and migration; and prevention (the RIS trials and EBV vaccine efforts). Numbers come from PubMed/Europe PMC abstracts, plus open full texts for Westerlind 2014 (Tables 3 and 5), IMSGC 2019 (PMC author manuscript) and Moutsianas 2015 (Table 1). Four gaps should be noted. (1) The Bjornevik 2022 Science full text was blocked (HTTP 403). Its abstract gives the cohort size, the "32-fold" risk and the NfL and CMV findings; the exact HR (32.4, 95% CI 4.3–245.3) and the 26.5 HR for baseline-positive comparisons were cross-checked in a 2024 review table [Kettunen2024], and the 7.5-year median lag in a 2022 journal-club summary [Hrastelj2022]. (2) The Rotstein 2026 JAMA Neurology research letter has no abstract and its full text could not be reached, so only its title is used. (3) There is no peer-reviewed guideline sentence saying "there is no genetic test for MS". The statement rests on an MSIF patient document (read in full, so marked unverified as a society document) and on a peer-reviewed review [Hone2021]. (4) A parent-of-proband recurrence figure could not be extracted from the population registries read. Nielsen 2005 reports first-degree relatives pooled, and Westerlind 2014 reports children and siblings of probands. Shared keys used: [IMSGC2019] (PMID 31604244), [Bjornevik2022] (PMID 35025605) and [Walton2020] (PMID 33174475), all checked against PubMed. Their details as given in the format file are correct.

## Facts
### HLA and the genomic map
- **Established** The HLA class II allele HLA-DRB1*15:01 is the strongest single genetic risk factor for MS. In 17,465 cases and 30,385 controls of European ancestry from 11 cohorts, its additive per-allele odds ratio was 3.92 (95% CI 3.74–4.12; P = 2 × 10^-686), with partial dominance (homozygous OR 8.30) [Moutsianas2015].
- **Established** Other class II risk alleles are HLA-DRB1*13:03, DRB1*03:01, DRB1*08:01 and DQB1*03:02. Class I alleles are protective: HLA-A*02:01 (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.64–0.70; homozygous OR 0.56), HLA-B*44:02, B*38:01 and B*55:01 [Moutsianas2015].
- **Established** HLA alleles interact: HLA-DQA1*01:01 is strongly protective only in carriers of DRB1*15:01 (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.59–0.72 in replication). A second interaction exists between DQB1*03:01 and DQB1*03:02. No interaction was found between classical HLA alleles and non-HLA risk variants [Moutsianas2015].
- **Established** The 2011 IMSGC/WTCCC2 GWAS (9,772 cases) confirmed that variation in HLA-A underlies the independent protective class I signal. It identified at least 29 new non-MHC loci, which pointed mainly to T-helper-cell differentiation [IMSGC2011].
- **Established** The 2019 IMSGC genomic map analysed 47,429 people with MS and 68,374 controls. It established 200 autosomal susceptibility variants outside the MHC, 1 X-chromosome variant and 32 independent variants within the extended MHC, and prioritised 551 putative susceptibility genes [IMSGC2019].
- **Established** The X-chromosome variant is rs2807267 (OR about 1.07), which lies in a T-cell-specific enhancer [IMSGC2019].
- **Established** Together the identified effects explain "up to 48%" of the MS heritability that can be estimated from GWAS data. The MHC accounts for 21.4% of the SNP-based heritability and the rest lies outside the MHC [IMSGC2019].
- **Established** MS susceptibility genes are enriched in every major peripheral immune cell type. Within the brain, they are enriched in human microglia (p = 5 × 10^-14) but not in astrocytes or neurons. The authors read this as MS being most likely triggered first in the peripheral immune system, with microglia possibly helping to target the process to the CNS [IMSGC2019].

### Severity genetics (IMSGC 2023)
- **Early evidence** A GWAS of the age-related MS severity score in 12,584 cases, replicated in 9,805 cases, found rs10191329 in the DYSF–ZNF638 locus. People homozygous for the risk allele reached the need for a walking aid a median of 3.7 years sooner, and the allele was linked to more brainstem and cortical pathology at autopsy [IMSGC2023].
- **Early evidence** Unlike susceptibility genes, severity heritability was enriched in CNS tissues, not immune cells. A suggestive second locus (DNM3–PIGC, rs149097173) was found, and Mendelian randomisation suggested that higher educational attainment may be protective. The authors proposed that "CNS resilience" or neurocognitive reserve shapes outcome [IMSGC2023].
- **Early evidence** Replication is mixed. In 1,813 people with relapse-onset MS in the MSBase registry, rs10191329 was not associated with longitudinal disease severity (p > 0.05) [Campagna2024]. In 183 people with relapsing MS, each risk allele was associated with faster retinal layer atrophy (pRNFL +0.10%/year, 95% CI 0.05–0.19; GCIPL +0.11%/year, 95% CI 0.07–0.19) [Bsteh2025]. In 4,815 people without MS aged 45 or over, the allele was associated with lower total brain and grey-matter volume below age 55 [Corsten2026].

### Twins and familial recurrence
- **Established** In the nationwide Danish twin study (13,286 MS cases, 178 of them twins), probandwise concordance was 24% (95% CI 5–39%) in monozygotic twins and 3% (95% CI 0–8%) in dizygotic twins [Hansen2005].
- **Established** In a Canadian population-based twin study (n = 370 pairs, about 75% of all Canadian MS twin pairs), probandwise concordance was 25.3% (SE ±4.4) in monozygotic twins, 5.4% (±2.8) in dizygotic twins and 2.9% (±0.6) in their non-twin siblings. The excess in monozygotic twins came mainly from female pairs (34% versus 3.8% in female dizygotic pairs) [Willer2003].
- **Established** In a Swedish registry study of 28,396 people with MS, the sibling recurrence risk ratio (λs) was 7.1 (95% CI 6.42–7.86). The authors describe this as lower than earlier clinic-based estimates [Westerlind2014].
- **Established** Relative risks in Sweden versus matched population controls: monozygotic co-twin 23.6 (95% CI 8.7–64.0); dizygotic co-twin 2.18 (0.71–6.68, not significant); sibling 7.13 (6.42–7.93); child of a person with MS 5.77 (5.17–6.45); maternal half-sibling 4.45 (3.25–6.09); paternal half-sibling 3.29 (2.29–4.72); adopted relatives 1.78 (0.49–6.06, not significant) [Westerlind2014].
- **Established** Age-adjusted absolute risks for relatives in Sweden: monozygotic co-twin 17.3% (95% CI 8.4–26.1%); sibling 2.55% (2.09–3.01%); child 2.03% (1.86–2.20%); cousin 0.57% (0.47–0.67%) [Westerlind2014].
- **Established** Using 74,757 twin pairs and 2.5 million sibling pairs, Swedish heritability was estimated at 0.64 (0.36–0.76). The shared-environment component was 0.01 (0.00–0.18) [Westerlind2014].
- **Established** In a Danish nationwide cohort of 19,615 first-degree relatives of 8,205 people with MS (1968–1997), first-degree relatives had a 7.1-fold higher risk (95% CI 5.8–8.8). The familial excess lifetime risk was 2.5% (95% CI 2.0–3.2%), added to a sporadic lifetime risk of 0.5% in women and 0.3% in men [Nielsen2005].
- **Established** Spouses of people with MS had no increased risk in the Danish cohort. The authors took this as evidence against a major role for environmental factors acting in adulthood [Nielsen2005].
- **Established** A patient-facing MSIF summary puts lifetime risk at 20–30% for an identical twin, about 5% for a non-identical twin and about 1–3% for a sibling. It gives a worldwide average risk of about 1 in 625 (0.15%) [MSIF2022].

### Genetic testing and prediction
- **Established** No genetic test is used clinically to diagnose MS or to predict who will develop it. MSIF states that genetic tests for MS "are not definitive" and that genetic testing for MS is used only in research. A person can carry many risk variants and never develop MS [MSIF2022].
- **Established** A review of MS prediction concluded that combined genetic-environmental risk scores could help select high-risk people for prevention trials. The authors argue such scores are "unlikely to ever" be clinically helpful for predicting MS in an individual [Hone2021].
- **Early evidence** A polygenic risk score built from about 41,505 people discriminated MS from controls with an AUC of 0.73 (95% CI 0.72–0.74) in UK Biobank and 0.80 (0.76–0.82) at Kaiser Permanente Northern California. People in the top PRS decile had more than 5-fold (UK Biobank) and 15-fold (KPNC) higher risk than those in the median decile [Shams2023].
- **Early evidence** The GEMS project enrolled 2,632 first-degree relatives in the USA. An integrated genetic-environmental score identified relatives with twice the average familial risk, with an early incidence about 30 times that of sporadic MS [Xia2016].

### Epstein-Barr virus
- **Established** In a cohort of more than 10 million young adults on active US military duty (955 diagnosed with MS during service), MS risk rose 32-fold after EBV infection. Risk did not rise after infection with other viruses, including cytomegalovirus, which spreads in a similar way [Bjornevik2022].
- **Established** The seroconversion hazard ratio was 32.4 (95% CI 4.3–245.3; p < 0.001). For EBV-positive versus EBV-negative status, the HR was 26.5 (95% CI 3.7–191.6). The wide intervals reflect the very small number of EBV-negative people who developed MS [Bjornevik2022; Kettunen2024].
- **Established** Serum neurofilament light chain (a marker of nerve-fibre damage) did not differ between future cases and controls before or at EBV seroconversion. It rose only after seroconversion in people who later developed MS, so EBV infection came before the first sign of neuroaxonal injury [Bjornevik2022; Hrastelj2022].
- **Established** In the 2022 study, all but one MS case seroconverted before clinical onset, with a median gap of 7.5 years. Screening against more than 200 human virus species found no other virus that differed between cases and controls [Hrastelj2022].
- **Established** An earlier nested study in the same military repository (305 MS cases, 610 controls) found that all 10 initially EBV-negative cases became EBV-positive before MS onset. Only 35.7% of initially EBV-negative controls with follow-up seroconverted (exact p = 0.0008) [Levin2010].
- **Established** In the German National MS cohort, 901 of 901 people (100%) with CIS or early relapsing-remitting MS were EBV-seropositive once immunoblot was used for samples that were negative on standard assays. EBV seropositivity in a hospital population of 16,163 never reached 100% in any age group [Abrahamyan2020].
- **Established** A meta-analysis of 18 studies (19,390 people with MS, 16,007 controls) found that a history of infectious mononucleosis roughly doubles MS risk (RR 2.17, 95% CI 1.97–2.39) [Handel2010].
- **Established** Molecular mimicry: clonally expanded B cells from the cerebrospinal fluid of people with MS make antibodies that bind both EBV nuclear antigen 1 (EBNA1) and the CNS protein GlialCAM with high affinity. A post-translational modification of GlialCAM makes the cross-reaction possible. Immunising mice with EBNA1 worsened an MS-like disease (EAE), and anti-EBNA1/GlialCAM antibodies were common in MS [Lanz2022].
- **Early evidence** A second mimicry target is anoctamin-2 (ANO2). In 8,746 people with MS and 7,228 controls, 14.6% of cases versus 7.8% of controls were ANO2-seropositive (OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.5–1.8). Antibodies cross-reacted between ANO2 amino acids 140–149 and EBNA1 amino acids 431–440 [Tengvall2019].
- **Early evidence** In people who were ANO2-seropositive and also carried DRB1*15:01, lacked A*02:01 and had high anti-EBNA1 levels, the OR for MS was 24.9 (95% CI 17.9–34.8) [Tengvall2019].
- **Early evidence** CD4+ T cells specific for ANO2 are more frequent in MS. T-cell clones from treated patients cross-react with EBNA1 and ANO2, and immunising mice with ANO2 made EAE worse through CD4+ T cells [Thomas2026].
- **Early evidence** Autoreactive GlialCAM370–389-specific cells (driven by EBNA1 386–405 cross-reactivity) are normally killed by NKG2C+/NKG2D+ natural killer cells and EBV-specific T cells. Host genetic variants, EBV variants and HCMV variants that impair this control were linked to an up to 260-fold increase in MS risk [Vietzen2023].
- **Established** "Necessary but not sufficient": about 90% or more of adults carry EBV, yet only a small minority develop MS. Reviews and commentary conclude that EBV appears to be required in almost all cases, but that genetic susceptibility (for example HLA) and other exposures decide who goes on to MS [Aloisi2023; Robinson2022; Sundqvist2012].
- **Early evidence** A 2026 population-based research letter, titled "Multiple Sclerosis Onset Preceding Epstein-Barr Virus Infection", examined people whose primary EBV infection appeared to come after MS onset. Its numbers could not be verified for this file [Rotstein2026].

### Vitamin D, sunlight, latitude and month of birth
- **Established** In a nested case-control study within more than 7 million US military personnel (257 cases), white participants had lower MS risk with higher serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D: OR 0.59 per 50 nmol/L increase (95% CI 0.36–0.97). The top quintile (>99.1 nmol/L) had OR 0.38 (0.19–0.75) versus the bottom quintile (<63.3 nmol/L). The association was strongest for levels measured before age 20 [Munger2006].
- **Established** No significant association was found among Black and Hispanic service members (109 cases), who had lower vitamin D levels [Munger2006].
- **Early evidence** In the Danish Newborn Screening Biobank (521 cases, 972 controls), lower neonatal 25(OH)D was linked to higher MS risk. The top versus bottom quintile OR was 0.53 (95% CI 0.36–0.78), and each 25 nmol/L increase gave OR 0.70 (0.57–0.84) [Nielsen2017].
- **Established** Mendelian randomisation using 4 SNPs that lower 25(OH)D, tested in IMSGC data (up to 14,498 cases and 24,091 controls), found that a 1-SD genetically determined decrease in log 25(OH)D doubled the odds of MS (OR 2.0, 95% CI 1.7–2.5). The result held in sensitivity analyses (OR 1.7, 1.3–2.2) [Mokry2015].
- **Established** An updated meta-analysis (880 prevalence points) found that MS prevalence rises with latitude by 5.27 per 100,000 per degree (4.34 per 100,000 after age standardisation), and that the gradient has grown stronger since 2011 [Simpson2019].
- **Established** In a pooled analysis of 42,045 people with MS from Canada, Great Britain, Denmark and Sweden, 8.5% fewer were born in November and 9.1% more in May than expected. The effect was stronger in familial cases [Willer2005].
- **Early evidence** D-Lay MS (France, 36 centres) randomised 316 untreated people with CIS typical of MS to cholecalciferol 100,000 IU every 2 weeks or placebo for 24 months. Disease activity (relapse and/or MRI activity) occurred in 60.3% versus 74.1% (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.50–0.87; P = .004), and median time to activity was 432 versus 224 days [Thouvenot2025].
- **Early evidence** In D-Lay MS, all 3 MRI secondary outcomes favoured vitamin D (new lesions HR 0.61; contrast-enhancing lesions HR 0.47). None of the 10 clinical secondary outcomes differed, including relapse (17.9% vs 21.8%; HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.42–1.16) [Thouvenot2025].
- **Early evidence** SOLAR (229 people with relapsing-remitting MS on interferon beta-1a; vitamin D3 14,007 IU/day for 48 weeks) missed its primary endpoint, NEDA-3 (36.3% vs 35.3%; OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.53–1.63). Combined unique active lesions were fewer with vitamin D (rate ratio 0.68, 0.52–0.89) [Hupperts2019].
- **Early evidence** CHOLINE (181 people with relapsing-remitting MS on interferon beta-1a with low 25(OH)D; 100,000 IU every other week for 96 weeks) missed its primary endpoint, change in relapse rate. Results in the 90 completers favoured cholecalciferol [Camu2019].

### Smoking
- **Established** A meta-analysis that applied the Bradford Hill causation criteria found that smoking is associated with MS risk (conservative pooled OR/RR 1.54, 95% CI 1.46–1.63) and with conversion to secondary progressive MS (HR 1.80, 1.04–3.10). The authors judged the evidence for a causal role in MS risk to be strong [Degelman2017].
- **Established** In a Swedish incident case-control study (902 cases, 1,855 controls), smokers had higher MS risk (women OR 1.4, 95% CI 1.2–1.7; men OR 1.8, 1.3–2.5). Risk rose with cumulative dose (p < 0.0001), was raised even at 5 pack-years or fewer, and stayed raised for up to 5 years after quitting [Hedstrom2009].
- **Established** Long-term Swedish snuff (oral tobacco) use for more than 15 years was associated with lower MS risk (OR 0.3, 95% CI 0.1–0.8). The authors suggest nicotine is not what drives the smoking risk and propose lung irritation instead [Hedstrom2009].
- **Early evidence** Among never-smokers (695 cases, 1,635 controls), exposure to passive smoking was associated with OR 1.3 (95% CI 1.1–1.6), rising with duration of exposure (p = 0.003) [Hedstrom2011Passive].

### Adolescent obesity
- **Established** In the Nurses' Health Studies (238,371 women, 593 MS cases), obesity at age 18 (BMI ≥30) was associated with more than double the MS risk (pooled RR 2.25, 95% CI 1.50–3.37). Large body size at age 20 raised risk by 96% (1.33–2.89), whereas adult BMI showed no association [Munger2009].
- **Established** In a Swedish case-control study (1,571 cases, 3,371 controls), BMI above 27 kg/m² at age 20 was associated with about twice the MS risk in both men and women [Hedstrom2012].
- **Established** Mendelian randomisation (70 BMI SNPs from GIANT tested in IMSGC data) found that a 1-SD increase in genetically determined BMI raised the odds of MS by 41% (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.20–1.66), with no evidence of pleiotropy [Mokry2016].

### Other lifestyle factors and the microbiome
- **Early evidence** Shift work at a young age was associated with MS in two Swedish studies (OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.2–2.1 in the incident study; OR 1.3, 1.0–1.6 in the prevalent study). Three or more years of shift work before age 20 gave OR 2.0 (1.2–3.6) and 2.1 (1.3–3.4) respectively [Hedstrom2011Shift].
- **Early evidence** In the Nurses' Health Studies (198,419 women, 579 MS cases), a history of rotating night-shift work was not associated with MS overall (for example NHSII, 10+ years: HR 1.03, 0.72–1.49). Only 20 or more years was associated with definite MS (HR 2.62, 1.06–6.46) [Papantoniou2019].
- **Early evidence** Alcohol showed a dose-dependent inverse association with MS in two Swedish studies. High consumption versus none gave OR 0.6 (0.4–1.0) in women and 0.5 (0.2–1.0) in men in EIMS, and OR 0.7 for both sexes in GEMS. The harmful effect of smoking was stronger among non-drinkers [Hedstrom2014Alcohol].
- **Early evidence** Drinking more than 900 mL of coffee daily, compared with none, was associated with lower MS risk in a Swedish study (OR 0.70, 0.49–0.99) and a US study (OR 0.69, 0.50–0.96) [Hedstrom2016].
- **Early evidence** The iMSMS compared the gut microbiome of 576 people with MS (36% untreated) with genetically unrelated household controls (1,152 participants in total). MS was associated with more Akkermansia muciniphila, Ruthenibacterium lactatiformans, Hungatella hathewayi and Eisenbergiella tayi, and with less Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Blautia [iMSMS2022].
- **Hypothesis** In 34 monozygotic twin pairs discordant for MS, gut microbiota from the twin with MS, transplanted into transgenic mice, caused spontaneous CNS autoimmunity more often than microbiota from the healthy twin. Whether the microbiome contributes to MS in humans remains unproven [Berer2017].
- **Established** A Nature Reviews Neurology review lists these as established risk factors: high latitude, female sex, smoking, low vitamin D, EBV infection and adolescent obesity. It notes reported associations with organic solvents and shift work, and inverse associations with nicotine use, alcohol, cytomegalovirus infection and high coffee intake [Olsson2017].

### Gene-environment interaction
- **Established** In a Swedish study (843 cases, 1,209 controls), compared with non-smokers lacking both genetic risk factors, the OR was 13.5 (95% CI 8.1–22.6) for smokers who carried DRB1*15 and lacked A*02. The OR was 4.9 (3.6–6.6) for non-smokers with both genetic factors and 1.4 (0.9–2.1) for smokers without them [Hedstrom2011Smoking].
- **Established** Smoking multiplied risk by 2.8 in people with both HLA risk factors, compared with 1.4 in those with neither [Hedstrom2011Smoking].
- **Early evidence** Passive smoking also interacts with HLA. Among never-smokers with both risk genotypes, the OR was 4.5 (3.3–6.1) without passive smoke exposure and 7.7 (5.5–10.8) with it [Hedstrom2014Passive].
- **Established** In the incident cohort, obese adolescents carrying DRB1*15 and lacking A*02 had OR 16.2 (95% CI 7.5–35.2) compared with non-obese people with neither genetic factor. The prevalent cohort gave OR 13.8 (4.1–46.8) [Hedstrom2014Obesity].
- **Early evidence** Adolescent obesity combined with infectious mononucleosis after age 10 gave OR 14.7 (95% CI 5.9–36.6) in the incident study and 13.2 (5.2–33.6) in the prevalent study, regardless of HLA status (attributable proportion due to interaction 0.7–0.8) [Hedstrom2015].
- **Early evidence** Antibodies to the EBNA1 fragment 385–420 were more strongly associated with MS (OR 3.60, 2.75–4.72) than total EBNA1 IgG. They interacted with DRB1*15 (attributable proportion due to interaction 0.60) and with absence of A*02 (0.39) [Sundqvist2012].
- **Early evidence** Current smokers had higher EBNA-1 antibody levels. Current smoking interacted additively with high EBNA-1 levels (attributable proportion 0.3, 95% CI 0.2–0.4) and with a history of infectious mononucleosis (0.2), but past smoking did not (6,340 cases, 6,219 controls) [Hedstrom2020].
- **Established** Reviews conclude that smoking, EBV infection and obesity each interact with HLA risk genes, pointing to a shared pathway through adaptive immunity [Olsson2017].

### Sex, migration and time trends
- **Established** In 27,074 Canadian people with MS, the female-to-male ratio by birth year rose for at least 50 years and exceeded 3.2:1 (rank correlation with birth year r = 0.84; p < 0.0001). The authors attribute this to a disproportionate rise in MS incidence among women, which must have environmental causes [Orton2006].
- **Established** A literature meta-regression found an almost universal rise in MS prevalence and incidence over time and a general, though not universal, rise in incidence among females [KochHenriksen2010].
- **Established** Worldwide, women are twice as likely as men to live with MS. Of an estimated 2.8 million people with MS (35.9 per 100,000), the mean age at diagnosis is 32 years [Walton2020].
- **Established** A systematic review of migrant studies found that people moving from high- to low-risk areas acquire lower rates, while those moving from low- to high-risk areas keep their low risk. Their children's risk approaches that of the host country. The few age-at-migration studies suggested risk is set largely in the first two decades of life [Gale1995].
- **Early evidence** In Norway, MS prevalence in second-generation Pakistani immigrants was close to the Norwegian level (standardised prevalence ratio 1.62, 95% CI 0.88–2.76), compared with 0.13 (0.05–0.28) in the first generation. This shows how quickly environmental risk can change within one generation [BergHansen2015].

### Prevention
- **Established** No intervention has been proven to prevent MS in the general population. The only randomised evidence of delaying a first clinical event comes from people with radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS), who have MS-like MRI lesions found by chance but no symptoms [Okuda2023; LebrunFrenay2023TERIS].
- **Early evidence** ARISE (12 US centres, n = 87 with RIS) randomised dimethyl fumarate 240 mg twice daily or placebo for 96 weeks. Dimethyl fumarate reduced the risk of a first clinical demyelinating event (unadjusted HR 0.18, 95% CI 0.05–0.63; p = 0.007). Moderate adverse reactions were more frequent on the drug (32% vs 21%), while severe events were similar [Okuda2023].
- **Early evidence** TERIS (France, Switzerland and Turkey; n = 89 with RIS) randomised teriflunomide 14 mg daily or placebo. Teriflunomide lengthened the time to a first clinical event (unadjusted HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.16–0.84; adjusted HR 0.28, 0.11–0.71), a 63% and 72% relative risk reduction respectively. MRI secondary endpoints were not significant [LebrunFrenay2023TERIS].
- **Hypothesis** EBV vaccines that might one day be tested for MS prevention are in early trials. Moderna's mRNA-1189 is in a phase 1/2 trial in 10- to 30-year-olds (n = 867) [NCT05164094], and Moderna's mRNA-1195 is in phase 1 (n = 482) [NCT05831111]. An NIAID gp350-ferritin nanoparticle vaccine completed phase 1 (n = 83) [NCT04645147]. None of these trials has MS as an outcome [Aloisi2023].

## Controversies
- Is EBV a cause of MS or only a strong marker? For causation: a 32-fold risk after seroconversion, NfL rising only after seroconversion, and near-universal seropositivity in MS [Bjornevik2022; Levin2010; Abrahamyan2020]. Mechanistic mimicry data (EBNA1–GlialCAM, EBNA1–ANO2) support this [Lanz2022; Tengvall2019; Thomas2026]. Against a simple causal model: most infected people never develop MS, so host factors such as HLA and the control of autoreactive cells must decide outcome [Robinson2022; Vietzen2023]. A 2026 population-based letter reports MS onset before evidence of EBV infection, details unverified [Rotstein2026]. Current status: most reviews call EBV a probable necessary but not sufficient cause [Aloisi2023].
- Does vitamin D supplementation alter MS risk or activity? For a causal role: prospective serology, neonatal levels and Mendelian randomisation [Munger2006; Nielsen2017; Mokry2015]. D-Lay MS reduced combined disease activity in CIS, driven by MRI outcomes [Thouvenot2025]. Against: SOLAR and CHOLINE missed their primary endpoints [Hupperts2019; Camu2019], and D-Lay showed no difference in relapses or any clinical secondary outcome [Thouvenot2025]. Current status: no trial has tested whether vitamin D prevents MS onset, and the evidence for disease activity rests mainly on MRI outcomes.
- When does migration-related risk get fixed? Gale and Martyn's review suggests the first two decades of life [Gale1995]. An Australian migrant study found little effect of age at migration from the UK/Ireland and argued that exposure acts over many years [Hammond2000]. Current status: unresolved, and the evidence for a "before age 15" cut-off is weak.
- Is the latitude gradient still present? A 2010 meta-regression questioned the gradient for incidence in Europe and North America [KochHenriksen2010]. A 2019 prevalence meta-analysis found it persists and has grown stronger [Simpson2019]. Current status: the two papers measured different things (incidence versus prevalence) over different study periods.
- Is shift work a true risk factor? Two Swedish case-control studies found ORs of about 1.3–2.1 for shift work in youth [Hedstrom2011Shift]. The prospective Nurses' Health Studies found no overall association [Papantoniou2019]. Current status: inconsistent, with possible recall bias in the case-control designs.
- Is rs10191329 a real severity locus? It was found and replicated in more than 22,000 cases [IMSGC2023], and it is linked to retinal and brain atrophy [Bsteh2025; Corsten2026]. It showed no link with longitudinal severity in MSBase [Campagna2024]. Current status: promising but not yet clinically applicable.
- How large is familial risk? Older clinic-based figures were higher. Population registries give λs of about 7, with age-adjusted sibling risks of about 2.5% and child risks of about 2%, and a dizygotic-twin risk that did not differ significantly from controls in Sweden [Westerlind2014; Nielsen2005]. Twin concordance estimates range from about 17% (age-adjusted, Sweden) to about 25% (Denmark, Canada) [Westerlind2014; Hansen2005; Willer2003].
- Are polygenic risk scores useful? They achieve AUCs of about 0.73–0.80 and identify a high-risk top decile [Shams2023]. A prediction review argues they help enrich prevention trials but are unlikely to be clinically useful for individuals [Hone2021].
- Do alcohol and coffee protect? Inverse associations come mainly from retrospective case-control studies by one research group [Hedstrom2014Alcohol; Hedstrom2016]. No trial or Mendelian randomisation data were reviewed here, so these remain associations only.
