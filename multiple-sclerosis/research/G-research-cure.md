# G. Research toward a cure
This area covers what a cure for MS would have to achieve and where research stands on each part of it: progressive MS and "smouldering" (compartmentalised) inflammation; trials in progressive MS, including the failures; BTK inhibitors; remyelination; autologous haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (aHSCT); EBV-targeted approaches; CAR-T and tolerance strategies; the biomarkers that make shorter trials possible; and ideas that have been rejected (CCSVI). Every trial ID below was checked against the ClinicalTrials.gov v2 API on 2026-10-20 (status, enrolment and whether results are posted). Papers were checked in PubMed, and the numbers come from Europe PMC abstracts. Notes and limits: (1) I could read abstracts only, not full texts (NEJM and Lancet), so per-arm details not in an abstract are left out. (2) Several 2025–2026 phase 3 readouts (PERSEUS, FENtrepid, FENhance 1/2, REMODEL-1/2, PIPE-307 VISTA, ATA188 EMBOLD) have no peer-reviewed paper yet; they are cited from the sponsors' own press releases, marked "(unverified)" and should be treated as topline only. (3) The brief gave MIST (Burt 2019) as "3/52 vs 30/55". The JAMA abstract says progression occurred in 3 patients in the HSCT group and 34 in the DMT group (55 randomised per arm), and I use those numbers. (4) The brief's "~0.3% treatment-related mortality in recent series" is not in any abstract I read. I use the figures that are: a pooled 2.1% that was higher in older studies (Sormani 2017), 2.8% in the 1995–2006 EBMT cohort, and 0 deaths in 174 Swedish patients. (5) "NET-MS" was not found on ClinicalTrials.gov or ISRCTN and is left out. STAR-MS is registered as ISRCTN88667898 (StarMS), not as an NCT. (6) CCMR One is ISRCTN14265371. (7) Shared keys Montalban2017 (PMID 28002688), Thompson2018Lancet (PMID 29576504) and Bjornevik2022 (PMID 35025605) were re-checked and are correct. Reich2018 was confirmed (PMID 29320652) but is not cited, because it has no abstract to check numbers against.

## Facts
### What a cure would need to do
- **Established** The Lancet seminar on MS names the treatment gap: the biggest remaining challenge is to develop treatments that add neuroprotection and remyelination, to treat and ultimately prevent the disabling progressive forms [Thompson2018Lancet].
- **Established** An international expert group led by Kuhlmann (Lancet Neurol 2023) proposes treating MS as a continuum rather than separate relapsing-remitting, secondary progressive and primary progressive types. In this view, apparent progression reflects a partial shift from localised acute injury to widespread inflammation and neurodegeneration, together with failing compensation (neuroplasticity, remyelination); ageing raises vulnerability and lowers resilience [Kuhlmann2023].
- **Early evidence** Taken together, these reviews mean a cure would have four parts: stop relapsing (peripheral) inflammation, stop compartmentalised or smouldering CNS inflammation, repair myelin, and protect axons and neurons [Kuhlmann2023; Giovannoni2022; Thompson2018Lancet].
- **Established** In the pooled OPERA I/II trials of relapsing MS, progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA) caused most 12-week composite confirmed disability accumulation over 96 weeks: 78.0% of events on interferon beta-1a and 88.0% on ocrelizumab. Relapse-associated worsening explained about 17.7% [Kappos2020].
- **Hypothesis** Giovannoni and colleagues argue that the "real MS" is a smouldering process. On this view, aiming for "no evident inflammatory disease activity" cannot fully prevent disability accumulation, and treatment must also target other brain and spinal cord processes [Giovannoni2022].
- **Early evidence** A 2024 international consensus statement proposes the term smouldering-associated worsening (SAW). It covers physical and cognitive decline driven by smouldering processes, which goes beyond the definition of PIRA, and sets out candidate clinical, MRI and fluid biomarkers to track it [Scalfari2024].
- **Early evidence** Pathology shows that compartmentalised inflammation in established lesions consists of tissue-resident CD8+ T cells and B cells, some of which become plasma cells. These cells lose molecules needed to leave tissue (S1P1, CCR7) and gain CD103, which may explain why inflammation stays trapped behind the blood-brain barrier [MachadoSantos2018].
- **Established** The International Progressive MS Alliance states that infiltrative inflammation can now be treated well but that neurodegeneration and compartmentalised inflammation remain "virtually untargeted". It is funding experimental-medicine trials to speed up therapies for progressive MS [Dangond2021].
- **Early evidence** The National MS Society's refined "Pathways to Cures" roadmap (2024 Global Summit, about 200 participants from 15 countries) sets out three pathways toward cures and calls for global data sharing and sustained, coordinated funding [Bebo2024].

### Progressive MS trials: what worked, what failed
- **Established** ORATORIO (ocrelizumab vs placebo, PPMS, n=732; NCT01194570, completed, results posted): 12-week confirmed disability progression was 32.9% vs 39.3% (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.59–0.98). Section E has the details [Montalban2017].
- **Established** EXPAND (siponimod vs placebo, SPMS, n=1651; NCT01665144, completed, results posted): 3-month confirmed disability progression was 26% vs 32% (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.65–0.95; relative risk reduction 21%). 64% of participants had no relapse in the previous 2 years [Kappos2018].
- **Established** MS-STAT2 (simvastatin 80 mg vs placebo, SPMS, EDSS 4.0–6.5, n=964, 31 UK centres; NCT03387670, completed, no results posted): 6-month confirmed progression was 40% vs 36% (adjusted HR 1.13, 95% CI 0.91–1.39; p=0.26). This was negative, even though the phase 2b MS-STAT had shown 43% slower brain atrophy. The authors conclude that simvastatin use in MS should be limited to existing vascular indications [Chataway2025].
- **Early evidence** SPRINT-MS (ibudilast up to 100 mg/day vs placebo for 96 weeks, n=255, PPMS or SPMS; NCT01982942, completed, results posted): brain parenchymal fraction changed by −0.0010/year vs −0.0019/year (difference 0.0009, 95% CI 0.00004–0.0017; p=0.04), about 2.5 mL less tissue loss over 96 weeks. Gastrointestinal effects, headache and depression were more common on ibudilast [Fox2018].
- **Early evidence** In a single-centre pilot (SPMS, n=51; NCT01188811), lipoic acid 1200 mg/day cut annualised brain atrophy by 68% (−0.21% vs −0.65%/year; p=0.002) [Spain2017].
- **Established** The phase 2 follow-up trial, LAPMS (lipoic acid 1200 mg/day vs placebo for 24 months, n=115, PPMS or SPMS; NCT03161028, completed, results posted), was negative: walking speed did not differ (−0.39 vs −0.30 ft/s; difference −0.08, 95% CI −0.33 to 0.17). Discontinuation was higher on lipoic acid (37% vs 17%), and proteinuria was a newly described adverse event [Spain2026].
- **Established** MS-SPI2 (high-dose biotin MD1003 100 mg three times daily, n=642, progressive MS; NCT02936037, terminated, results posted): confirmed improvement at month 12 (confirmed at month 15) occurred in 12% vs 9% (OR 1.35, 95% CI 0.81–2.26), not significant. The authors say MD1003 cannot be recommended and note that it interferes with laboratory tests [Cree2020].
- **Established** MS-SMART (amiloride, fluoxetine or riluzole vs placebo, SPMS, n=445; NCT01910259, completed): no drug changed percentage brain volume at 96 weeks (amiloride 0.0%, fluoxetine −0.1%, riluzole −0.1% vs placebo). The trial is a template for multi-arm testing [Chataway2020].
- **Established** ASCEND (natalizumab vs placebo, SPMS, n=889; NCT01416181, terminated, results posted): the primary multicomponent endpoint was negative (44% vs 48% progressed; OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.66–1.13). The 9-Hole Peg Test component favoured natalizumab (OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.40–0.80; nominal p=0.001) [Kapoor2018].
- **Early evidence** Masitinib study AB07002 (PPMS or non-active SPMS, n=611; NCT01433497): at 4.5 mg/kg/day, overall EDSS change was 0.001 vs 0.098 on placebo (difference −0.097, 97% CI −0.192 to −0.002; p=0.0256); the uptitrated 6.0 mg/kg arm was inconclusive. The confirmatory phase 3, MAXIMS (NCT05441488, n=800), is listed as SUSPENDED on ClinicalTrials.gov [Vermersch2022].
- **Established** MUSETTE (RMS, n=860) and GAVOTTE (PPMS, n=753) found that a bodyweight-adjusted higher dose of ocrelizumab (1200/1800 mg) was no better than 600 mg. 12-week composite confirmed disability progression was 34% vs 37% (HR 0.93) in MUSETTE and 47% vs 49% (HR 0.95) in GAVOTTE. More peripheral anti-CD20 exposure did not reach the progressive process (NCT04544436, NCT04548999) [Hauser2026].

### BTK inhibitors: the first drugs aimed at smouldering inflammation
- **Established** HERCULES (tolebrutinib 60 mg vs placebo, non-relapsing SPMS, n=1131, 2:1, median follow-up 133 weeks; NCT04411641, completed, results posted): 6-month confirmed disability progression was 22.6% vs 30.7% (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.55–0.88; p=0.003) [Fox2025].
- **Established** In HERCULES, serious adverse events occurred in 15.0% vs 10.4%, and ALT rose above 3× the upper limit of normal in 4.0% vs 1.6% [Fox2025].
- **Established** GEMINI 1 and 2 (tolebrutinib vs teriflunomide, relapsing MS, n=974 and 899; NCT04410978, NCT04410991, completed, results posted) were not superior on relapse rate. Annualised relapse rates were 0.13 vs 0.12 (rate ratio 1.06) and 0.11 vs 0.11 (rate ratio 1.00). Pooled 6-month confirmed disability worsening was 8.3% vs 11.3% (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.53–0.95), but this was not formally tested. Petechiae occurred in 4.5% vs 0.3% [Oh2025].
- **Established** Across 2958 people exposed to tolebrutinib in phase 3 trials, ALT rose above 3× the upper limit of normal in 4.7% and above 20× in 0.57%. Six people (0.20%) met Hy's Law criteria; all cases began within 90 days. One participant needed a liver transplant and died of postoperative complications. Weekly liver testing for 12 weeks detected rises earlier (40 vs 60 days) [Maxwell2026].
- **Established** PERSEUS (tolebrutinib vs placebo, PPMS; NCT04458051, completed, n=767, no results posted) did not meet its primary endpoint, time to 6-month composite confirmed disability progression (Sanofi, 15 December 2025). Sanofi will not seek approval in PPMS, and no effect sizes were released [Sanofi2025PERSEUS; Maxwell2026].
- **Established** The FDA issued a complete response letter for tolebrutinib in non-relapsing SPMS (Sanofi release, 24 December 2025), calling it a change from earlier agency feedback. The drug is not approved in the US [Sanofi2025CRL].
- **Established** The EMA's CHMP gave a positive opinion on 23 April 2026 for tolebrutinib (Cenrifki) "for the treatment of adult patients with SPMS without relapses in the last 2 years". It cites a 31% reduction in 6-month confirmed disability progression and 38% fewer new or enlarging T2 lesions, and calls drug-induced liver injury the "most worrisome side effect". The EMA page lists the medicine as authorised on 19 June 2026, under additional monitoring, with mandatory liver testing [EMA2026Cenrifki].
- **Established** evolutionRMS 1 and 2 (evobrutinib vs teriflunomide, n=2290; NCT04338022, NCT04338061, both terminated, results posted) were not superior: annualised relapse rates were 0.15 vs 0.14 and 0.11 vs 0.11. Liver enzymes above 5× the upper limit of normal were more common on evobrutinib (5.0%), mostly in the first 12 weeks. The authors say the data do not support its use [Montalban2024].
- **Early evidence** FENopta (fenebrutinib vs placebo, relapsing MS, phase 2, n=109): 69% fewer new gadolinium-enhancing lesions at weeks 4–12 (p=0.0022). The annualised relapse rate was 0.04 through week 48 of the open-label extension [BarOr2025].
- **Early evidence** FENtrepid (fenebrutinib vs ocrelizumab, PPMS, n=985; NCT04544449, ACTIVE_NOT_RECRUITING, no results posted) met non-inferiority on 12-week composite confirmed disability progression (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.75–1.03) according to the sponsor (7 February 2026). Liver enzyme rises occurred in 13.3% vs 2.9%, and deaths in 1.4% vs 0.2% (judged unrelated by investigators). There is no peer-reviewed paper yet [Roche2026FENtrepid].
- **Early evidence** FENhance 1 and 2 (fenebrutinib vs teriflunomide, relapsing MS; NCT04586010, NCT04586023, no results posted) cut annualised relapse rates by 51.1% and 58.5% according to the sponsor (AAN, April 2026). Disability endpoints did not reach significance (cCDP12 HR 0.80 and 0.87). Seven deaths (0.9%) occurred on fenebrutinib vs 1 (0.1%) on teriflunomide during the reporting period [Roche2026FENhance].
- **Early evidence** REMODEL-1 and 2 (remibrutinib 100 mg vs teriflunomide, relapsing MS, about 2000 participants; NCT05147220, NCT05156281, ACTIVE_NOT_RECRUITING) met their relapse-rate primary endpoints according to Novartis (1 September 2026). 6-month confirmed disability progression was nominally significant in a pooled analysis, and Novartis reports no Hy's Law cases. No numbers have been published yet [Novartis2026REMODEL].

### Remyelination: repairing myelin
- **Early evidence** ReBUILD (clemastine fumarate, relapsing MS with chronic optic neuropathy, n=50, crossover; NCT02040298, completed, results posted) shortened VEP P100 latency delay by 1.7 ms per eye (95% CI 0.5–2.9; p=0.0048). This was the first RCT to show a remyelinating drug effect. Fatigue was the main side effect [Green2017].
- **Early evidence** In the NIH TRAP-MS platform trial (NCT03109288, still recruiting overall), the clemastine arm in people with PIRA was stopped by protocol when 3 of 9 patients triggered safety stopping criteria. Disability progression slopes were higher than in other participants, and CSF proteomics showed increased purinergic signalling and pyroptosis [Kocot2025].
- **Hypothesis** Laboratory work in the same report found that clemastine plus extracellular ATP triggered pyroptotic death of macrophages and iPSC-derived human oligodendrocytes, which a P2RX7 antagonist blocked. A CSF pyroptosis score was higher in progressive than in relapsing MS [Kocot2025].
- **Established** Opicinumab (anti-LINGO-1) failed in three phase 2 trials. In RENEW (acute optic neuritis, n=82; NCT01721161), the ITT VEP latency difference was −3.5 ms (p=0.33) [Cadavid2017].
- **Established** In SYNERGY (relapsing MS, n=419; NCT01864148), confirmed disability improvement showed no linear dose-response: 49% on placebo vs 40–65% across doses (trend p=0.89) [Cadavid2019].
- **Established** In AFFINITY Part 1 (n=263; NCT03222973, terminated, results posted), the Overall Disability Response Score difference was 0.15 (95% CI −0.05 to 0.35; p=0.148) [Calabresi2026].
- **Established** CCMR One (bexarotene, relapsing-remitting MS on dimethyl fumarate, n=52; ISRCTN14265371) missed its primary MRI endpoint: the lesional magnetisation transfer ratio difference was 0.16 pu (95% CI −0.39 to 0.71). Every participant on bexarotene had an adverse event, including central hypothyroidism in all 26 and hypertriglyceridaemia in 24. The authors do not recommend it [Brown2021].
- **Early evidence** A follow-up of 20 CCMR One participants about 27 months later found a lasting VEP latency difference of −7.79 ms (95% CI −14.76 to −0.82) favouring bexarotene. The authors suggest this reflects long-term remyelination [McMurran2024].
- **Early evidence** CCMR Two (metformin plus clemastine vs placebo for 6 months, relapsing-remitting MS on DMT, n=70; NCT05131828, completed, no results posted) has VEP P100 latency as its primary outcome. It is powered to detect a 3 ms difference, and the rationale is that metformin reverses age-related unresponsiveness of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells [RiboniVerri2025].
- **Early evidence** Preliminary CCMR Two results (ECTRIMS, September 2025) were reported by the University of Cambridge: VEP signal speed slowed on placebo but held steady on the combination. Participants did not feel better, and no peer-reviewed numbers are available [Cambridge2025].
- **Early evidence** PIPE-307, a selective M1 muscarinic receptor antagonist, promoted remyelination in mouse EAE [Poon2024]. PNAS later published an Editorial Expression of Concern about that paper [PNAS2025EoC].
- **Established** The phase 2 VISTA trial of PIPE-307 (relapsing-remitting MS; NCT06083753, completed, n=182) missed its primary endpoint (low-contrast letter acuity) and all secondary efficacy endpoints, according to the sponsor (20 November 2025) [Contineum2025].
- **Established** High-dose biotin, proposed to boost neuronal and oligodendrocyte energy for repair, did not improve disability in MS-SPI2 [Cree2020].

### Autologous haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (aHSCT)
- **Early evidence** MIST (non-myeloablative cyclophosphamide/ATG aHSCT vs higher-efficacy or different-class DMT, relapsing-remitting MS with at least 2 relapses on DMT in the prior year, n=110; NCT00273364, completed, results posted): progression occurred in 3 patients after aHSCT vs 34 on DMT (HR 0.07, 95% CI 0.02–0.24). Median time to progression was 24 months in the DMT arm. The authors call it a preliminary study, and the comparator arm excluded alemtuzumab and ocrelizumab-era options [Burt2019].
- **Early evidence** EBMT long-term cohort (n=281, 1995–2006, 78% progressive MS, median EDSS 6.5): 5-year progression-free survival was 46% and overall survival 93%. Transplant-related mortality within 100 days was 2.8% (8 deaths). Older age, progressive course and more than 2 prior DMTs predicted progression [Muraro2017].
- **Early evidence** A meta-analysis of 15 studies (764 patients) found pooled transplant-related mortality of 2.1% (95% CI 1.3–3.4%). Mortality was higher in older studies, in studies with fewer relapsing-remitting patients and with higher baseline EDSS. NEDA was 83% at 2 years and 67% at 5 years [Sormani2017].
- **Early evidence** Swedish national cohort (relapsing-remitting MS, n=174, median follow-up 5.5 years): NEDA was 73% at 5 years and 65% at 10 years. Of 149 patients with baseline disability, 54% improved, 37% were stable and 9% worsened. There were no treatment-related deaths, and febrile neutropenia occurred in 68% [Silfverberg2024].
- **Early evidence** Norwegian cohort (aggressive relapsing-remitting MS, n=29, mean follow-up 70 months): 69% kept NEDA-3, 90% were free of progression, and full-time work rose from 3% to 52% at 5 years [Kvistad2024].
- **Early evidence** Italian long-term cohort (n=210, mean follow-up 6.2 years): disability worsening-free survival was 85.5% at 5 years and 71.3% at 10 years in relapsing-remitting MS, vs 71.0% and 57.2% in progressive MS [Boffa2021].
- **Established** Guidelines: the EBMT 2020 guidelines describe growing evidence for aHSCT in highly active relapsing-remitting MS that fails DMTs, with a possible role in inflammatory progressive MS. They state that treatment should take place in accredited centres [Sharrack2020].
- **Established** The 2025 ECTRIMS–EBMT consensus calls aHSCT after failure of high-efficacy DMT in aggressive relapsing-remitting MS a "generally accepted indication". Its use in treatment-naive aggressive MS, progressive MS and NMOSD remains uncertain [Muraro2025].
- **Early evidence** Ongoing randomised trials of aHSCT against high-efficacy DMT:
  - BEAT-MS: BEAM/ATG aHSCT vs best available therapy, including ocrelizumab, natalizumab, alemtuzumab and cladribine (NCT04047628, RECRUITING, n=156, primary completion 2030).
  - RAM-MS: aHSCT vs alemtuzumab, cladribine or ocrelizumab (NCT03477500, Norway, ACTIVE_NOT_RECRUITING, n=100, primary completion November 2026).
  - StarMS (UK): aHSCT vs alemtuzumab, ocrelizumab, ofatumumab or cladribine, with NEDA as primary outcome (ISRCTN88667898, target 198, final enrolment 94, recruitment ended September 2024).
  - COAST (Germany; NCT04971005) was terminated after enrolling 1 participant [Muraro2025; CTgov2026].

### EBV-targeted approaches
- **Established** In a cohort of more than 10 million young adults on active US military duty (955 MS cases), MS risk rose 32-fold after EBV infection but not after other viruses such as cytomegalovirus. Serum NfL rose only after EBV seroconversion, which makes EBV the leading candidate cause and a target for prevention and treatment [Bjornevik2022].
- **Hypothesis** Molecular mimicry: clonally expanded CSF B cells in MS bind both EBV EBNA1 and the CNS protein GlialCAM, with high-affinity cross-reactivity. EBNA1 immunisation worsens disease in a mouse model [Lanz2022].
- **Early evidence** EMBOLD (ATA188, allogeneic EBV-targeted T cells, non-active progressive MS; NCT03283826, terminated, n=134, no results posted) missed its primary endpoint of confirmed disability improvement on EDSS at 12 months. The ATA188 arm improved by about 6% vs a placebo rate of about 16% (sponsor, 8 November 2023), and biomarkers gave no support [Atara2023].
- **Early evidence** Moderna's mRNA-1189 EBV vaccine is in phase 1/2 in healthy 10–30-year-olds (NCT05164094, ACTIVE_NOT_RECRUITING, n=867). mRNA-1195 is in phase 1 in healthy adults (NCT05831111) and in a phase 2 safety trial in people with early relapsing MS, CIS or RIS (NCT06735248, RECRUITING, n=180, EBV-seropositive) [CTgov2026].
- **Early evidence** The NIAID gp350-ferritin nanoparticle EBV vaccine completed phase 1 in healthy adults (NCT04645147, n=83, results posted). It aims to prevent infection, so any effect on MS would take years to show [CTgov2026].
- **Early evidence** Antivirals: the raltegravir INSPIRE trial (n=20, 3 months) had no effect on gadolinium-enhancing lesions [Gold2018].
- **Hypothesis** Tenofovir: a single case report describes no relapses for nearly 5 years while a person with MS and HIV took a tenofovir-containing regimen, with activity returning after switching [Torkildsen2024]. A planned tenofovir alafenamide trial (NCT04880577) was withdrawn [CTgov2026].
- **Early evidence** Other antiviral trials: a famciclovir trial is registered (NCT05283551, status UNKNOWN). The Australian STOP-MS multi-arm trial of spironolactone and famciclovir in progressive MS began enrolling in April 2025 (ACTRN12623000849695) [Ward2025; CTgov2026].

### CAR-T, tolerance and getting therapy into the CNS
- **Early evidence** First reports of CD19 CAR-T (KYV-101) in 2 people with progressive MS: safety was acceptable, there was no neurotoxicity (ICANS) even though CAR-T cells were found in CSF, and intrathecal antibody production fell in one patient [Fischbach2024].
- **Early evidence** Anti-BCMA CAR-T (CT103A; NCT04561557) in 5 people with progressive MS: only grade 1 cytokine release syndrome occurred. Plasma cells were depleted in CNS compartments and microglial activation was reduced [Qin2025].
- **Early evidence** Registered CD19 CAR-T trials in progressive MS include:
  - KYSA-7, Kyverna (NCT06384976, phase 2, n=120, ACTIVE_NOT_RECRUITING), for disease that kept progressing on anti-CD20.
  - A UCSF KYV-101 study (NCT06451159, phase 1, n=10).
  - A Stanford study (NCT06138132, phase 1, n=12, RECRUITING).
  - A Novartis YTB323 study (NCT06617793), in relapsing MS [CTgov2026].
- **Early evidence** Tolerance: ATX-MS-1467, a myelin-peptide immunotherapy, reduced gadolinium-enhancing lesions in open-label studies of relapsing MS (n=43 and n=37; NCT01973491). This is Class IV evidence, and no randomised efficacy trial followed [Chataway2018].
- **Hypothesis** In mice, a non-inflammatory nanoparticle mRNA vaccine encoding autoantigens suppressed several MS models by inducing regulatory T cells with bystander suppression. There is no human trial evidence yet [Krienke2021].
- **Established** RIVITaLISe, a trial of intravenous plus intrathecal rituximab (NCT01212094, terminated), killed CSF B cells (median −79.7%) but depleted B cells in CNS tissue poorly (about 10–20%). This shows why antibody drugs struggle to reach compartmentalised inflammation [Komori2016].
- **Early evidence** Frexalimab (anti-CD40L) reduced new gadolinium-enhancing lesions in phase 2 (rate ratio 0.11 vs placebo for 1200 mg IV) [Vermersch2024]. Phase 3 trials are ongoing in non-relapsing SPMS (FREVIVA, NCT06141486, n=943) and relapsing MS (FREXALT, NCT06141473, n=1655) [CTgov2026].

### Biomarkers that make faster trials possible
- **Established** Serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL): a reference database of 10,133 control samples shows sNfL rises with age, faster after about 50 years. In MS, an sNfL Z score above 1.5 was linked to future clinical or MRI activity (OR 3.15, 95% CI 2.35–4.23) [Benkert2022].
- **Early evidence** The International Progressive MS Alliance's biomarker panel concluded that sNfL can be measured reliably and predicts the disease course. However, active inflammation drives much of it, so separating smouldering progression from focal activity is hard [Kapoor2020].
- **Early evidence** Serum GFAP (Swiss MS Cohort, n=355) was prognostic for future PIRA and complements sNfL. It rises about 1.5% per year with age in healthy controls [Meier2023].
- **Early evidence** Paramagnetic rim lesions (PRL), seen on susceptibility MRI in 56% of 192 patients, mark chronic active lesions. People with 4 or more rims reached motor and cognitive disability earlier, and rim lesions expanded (+2.2%/year) while rimless lesions shrank (−3.6%/year) [Absinta2019].
- **Early evidence** Slowly expanding/evolving lesions (SELs) can be detected automatically on conventional MRI. In ocrelizumab trial cohorts they were more frequent in PPMS (n=555) than in relapsing MS (n=1334), and they are a candidate marker of smouldering inflammation [Elliott2019].
- **Early evidence** A NAIMS consensus (2024) sets definitions for chronic active lesion biomarkers: PRL, SELs and TSPO-PET. It notes that the measures overlap but are not equivalent and that standardisation is lacking [Bagnato2024].

### Rejected idea: CCSVI and venous angioplasty
- **Rejected** In 2009, Zamboni reported a strong association between MS and "chronic cerebrospinal venous insufficiency" (CCSVI) on Doppler ultrasound in 65 patients vs 235 controls (OR 43) [Zamboni2009].
- **Rejected** A blinded Canadian case-control study using catheter venography found CCSVI criteria in 2% of people with MS, 2% of siblings and 3% of controls. More than 50% vein narrowing was equally common in all three groups (74%, 66% and 70%) [Traboulsee2014].
- **Rejected** Brave Dreams (sham-controlled venous angioplasty, relapsing-remitting MS, n=115; NCT01371760) found no functional benefit: the composite improved in 41.7% after angioplasty vs 48.7% after sham (OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.34–1.68). The authors' own conclusion was that angioplasty is not recommended [Zamboni2018].

## Controversies
- Is tolebrutinib's benefit in non-relapsing SPMS real progression control? Yes: HERCULES met its primary endpoint (HR 0.69) [Fox2025], and the EMA approved it for SPMS without relapses in the last 2 years [EMA2026Cenrifki]. Doubtful: the FDA issued a complete response letter [Sanofi2025CRL], and tolebrutinib showed no meaningful benefit in PPMS (PERSEUS) [Maxwell2026; Sanofi2025PERSEUS]. Status: the US and EU disagree as of October 2026, and liver injury (6 Hy's Law cases, 1 death after transplant) shapes the benefit-risk debate [Maxwell2026].
- Do BTK inhibitors work through CNS (microglial) effects or only peripheral ones? Class effect: tolebrutinib slowed disability with no relapse-rate advantage over teriflunomide [Oh2025], and fenebrutinib was non-inferior to ocrelizumab in PPMS [Roche2026FENtrepid]. Drug-specific: evobrutinib failed [Montalban2024] and tolebrutinib failed in PPMS [Sanofi2025PERSEUS]. Status: unresolved; differences in CNS penetration, selectivity and dosing are proposed explanations.
- Is clemastine a remyelinating drug or a harmful one in progressive MS? Remyelinating: ReBUILD showed shorter VEP latency (1.7 ms/eye) in relapsing MS [Green2017]. Harmful: the TRAP-MS arm in PIRA patients was stopped for faster disability accumulation and signs of pyroptosis [Kocot2025]. Status: the CCMR Two combination trial has completed, but full peer-reviewed results are not yet out [RiboniVerri2025; Cambridge2025].
- Does a remyelination signal on VEP mean clinical benefit? Possibly: the bexarotene VEP effect lasted about 27 months [McMurran2024]. Not shown: no remyelination trial has yet improved disability, and CCMR Two participants did not feel better [Brown2021; Cambridge2025; Calabresi2026].
- Should aHSCT be standard of care for highly active relapsing MS? Yes: Swedish authors recommend it as standard of care for highly active RRMS [Silfverberg2024], and ECTRIMS–EBMT calls it generally accepted after high-efficacy DMT fails [Muraro2025]. Caution: MIST's comparator excluded today's high-efficacy antibodies, and pooled transplant-related mortality was 2.1% [Burt2019; Sormani2017]. Status: BEAT-MS, RAM-MS and StarMS are head-to-head trials against high-efficacy DMT and are still awaited.
- Are brain-atrophy phase 2 signals reliable stepping stones? Supportive: ibudilast and lipoic acid slowed atrophy in phase 2 [Fox2018; Spain2017]. Against: simvastatin's 43% atrophy reduction did not lead to a disability benefit in phase 3, and lipoic acid failed on walking speed [Chataway2025; Spain2026]. Status: imaging surrogates are being joined by sNfL, GFAP, PRL and SELs, which are not yet validated as trial endpoints [Kapoor2020; Bagnato2024].
- Can targeting EBV treat established MS? Plausible: causal epidemiology and EBNA1–GlialCAM mimicry support the idea [Bjornevik2022; Lanz2022]. Not yet: EBV-specific T cells (ATA188) and raltegravir failed [Atara2023; Gold2018]. Status: vaccines may be more relevant to prevention, and antiviral trials are under way (STOP-MS) [Ward2025].
- Was CCSVI ever a real MS mechanism? The original report claimed it [Zamboni2009]. Blinded venography [Traboulsee2014] and a sham-controlled trial by the original investigators [Zamboni2018] did not support it. Status: rejected.
