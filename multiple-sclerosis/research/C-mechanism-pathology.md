## Facts
### Myelin, nodes of Ranvier and normal saltatory conduction
- Compact multilayered myelin raises conduction velocity from less than 1 m/s (unmyelinated) to 50–100 m/s without any increase in axon diameter [Kister2022].
- In the CNS a single oligodendrocyte myelinates 40–60 different axons but only one segment (internode) on each, so each CNS axon is myelinated by many oligodendrocytes. In the PNS a Schwann cell myelinates only one segment of a single axon, and many Schwann cells line one long peripheral axon [Kister2022].
- Individual oligodendrocytes in similar tracts vary greatly in the number and length of the internodes they make. Competition between neighbouring oligodendroglia, acting through the amino-terminal region of Nogo-A, limits this "myelinogenic potential" (mouse) [Chong2012].
- Sheath length is partly intrinsic to the oligodendrocyte. Spinal-cord oligodendrocytes make longer sheaths than cortical ones even on inert microfibres, and sheath length sets node spacing and therefore conduction velocity [Bechler2015].
- The node of Ranvier is a ~1 μm gap in the myelin where the axon membrane meets the extracellular space and Na+ enters through voltage-gated channels. In the CNS the nodal sodium channels are Nav1.6 and Nav1.1 (Nav1.2 earlier in development), with KCNQ (Kv7) and Kv3.1b potassium channels also at the node [ArancibiaCarcamo2014].
- Drawable order along the axon: node (Nav) → paranode (myelin loops glued to the axon by Caspr/contactin on the axon and neurofascin-155 on the glia) → juxtaparanode, under compact myelin, enriched in Shaker-type Kv1.1/Kv1.2 → internode. The paranode is a diffusion barrier that keeps nodal Nav and juxtaparanodal Kv1 apart, and a seal that stops current leaking under the myelin [ArancibiaCarcamo2014].
- Kv1.1, Kv1.2 and the Kvβ2 subunit colocalise at juxtaparanodes of normal adult rat nerve. In normal nerve neither 4-aminopyridine (4-AP) nor tetraethylammonium alters conduction, consistent with these channels being hidden under myelin [Rasband1998].
- Oligodendrocytes do more than insulate. They are metabolically coupled to the axon through cytoplasm-rich myelinic channels and support long-term axonal integrity, so losing them can harm axons independently of conduction [Kister2022].

### What demyelination does to conduction
- The main cause of the negative symptoms of a relapse (paralysis, blindness, numbness) is conduction block, mainly from demyelination and inflammation. Remission comes from remyelination, resolution of inflammation, or restored conduction along axons that remain demyelinated [Smith1999].
- Some segmentally demyelinated central axons (rat) still conduct, continuously, over demyelinated stretches longer than 2500 μm (several internodes). They conduct more slowly, and their refractory period of transmission is typically 2–5 times normal (up to 34 times) [Felts1997].
- Persistently demyelinated axons conduct single impulses but transmit trains of impulses poorly, which is linked to weakness and fatigue. Positive symptoms (tingling, Lhermitte's phenomenon, pain) come from ectopic impulse generation, ephaptic interactions between axons, and mechanosensitivity of demyelinated axons [Smith1999].
- Ephaptic ("cross-talk") transmission between single fibres has been recorded directly in dysmyelinated spinal roots of dystrophic mice. It ran from a bare axon (<1 m/s) to a myelinated axon (2–10 m/s), took ≤240 μs to cross, and stayed stable at ≥70 Hz. This is an animal model of dysmyelination, not MS tissue [Rasminsky1980].
- In control human tissue Nav1.6 is confined to nodes. In acute MS plaques Nav1.2 and Nav1.6 are spread diffusely along long stretches of demyelinated axon (sodium-channel redistribution that can restore continuous conduction) [Craner2004].
- Nav1.6 carries a persistent sodium current. In acute MS lesions it colocalises with the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger and with β-amyloid precursor protein (an axonal-injury marker), so persistent Na+ influx can drive the exchanger in reverse and load the axon with damaging Ca2+ [Craner2004; Waxman2006].
- Sodium channels have a dual role in MS: they restore conduction after demyelination, and they contribute to axonal degeneration (and to mistuned Purkinje-neuron firing in cerebellar dysfunction) [Waxman2006].
- In MS and EAE the paranode becomes disrupted and myelin retracts. Nodes lengthen, neurofascin-155 is lost, and 4-AP-sensitive Kv1 channels, normally hidden under myelin, move toward or out from under the sheath. There they leak repolarising K+ current and can cause conduction failure [ArancibiaCarcamo2014].
- After demyelination of rat sciatic nerve with lysolecithin, Kv1.1/Kv1.2 spread across nodal and internodal zones by day 6. During remyelination 4-AP markedly increased compound action potential amplitude and duration, tracking how many K+ channels were exposed at nodes [Rasband1998].
- 4-AP and tetraethylammonium prolong action potentials of demyelinated (and unmyelinated) mammalian fibres and restore conduction in fibres blocked by demyelination. 4-AP works at lower concentrations [Sherratt1980].
- The dalfampridine (prolonged-release 4-AP) label describes the drug as a broad-spectrum potassium-channel blocker. In animal studies it increased conduction of action potentials in demyelinated axons. The label states that its mechanism of effect "has not been fully elucidated" [AmpyraLabel2026].
- In the phase III trial, timed-walk responders were 35% (78/224) with fampridine 10 mg twice daily vs 8% (6/72) with placebo. Walking speed improved 25.2% (95% CI 21.5–28.8) in fampridine responders vs 4.7% with placebo (14 weeks) [Goodman2009].
- Temperature sensitivity: cooling, like a drug that prolongs the action potential, can overcome conduction failure in demyelinated fibres. This observation led directly to testing 4-AP [Sherratt1980].
- An estimated 60–80% of people with MS have temporary worsening of signs and symptoms with heat exposure. Higher temperature impairs action-potential propagation in demyelinated axons, causing conduction slowing and/or block [Davis2010].
- Uhthoff's phenomena are brief, stereotyped, reversible symptoms (first described with optic neuritis). Triggers include exercise, fever, infection, hot surroundings, the perimenstrual period and psychological stress. Relapses depend on immune dysregulation, whereas Uhthoff's phenomena come from ion-channel changes plus thermoregulatory derangement that transiently alter conduction in demyelinated axons [Frohman2013].
- Halliday, McDonald and Mushin first reported the delayed (prolonged-latency) visual evoked response in optic neuritis in 1972. This is the electrophysiological signature of slowed conduction in a demyelinated optic nerve [Halliday1972].
- The latency of the P100 wave of the visual evoked potential is now used as a functional readout of remyelination. In ReBUILD, clemastine shortened the P100 latency delay by 1.7 ms per eye (95% CI 0.5–2.9) in chronic demyelinating optic neuropathy (details in pass G) [Green2017].

### Immune attack: activation, egress, entry, reactivation
- **Established** working model: early in MS a peripheral immune response targeting the CNS drives disease, whereas in the progressive phase immune reactions confined within the CNS dominate [Hemmer2015].
- T cells reactive to myelin antigens occur in similar proportions in people with and without MS. So either these cells are dysfunctional in MS or other immune factors are also critical [Reich2018].
- Once inside the CNS, activated Th1, Th17, γδ and CD8+ T cells secrete proinflammatory cytokines. Deficient function of regulatory T cells (CD4+CD25+FoxP3+) is proposed to let the inflammation proceed unchecked [Kieseier2011].
- Th17 cells and the barrier: IL-17 and IL-22 receptors sit on blood-brain-barrier endothelial cells in MS lesions. IL-17 and IL-22 disrupt endothelial tight junctions. Human Th17 cells cross the barrier efficiently, express granzyme B, kill human neurons in vitro and recruit more CD4+ cells [Kebir2007].
- Leaving the lymph node: T and B lymphocytes need the receptor S1P1 to exit thymus and lymph nodes. S1P1 is switched down during lymphocyte activation, which holds cells in the node, and FTY720 (fingolimod) creates a temporary "S1P1-null" state that traps lymphocytes (mouse) [Matloubian2004].
- Fingolimod is phosphorylated to fingolimod-phosphate, which binds 4 of the 5 S1P receptors. It first activates lymphocyte S1P1, then causes its down-regulation (functional antagonism), which blocks egress. It also crosses the blood-brain barrier [Chun2010].
- Getting into the brain (VLA-4/VCAM-1): lymphocytes and monocytes bind inflamed EAE brain vessels through α4β1 integrin (VLA-4). An anti-α4 antibody blocked this binding, prevented leukocyte accumulation in the CNS and prevented EAE (rat) [Yednock1992].
- Natalizumab binds the α4 subunit of α4β1 and α4β7 integrins on all leukocytes except neutrophils. This blocks their adhesion to VCAM-1 on activated endothelium (and MAdCAM-1 in the gut) and so prevents transmigration into inflamed tissue [TyrukoLabel2025].
- Choroid-plexus route: the chemokine CCL20 is constitutively expressed by choroid-plexus epithelium in mice and humans. In EAE, CCR6+ Th17 cells enter first through the choroid plexus and then trigger a second, CCR6-independent wave of effector T cells across activated parenchymal vessels. CCR6-deficient mice resist EAE [Reboldi2009].
- Meningeal route and reactivation (two-photon imaging, rat EAE): effector T cells arrest on leptomeningeal vessels, crawl along the inner vessel surface against blood flow, cross the wall, then scan the outer vessel surface and pia. There they meet phagocytes presenting myelin antigen, which reactivates them to make proinflammatory mediators and invade the tissue [Bartholomaus2009].
- Blood-brain-barrier opening in a new human lesion: vessels near the lesion centre first leak gadolinium. Days later the central breach starts to repair while small capillaries at the lesion edge become leaky [Reich2018].
- Where T cells sit: CD4+ T cells concentrate in the perivascular "cuff", whereas CD8+ T cells spread widely through the parenchyma [Reich2018].
- CD8+ T cells dominate MS lesion infiltrates in all disease courses and lesion stages, including very early acute MS, with a prominent contribution of CD20+ B cells and sparse CD4+ cells [MachadoSantos2018].
- In actively demyelinating lesions most CD8+ T cells belong to a few expanded clones (one clone was 35% of all CD8+ cells in one case), whereas CD4+ cells were more heterogeneous [Babbe2000].
- **Established** for B cells: response to B-cell depletion comes fast (as early as 8–12 weeks), before circulating immunoglobulin falls. So B-cell antigen presentation to helper T cells and cytokine production, rather than antibody, are thought to matter most [Reich2018].
- A single course of rituximab (anti-CD20) cut new gadolinium-enhancing lesions (P<0.001), and the proportion of patients with relapses was 20.3% vs 40.0% with placebo at week 48 (phase 2, n=104) [Hauser2008].
- People with MS have more proinflammatory memory B cells that make GM-CSF. These B cells activate myeloid cells, and B-cell depletion therapy reduced proinflammatory myeloid responses in a GM-CSF-dependent way [Li2015].
- Blood-borne macrophages infiltrate active lesions and clear myelin debris. Microglia are abundant, and once activated they cannot be told apart from macrophages by standard pathology [Reich2018].
- Microglial marker TMEM119 indicates that on average 45% of macrophage-like cells in active lesions come from resident microglia. Early active lesions contain proinflammatory microglia expressing molecules for phagocytosis, oxidative injury, antigen presentation and T-cell co-stimulation. The homeostatic marker P2RY12 is reduced in normal-appearing white matter and lost in active and slowly expanding lesions [Zrzavy2017].
- In established MS, complement and antibodies are consistently associated with macrophages at sites of active demyelination, together with Fcγ receptors on phagocytic macrophages. The authors propose antibody- and complement-mediated myelin phagocytosis as the dominant mechanism [Breij2008].
- EAE is the most widely used animal model. It reproduces inflammation, demyelination, axonal loss and gliosis, plus resolution and remyelination, and many current MS drugs were developed or validated in it [Constantinescu2011].
- Limits of EAE: T cells are its main driver, whereas MS clearly also involves B cells, antibodies and innate immunity. Some models progress clinically, but none reproduces the full spectrum of MS pathology [Reich2018].

### Lesion types (what to draw)
- The 2017 consensus histological classification has three types. Active lesions have macrophages/microglia throughout. Mixed active/inactive (chronic active) lesions have a hypocellular centre with macrophages/microglia only at the border. Inactive lesions are almost free of macrophages [Kuhlmann2017].
- Ongoing demyelination is identified by myelin debris inside macrophages. "Early" demyelinating lesions contain the minor myelin proteins MOG, CNP or MAG in macrophages; "late" ones contain only the major proteins MBP/PLP [Kuhlmann2017].
- Across 120 autopsies (2,476 white-matter plaques), active plaques were most common early and in relapsing-remitting MS. Smouldering, inactive and shadow plaques predominated later, and smouldering plaques were found almost only in progressive MS. Active and inactive plaques were balanced at about age 47, when smouldering plaques began to peak, and men had a higher proportion of smouldering plaques [Frischer2015].
- Iron rims: iron accumulates in proinflammatory microglia/macrophages at the edges of slowly expanding lesions (and some inactive ones) but hardly ever around remyelinated shadow plaques. Rim lesions grew significantly over 3.5 years on 7 T MRI compared with rimless lesions [DalBianco2017].
- In vivo paramagnetic rim lesions: 56% of patients had at least one (cohort of 192 analysable). People with ≥4 rims reached motor and cognitive disability at a younger age. Rimless lesions shrank by 3.6% per year, whereas rim lesions were stable or grew by 2.2% per year, and all 10 expanding rim lesions examined at autopsy were chronic active [Absinta2019].
- At the chronic active lesion edge, single-nucleus RNA sequencing defines "microglia inflamed in MS" (MIMS) and "astrocytes inflamed in MS", which carry neurodegenerative programmes. Complement C1q drives MIMS activation, and blocking C1q damped chronic EAE (mouse) [Absinta2021].
- The NAIMS consensus names three candidate in-vivo markers of chronic active lesions: paramagnetic rim lesions (susceptibility MRI), slowly expanding lesions (T1/T2 MRI) and lesions positive for the 18-kDa translocator protein (TSPO) on PET. They partly overlap, are not equivalent to histology, and standardisation is lacking [Bagnato2024].
- Shadow plaques (remyelinated lesions): remyelination of more than 10% of plaque area was seen in 3 of 82 plaques when death came within 10 weeks of onset, 38/105 at 3–10 months and 19/92 at ≥18 months. The authors concluded that new lesions normally remyelinate unless new activity interrupts them [Prineas1993].
- In 51 autopsies, 20% of patients had extensive remyelination (60–96% of total lesion area remyelinated), including some with progressive MS. Older age at death and longer disease duration went with more remyelinated lesions [Patrikios2006].
- Lucchinetti 2000 (51 biopsies, 32 autopsies with actively demyelinating lesions) described four patterns. Pattern I resembles T-cell-mediated EAE. Pattern II resembles T-cell plus antibody/complement-mediated EAE. Patterns III and IV suggest a primary oligodendrocyte dystrophy (virus- or toxin-like). Patterns differed between patients but were uniform within a patient [Lucchinetti2000].
- In 22 patients biopsied or autopsied at two or more time points, 21 (95%) kept the same immunopathological pattern over time [Metz2014].
- In 12 people with relapsing-remitting MS who died during or soon after a relapse, 7 showed extensive oligodendrocyte apoptosis and microglial activation in myelinated tissue with few or no lymphocytes or myelin phagocytes, a picture not seen in EAE [Barnett2004].

### Cortex and meninges
- Cortical demyelination occurs early. In biopsies of early MS it was found in 53/138 patients (38%); 82% of cortical lesions had CD3+ T-cell infiltrates and 41% showed macrophage-associated demyelination, and meningeal inflammation was topographically associated with cortical demyelination [Lucchinetti2011].
- In 52 autopsies, new active focal white-matter lesions were mainly in acute and relapsing MS. Cortical demyelination and diffuse injury of normal-appearing white matter (with profound microglial activation) were hallmarks of primary and secondary progressive MS and correlated only marginally with focal lesion load [Kutzelnigg2005].
- Ectopic B-cell follicles in the meninges were found in 41.4% of 29 secondary progressive cases and in none of 7 primary progressive cases. Cases with follicles had younger onset, earlier irreversible disability and death, and worse cortical demyelination, and every follicle lay next to a large subpial lesion [Magliozzi2007].
- In 123 secondary progressive cases, B-cell follicle-like structures (with follicular dendritic cells, proliferating B cells and plasma cells) occurred in 49 (40%), mostly in deep sulci of the temporal, cingulate, insular and frontal cortex. The extent of diffuse meningeal inflammation tracked microglial activation and cortical demyelination [Howell2011].
- **Hypothesis**: meningeal and perivascular infiltrates release still-unidentified soluble factors that cause demyelination or neurodegeneration directly or through microglia. The final common pathway is oxidative injury and mitochondrial damage ("virtual hypoxia") [Lassmann2018].

### Axonal injury, atrophy and blood biomarkers
- Axonal transection (counted as terminal axonal ovoids) was found in all 47 lesions examined. Counts were 11,236 transected axons per mm³ in active lesions, 3,138 at the hypocellular edges of chronic active lesions, 875 in their centres, and fewer than 1 in control white matter, with frequency related to inflammation (11 MS brains, 4 controls) [Trapp1998].
- In 67 autopsies, inflammation was present in secondary and primary progressive MS as well as relapsing MS, and plasma cells were most prominent in progressive MS. Axonal injury tracked inflammation, but in elderly patients with long disease (median 76 years old, 372 months' duration) both declined to levels in age-matched controls [Frischer2009].
- Brain atrophy (SIENA, mean 7.5 years' follow-up): annualised brain-volume change was −0.51±0.27%/year in 206 people with MS vs −0.27±0.15%/year in 35 healthy controls. A cut-off of −0.37% best separated the groups (67% sensitivity, 80% specificity), and losses beyond −0.52%/year defined a "pathological" rate with 95% specificity [DeStefano2016].
- Neurofilament light (NfL) is specific to neurons. Serum NfL correlated with CSF NfL, and median levels were 29.6 pg/ml without gadolinium-enhancing lesions, 43.4 pg/ml with brain or spinal enhancing lesions and 62.5 pg/ml with both. Levels above the 97.5th percentile of healthy controls predicted relapses (incidence rate ratio 1.94) and EDSS worsening (OR 2.41) [Disanto2017].
- Reference values: in 10,133 samples from 5,390 people without CNS disease, serum NfL rose exponentially with age, more steeply after about 50, and varied with body-mass index. An age- and BMI-adjusted Z score above 1.5 predicted future clinical or MRI activity (OR 3.15, 95% CI 2.35–4.23), including in people with no evidence of disease activity (OR 2.66, 1.08–6.55). Findings were replicated in 4,341 Swedish patients [Benkert2022].
- Serum NfL Z scores fell to control levels on monoclonal antibodies (alemtuzumab, natalizumab, ocrelizumab, rituximab), fell less on oral DMTs, and stayed elevated on interferons and glatiramer acetate [Benkert2022].
- GFAP (an astrocyte protein) as a progression marker: in controls serum GFAP rose 1.5% per year of age and was 14.9% higher in women. It was 50.9% higher in worsening than stable progressive MS after adjustment for NfL and did not change during relapses. Elevation of both GFAP and NfL Z scores predicted confirmed disability worsening (HR 4.09) and PIRA (HR 4.71) [Meier2023].

### Remyelination biology
- **Established** sequence of remyelination: oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) are activated, migrate, proliferate and differentiate into new myelinating oligodendrocytes [Franklin2017].
- Olig2+ precursors are present in early lesions and periplaque white matter and remain, in lower numbers, in chronic lesions. Maturing precursors (Olig2+/NogoA+) are common in early lesions but rare in chronic ones, pointing to a differentiation block rather than an absence of precursors [Kuhlmann2008].
- In MS, OPCs are often arrested at the plaque edge, or they become premyelinating oligodendrocytes that fail to wrap myelin [Reich2018].
- Ageing: OPCs from aged rats (20–24 months) lose differentiation capacity and stop responding to pro-differentiation signals, with lower metabolic function and more DNA damage. Alternate-day fasting or metformin restored their capacity and improved remyelination in aged animals, through an AMPK-dependent effect (shown by CRISPR knockout of Prkaa2) [Neumann2019].
- A micropillar-array screen (BIMA) of 1,000 bioactive molecules found a cluster of antimuscarinic compounds that enhance oligodendrocyte differentiation and remyelination. This screen is the origin of the clemastine programme (the ReBUILD trial is covered in pass G) [Mei2014; Green2017].
- Carbon-14 birth-dating showed that oligodendrocytes inside human shadow plaques are old. Either remyelination is transient or absent, or pre-existing (not new) oligodendrocytes make the new myelin in MS. New-oligodendrocyte generation rose several-fold in normal-appearing white matter only in a subset with very aggressive MS [Yeung2019].
- **Early evidence**: in large-animal models, surviving mature oligodendrocytes extend new processes and ensheath demyelinated axons while keeping their existing internodes [Duncan2018].
- **Early evidence**: 11C-PiB PET measures individual myelin dynamics. In 20 people with relapsing-remitting MS, the dynamic remyelination index correlated inversely with disability (EDSS β −0.67, p=0.006) [Bodini2016].

### Progressive MS and smouldering disease
- In progressive MS, inflammation persists but sits at least partly behind a closed blood-brain barrier, which makes it harder to treat. Oxidative stress from microglial oxidative burst, amplified by age-related iron accumulation and mitochondrial DNA deletions, is proposed to drive demyelination and neurodegeneration [Lassmann2012].
- Proposed cascade: microglial activation → chronic oxidative injury → accumulating mitochondrial damage in axons, plus age-related iron release → ionic imbalance → axonal and neuronal death [Mahad2015].
- In 13 secondary progressive MS brains, neurons lacking respiratory-chain complex IV activity were far more common than in aged controls, mostly in cortical layer VI and the subcortical white matter. These neurons carried high levels of clonally expanded mitochondrial DNA deletions [Campbell2011].
- Compartmentalisation: lesional CD8+ T cells take on a tissue-resident memory phenotype. They gain CD103 and lose the egress molecules S1P1 and CCR7, which may trap the inflammatory response in established lesions, while B cells gradually become plasma cells [MachadoSantos2018].
- In the pooled OPERA I/II relapsing-MS population, progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA) accounted for 78.0% (interferon β-1a arm) and 88.0% (ocrelizumab arm) of 12-week confirmed disability accumulation over 96 weeks. Relapse-associated worsening accounted for 17.7% [Kappos2020].
- A systematic review of 48 studies estimates that PIRA occurs in roughly 5% of people with relapsing-remitting MS per year and causes at least 50% of disability-accrual events. Its share rises with age, disease duration and potent relapse suppression [Muller2023].
- **Hypothesis**: a diffuse "smouldering" process across the whole CNS, rather than focal relapses, is the "real MS" driving long-term disability [Giovannoni2022]. A 2024 international consensus proposes the term "smouldering-associated worsening" (SAW), broader than PIRA, covering physical and cognitive decline [Scalfari2024].
- Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) regulates maturation, survival, migration and activation of B cells and microglia. CNS-penetrant BTK inhibitors are being tested because they could reach compartmentalised B cells and microglia on both sides of the blood-brain barrier, whereas anti-CD20 antibodies penetrate the brain poorly. Five BTK inhibitors differ in selectivity, binding mode and CNS penetrance (trials are in pass G) [Kramer2023].

### Mechanism of action by DMT class (published summaries)
- Interferon-β: raises anti-inflammatory and lowers proinflammatory cytokine expression, may reduce immune-cell trafficking across the blood-brain barrier, and expands CD56bright regulatory NK cells. Its mechanism remains "multifactorial and incompletely understood" [Kieseier2011].
- Glatiramer acetate: binds MHC molecules promiscuously and competes with myelin antigens for presentation to T cells. It also induces glatiramer-specific Th2 suppressor cells that enter the brain and release IL-10, TGF-β and BDNF, producing "bystander suppression" [Arnon2004].
- Teriflunomide: reversibly inhibits dihydro-orotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), a mitochondrial enzyme of de novo pyrimidine synthesis. Rapidly proliferating activated T and B cells depend on that pathway, whereas resting lymphocytes use the salvage pathway, so proliferation is slowed without cell death [BarOr2014].
- Fumarates (Nrf2): dimethyl fumarate and its metabolite monomethyl fumarate stabilise the antioxidant transcription factor Nrf2, and monomethyl fumarate modifies its inhibitor Keap1 at cysteine 151. Protection in chronic EAE was almost abolished in Nrf2-deficient mice [Linker2011].
- Fumarates (HCAR2): monomethyl fumarate activates hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (HCA2/HCAR2). Dimethyl fumarate's benefit in EAE, including reduced neutrophil infiltration, was lost in HCA2-knockout mice [Chen2014].
- S1P receptor modulators: siponimod binds S1P1 and S1P5 with high affinity and blocks lymphocyte egress from lymph nodes, lowering blood lymphocyte counts. Fingolimod-phosphate also binds S1P3 and S1P4 [MayzentLabel2026; Chun2010].
- Cladribine: an adenosine deaminase-resistant purine analogue, so lymphocytes cannot inactivate it [Murphy2010]. Cells with a high ratio of deoxycytidine kinase to 5′-nucleotidase (lymphocytes) accumulate cladribine phosphates, which impair DNA synthesis and repair, causing strand breaks and cell death [Leist2011].
- Cladribine tablets: lymphocyte counts reach their lowest 2–3 months after each treatment cycle. Median recovery from <500 to ≥800 cells/μL took about 28 weeks [MavencladLabel2026].
- Natalizumab: anti-α4-integrin, blocking VLA-4/VCAM-1-dependent entry [TyrukoLabel2025; Yednock1992]. In AFFIRM it reduced the one-year relapse rate by 68% and gadolinium-enhancing lesions by 92% vs placebo (trial detail in pass E) [Polman2006].
- Anti-CD20 antibodies: CD20 is on pre-B and mature B cells, and binding kills B cells by antibody-dependent cellular cytolysis and complement-mediated lysis. Ocrelizumab lowers blood B cells by day 14, and after the last infusion B cells took a median of 72 weeks to return [OcrevusLabel2026; Hauser2017].
- What anti-CD20 spares: stem cells/pro-B cells, many plasmablasts and long-lived plasma cells do not carry CD20. So B-cell regeneration and existing humoral immunity are preserved, IgG levels generally do not change with initial courses, and a small fall in IgM can occur [Greenfield2018].
- Anti-CD20 therapy does not directly target plasma cells and does not appear to change the abnormal CSF antibody profile (oligoclonal bands), consistent with efficacy that does not depend on antibody [Comi2021].
- Alemtuzumab: binds CD52 on T and B lymphocytes, NK cells, monocytes and macrophages and depletes them. Counts are lowest 1 month after a course; B cells usually recover within 6 months while T cells recover more slowly (mean CD4+ count 40/μL at month 1 and 270/μL at month 12) [LemtradaLabel2025].
- Alemtuzumab reconstitution: B cells overshoot to 124–165% of baseline at 12 months. CD8+ T cells take about 31 months and CD4+ T cells about 60 months to recover fully. Secondary autoimmunity, mostly thyroid, affects 30–40% of treated patients [Ruck2015].

## Controversies
- Is MS lesion pathology heterogeneous between patients? Position A: there are four patterns, uniform within a person and different between people [Lucchinetti2000], and the pattern persists across serial biopsies in 95% [Metz2014]. Position B: active demyelinating lesions in established (autopsy) MS look uniform, with antibody, complement and Fc-receptor macrophages in all [Breij2008]. Status: unresolved. One reconciling view is that early heterogeneity converges with time (proposed by Breij2008), and cohorts differ (biopsy vs autopsy).
- "Outside-in" vs "inside-out": the classical model has peripheral autoreactive lymphocytes crossing the blood-brain barrier first [Hemmer2015; Yednock1992]. Barnett and Prineas found oligodendrocyte apoptosis and microglial activation with few lymphocytes in newly forming lesions, suggesting a primary process within the CNS [Barnett2004]. Status: open. Both are compatible with a CNS-compartmentalised immune response later in disease [MachadoSantos2018].
- Is EAE a valid model? It has been central to drug development (fingolimod, natalizumab, glatiramer) [Constantinescu2011; Yednock1992]. Critics call it a model of acute CNS inflammation rather than MS [Sriram2005], and no animal model recapitulates the full MS pathology [Reich2018].
- Why does anti-CD20 work? One view is antibody-independent, through antigen presentation and cytokines: the rapid effect precedes any fall in immunoglobulin, and oligoclonal bands persist [Reich2018; Comi2021; Li2015]. The other view gives antibody and complement a role in demyelination in at least some lesions [Breij2008; Lucchinetti2000]. Status: both may operate, with weighting differing by lesion and patient.
- Who remyelinates human lesions? Rodent work says new oligodendrocytes from OPCs [Franklin2017]. Carbon-14 dating finds only old oligodendrocytes in human shadow plaques [Yeung2019], and large-animal models show mature oligodendrocytes can re-ensheath axons [Duncan2018]. Status: open, with consequences for OPC-differentiation drugs.
- Does progression stay inflammation-driven? Pathology links axonal injury to inflammation at all stages, with burnout in the very old [Frischer2009; Lassmann2012]. The smouldering/PIRA view stresses diffuse CNS-intrinsic processes that current DMTs reach poorly [Giovannoni2022; Scalfari2024; Kramer2023]. Status: converging on "compartmentalised inflammation plus neurodegeneration" rather than either alone.
- Where does 4-AP act? Classic physiology: on K+ channels exposed along demyelinated axons [Sherratt1980; Rasband1998; AmpyraLabel2026]. A review notes it has also been suggested that the relevant block is at synaptic terminals rather than demyelinated axons [ArancibiaCarcamo2014]. The label says the mechanism is not fully elucidated.
- Brain-atrophy reference ranges: the frequently quoted "0.5–1.35%/year in MS vs 0.1–0.3% in healthy people" could not be traced to a validated source here. De Stefano 2016 measured −0.51%/year (MS) vs −0.27%/year (controls), with cut-offs of −0.37% to −0.52% [DeStefano2016]. Values depend on the software (SIENA) and on age.
