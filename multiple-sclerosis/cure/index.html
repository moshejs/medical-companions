Part 4 · Treatment

Toward a cure

No treatment cures MS today. A cure would have to stop relapses, quiet smouldering inflammation, repair myelin and protect axons. Press Play to add the four jobs to one lesion, then see where the trials stand, with every finding labelled by its strength of evidence.

The starting point

Four things go wrong at once

The drawing is one schematic lesion with the four processes a cure would have to stop or reverse: (1) immune cells crossing a leaky blood-brain barrier, (2) a smouldering rim of activated microglia, with B and T cells trapped behind the barrier, (3) an axon stripped of myelin, so its signal fails, and (4) a bare axon under stress, next to one already cut. The Lancet seminar on MS names adding neuroprotection and remyelination as the biggest remaining challenge 464. An expert group led by Kuhlmann proposes treating MS as one continuum, in which apparent progression reflects a shift from focal acute injury toward widespread inflammation and neurodegeneration, with failing repair 265. Early evidence Taken together, these reviews mean a cure would have four parts 167,265,464.

In plain wordsMS is not one problem but four: an attack from outside the brain, a slow fire inside it, missing insulation on the nerve fibres, and fibres that wear out. Today's drugs mostly deal with the first.
Exam hookA cure = stop relapsing inflammation + stop smouldering inflammation + repair myelin + protect axons. Only the first is established.

The routes, and how strong the evidence is

Each finding below carries a label. Established marks a firm finding: a peer-reviewed trial result, a large cohort, a guideline, or a settled fact such as a trial missing its endpoint or a regulator's decision. Early evidence marks small, early-phase, uncontrolled or not yet peer-reviewed efficacy results. Hypothesis marks laboratory, animal or single-case work. Rejected marks an idea that later trials did not support. Findings that rest on a company press release say so. Failed trials are shown as plainly as successful ones.

One drug, two regulators

Regulators in the United States and the European Union reached different decisions on tolebrutinib for non-relapsing secondary progressive MS. Both statements below are given as the sources word them.

United States · FDA

Complete response letter

The FDA issued a complete response letter for tolebrutinib in non-relapsing SPMS (Sanofi release, 24 December 2025), calling it a change from earlier agency feedback. The drug is not approved in the US. 420

Source: company press release. The FDA letter itself was not accessed.

European Union · EMA

Positive opinion, then authorisation

The EMA's CHMP gave a positive opinion on 23 April 2026 for tolebrutinib (Cenrifki) "for the treatment of adult patients with SPMS without relapses in the last 2 years". It cites a 31% reduction in 6-month confirmed disability progression and 38% fewer new or enlarging T2 lesions, and calls drug-induced liver injury the "most worrisome side effect". The EMA page lists the medicine as authorised on 19 June 2026, under additional monitoring, with mandatory liver testing. 124

Source: EMA summary of opinion and EPAR page.

Why they may differ. HERCULES met its primary endpoint (HR 0.69) 156, but tolebrutinib showed no meaningful benefit in primary progressive MS (PERSEUS) 304,421, and liver injury (six Hy's Law cases, one death after a liver transplant) shapes the benefit-risk debate 304. As of October 2026 the US and EU positions differ. This page reports those decisions; it is not advice about any person's treatment.

Where the experts disagree

01 · Is tolebrutinib's benefit in non-relapsing SPMS real progression control?

Yes: HERCULES met its primary endpoint (HR 0.69) 156, and the EMA approved it for SPMS without relapses in the last 2 years 124. Doubtful: the FDA issued a complete response letter 420, and tolebrutinib showed no meaningful benefit in PPMS 304,421. Status: the US and EU disagree as of October 2026, and liver injury (6 Hy's Law cases, 1 death after transplant) shapes the benefit-risk debate 304.

02 · Do BTK inhibitors work through the brain (microglia) or only in the blood?

For a class effect inside the CNS: tolebrutinib slowed disability with no relapse-rate advantage over teriflunomide 365, and fenebrutinib was non-inferior to ocrelizumab in PPMS 411. Against: evobrutinib failed 325 and tolebrutinib failed in PPMS 421. Status: unresolved; differences in CNS penetration, selectivity and dosing are proposed explanations.

03 · Is clemastine a remyelinating drug or a harmful one in progressive MS?

Remyelinating: ReBUILD showed shorter VEP latency (1.7 ms per eye) in relapsing MS 174. Harmful: the TRAP-MS arm in people with PIRA was stopped for faster disability accumulation and signs of pyroptosis 253. Status: the CCMR Two combination trial has completed, but full peer-reviewed results are not yet out 68,403.

04 · Does a remyelination signal on VEP mean clinical benefit?

Possibly: the bexarotene VEP effect lasted about 27 months 308. Not shown: no remyelination trial has yet improved disability, and CCMR Two participants did not feel better 52,67,68.

05 · Should aHSCT be standard care for highly active relapsing MS?

For: Swedish authors recommend it as standard of care for highly active RRMS 436, and ECTRIMS–EBMT calls it generally accepted after high-efficacy DMT fails 340. Caution: MIST's comparator excluded today's high-efficacy antibodies, and pooled transplant-related mortality was 2.1% 58,446. Status: BEAT-MS, RAM-MS and StarMS, head-to-head against high-efficacy DMT, are still awaited.

06 · Are brain-atrophy signals in phase 2 reliable stepping stones?

Supportive: ibudilast and lipoic acid slowed atrophy in phase 2 155,448. Against: simvastatin's 43% atrophy reduction did not lead to a disability benefit in phase 3, and lipoic acid failed on walking speed 77,449. Status: imaging is being joined by sNfL, GFAP, rim lesions and slowly expanding lesions, none yet validated as trial endpoints 18,233.

07 · Can targeting EBV treat MS that is already established?

Plausible: causal epidemiology and EBNA1–GlialCAM mimicry support the idea 39,271. Not yet: EBV-specific T cells (ATA188) and raltegravir failed 13,169. Status: vaccines may matter more for prevention, and antiviral trials are under way (STOP-MS) 491.

08 · Was CCSVI ever a real MS mechanism?

The original report claimed it 510. Blinded venography 471 and a sham-controlled trial by the original investigators 511 did not support it. Status: rejected.

Where this leaves us. Drugs that keep immune cells out of the brain are established. The other three jobs are not done: the International Progressive MS Alliance describes neurodegeneration and compartmentalised inflammation as "virtually untargeted" and funds experimental-medicine trials to speed things up 108, and the National MS Society's refined Pathways to Cures roadmap calls for global data sharing and sustained, coordinated funding 25. Biomarkers such as serum neurofilament light, GFAP and paramagnetic rim lesions may make trials shorter, but none is yet validated as a trial endpoint 18,233.

In plain wordsNo treatment repairs MS damage yet. Today's drugs are good at stopping new waves of immune attack. Researchers are now testing ways to stop the slow, hidden inflammation inside the brain and to rebuild myelin, and some of the biggest trials have failed.
