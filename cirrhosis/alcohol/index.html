Part 2 · Causes

Alcohol-related liver disease

Alcohol is one of the two commonest causes of cirrhosis in many countries, alongside fatty liver disease linked to metabolism. Viral hepatitis, genetic and autoimmune diseases cause it too. Follow 100 people who drink heavily from fatty liver to scarring, and see what stopping does. 415,509

The starting point

Heavy drinking

ACG 2018 defines a US standard drink as about 14 g of alcohol and describes heavy drinking as more than 3 drinks a day for men and more than 2 for women. Most people who drink more than 40 g a day develop a fatty liver, but only a subset go on to more advanced disease. The 100 icons are one group of people who drink heavily. 415,428

In plain wordsA "standard drink" is a small can of beer, a small glass of wine or a single shot of spirits. Heavy drinking means more than 3 a day for men or 2 for women.

How much, and how often?

A 2019 meta-analysis pooled 7 cohort and 2 case-control studies: 2,629,272 people and 5,505 cases of cirrhosis. It compared each drinking level with long-term abstainers. Press the buttons to show women, men or both, and move the cursor along the drinking scale. 385

7 or more drinks a day

The highest risk

Relative risk: women 24.58 (95% CI 14.77–40.90); men 6.93 (95% CI 1.07–44.99). The wide intervals for men reflect fewer cases at each level. 385

Sex

Smaller amounts harm women's livers

In the Copenhagen City Heart Study (13,285 adults, 12 years), alcohol-related cirrhosis occurred at 0.2% a year in men and 0.03% a year in women overall, but at any given intake women had the higher relative risk. Risk became significantly raised from 7–13 drinks a week in women and from 14–27 in men; the lowest risk was at 1–6 drinks a week. An earlier meta-analysis of 17 studies agrees. 29,373

Early evidence

Daily drinking carries more risk

At the same total intake, drinking every day rather than on some days raised cirrhosis risk: RR 1.71 in men and 1.56 in women in a meta-analysis of 5 studies. In a Danish cohort of 55,917 adults, daily drinking in men carried HR 3.65 compared with drinking 2–4 days a week, and recent intake (ages 40–59) mattered more than intake at ages 20–39. 20,270

Hypothesis

Is wine different?

Among Danish men drinking 14–28 drinks a week, risk compared with under 14 a week was HR 7.47 when almost none of it was wine and HR 1.69 (95% CI 0.79–3.65) when most of it was. The authors say wine "might be" linked to lower risk; lifestyle differences between wine and beer or spirits drinkers may explain it. 20

Cutting down

Every step down helps

In a US national survey of 21,925 current drinkers followed for 3 years, very-high-risk drinkers who dropped 1, 2 or 3 WHO drinking-risk levels had lower odds of liver disease (adjusted OR 0.34, 0.23 and 0.17), without stopping completely. Stopping helps most. 242,503

Established

Genes change the odds

A genome-wide study of alcohol-related cirrhosis confirmed PNPLA3 rs738409 (the I148M variant) as a major risk locus and found TM6SF2 and MBOAT7, all involved in handling fat. The risk allele is more frequent in Hispanic populations. The same variant raises risk in metabolic fatty liver disease. 52,390,428

What counts as a drink

Standard drinks

ACG 2018 defines a US standard drink as about 14 g of alcohol: 12 oz of 5% beer, 5 oz of wine or 1.5 oz of 40–45% liquor. It describes heavy drinking as more than 3 drinks a day for men and more than 2 for women; binge drinking (NIAAA) is more than 5 drinks for men and more than 4 for women within 2 hours. 428

Alcohol-associated hepatitis

Severe steatohepatitis, with or without cirrhosis, can present suddenly as alcohol-associated hepatitis: jaundice, liver failure and a high risk of death. It needs hospital care. Step through how it is recognised, graded and treated. 219,415

Recognising it

A recognisable pattern

The NIAAA consortium criteria for trials: jaundice starting within the prior 8 weeks; ongoing intake of more than 40 g a day (women) or 60 g a day (men) for 6 months or more, with less than 60 days of abstinence before the jaundice; AST over 50 IU/L, AST/ALT over 1.5 and both under 400 IU/L; and total bilirubin over 3.0 mg/dL. An AST/ALT ratio below 1.5 is seen in under 2% of biopsy-proven cases, and biopsy is recommended when the picture is atypical. 87

What stopping does

ACG 2024 calls abstinence "a crucial determinant of long-term outcomes", and the disease primer calls it "the best treatment for all stages". Scrub through five years after a diagnosis of alcohol-related cirrhosis. 219,415

Published points, smooth lines drawn between them. Left: meta-analysis of 7 cohorts, 1,235 people. Right: 633 people with decompensated cirrhosis who stopped drinking, 17 centres. Population statistics, not a promise for any one person. 202,503

Year 5.0

Stopping keeps paying off

The survival benefit of abstinence reached significance from 1.5 years (HR 0.51; 95% CI 0.33–0.81) and persisted to 5 years (HR 0.63; 95% CI 0.52–0.76). Recompensation reached 33.8% by 5 years and was linked to lower all-cause mortality (aHR 0.255); no recompensated person who stayed abstinent died of liver causes or developed liver cancer. 202,503

Treating alcohol use disorder alongside liver disease

ACG 2024 notes that people with alcohol-related liver disease are "rarely treated for AUD", and calls for integrated care with hepatology, addiction medicine and social work. Among 35,682 US veterans with cirrhosis and alcohol use disorder, only 14% received AUD treatment within 180 days: 0.4% received a medicine alone and 1% a medicine plus behavioural therapy. 219,386

Early evidence

Treatment goes with fewer complications

In those veterans, AUD treatment was linked to less hepatic decompensation (6.5% vs 11.6%; adjusted OR 0.63) and lower long-term mortality (51% vs 58%; adjusted OR 0.87). In 9,635 people with AUD in a US biobank, medicine for AUD was linked to less new alcohol-related liver disease (adjusted OR 0.37) and, with cirrhosis, less decompensation (adjusted OR 0.35). Both studies are observational. 386,478

Randomised trial

Baclofen in cirrhosis

In 84 people with AUD and cirrhosis, 71% on baclofen achieved and kept abstinence over 12 weeks vs 29% on placebo (OR 6.3), with no hepatic side effects recorded. ACG 2018 states that of the AUD medicines, "only baclofen ... has been found to be safe in patients with ALD and cirrhosis". Quoted as published, not as advice. 8,428

Label

Acamprosate

The US label states that acamprosate "is not metabolized by the liver" and that its pharmacokinetics "are not altered" in Child–Pugh A or B hepatic impairment. It is cleared by the kidneys: contraindicated at creatinine clearance ≤30 mL/min, with a reduced dose at 30–50 mL/min. 129

Practice is moving

Naltrexone

The tablet label reports cases of hepatitis and "clinically significant liver dysfunction", often with alcohol-related liver disease, viral hepatitis or other hepatotoxic drugs as contributors. In 3,285 US veterans with cirrhosis who started naltrexone, 2% had enzyme rises and no case was classed as drug-induced liver injury; a smaller cohort says "more safety data are needed" in decompensated cirrhosis. 23,140,464

Transplant

Early transplant for severe hepatitis

The old rule asked for 6 months of abstinence first, yet most deaths from severe alcohol-associated hepatitis come within 2 months. Early transplant for carefully selected people now has published survival of 94% at 1 year and 84% at 3 years, with return to drinking still debated. See Liver transplant. 262,281,293
