## Findings

### what · What cirrhosis is and its many causes
- Cirrhosis develops after a long period of inflammation that replaces healthy liver tissue with fibrous scar and regenerative nodules, which leads to portal hypertension [[Gines2021Lancet]].
- Causes include obesity and fatty liver disease, high alcohol consumption, hepatitis B or C, autoimmune diseases, cholestatic diseases, and iron or copper overload. Cirrhosis is not caused by alcohol alone [[Gines2021Lancet]].
- The disease moves from an asymptomatic phase (compensated cirrhosis) to a symptomatic phase (decompensated cirrhosis). Complications of the decompensated phase often lead to hospital admission, poorer quality of life and high mortality [[Gines2021Lancet]].
- Progressive portal hypertension, systemic inflammation and liver failure drive outcomes. Management centres on treating the cause and the complications, and some people need a liver transplant [[Gines2021Lancet]].
- In the US the most common causes overlap: alcohol use disorder in about 45% of cases, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease in 26% and hepatitis C in 41% (these add to more than 100% because many people have more than one cause) [[Tapper2023JAMA]].
- Baveno VII defines compensated cirrhosis by the absence of present or past complications. The move from compensated to decompensated cirrhosis raises the risk of death [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- Baveno VII splits compensated cirrhosis into two stages by the absence or presence of clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH). People with CSPH are at higher risk of decompensation [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- In 2023 a multisociety Delphi process renamed NAFLD as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). The diagnosis needs at least 1 of 5 cardiometabolic risk factors [[Rinella2023MASLD]].
- 236 panellists from 56 countries took part. 74% felt the old nomenclature was flawed enough to change, and 61% and 66% felt "nonalcoholic" and "fatty" were stigmatising [[Rinella2023MASLD]].
- The same consensus created MetALD for people with MASLD who also drink more alcohol: 140–350 g/week for women and 210–420 g/week for men [[Rinella2023MASLD]].

### what · Global burden (GBD 2017, GBD 2019, GBD 2021)
- In 2017 cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases caused more than 1.32 million deaths worldwide (95% UI 1.27–1.45 million): 440,000 (33.3%) in women and 883,000 (66.7%) in men. In 1990 there were fewer than 899,000 deaths [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Cirrhosis accounted for 2.4% of all global deaths in 2017, up from 1.9% in 1990 [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- The global age-standardised death rate fell over the same period, from 21.0 per 100,000 in 1990 to 16.5 per 100,000 in 2017. Death counts rose mainly because the population grew and aged [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- In 2017 there were an estimated 112 million prevalent cases of compensated cirrhosis (95% UI 107–119 million) and 10.6 million of decompensated cirrhosis (10.3–10.9 million) worldwide [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Cirrhosis caused nearly 41.4 million DALYs in 2017, up from 30.5 million in 1990. The age-standardised DALY rate fell from 656.4 to 510.7 per 100,000 [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Regional age-standardised death rates in 2017 ranged from 32.2 per 100,000 in sub-Saharan Africa (highest super-region) to 10.1 per 100,000 in the high-income super-region (lowest) [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- By country in 2017, the age-standardised death rate was lowest in Singapore (3.7 per 100,000) and highest in Egypt (103.3 per 100,000). Egypt was highest in every year since 1990 [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Eastern Europe and central Asia were the only regions where age-standardised death rates rose from 1990 to 2017: by 137.0% in eastern Europe and 50.5% in central Asia. This was driven mainly by alcohol-related liver disease [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- In 2019 cirrhosis was associated with 2.4% of global deaths [[Huang2023NRGH]].
- In GBD 2021 there were an estimated 58,417,006 incident cases, 1,425,142 deaths and 46,417,777 DALYs from cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases [[Tham2025]].
- From 2010 to 2021 the age-standardised incidence rate rose (annual percentage change +0.35%), while age-standardised death rates (−1.74%/year) and DALY rates (−1.85%/year) fell [[Tham2025]].
- Liver disease as a whole causes about 2 million deaths a year: roughly 1 million from complications of cirrhosis and 1 million from viral hepatitis and hepatocellular carcinoma [[Asrani2019JHep]].
- Cirrhosis was the 11th most common cause of death worldwide and liver cancer the 16th. Together they accounted for 3.5% of all deaths [[Asrani2019JHep]].
- Cirrhosis accounts for 1.6% of worldwide DALYs and 2.1% of years of life lost [[Asrani2019JHep]].
- The 2023 update put liver disease at about 4% of all deaths worldwide (1 in every 25 deaths). About two-thirds of liver-related deaths occur in men [[Devarbhavi2023]].
- Around 1.5 billion people worldwide have chronic liver disease. The age-standardised incidence of chronic liver disease and cirrhosis was 20.7 per 100,000, a 13% increase since 2000 [[Moon2020CGH]].
- Liver transplantation is the second most common solid-organ transplant, yet less than 10% of global transplant need is met [[Asrani2019JHep]].

### what · Causes by region and the shift toward MASLD
- In 2017, among men worldwide, cirrhosis deaths were attributed to hepatitis B in 31.5%, hepatitis C in 25.5%, alcohol-related liver disease in 27.3%, NASH in 7.7% and other causes in 8.0% [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Among women the shares were hepatitis B 24.0%, alcohol-related liver disease 20.6%, hepatitis C 26.7%, NASH 11.3% and other causes 17.3%. Women had higher NASH and "other" shares than men [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Hepatitis B caused the largest share of cirrhosis deaths in 2017: about 384,000 deaths, up from about 287,000 in 1990. Its age-standardised death rate fell from 6.7 to 4.8 per 100,000 [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- In western sub-Saharan Africa hepatitis B caused 48.9% of cirrhosis deaths and hepatitis C 7.8%. Cirrhosis deaths in most Latin American regions were driven mainly by alcohol-related liver disease (34.6–38.1%), except tropical Latin America, where hepatitis C led [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- NASH-related cirrhosis was the only cause whose age-standardised death rate did not fall from 1990 to 2017. Its age-standardised prevalence rose more than any other cause: by 33.2% for compensated and 54.8% for decompensated cirrhosis [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Prevalent cases of NASH-related compensated cirrhosis more than doubled from 1990 to 2017, and NASH-related decompensated cases more than tripled [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Viral hepatitis remains the leading cause of cirrhosis worldwide, but NAFLD-related and alcohol-associated cirrhosis are rising in several regions. Deaths from cirrhosis are projected to increase over the next decade [[Huang2023NRGH]].
- MASH accounted for 48,310,981 of the 58.4 million incident cases of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases in 2021, and was the only cause with a rising age-standardised incidence rate (APC +0.86%) [[Tham2025]].
- Age-standardised deaths and DALYs from MASH-related liver disease rose in the Americas from 2010 to 2021, unlike every other WHO region [[Tham2025]].
- A separate GBD 2021 analysis found that NAFLD had become the leading global cause of incident cirrhosis by 2021 (ASIR EAPC 0.73, 95% CI 0.69–0.77) [[Zhang2025BMCGastro]].
- The 2023 global update lists viral hepatitis, alcohol and NAFLD as the commonest causes of cirrhosis worldwide [[Devarbhavi2023]].
- About 2 billion people drink alcohol worldwide, over 75 million have a diagnosed alcohol use disorder, about 2 billion adults are overweight or obese and over 400 million have diabetes [[Asrani2019JHep]].

### what · United States: prevalence and awareness
- NHANES 1999–2010 put US cirrhosis prevalence at about 0.27%, or 633,323 adults. Cirrhosis was defined by an AST-to-platelet ratio above 2 plus abnormal liver tests [[Scaglione2015]].
- 69% of these adults reported that they were unaware of having liver disease [[Scaglione2015]].
- Prevalence was higher in non-Hispanic Black and Mexican American adults, people living below the poverty level and those with less than a 12th-grade education [[Scaglione2015]].
- Viral hepatitis (mostly hepatitis C), diabetes and alcohol misuse together had a population-attributable fraction of 53.5%. More than half of cases are potentially preventable [[Scaglione2015]].
- Mortality was 26.4% per 2-year interval in adults with cirrhosis, compared with 8.4% in propensity-matched controls [[Scaglione2015]].
- A 2023 JAMA review estimates that about 2.2 million US adults have cirrhosis [[Tapper2023JAMA]].
- NHANES 2017–2020 data using transient elastography or FIB-4 put US cirrhosis prevalence at 2.6% and clinically significant portal hypertension at 0.98%. People with MASLD made up the majority of US residents with cirrhosis [[Younossi2025Prev]].
- In that analysis, population prevalence of cirrhosis by cause was MASLD 1.27%, MetALD 0.19%, hepatitis C 0.10%, alcohol-related 0.08%, alcohol plus viral 0.08%, hepatitis B 0.01% and other 0.87% [[Younossi2025Prev]].

### what · Mortality trends (US, UK)
- From 1999 to 2016, annual US deaths from cirrhosis rose by 65%, to 34,174. Annual deaths from hepatocellular carcinoma doubled, to 11,073 [[Tapper2018BMJ]].
- US cirrhosis mortality began rising in 2009, by 3.4% a year (95% CI 3.1% to 3.8%) through 2016 [[Tapper2018BMJ]].
- From 2009 to 2016, people aged 25–34 had the largest average annual rise in cirrhosis mortality: 10.5% a year (8.9% to 12.2%), driven entirely by alcohol-related liver disease [[Tapper2018BMJ]].
- Annual rises in US cirrhosis mortality were largest for Native Americans: 4.0% a year (2.2% to 5.7%) [[Tapper2018BMJ]].
- Deaths from peritonitis and sepsis in people with cirrhosis rose by 6.1% and 7.1% a year, respectively [[Tapper2018BMJ]].
- By state, only Maryland improved (−1.2% a year). Kentucky (+6.8%), New Mexico (+6.0%) and Arkansas (+5.7%) had the largest annual increases [[Tapper2018BMJ]].
- US age-adjusted cirrhosis mortality rose from 14.9 per 100,000 in 2010 to 21.9 per 100,000 in 2021 [[Tapper2023JAMA]].
- Age-adjusted US alcohol-associated liver disease mortality rose from 13.1 per 100,000 in 2017 to 16.9 in 2020 among men, and from 5.6 to 7.7 among women [[DeutschLink2022]].
- Joinpoint analysis found one change-point in February 2020. Among men, the monthly rise in crude alcohol-associated liver disease deaths was 0.36% before and 3.18% after that point [[DeutschLink2022]].
- Official US statistics undercount liver deaths. The narrow NCHS category ("chronic liver disease and cirrhosis", 12th leading cause of death) captured only 27.2% of liver-related deaths in Rochester, Minnesota [[Asrani2013Gastro]].
- With a broader definition, US liver mortality in 2008 more than doubled, from 11.7 to 25.7 per 100,000. It was essentially unchanged from 1979 (25.8) to 2008 (25.7), whereas the narrow NCHS definition suggested a 38% fall [[Asrani2013Gastro]].
- In the UK (GPRD, 1992–2001), crude cirrhosis incidence rose from 12.05 to 16.99 per 100,000 person-years: a 45% rise in incidence and a 68% rise in prevalence over the decade [[Fleming2008UKIncidence]].
- UK incidence was more than 50% higher in men than in women (adjusted IRR 1.52). The study estimated about 30,000 people living with cirrhosis and at least 7,000 new diagnoses a year [[Fleming2008UKIncidence]].
- The Lancet Standing Commission projected that alcohol-related liver disease would soon overtake ischaemic heart disease in years of working life lost in the UK. It estimated 63,000 preventable deaths over the next 5 years [[Williams2018LancetComm]].
- In a UK GPRD cohort (4,537 people with cirrhosis vs 44,403 matched controls, 1987–2002), people with compensated cirrhosis had a nearly 5-fold higher risk of death (HR 4.7, 95% CI 4.4–5.0) and those with decompensated cirrhosis a nearly 10-fold higher risk (HR 9.7, 8.9–10.6) [[Fleming2012Mortality]].
- UK mortality from cirrhosis tripled over the 30 years before that study [[Fleming2012Mortality]].

### what · Hospital use and costs
- In the US National Inpatient Sample, chronic liver disease-related hospitalisations rose from 3,056 to 3,757 per 100,000 hospitalisations between 2012 and 2016 [[Hirode2020]].
- Total US inpatient costs for chronic liver disease rose from $14.9 billion in 2012 to $18.8 billion in 2016, totalling $81.1 billion over the 5 years [[Hirode2020]].
- In England and Wales alcohol misuse costs about £21 billion a year (possibly up to £52 billion), and obesity about £27 billion a year [[Williams2018LancetComm]].

### course · Compensated and decompensated cirrhosis: survival
- Compensated and decompensated cirrhosis have very different survival: median survival is over 12 years for compensated cirrhosis and under 2 years for decompensated cirrhosis, as summarised from the D'Amico 118-study review [[Zipprich2012,DAmico2006]].
- In a cohort of 443 patients, 1-year mortality was 5.4% in compensated and 20.2% in decompensated cirrhosis [[Zipprich2012]].
- In 293 people with compensated cirrhosis (biopsy-diagnosed 1968–1980), the 10-year probability of decompensation was 58% and 10-year survival was 47% [[Gines1987]].
- In 384 European patients with compensated hepatitis C cirrhosis, the 5-year risk of decompensation was 18% and of hepatocellular carcinoma 7%. Survival was 91% at 5 years and 79% at 10 years [[Fattovich1997]].
- In 200 patients with decompensated hepatitis C cirrhosis, survival after the first decompensation was 81.8% at 1 year and 50.8% at 5 years [[Planas2004]].
- Median survival after hepatic encephalopathy develops is 0.92 years, and after ascites develops 1.1 years [[Tapper2023JAMA]].
- In England (5,118 incident cases, 1998–2009), average survival for people diagnosed as outpatients was 84% at 1 year and 66% at 5 years. After an emergency liver-related admission it was 55% and 31% [[Ratib2014]].
- An emergency hospital admission for liver disease worsened outlook independently of stage (HR 2.78, 95% CI 2.53–3.06) [[Ratib2014]].
- About 1% to 4% of people with cirrhosis develop hepatocellular carcinoma each year. Five-year survival after HCC is about 20% [[Tapper2023JAMA]].

### course · Prognostic stages (D'Amico and successors)
- D'Amico's 4-stage model is based on an Italian cohort of 1,649 patients. Stage 1 is compensated without varices, with about 1% 1-year mortality. Stage 2 is compensated with varices, with about 3% per year [[Zipprich2012,DAmico2006]].
- Stage 3 is ascites without bleeding, with about 20% 1-year mortality. Stage 4 is variceal bleeding with or without ascites, with about 57% [[Zipprich2012,DAmico2006]].
- In a German validation cohort, 1-year mortality was 3.4% in stage 1 and 7.3% in stage 2 (HR 2.1 for stage 2 vs 1) [[Zipprich2012]].
- In the same cohort, 1-year mortality was 21.9% in stage 3 and 18.1% in stage 4 (not significantly different). Ascites stratified risk better than bleeding [[Zipprich2012]].
- In a 25-year inception cohort of 494 patients (404 HCV-positive), D'Amico defined 5 stages:
  - stage 1: compensated without varices
  - stage 2: compensated with varices
  - stage 3: bleeding alone
  - stage 4: first non-bleeding decompensation
  - stage 5: any second decompensating event [[DAmico2014APT]]
- 5-year mortality across stages 1–5 was 1.5%, 10%, 20%, 30% and 88%. 5-year transition rates out of stages 1–4 were 34.5%, 42%, 65% and 78% [[DAmico2014APT]].
- Over a mean follow-up of 145 months, that cohort recorded 380 deaths, 326 new varices, 283 ascites, 158 bleeds, 146 encephalopathy, 113 jaundice, 126 HCC and 19 transplants [[DAmico2014APT]].
- A later D'Amico review describes cirrhosis as a set of clinical states: compensated, decompensated and late decompensated. People do not move through these in a fixed order, and ACLF can arise in either compensated or decompensated cirrhosis [[DAmico2018JHep]].
- Because outcomes such as bleeding, ascites, HCC and death compete to occur first, the review argues for competing-risks (multistate) analysis rather than simple Kaplan-Meier curves [[DAmico2018JHep]].

### course · Decompensating events and further decompensation
- Baveno VII names three events that define decompensation in a compensated patient:
  - overt ascites (or pleural effusion with SAAG >1.1 g/dL)
  - overt hepatic encephalopathy (West Haven grade II or higher)
  - variceal bleeding [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]]
- Ascites was the most common first decompensation (48%) in decompensated hepatitis C cirrhosis, followed by portal-hypertensive bleeding (32.5%), severe bacterial infection (14.5%) and hepatic encephalopathy (5%) [[Planas2004]].
- Jaundice alone may be the first sign of cirrhosis in a minority of patients. Baveno VII says whether it counts as true decompensation needs more research [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- Bacterial infections, superimposed liver injury (alcohol-related hepatitis, acute hepatitis A or E, HBV flares, drug-induced liver injury), HCC and major surgery can each trigger decompensation [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- Baveno VII describes "further decompensation" as a stage with even higher mortality than first decompensation. It is defined as any of:
  - a second portal hypertension-driven event, or jaundice
  - recurrent bleeding
  - recurrent ascites (3 or more large-volume paracenteses within 1 year)
  - recurrent encephalopathy
  - SBP or HRS-AKI [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]]
- About 40% of people with cirrhosis are first diagnosed when they present with a complication such as hepatic encephalopathy or ascites [[Tapper2023JAMA]].

### course · Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF)
- The CANONIC study (1,343 patients admitted with acute decompensation at 29 European liver units in 2011) defined ACLF. 303 patients had ACLF at enrolment and 112 developed it during the stay [[Moreau2013CANONIC]].
- 28-day mortality was 33.9% for patients with ACLF at enrolment, 29.7% for those who developed it and 1.9% for those without ACLF [[Moreau2013CANONIC]].
- Patients with ACLF were younger, more often had alcohol-related disease and bacterial infections, and had higher white cell counts and C-reactive protein. ACLF was most severe in people with no prior decompensation [[Moreau2013CANONIC]].
- Of 388 patients with ACLF, 49.2% resolved or improved, 30.4% had a steady or fluctuating course and 20.4% worsened [[Gustot2015]].
- 28-day transplant-free mortality was 6–18% when the final grade was no ACLF or ACLF-1, and 42–92% when it was ACLF-2 or ACLF-3 [[Gustot2015]].
- 81% of patients reached their final ACLF grade within 1 week. 75% of those who had an early transplant survived at least 1 year [[Gustot2015]].
- Across 30 cohorts (43,206 patients with ACLF), global ACLF prevalence among people admitted with decompensated cirrhosis was 35% (95% CI 33–38%), highest in South Asia at 65% [[Mezzano2022Gut]].
- Pooled 90-day mortality was 58% (95% CI 51–64%), highest in South America at 73%. Infection was the commonest trigger (35%) and kidney dysfunction the commonest organ failure (49%) [[Mezzano2022Gut]].
- EASL describes ACLF (first described in 2013) as a severe form of acutely decompensated cirrhosis with organ failure(s) and high short-term mortality, caused by an excessive systemic inflammatory response to precipitants such as infection with sepsis or severe alcohol-related hepatitis [[EASL2023ACLF]].

### course · Regression and recompensation after the cause is removed
- Baveno VII defines removal or suppression of the cause as:
  - sustained virological response (SVR) for hepatitis C
  - HBV suppression (without HDV co-infection) for hepatitis B
  - long-term abstinence from alcohol for alcohol-related liver disease [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]]
- Removing or suppressing the cause leads to meaningful falls in portal pressure (HVPG) in most patients and substantially reduces the risk of decompensation [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- Overweight, diabetes and alcohol still contribute to progression after the main cause is removed. Baveno VII says these should be addressed [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- Baveno VII states that recompensation implies at least partial regression of the structural and functional changes of cirrhosis after the cause is removed (Established, consensus grade A.1) [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- The clinical definition of recompensation (expert consensus) needs all three of:
  - removal, suppression or cure of the cause
  - resolution of ascites off diuretics and of encephalopathy off lactulose/rifaximin, with no recurrent variceal bleeding for at least 12 months
  - stable improvement in albumin, INR and bilirubin [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]]
- Because CSPH may persist despite recompensation, Baveno VII states that non-selective beta-blockers should not be stopped unless CSPH resolves [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- Resolution of ascites on diuretics or after TIPS, without removal of the cause and without better liver function, is not recompensation under Baveno VII [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- After 5 years of tenofovir for chronic hepatitis B, 71 of 96 patients (74%) who had cirrhosis on biopsy at baseline no longer met histological criteria for cirrhosis (Early evidence for regression; open-label, biopsy subset) [[Marcellin2013]].
- Of 283 people with HBV-related decompensated cirrhosis treated with entecavir, 56.2% met the Baveno VII definition of recompensation by 120 weeks [[Wang2022Recomp]].
- Of 1,213 US veterans with decompensated hepatitis C cirrhosis, 13% recompensated within 24 months of starting direct-acting antivirals. Recompensation was linked to lower later mortality [[Goyes2026]].
- In 633 people with decompensated alcohol-related cirrhosis who stopped drinking, cumulative recompensation was 12.3% at 1 year, 23.4% at 2 years and 33.8% at 5 years [[Hofer2026JHep]].
- In that cohort, abstinence within 1 month of decompensation doubled the likelihood of recompensation (aSHR 2.04). Recompensation was linked to lower all-cause mortality (aHR 0.255) [[Hofer2026JHep]].
- In 530 people with hepatitis C and advanced fibrosis, 10-year all-cause mortality was 8.9% after SVR vs 26.0% without SVR (HR 0.26, 95% CI 0.14–0.49) [[vanderMeer2012]].
- 10-year liver-related death or transplant was 1.9% with SVR vs 27.4% without, and liver failure 2.1% vs 29.9% [[vanderMeer2012]].
- In a meta-analysis of 7 cohorts (1,235 patients with alcohol-related cirrhosis), abstinence was linked to better survival from 1.5 years onward. The 5-year HR was 0.63 (95% CI 0.52–0.76) [[Xie2014HepatolRes]].

### what · How cirrhosis presents
- Compensated cirrhosis is the asymptomatic phase. Symptoms usually appear with decompensation [[Gines2021Lancet]].
- Common symptoms in people with cirrhosis include muscle cramps (about 64%), poor-quality sleep (63%), sexual dysfunction (53%) and itch (pruritus, 39%) [[Tapper2023JAMA]].
- Decompensation shows as fluid in the belly (ascites), confusion (hepatic encephalopathy), bleeding from varices (vomiting blood or black stools) or yellowing (jaundice) [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII,Tapper2023JAMA]].
- Many cases are undiagnosed. In NHANES, 69% of people with laboratory evidence of cirrhosis did not know they had liver disease [[Scaglione2015]].

### history · Naming and early description
- René Laennec named cirrhosis from the Greek *kirrhos* ("tawny", an orange-yellow colour) in a brief footnote to his treatise *De l'auscultation médiate* (1819) [[Duffin1987]].
- The liver lesion had first been recognised in England more than a century before Laennec named it [[Duffin1987]].
- The eponym "Laennec's cirrhosis" came into common North American use only after William Osler's textbook, and it is rarely used in France [[Duffin1987]].

### history · Scoring severity
- Child and Turcotte published their classification in a 1964 monograph, *Surgery and portal hypertension*, to stratify patients facing portal-hypertension surgery [[ChildTurcotte1964]].
- Pugh and colleagues' modification (the Child–Pugh score) appeared in a 1973 paper on oesophageal transection for bleeding oesophageal varices [[Pugh1973]].
- MELD began as the Mayo TIPS model (2000). Bilirubin, creatinine, INR and the cause of liver disease predicted survival in 231 patients having elective TIPS. A risk score above 1.8 meant a median survival of 3 months or less [[Malinchoc2000]].
- In 2001 the model was validated as MELD in 4 independent groups. C-statistics for 3-month death were 0.87 (hospitalised), 0.80 (ambulatory non-cholestatic), 0.87 (primary biliary cirrhosis) and 0.78 (historical cohort) [[Kamath2001MELD]].
- Applied to 3,437 US waiting-list candidates (1999–2001), 3-month mortality was 1.9% at MELD below 9 and 71.3% at MELD 40 or above. The c-statistic was 0.83 vs 0.76 for Child–Turcotte–Pugh, which supported MELD-based allocation of donor livers [[Wiesner2003]].
- MELD-Na (2008): serum sodium predicted waiting-list death on top of MELD (HR 1.05 per 1 mmol/L fall between 125 and 140; HR 1.21 per MELD point) [[Kim2008MELDNa]].
- MELD 3.0 (2021) added female sex and albumin, interaction terms and a creatinine cap of 3.0 mg/dL. Its C-statistic was 0.869 vs 0.862 for MELD-Na [[Kim2021MELD3]].
- MELD 3.0 reclassified a net 8.8% of decedents to a higher tier. In simulation it gave fewer waiting-list deaths than MELD-Na (7,788 vs 7,850) [[Kim2021MELD3]].
- The US replaced MELD-Na with MELD 3.0 for allocation in July 2023, partly to narrow the sex gap in transplant access [[Kwong2025OPTN]].
- Across 119 studies, Child–Pugh and MELD had similar prognostic value in most settings, with differences in specific groups such as ACLF, ICU and surgical patients [[Peng2016ChildMELD]].

### history · Liver transplantation
- Francis Moore described orthotopic liver transplantation in dogs in 1958 [[Meirelles2015]].
- Starzl and colleagues performed the first human liver transplant in 1963. None of the first five patients survived more than 23 days [[Starzl1963,Meirelles2015]].
- In 1967 Starzl began a successful series, encouraged by Calne's use of antilymphocyte serum. A 1968 report described extended survival in 3 recipients [[Meirelles2015,Starzl1968Surgery]].
- By 1977 about 200 liver transplants had been performed worldwide [[Meirelles2015]].
- In 1979 Calne reported cyclosporin A as the first immunosuppressant in 34 recipients of cadaveric organs, including 2 livers that were functioning at the time of the report [[Calne1979]].
- Starzl reported liver transplantation using cyclosporin A plus prednisone in 1981 [[Starzl1981NEJM]].
- A US NIH Consensus Development Conference on liver transplantation was held in June 1983 [[NIH1984LT]].
- In 1989 Starzl reported 1,179 consecutive liver transplants with 1-year and 5-year survival of 73% and 64%. Tacrolimus followed in 1990 [[Meirelles2015]].
- US liver transplants reached a record 10,659 in 2023 (10,125 adult, 534 paediatric). Alcohol-associated liver disease and MASH were the leading indications [[Kwong2025OPTN]].

### history · TIPS
- Rösch, Hanafee and Snow described an experimental transjugular radiologic portacaval shunt in 1969 [[Rosch1969]].
- Richter and colleagues in Heidelberg reported the first stent-supported transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt done entirely by interventional radiology in 1989. The patient was a 49-year-old man with Child C cirrhosis whose portosystemic gradient fell from 38 to 18 mmHg [[Richter1989Radiologe]].
- Their 1990 pilot series of 9 patients lowered the mean portosystemic gradient from 29 to 17.8 mmHg using Palmaz stents [[Richter1990CVIR]].

### history · Viral hepatitis and cure
- Blumberg, Alter and Visnich described a "new" antigen in leukaemia sera in 1965, later known as the Australia antigen of hepatitis B [[Blumberg1965]].
- In 1989 Choo, Houghton and colleagues isolated a cDNA clone from the blood-borne non-A, non-B hepatitis agent, now known as hepatitis C virus [[Choo1989]].
- Harvey Alter, Michael Houghton and Charles Rice shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery of hepatitis C virus [[Lin2021Nobel]].
- Hepatitis C causes about 700,000 deaths a year. Modern antivirals clear the virus in up to 95% of those treated, and WHO set a goal of global elimination by 2030 [[Lin2021Nobel]].
- In the 2013 phase 3 trials of sofosbuvir, 90% of previously untreated patients (mainly genotype 1 or 4) given 12 weeks of sofosbuvir plus peginterferon-ribavirin had SVR [[Lawitz2013]].
- The FDA first approved sofosbuvir (Sovaldi) on 6 December 2013 [[FDA-Sovaldi]].
- The FDA first approved the interferon-free combination ledipasvir/sofosbuvir (Harvoni) on 10 October 2014 [[FDA-Harvoni]].

### history · Consensus milestones
- The Baveno consensus series began with a meeting in Groningen in 1986. Baveno I followed in 1990, then later rounds through Baveno VI (2015) and Baveno VII (October 2021) [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- In 2023 the term MASLD replaced NAFLD [[Rinella2023MASLD]].

### Exam hooks
- Cirrhosis caused more than 1.32 million deaths worldwide in 2017, or 2.4% of all deaths [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Death counts rose from 1990 to 2017 while age-standardised death rates fell, from 21.0 to 16.5 per 100,000 [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Hepatitis B caused the largest share of cirrhosis deaths in 2017. NASH/MASH is the fastest-growing cause [[GBD2020Cirrhosis,Tham2025]].
- In NHANES 1999–2010, 69% of US adults with cirrhosis were unaware they had liver disease [[Scaglione2015]].
- Median survival is over 12 years for compensated cirrhosis vs under 2 years for decompensated cirrhosis [[Zipprich2012,DAmico2006]].
- D'Amico stage 1-year mortality is about 1% (no varices), 3% (varices), 20% (ascites) and 57% (bleeding) [[Zipprich2012,DAmico2006]].
- Ascites is the most common first decompensating event [[Planas2004]].
- Baveno VII's three decompensating events are overt ascites, overt HE (West Haven grade II or higher) and variceal bleeding [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- Recompensation needs cause removal, no ascites/HE/variceal rebleeding off treatment for at least 12 months, and stable improvement in albumin, INR and bilirubin [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- ACLF 28-day mortality in CANONIC was 33.9% vs 1.9% without ACLF [[Moreau2013CANONIC]].
- Globally, ACLF affects about 35% of people admitted with decompensated cirrhosis [[Mezzano2022Gut]].
- Laennec coined "cirrhosis" in 1819, from Greek *kirrhos*, "tawny" [[Duffin1987]].
- Starzl performed the first human liver transplant in 1963 and began the first successful series in 1967 [[Meirelles2015]].
- MELD was born from a TIPS survival model (2000), using bilirubin, creatinine and INR [[Malinchoc2000,Kamath2001MELD]].
- MELD-Na added sodium in 2008. MELD 3.0 added female sex and albumin and was adopted in the US in July 2023 [[Kim2008MELDNa,Kim2021MELD3,Kwong2025OPTN]].
- Hepatitis C was discovered in 1989, and the discovery won the 2020 Nobel Prize [[Choo1989,Lin2021Nobel]].
- The first TIPS was done in 1989 in Heidelberg (Richter, Palmaz stents) [[Richter1989Radiologe]].
- SVR in hepatitis C with advanced fibrosis cut 10-year all-cause mortality from 26.0% to 8.9% [[vanderMeer2012]].

### In plain words
- Cirrhosis means the liver has been hurt for a long time and has become scarred and lumpy. Over time the scarring makes it harder for the liver to do its jobs [[Gines2021Lancet]].
- Many things can cause it: fat building up in the liver along with diabetes or excess weight (MASLD), hepatitis B or C, alcohol, and some autoimmune and inherited conditions. It is not only an alcohol disease [[Gines2021Lancet]].
- Cirrhosis is common. More than 100 million people around the world are estimated to live with early-stage cirrhosis [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]].
- Most people with early cirrhosis feel well and do not know they have it. In one US survey, about 7 in 10 did not know [[Scaglione2015]].
- Doctors talk about two phases. In "compensated" cirrhosis the liver is still coping. In "decompensated" cirrhosis problems such as fluid in the belly, confusion or bleeding appear [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]].
- On average, people stay in the early phase for many years. Life expectancy drops after problems begin. These are averages for large groups and cannot predict what will happen to any one person [[Zipprich2012]].
- Treating the cause can help a lot. Curing hepatitis C, controlling hepatitis B or stopping alcohol can lower the chance of problems, and some people's livers improve [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII,vanderMeer2012,Hofer2026JHep]].
- After stopping alcohol, about 1 in 3 people with advanced alcohol-related cirrhosis "recompensated" (became stable again) within 5 years in one large study [[Hofer2026JHep]].
- Common day-to-day symptoms include muscle cramps, poor sleep, itching and low sex drive. Many of these can be treated, so tell your care team [[Tapper2023JAMA]].
- Liver transplant has been possible since the 1960s, and more than 10,000 were done in the US in 2023 [[Meirelles2015,Kwong2025OPTN]].
- Urgent care: vomiting blood, black or tarry stools, new confusion or drowsiness, or fever or belly pain with a swollen abdomen need urgent care. Call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department.

## Open questions and controversies
- How many US adults have cirrhosis? NHANES 1999–2010 using APRI >2 plus abnormal liver tests gave 0.27% (about 633,000) [[Scaglione2015]]. A 2023 review cites about 2.2 million [[Tapper2023JAMA]]. NHANES 2017–2020 using transient elastography or FIB-4 gave 2.6% [[Younossi2025Prev]]. The roughly 10-fold gap reflects different case definitions (blood-test ratio vs liver stiffness, which can overcall cirrhosis in steatotic livers) and different eras. Status: unresolved. Builders should present these as a range with methods, not one number.
- Is global cirrhosis burden rising or falling? Death counts and the share of all deaths are rising [[GBD2020Cirrhosis,Tham2025]], while age-standardised death rates are falling [[GBD2020Cirrhosis,Tham2025]]. Both are true: ageing and population growth push counts up even as age-specific risk falls. Status: established, but often misreported.
- Which cause leads worldwide? In 2017 hepatitis B caused the largest share of cirrhosis deaths [[GBD2020Cirrhosis]], and a 2023 review still names viral hepatitis the leading cause [[Huang2023NRGH]]. GBD 2021 analyses report MASH/NAFLD as the leading cause of incident cases [[Tham2025,Zhang2025BMCGastro]]. The answer depends on whether you count deaths, prevalent cases or new cases. Status: shifting (Early evidence for metabolic causes leading incidence).
- Are liver deaths undercounted? Narrow official coding missed about 73% of liver-related deaths in one US county and suggested a false 38% fall since 1979 [[Asrani2013Gastro]]. Status: established for the US; extent elsewhere unclear.
- Is ascites or bleeding the worse first event? The Italian staging put variceal bleeding (stage 4, about 57% 1-year mortality) above ascites (stage 3, about 20%) [[Zipprich2012,DAmico2006]]. A German cohort found ascites stratified risk better, and bleeding without ascites had lower 1-year mortality (14.1%) [[Zipprich2012]]. D'Amico's later 5-stage model moved bleeding alone below first non-bleeding decompensation [[DAmico2014APT]]. Status: the newer view (ascites and other non-bleeding events worse) predominates.
- How should recompensation be defined and timed? Baveno VII's definition is expert consensus (grade C.2), and its research agenda asks how long stability must last before someone counts as truly recompensated [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]]. Reported rates vary widely by cause, definition and follow-up: 13% at 24 months after DAAs in HCV [[Goyes2026]], 56.2% at 120 weeks on entecavir in HBV [[Wang2022Recomp]] and 33.8% at 5 years with abstinence in alcohol-related cirrhosis [[Hofer2026JHep]]. Status: Early evidence. Data for MASLD are lacking.
- Does histological "regression" of cirrhosis mean lower risk? 74% of a tenofovir-treated biopsy subset no longer met cirrhosis criteria at 5 years [[Marcellin2013]]. Only 54% of the trial population had paired biopsies, and Baveno VII notes CSPH can persist after recompensation [[deFranchis2022BavenoVII]]. Status: Early evidence.
- One global ACLF definition? European (EASL-CLIF) criteria [[Moreau2013CANONIC,EASL2023ACLF]] differ from Asian and North American ones. EASL, AASLD, APASL, ALEH and SOLDA published a global multisociety consensus on ACLF definitions in 2026 [[ACLFConsensus2026]]. Prevalence estimates vary by region (35% overall, 65% in South Asia), partly because of definitions and case mix [[Mezzano2022Gut]]. Status: harmonisation under way.
