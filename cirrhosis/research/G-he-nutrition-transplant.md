# G · Hepatic encephalopathy, nutrition & sarcopenia, medicine safety & liver transplant

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: encephalopathy, nutrition, transplant.

This pass covers hepatic encephalopathy (HE), nutrition, sarcopenia and frailty, medicine safety, vaccination and liver transplantation in people with cirrhosis. Cirrhosis has many causes, including MASLD, alcohol-related liver disease, viral hepatitis, autoimmune, cholestatic and genetic diseases [[Gines2021Lancet]]. Trial numbers come from PubMed abstracts. ## Findings

### encephalopathy · Definition and classification
- AASLD/EASL 2014 definition: "a brain dysfunction caused by liver insufficiency and/or PSS [portosystemic shunting]". It shows as "a wide spectrum of neurological or psychiatric abnormalities ranging from subclinical alterations to coma" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- The guideline classifies HE by four factors [[Vilstrup2014HE]]:
  - underlying disease: type A from acute liver failure, type B mainly from portosystemic bypass, type C from cirrhosis
  - severity
  - time course
  - whether it was precipitated
- By time course, HE is "episodic", "recurrent" (bouts 6 months or less apart) or "persistent" (behaviour changes always present, with relapses of overt HE) [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Precipitating factors "can be identified in nearly all bouts of episodic HE type C" and should be "actively sought and treated" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Worked example from the guideline: "The patient has HE, Type C, Grade 3, Recurrent, Precipitated (by urinary tract infection)" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- HE is diagnosed by excluding other causes of brain dysfunction. Overt HE is diagnosed on clinical criteria and graded with the West Haven Criteria (WHC) and the Glasgow Coma Scale [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- The guideline calls the WHC the gold standard. It also notes they have "limited interobserver reliability, especially for grade I HE" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].

### encephalopathy · West Haven grades as published (AASLD/EASL 2014, Table 2)
- Minimal HE (grouped as covert): abnormal psychometric or neurophysiological tests "without clinical evidence of mental change" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Grade I (grouped as covert): "trivial lack of awareness", euphoria or anxiety, shortened attention span, impaired addition or subtraction, altered sleep rhythm. The person is still oriented in time and space [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Grade II (overt): lethargy or apathy, disorientation for time, obvious personality change, inappropriate behaviour, dyspraxia, asterixis [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
  - Operative criterion: at least three of day of the month, day of the week, month, season or year are wrong.
- Grade III (overt): somnolence to semistupor, responsive to stimuli, confused, gross disorientation, bizarre behaviour [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
  - Also disoriented for space: at least three of country, state or region, city or place are wrong.
- Grade IV (overt): coma, "does not respond even to painful stimuli" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- "Covert" HE = minimal HE plus grade I. "Overt" HE = grades II–IV (ISHEN grouping in the same table) [[Vilstrup2014HE]].

### encephalopathy · How common it is and what it means
- Overt HE "will occur in 30%-40% of those with cirrhosis at some time during their clinical course and in the survivors in most cases repeatedly" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Minimal or covert HE "occurs in 20%-80% of patients with cirrhosis" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Pooled prevalence of covert/minimal HE in a 2024 meta-analysis of 101 studies: 40.9% (95% CI 38.3–43.5%). In studies using the PHES it was 39.9% (36.7–43.1%) [[Lv2024CHE]].
- Risk of a first bout of overt HE: 5–25% within 5 years of cirrhosis diagnosis, depending on risk factors [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
  - Risk factors named: minimal or covert HE, infections, variceal bleeding, ascites, and probably diabetes and hepatitis C.
- Recurrence: 40% cumulative risk of overt HE recurring at 1 year after a first bout, and 40% risk of another recurrence within 6 months in people with recurrent HE, "despite lactulose treatment" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- After TIPS, the median cumulative 1-year incidence of overt HE is 10–50% [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- HE accounted for about 110,000 US hospitalisations a year (2005–2009) [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- US prospective cohort: 294 people with Child A–B cirrhosis and portal hypertension, no prior HE [[Tapper2020OHE]].
  - 21% developed overt HE over 548 ± 281 days.
  - 1-year probability: 14% overall, 10% Child A, 25% Child B.
- Covert HE and outcomes: 170 outpatients, 56% with covert HE, MELD-adjusted Cox models [[Patidar2014]]:
  - first overt HE: HR 2.1 (95% CI 1.01–4.5)
  - hospitalisation: HR 2.5 (1.4–4.5)
  - death or transplant: HR 3.4 (1.2–9.7)
- Danish population cohort, alcohol-related cirrhosis (n = 466): 1-year mortality was 64% after HE, 17% with no complications, 20% after variceal bleeding alone and 29% after ascites alone [[Jepsen2010]].
- Barcelona cohort, 111 people after a first episode of acute HE: survival probability 42% at 1 year and 23% at 3 years [[Bustamante1999]].
  - The authors concluded that such people "should be considered as potential candidates" for transplant.
- All mortality figures in this section are population statistics from those cohorts (Established).

### encephalopathy · Precipitants
- Precipitants of episodic overt HE, by decreasing frequency (AASLD/EASL 2014, Table 3): infections, gastrointestinal bleeding, diuretic overdose, electrolyte disorder, constipation, unidentified [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- For recurrent HE the order is electrolyte disorder, infections, unidentified, constipation, diuretic overdose, GI bleeding [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- The guideline adds that newer case series "confirm the dominant role of infections" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Hyponatraemia and sepsis can each cause encephalopathy on their own and also precipitate HE [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Medicines as precipitants are covered under medicine safety below: opioids [[Moon2020Opioids]], benzodiazepines [[Lee2014BZD]] and proton pump inhibitors [[Dam2016PPI]].

### encephalopathy · Ammonia pathway (mechanism)
- Ammonia "is primarily produced in the gut, as an end product of protein digestion, amino acid deamination and bacterial urease activity" [[Rose2020]].
- A healthy liver with an intact urea cycle keeps blood ammonia in "the low 35–50 μM range" [[Rose2020]].
- In liver disease, inter-organ ammonia handling changes [[Rose2020]]:
  - glutamine synthetase and glutaminase are active in brain, muscle and kidney
  - muscle, which "houses GS", becomes an alternative ammonia sink
  - this is why sarcopenia raises HE risk
- Ammonia "passes freely into the brain which is exclusively removed by astrocytes via glutamine synthetase" [[Rose2020]].
  - "The generation of glutamine renders the astrocyte hypertonic resulting in swelling and impaired function and brain oedema."
- Ammonia "remains the central player in the pathogenesis of HE". Systemic and neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, blood–brain barrier dysfunction and increased GABAergic tone (via neurosteroids) also contribute [[Rose2020]].
- HE has been seen as fully reversible after transplant. Evidence of neuronal cell death and persistent deficits after overt HE episodes "fundamentally changes our understanding of HE as a reversible syndrome" (Early evidence) [[Rose2020]].
- Blood ammonia testing: "High blood-ammonia levels alone do not add any diagnostic, staging, or prognostic value in HE patients with CLD". A normal level in someone with overt HE puts the diagnosis "in question" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].

### encephalopathy · Testing for covert HE and driving
- The PHES is the summed score of a paper-and-pencil test battery. The guideline notes it is copyrighted by Hannover Medical School and translated for many countries [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Animal naming test (ANT1, animals named in 1 minute), in 327 people with cirrhosis plus controls [[Campagna2017ANT]]:
  - A simplified score (S-ANT1), adjusted for age and education, of under 10 animals was abnormal.
  - 15 animals best separated unimpaired people from those with minimal or grade 1 HE.
  - Three-level score: 0 for ≥15, 1 for 10–14, 2 for under 10.
  - It predicted 1-year risk of overt HE and death.
- Driving: people with minimal HE diagnosed by the inhibitory control test had more crashes in the prior year (DOT records 17% vs 3%; relative risk 5.77) [[Bajaj2009Crashes]].
  - They also had more crashes or violations on 1-year follow-up (22% vs 7%).
  - Standard psychometric tests did not separate the groups.
- AASLD/EASL 2014: a minimal or covert HE diagnosis "does not automatically mean that the affected subject is a dangerous driver" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
  - It also says doctors should counsel on driving risks, and "often, the safest advice is to stop driving until the responsible driving authorities have formally cleared the patient" (quoted, not advice).
- Falls: 40% of outpatients with minimal HE (by PHES) reported falls in the prior year, against 12.9% without minimal HE (controls 11.6%) [[Roman2011Falls]].
  - Adjusted OR for minimal HE: 2.91 (95% CI 1.13–7.48).

### encephalopathy · Treatment and prevention: guideline statements (quoted, not instructions)
- "An episode of OHE (whether spontaneous or precipitated) should be actively treated" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Four-pronged approach: care for altered consciousness, look for other causes, identify and correct precipitants, and start empirical HE treatment [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- "Lactulose is the first choice for treatment of episodic OHE" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Lactulose dosing as published: "25 mL of lactulose syrup every 12 hours until at least two soft or loose bowel movements per day are produced", then "titrated to maintain two to three bowel movements per day" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
  - The guideline warns that overuse can cause "aspiration, dehydration, hypernatremia, and severe perianal skin irritation, and overuse can even precipitate HE".
- "Rifaximin is an effective add-on therapy to lactulose for prevention of OHE recurrence" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- "Lactulose is recommended for prevention of recurrent episodes of HE after the initial episode" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- "Rifaximin as an add-on to lactulose is recommended for prevention of recurrent episodes of HE after the second episode" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- "Primary prophylaxis for prevention of episodes of OHE is not required, except in patients with cirrhosis with a known high risk to develop HE" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- "Recurrent intractable OHE, together with liver failure, is an indication for LT [liver transplantation]" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- The guideline lists these as alternatives or additions when standard therapy fails [[Vilstrup2014HE]]:
  - oral branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs)
  - IV L-ornithine L-aspartate
  - neomycin
  - metronidazole

### encephalopathy · Lactulose and other non-absorbable disaccharides: trial evidence
- Secondary prophylaxis (open-label RCT, India), people who had recovered from HE: overt HE recurred in 12/61 (19.6%) on lactulose and 30/64 (46.8%) on placebo over a median 14 months (P = .001) [[Sharma2009Lactulose]].
  - Deaths: 5 vs 11 (P = .18).
- Primary prophylaxis (open-label RCT), no prior overt HE: overt HE developed in 6/55 (11%) on lactulose and 14/50 (28%) without, over 12 months (P = 0.02) [[Sharma2012Primary]].
- Meta-analysis of 38 RCTs (1,828 participants), lactulose and lactitol against placebo or no intervention [[Gluud2016NAD]]:
  - treatment: effect on HE RR 0.63 (95% CI 0.53–0.74; NNT 4); mortality in overt HE RR 0.36 (0.14–0.94; NNT 20)
  - prevention: development of HE RR 0.47 (0.33–0.68; NNT 6); mortality RR 0.63 (0.40–0.98)
- Lactulose label (Enulose): "2 to 3 tablespoonfuls (30 to 45 ml, containing 20 g to 30 g of lactulose) three or four times daily", "adjusted every day or two to produce 2 or 3 soft stools daily" (as published) [[FDA-Enulose]].
  - The label says lactulose reduces blood ammonia "by 25 to 50%", with clinical response in "about 75% of patients".
- HELP trial: 50 people hospitalised with HE, 4-L PEG 3350-electrolyte solution vs standard lactulose [[Rahimi2014HELP]].
  - HE grade improved by ≥1 at 24 h in 21/23 (91%) on PEG and 13/25 (52%) on lactulose.
  - Median time to HE resolution: 1 day vs 2 days (Early evidence: single centre, small).

### encephalopathy · Rifaximin
- Bass 2010 RCT: 299 people in remission from recurrent HE, rifaximin 550 mg twice daily vs placebo for 6 months [[Bass2010Rifaximin]].
  - Breakthrough HE: 22.1% vs 45.9% (HR 0.42, 95% CI 0.28–0.64).
  - HE-related hospitalisation: 13.6% vs 22.6% (HR 0.50, 0.29–0.87).
  - "More than 90%" of patients were also taking lactulose.
- In the 24-month open-label extension (n = 392), the exposure-normalised HE-related hospitalisation rate was 0.21. It was 0.30 in the rifaximin arm and 0.72 in the placebo arm of the original trial. Infections, including C. difficile, did not increase [[Mullen2014]].
- Treatment of overt HE (double-blind RCT, n = 120), rifaximin plus lactulose vs lactulose plus placebo [[Sharma2013RifaxLact]]:
  - complete reversal: 76% vs 50.8%
  - mortality: 23.8% vs 49.1%
- Meta-analysis of 19 RCTs (1,370 patients): rifaximin increased recovery from HE (RR 0.59, 95% CI 0.46–0.76) and reduced mortality (RR 0.68, 0.48–0.97) [[Kimer2014]].
- US label [[FDA-Xifaxan]]:
  - Indication: "Reduction in risk of overt hepatic encephalopathy (HE) recurrence in adults."
  - Dose: "550 mg taken orally two times a day".
  - Caution in "severe (Child-Pugh Class C) hepatic impairment" because of increased systemic exposure. Trials "were limited to patients with MELD scores <25".
- L-ornithine L-aspartate (Cochrane, 29 trials with data): benefit on HE against placebo or no intervention (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.59–0.83) on "very low quality evidence". No benefit in the one low-bias trial (Early evidence) [[Goh2018LOLA]].

### nutrition · Malnutrition, sarcopenia and frailty: how common
- Malnutrition "has been reported in 20% of patients with compensated cirrhosis and in more than 50% of patients with decompensated liver disease" [[EASL2019Nutrition]].
- Sarcopenia affects "30%-70% of patients with end-stage liver disease" [[Lai2021AASLD]].
  - In one multicentre study, 21% of women and 54% of men awaiting transplant met sarcopenia criteria by skeletal muscle index.
- AASLD 2021 reports sarcopenia in about 80% of people with decompensated alcohol-related cirrhosis and about 60% with NASH, chronic HCV or autoimmune hepatitis [[Lai2021AASLD]].
- Meta-analysis of 36 studies (N = 8,821): pooled sarcopenia prevalence 33%. By Child–Pugh class: 33% (A), 36% (B), 46% (C) [[Mazeaud2023]].
- Waitlist cohort (n = 142) [[Tandon2012]]:
  - 41% sarcopenic overall: 54% of men, 21% of women
  - by Child–Pugh class: 10% (A), 34% (B), 54% (C)
- Frailty prevalence among outpatients with cirrhosis "has ranged from 17% to 43%" [[Lai2021AASLD]].
- Sarcopenic obesity in cirrhosis: 20% to 35% [[Lai2021AASLD]].
- Disagreement: guideline ranges (30–70%) are higher than the pooled meta-analytic figure (33%). Definitions vary (CT skeletal muscle index, cut-offs, populations) [[Lai2021AASLD,Mazeaud2023]].

### nutrition · Why muscle matters
- Sarcopenia defined by CT skeletal muscle index (SMI) at L3 [[Carey2017SMI]]:
  - In a 5-centre study of 396 candidates, the best cut-offs for waitlist mortality were SMI under 50 cm²/m² for men and under 39 cm²/m² for women.
  - Median SMI was 50.0 (men) and 42.0 (women).
- Sarcopenia independently predicted waitlist mortality: HR 2.36 (95% CI 1.23–4.53), adjusted for age and MELD [[Tandon2012]].
- In 112 people evaluated for transplant [[MontanoLoza2012]]:
  - 40% had sarcopenia
  - median survival: 19 ± 6 months with sarcopenia vs 34 ± 11 months without
  - adjusted HR 2.21
  - SMI barely correlated with MELD (r = −0.07)
- Cirrhosis is a state of "accelerated starvation". Gluconeogenesis starts early after short fasts, which drives muscle breakdown [[Tsien2012,Plank2008]].

### nutrition · Frailty and the Liver Frailty Index (teaching tool)
- The Liver Frailty Index (LFI) combines grip strength, chair stands and balance [[Lai2017LFI]].
  - Derived in 536 listed outpatients.
  - C-statistic for 3-month waitlist mortality: 0.80 for MELDNa, 0.76 for LFI, 0.82 for both together.
- AASLD 2021 bands: robust LFI under 3.2, prefrail 3.2–4.3, frail ≥4.4 [[Lai2021AASLD]].
  - Some studies use ≥4.5 as the frail cut-off [[Haugen2020]].
- Optimal LFI cut-off for waitlist mortality in 1,405 candidates: 4.4 at 3 months and 4.2 at 6 and 12 months. Adding LFI raised the 3-month AUC from 0.73 (MELDNa) to 0.79 [[Kardashian2021]].
- Frailty roughly doubled waitlist mortality in both younger and older candidates (adjusted sHR 1.92). Candidates aged 65 or over were more often frail (33.3% vs 21.7%) [[Haugen2020]].
- Longitudinal LFI in 1,093 outpatients [[Lai2020DeltaLFI]]:
  - each 0.1-unit worsening per 3 months: sHR 2.04 (95% CI 1.35–3.09) for death or delisting
  - 6-month waitlist mortality: 12.1% with severe worsening, 7% when stable, 0.6% when frailty improved (observational) [[Lai2021AASLD]]
- After transplant, the share who were frail (LFI ≥4.5) fell from 29% to 9% at 12 months. Only 30% reached "robust" (LFI under 3.2) [[Lai2021AASLD]].
- LFI and SMI bands are teaching tools here. They are not for diagnosis, prognosis or transplant decisions.

### nutrition · Energy, protein and meal timing (quoted as published, not instructions)
- EASL 2019: "Optimal daily energy intake should not be lower than the recommended 35 kcal/kg.BW/d (in non-obese individuals)" [[EASL2019Nutrition]].
- EASL 2019: "Optimal daily protein intake should not be lower than the recommended 1.2–1.5 g/kg.BW/d" [[EASL2019Nutrition]].
- EASL 2019: "Include late evening oral nutritional supplementation and breakfast in dietary regime of malnourished decompensated cirrhotic patients" [[EASL2019Nutrition]].
  - Patient leaflet text: "The late-evening snack is the most important, as it covers the long interval between dinner and breakfast."
- AASLD 2021: "Recommended protein intake for adults with cirrhosis is 1.2-1.5 g/kg ideal body weight per day". For critically ill adults: 1.2–2.0 g/kg ideal body weight per day [[Lai2021AASLD]].
- AASLD 2021: "Fasting time should be minimized, with a maximum interval of 3-4 hours between nutritional intake while awake" [[Lai2021AASLD]].
- AASLD 2021 energy: at least 35 kcal/kg/day. This may be modified to 25–35 kcal/kg/day at BMI 30–40 and 20–25 kcal/kg/day at BMI ≥40 [[Lai2021AASLD]].
  - Measured total energy expenditure in cirrhosis is 28–38 kcal/kg/day.
- AASLD/EASL 2014 HE guideline: "Daily energy intakes should be 35-40 kcal/kg ideal body weight" and "Daily protein intake should be 1.2-1.5 g/kg/day" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
  - Also: "small meals or liquid nutritional supplements evenly distributed throughout the day and a late-night snack should be offered."
- ISHEN 2013: 35–45 kcal/kg and 1.2–1.5 g/kg protein daily, small frequent meals and "a late-night snack of complex carbohydrates" [[Amodio2013ISHEN]].
  - It notes vegetable and dairy protein may be better tolerated.
- Dry weight when ascites is present: subtract 5% (mild), 10% (moderate) or 15% (severe), plus 5% for bilateral pedal oedema [[EASL2019Nutrition,Lai2021AASLD]].
- Late-evening snack RCT: 103 people randomised to a 710 kcal/day supplement given at night or in the daytime for 12 months [[Plank2008]].
  - Night feeding increased total body protein by 0.38 kg at 3 months, 0.48 kg at 6 months and 0.53 kg at 12 months.
  - This equals "about 2 kg of lean tissue". The daytime group showed no gain.
- Systematic review: a late-evening snack improved nitrogen balance and quality of life. Effects on lean mass were inconsistent, and no mortality benefit has been shown (Early evidence) [[Tsien2012]].
- Weight loss in obesity: EASL 2019 advises a nutrition and lifestyle programme for progressive weight loss (>5–10%) in obese people with cirrhosis [[EASL2019Nutrition]].
  - The moderately hypocaloric diet (−500 to 800 kcal/d) keeps protein above 1.5 g/kg/d.
- Exercise: AASLD 2021 recommends combined aerobic and resistance exercise, tailored to frailty or sarcopenia assessment [[Lai2021AASLD]].
  - It calls following CDC guidance of 150–300 minutes a week "reasonable".
- In hospital: AASLD 2021 recommends dietitian consultation "within 24 hours of admission" [[Lai2021AASLD]].
  - It also recommends cutting down repeated nil-by-mouth orders.
- Sodium-restricted diets can lower intake. Only 31% of 120 outpatients with ascites kept to a 2-g sodium diet, and those who did ate 20% fewer calories [[Lai2021AASLD]].

### nutrition · Protein restriction is no longer advised for HE
- RCT in 30 people admitted with episodic HE: a normal-protein diet gave the same HE outcome as a low-protein diet with stepwise increases. The low-protein group had more protein breakdown [[Cordoba2004]].
- EASL 2019: "Avoid protein restriction in patients with HE" [[EASL2019Nutrition]].
  - It also states that normal to high protein intake "does not precipitate HE".
- AASLD 2021: 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day "is safe, does not worsen HE, and minimizes protein loss compared with lower protein doses" [[Lai2021AASLD]].
- EASL 2019 says BCAA supplementation "can be considered to improve neuropsychiatric performance and to reach the recommended nitrogen intake" in HE [[EASL2019Nutrition]].

### nutrition · Medicine safety in cirrhosis
- Review: most medicines "have not been adequately studied in cirrhosis". Lower doses or less frequent dosing are often recommended because pharmacokinetics change [[Lewis2013]].
- Paracetamol (acetaminophen): "Paracetamol can be used safely when prescribed in relatively small doses (2-3 g or less/day) for short durations, and is recommended as first-line treatment of pain" (review wording, quoted as published) [[Lewis2013]].
- Evidence on reduced paracetamol doses is weak: "High-quality trials are not available to support the use of decreased acetaminophen doses in compensated cirrhosis patients" [[Schweighardt2018]].
- US OTC label (general population): "Severe liver damage may occur if you take more than 4,000 mg of acetaminophen in 24 hours". The label also says "Ask a doctor before use if you have liver disease" [[FDA-Tylenol]].
- NSAIDs "should be used cautiously (or not at all) in advanced cirrhosis" [[Lewis2013]].
  - Drugs that can precipitate "renal failure, gastrointestinal bleeding, SBP and encephalopathy should be identified and avoided".
- US OTC ibuprofen label: "Ask a doctor before use if ... you have high blood pressure, heart disease, liver cirrhosis, kidney disease". It also warns that NSAIDs "may cause severe stomach bleeding" [[FDA-Advil]].
- Proton pump inhibitors, in 865 people with cirrhosis and ascites from three 1-year trials [[Dam2016PPI]]:
  - 52% used a PPI at some point
  - HE: adjusted HR 1.36 (95% CI 1.01–1.84); overt HE: adjusted HR 1.88
  - SBP: adjusted HR 1.72 (1.10–2.69)
  - 1-year HE risk: 31% with baseline PPI use vs 25% without
- Disagreement on PPIs and SBP: one meta-analysis (16 studies, 8,145 patients) found an overall OR of 2.11. The link held in case-control studies (OR 2.97) but not cohort studies (OR 1.18). The authors "could not establish causality" [[Yu2016PPI]].
- Opioids, in 6,451 people with compensated cirrhosis [[Moon2020Opioids]]:
  - HE over the next year: 3.3% with no opioid, 5.0% short-term, 6.3% chronic prescriptions
  - adjusted HR 1.44 (short-term) and 1.83 (chronic)
- Benzodiazepines, Taiwan cohort of 1,612 people with cirrhosis: benzodiazepine-associated HE was linked to [[Lee2014BZD]]:
  - long-acting agents (t½ >12 h)
  - high doses (>1.5 defined daily doses)
  - use longer than 2 months
  - albumin under 3 g/dL
- Psychoactive drugs and falls: 75% of people with minimal HE taking psychoactive drugs reported falls, against 15.3% without minimal HE [[Roman2011Falls]].
- Dosing by Child–Pugh in labels:
  - rifaximin: caution in Child–Pugh C [[FDA-Xifaxan]]
  - tacrolimus: people with severe hepatic impairment (Child–Pugh ≥10) "may require lower doses" with close blood-level monitoring [[FDA-Prograf]]
- Vaccination (US ACIP, quoted as published):
  - Hepatitis A: ACIP lists "Vaccination of persons with chronic liver disease, including but not limited to persons with hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, cirrhosis, fatty liver disease, alcoholic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis" (ACIP's own wording) [[Nelson2020ACIP]]. It says hepatitis A is more severe in people with chronic liver disease.
  - Hepatitis B: ACIP 2022 recommends universal vaccination for adults aged 19–59 [[Weng2022ACIP]]. Self-reported coverage (≥3 doses) was only 33.0% among adults with chronic liver disease in 2018, and response to the vaccine is weaker in chronic liver disease.
  - Pneumococcal: ACIP 2022 lists "Chronic liver disease" (and, in ACIP's wording, "Alcoholism") among the conditions for PCV20, or PCV15 followed by PPSV23, in adults aged 19–64 [[Kobayashi2022ACIP]].

### transplant · Who is considered and when
- Survival benefit depends on MELD. Among 12,996 US adult waitlist candidates (2001–2003), deceased donor transplant cut mortality risk by 79% overall (HR 0.21) [[Merion2005]].
  - At MELD 6–11 and 12–14, recipients had higher first-year mortality than candidates (HR 3.64 and 2.35).
  - The benefit rose with MELD: 96% lower risk at MELD 40.
  - This is the evidence behind referral once MELD reaches the mid-teens (Established).
- MELD itself was validated on bilirubin, creatinine and INR. Its c-statistic for 3-month death was 0.78–0.87 across four cohorts [[Kamath2001MELD]].
- MELD 3.0 adds female sex and albumin and caps creatinine at 3.0 mg/dL [[Kim2021MELD3]].
  - C-statistic 0.869 vs 0.862 for MELDNa.
  - Simulated waitlist deaths: 7,788 vs 7,850.
- MELD 3.0 replaced MELD-Na for US allocation in July 2023 [[Kwong2025SRTR]].
- HE and transplant: "Recurrent intractable OHE, together with liver failure, is an indication for LT" [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Hepatocellular carcinoma, Milan criteria: a single tumour ≤5 cm, or no more than 3 nodules each ≤3 cm [[Mazzaferro1996]].
  - In 48 transplanted patients, 4-year survival was 75% overall.
  - Within the criteria on explant pathology: 85% survival and 92% recurrence-free survival.
  - Beyond them: 50% and 59%.
- AASLD 2023 calls Milan "the standard for optimal patient selection" [[Singal2023AASLDHCC]]:
  - transplant brings a median survival of 10 years
  - post-transplant HCC recurrence is 10–15% even within Milan
  - people beyond Milan but within UNOS downstaging criteria "should be considered for LT following successful downstaging"
- Frailty and sarcopenia are now part of transplant assessment (see nutrition) [[Lai2021AASLD]].

### transplant · US numbers (OPTN/SRTR) — population statistics
- 2023: 10,659 liver transplants, a record. 10,125 (95.0%) were in adults and 534 in children [[Kwong2025SRTR]].
  - Living donors provided 5.7% of adult and 14.6% of paediatric transplants.
- 2024: 11,458 liver transplants, another record and up 70.3% since 2014 [[Kwong2026SRTR]].
  - 1-year survival for adult deceased-donor recipients: 92.0% for the graft and 93.5% for the patient.
- 2023 waiting list: 24,492 adult candidates ever waiting, and a record 13,954 newly added [[Kwong2025SRTR]].
  - Of 14,747 removals: 64.5% deceased donor transplant, 3.9% living donor transplant, 6.4% death, 6.6% too sick.
- Adult pretransplant mortality fell from 16.9 to 12.9 deaths per 100 patient-years over the decade [[Kwong2025SRTR]].
  - It was highest for alcohol-associated hepatitis (29.4) and MASH (16.5).
  - Women had higher rates than men (13.9 vs 12.2).
- Fate of adults newly listed 2018–2020, 3 years later [[Kwong2025SRTR]]:
  - 58.3% had a deceased donor transplant
  - 3.3% had a living donor transplant
  - 8.6% had died
  - 22.8% were removed for other reasons
  - 7.0% were still waiting
- Alcohol-associated liver disease was the leading indication in 2023: 41.1% of adult recipients (34.6% cirrhosis, 6.5% hepatitis), followed by MASH at 20.3% [[Kwong2025SRTR]].
- 1-year outcomes, transplanted 2022 vs 2012 [[Kwong2025SRTR]]:
  - 2022: patient mortality 6.5%, graft failure 7.9%
  - 2012: 10.0% and 11.6%
- 5-year survival, adult deceased-donor recipients transplanted 2016–2018 [[Kwong2025SRTR]]:
  - graft survival by age: 80.8% (18–34), 82.2% (35–49), 79.3% (50–64), 75.0% (≥65)
  - patient survival exceeded 75.1% in every age group
- 5-year graft survival: 75.6% for livers donated after circulatory death and 79.2% for brain-death donors [[Kwong2025SRTR]].
- Immunosuppression at discharge: 66.6% on tacrolimus + mycophenolate + steroids, 18.7% on tacrolimus + mycophenolate, 4.4% on tacrolimus + steroids [[Kwong2025SRTR]].

### transplant · Living donor transplantation
- A2ALL, 807 potential living donor recipients: receiving a living donor transplant was linked to lower mortality than waiting for a deceased donor (adjusted HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.42–0.74) [[Berg2007A2ALL]].
  - At centres with more than 20 cases: HR 0.35.
  - The authors note this "must be balanced against the risks undertaken by the living donors themselves".
- US living donor transplant mortality was higher with [[Olthoff2011]]:
  - older donor (HR 1.13 per 10 years) or recipient age (HR 1.20 per 10 years)
  - ICU stay before transplant (HR 2.52)
  - cold ischaemia time over 4.5 h (HR 1.79)
  - early centre experience
- Adult living donor graft failure: 6.6% at 1 year, 21.2% at 5 years and 40.8% at 10 years [[Kwong2025SRTR]].
  - There were 659 living donors in 2023, an all-time high, and 49 (7.5%) of these transplants were paired donations.

### transplant · Alcohol-related liver disease and the six-month rule
- "A 6-month abstinence from alcohol is usually required before patients with severe alcoholic hepatitis are considered for liver transplantation" [[Mathurin2011NEJM]].
  - Most deaths from alcohol-related hepatitis occur within 2 months.
- Franco-Belgian early transplant study: 26 highly selected people with a first episode of severe alcohol-related hepatitis not responding to medical therapy [[Mathurin2011NEJM]].
  - 6-month survival: 77% with early transplant vs 23% in matched controls.
  - Fewer than 2% of admitted patients were selected.
  - 3 returned to drinking 720–1,140 days after transplant.
- US ACCELERATE-AH, 147 recipients with a median of 55 days' abstinence [[Lee2018Gastro]]:
  - survival 94% at 1 year and 84% at 3 years
  - sustained alcohol use: 10% at 1 year and 17% at 3 years
  - sustained use linked to higher mortality (HR 4.59)
- QuickTrans prospective controlled study, 2-year alcohol relapse [[Louvet2022]]:
  - 34% after early transplant vs 25% after standard transplant (≥6 months abstinent); non-inferiority not shown
  - high alcohol intake more frequent after early transplant
  - 2-year post-transplant survival similar (HR 0.87)
  - overall survival much better than in non-eligible patients (HR 0.27)
- Status: early transplant for selected people with severe alcohol-related hepatitis is now offered at many centres. Selection criteria and relapse support remain debated (Early evidence) [[Lee2018Gastro,Louvet2022]].

### transplant · Immunosuppression and life after transplant
- Meta-analysis of 16 RCTs, tacrolimus vs cyclosporin as primary immunosuppressant [[McAlister2006]]:
  - lower 1-year mortality (RR 0.85) and graft loss (RR 0.73)
  - less acute rejection (RR 0.81)
  - more new diabetes (RR 1.38)
  - per 100 recipients: rejection avoided in 9, graft loss in 5 and death in 2, but 4 more develop diabetes
- Tacrolimus label: indicated "for the prophylaxis of organ rejection ... receiving allogeneic liver" transplants, with other immunosuppressants [[FDA-Prograf]].
  - Boxed warning: "Increased risk for developing serious infections and malignancies".
  - Initial oral dose in liver transplant with corticosteroids: "0.1-0.15 mg/kg/day ... divided in two doses, every 12 hours", with trough 5–20 ng/mL in months 1–12 (as published).
- Tacrolimus warnings include [[FDA-Prograf]]:
  - new-onset diabetes after transplant
  - acute or chronic nephrotoxicity
  - neurotoxicity including PRES, tremor and seizures
  - hyperkalaemia and hypertension
  - "Avoid eating grapefruit or drinking grapefruit juice"
  - therapeutic drug monitoring is recommended
- Acute rejection by 1 year (recipients transplanted 2022): 20.2% at ages 18–34 and 7.9% at 65 or over [[Kwong2025SRTR]].
  - 5-year post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder: 1.0% (EBV-positive at transplant) vs 2.0% (EBV-negative).
- Function after transplant: at least 90% of recipients improve their Karnofsky score, with a median 20% gain by 1 year [[Lai2021AASLD]].
  - Only about 20% reach "robust" LFI by 1 year, so rehabilitation continues after surgery.

### Exam hooks
- West Haven: grade I is covert (oriented, but with trivial lack of awareness and shortened attention). Grade II adds disorientation for time and asterixis. Grade III adds disorientation for place and somnolence. Grade IV is coma [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Covert HE = minimal HE + grade I. Overt = grades II–IV [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Overt HE occurs in 30–40% of people with cirrhosis during their course. Minimal or covert HE affects 20–80% [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- The commonest precipitant of episodic overt HE is infection, then GI bleeding and diuretic overdose [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- A raised ammonia alone does not diagnose, stage or predict HE [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Lactulose is titrated to 2–3 soft bowel movements a day [[Vilstrup2014HE,FDA-Enulose]].
- Secondary prophylaxis with lactulose: overt HE recurrence 19.6% vs 46.8% on placebo [[Sharma2009Lactulose]].
- Rifaximin 550 mg twice daily (with lactulose in >90%): breakthrough HE 22.1% vs 45.9%, HE hospitalisation 13.6% vs 22.6% over 6 months [[Bass2010Rifaximin]].
- Rifaximin is recommended as an add-on after the second episode of overt HE [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Protein restriction is not advised in HE. Targets are 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day protein and ≥35 kcal/kg/day energy [[EASL2019Nutrition,Lai2021AASLD]].
- Late-evening snack: 710 kcal at night added about 2 kg of lean tissue over 12 months; the same snack in the daytime did not [[Plank2008]].
- CT sarcopenia cut-offs in end-stage liver disease: L3 SMI under 50 cm²/m² (men) and under 39 cm²/m² (women) [[Carey2017SMI]].
- Liver Frailty Index = grip strength + chair stands + balance. Frail at ≥4.4 (AASLD) [[Lai2017LFI,Lai2021AASLD]].
- Paracetamol 2–3 g or less a day for short courses is the review-quoted first-line analgesic. NSAIDs should be avoided or used cautiously in advanced cirrhosis [[Lewis2013]].
- PPIs are linked to HE (aHR 1.36) and SBP (aHR 1.72) in cirrhosis with ascites [[Dam2016PPI]].
- Milan criteria: one tumour ≤5 cm, or up to 3 tumours each ≤3 cm [[Mazzaferro1996]].
- At MELD under 15, deceased donor transplant had no first-year survival benefit (HR 3.64 at MELD 6–11) [[Merion2005]].
- Tacrolimus boxed warning: serious infections and malignancies [[FDA-Prograf]].
- Living donor transplant: adjusted mortality HR 0.56 against waiting for a deceased donor [[Berg2007A2ALL]].
- US liver transplants: 10,659 in 2023 and 11,458 in 2024. 1-year patient survival was 93.5% (2024 report) [[Kwong2025SRTR,Kwong2026SRTR]].

### In plain words
- Hepatic encephalopathy means the brain is affected because the liver cannot clear some toxins, mainly ammonia, from the blood [[Rose2020]].
- It can be so mild that only special tests pick it up, or it can cause confusion, sleepiness and even coma [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- New confusion or drowsiness is an emergency. Call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department.
- Infections, bleeding, constipation, dehydration and some medicines can set off an episode. Finding and treating the trigger is part of care [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Lactulose is a syrup that helps the bowel move ammonia out. The usual aim is 2 to 3 soft bowel movements a day [[Vilstrup2014HE,FDA-Enulose]].
- Rifaximin is an antibiotic tablet that acts mostly in the gut. In one trial it halved repeat episodes when added to lactulose [[Bass2010Rifaximin]].
- Even mild hepatic encephalopathy can slow reactions and has been linked to car crashes and falls. Ask your care team whether driving is safe for you [[Bajaj2009Crashes,Roman2011Falls]].
- Eating enough protein is important. Cutting protein does not help the brain and can weaken muscles [[Cordoba2004,EASL2019Nutrition]].
- A snack before bed and an early breakfast shorten the long overnight fast that wears down muscle [[Plank2008,Lai2021AASLD]].
- Muscle strength, such as grip and getting out of a chair, tells doctors a lot about how well someone will cope with illness and surgery [[Lai2017LFI]].
- Always ask before taking pain relievers. Ibuprofen-type medicines can harm the kidneys and cause bleeding in people with cirrhosis [[Lewis2013,FDA-Advil]].
- Vaccines against hepatitis A and B and pneumococcus are recommended for people with chronic liver disease [[Nelson2020ACIP,Weng2022ACIP,Kobayashi2022ACIP]].
- A liver transplant can be a treatment when the liver is failing or for some liver cancers. In the US, more than 9 in 10 adults are alive one year after transplant [[Kwong2026SRTR]]. These are averages for large groups, not a prediction for you.
- After a transplant, people take anti-rejection medicines such as tacrolimus for life and have regular blood tests [[FDA-Prograf]].
- Talking about palliative care does not mean stopping treatment. It can sit alongside active care, including transplant evaluation, to help with symptoms and planning.

## Open questions and controversies
- Treating covert HE:
  - Position A: covert HE predicts overt HE, hospitalisation and death independent of MELD, and lactulose improved minimal HE in 66% in one trial [[Patidar2014,Sharma2012Primary]].
  - Position B: AASLD/EASL 2014 notes treating minimal or covert HE "is not standard", and trials are short and use different diagnostic tests [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
  - Status: unresolved. Testing is often recommended only when it would change management.
- Primary prophylaxis of overt HE:
  - Position A: lactulose cut first episodes from 28% to 11% in one open-label RCT [[Sharma2012Primary]].
  - Position B: the guideline says primary prophylaxis "is not required" except in high-risk people [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
  - Status: individualised.
- PEG vs lactulose for acute overt HE:
  - Position A: faster resolution with PEG [[Rahimi2014HELP]].
  - Position B: single-centre trial with 50 patients.
  - Status: Early evidence. Lactulose remains first choice in the 2014 guideline [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
- Paracetamol limits:
  - Position A: a review quotes 2–3 g/day or less for short courses [[Lewis2013]].
  - Position B: a systematic review found no high-quality evidence that compensated cirrhosis needs lower doses [[Schweighardt2018]]. The US OTC label sets 4,000 mg/24 h as the general maximum and tells people with liver disease to ask a doctor [[FDA-Tylenol]].
  - Status: practice varies.
- PPIs and SBP:
  - Position A: cohort data show a raised risk of SBP and HE [[Dam2016PPI]].
  - Position B: a meta-analysis found the link in case-control but not cohort studies and could not establish causality [[Yu2016PPI]].
  - Status: association likely, causation uncertain. A prescribing review says PPIs "should be used with care" in cirrhosis [[Lewis2013]].
- The six-month abstinence rule:
  - Position A: early transplant for selected people with severe alcohol-related hepatitis gives large survival gains [[Mathurin2011NEJM,Lee2018Gastro,Louvet2022]].
  - Position B: early transplant did not meet non-inferiority for relapse, and high alcohol intake was more frequent afterwards [[Louvet2022]].
  - Status: centre-dependent. Selection tools and addiction support are active research areas.
- Defining sarcopenia and frailty:
  - Position A: CT SMI cut-offs (50 and 39 cm²/m²) [[Carey2017SMI]].
  - Position B: LFI cut-offs differ between sources (≥4.4 in AASLD 2021 vs ≥4.5 in some cohorts; 4.2–4.4 optimal by horizon) [[Lai2021AASLD,Haugen2020,Kardashian2021]].
  - Prevalence estimates also range from 33% pooled to 30–70% in guidance [[Mazeaud2023,Lai2021AASLD]].
  - Status: not standardised.
- Is HE fully reversible after transplant?
  - Position A: the operational assumption is that it is [[Vilstrup2014HE]].
  - Position B: neuroinflammation and neuronal loss after repeated overt HE may leave lasting deficits [[Rose2020]].
  - Status: Early evidence.
- Late-evening snack:
  - Position A: it improves protein status and quality of life [[Plank2008,Tsien2012]].
  - Position B: no mortality benefit has been shown [[Tsien2012]].
  - Status: recommended by guidelines on mechanistic and surrogate evidence [[EASL2019Nutrition,Lai2021AASLD]].
