Part 2 · Causes

Alcohol-related liver disease

Alcohol is one of the two commonest causes of cirrhosis in many countries, alongside fatty liver disease linked to metabolism. Viral hepatitis, genetic and autoimmune diseases cause it too. Follow 100 people who drink heavily from fatty liver to scarring, and see what stopping does. [[Seitz2018NRDP,Younossi2023Hepatology]]

How much, and how often?

A 2019 meta-analysis pooled 7 cohort and 2 case-control studies: 2,629,272 people and 5,505 cases of cirrhosis. It compared each drinking level with long-term abstainers. Press the buttons to show women, men or both, and move the cursor along the drinking scale. [[Roerecke2019Cirrhosis]]

Sex

Smaller amounts harm women's livers

In the Copenhagen City Heart Study (13,285 adults, 12 years), alcohol-related cirrhosis occurred at 0.2% a year in men and 0.03% a year in women overall, but at any given intake women had the higher relative risk. Risk became significantly raised from 7–13 drinks a week in women and from 14–27 in men; the lowest risk was at 1–6 drinks a week. An earlier meta-analysis of 17 studies agrees. [[Becker1996Hepatology,Rehm2010Cirrhosis]]

Early evidence

Daily drinking carries more risk

At the same total intake, drinking every day rather than on some days raised cirrhosis risk: RR 1.71 in men and 1.56 in women in a meta-analysis of 5 studies. In a Danish cohort of 55,917 adults, daily drinking in men carried HR 3.65 compared with drinking 2–4 days a week, and recent intake (ages 40–59) mattered more than intake at ages 20–39. [[LlamosasFalcon2023,Askgaard2015JHep]]

Hypothesis

Is wine different?

Among Danish men drinking 14–28 drinks a week, risk compared with under 14 a week was HR 7.47 when almost none of it was wine and HR 1.69 (95% CI 0.79–3.65) when most of it was. The authors say wine "might be" linked to lower risk; lifestyle differences between wine and beer or spirits drinkers may explain it. [[Askgaard2015JHep]]

Cutting down

Every step down helps

In a US national survey of 21,925 current drinkers followed for 3 years, very-high-risk drinkers who dropped 1, 2 or 3 WHO drinking-risk levels had lower odds of liver disease (adjusted OR 0.34, 0.23 and 0.17), without stopping completely. Stopping helps most. [[Knox2018Liver,Xie2014HepatolRes]]

Established

Genes change the odds

A genome-wide study of alcohol-related cirrhosis confirmed PNPLA3 rs738409 (the I148M variant) as a major risk locus and found TM6SF2 and MBOAT7, all involved in handling fat. The risk allele is more frequent in Hispanic populations. The same variant raises risk in metabolic fatty liver disease. [[Buch2015NatGenet,Romeo2008PNPLA3,Singal2018ACG]]

What counts as a drink

Standard drinks

ACG 2018 defines a US standard drink as about 14 g of alcohol: 12 oz of 5% beer, 5 oz of wine or 1.5 oz of 40–45% liquor. It describes heavy drinking as more than 3 drinks a day for men and more than 2 for women; binge drinking (NIAAA) is more than 5 drinks for men and more than 4 for women within 2 hours. [[Singal2018ACG]]

Alcohol-associated hepatitis

Severe steatohepatitis, with or without cirrhosis, can present suddenly as alcohol-associated hepatitis: jaundice, liver failure and a high risk of death. It needs hospital care. Step through how it is recognised, graded and treated. [[Seitz2018NRDP,Jophlin2024ACG]]

Treating alcohol use disorder alongside liver disease

ACG 2024 notes that people with alcohol-related liver disease are "rarely treated for AUD", and calls for integrated care with hepatology, addiction medicine and social work. Among 35,682 US veterans with cirrhosis and alcohol use disorder, only 14% received AUD treatment within 180 days: 0.4% received a medicine alone and 1% a medicine plus behavioural therapy. [[Jophlin2024ACG,Rogal2020Hepatology]]

Early evidence

Treatment goes with fewer complications

In those veterans, AUD treatment was linked to less hepatic decompensation (6.5% vs 11.6%; adjusted OR 0.63) and lower long-term mortality (51% vs 58%; adjusted OR 0.87). In 9,635 people with AUD in a US biobank, medicine for AUD was linked to less new alcohol-related liver disease (adjusted OR 0.37) and, with cirrhosis, less decompensation (adjusted OR 0.35). Both studies are observational. [[Rogal2020Hepatology,Vannier2022]]

Randomised trial

Baclofen in cirrhosis

In 84 people with AUD and cirrhosis, 71% on baclofen achieved and kept abstinence over 12 weeks vs 29% on placebo (OR 6.3), with no hepatic side effects recorded. ACG 2018 states that of the AUD medicines, "only baclofen ... has been found to be safe in patients with ALD and cirrhosis". Quoted as published, not as advice. [[Addolorato2007Lancet,Singal2018ACG]]

Label

Acamprosate

The US label states that acamprosate "is not metabolized by the liver" and that its pharmacokinetics "are not altered" in Child–Pugh A or B hepatic impairment. It is cleared by the kidneys: contraindicated at creatinine clearance ≤30 mL/min, with a reduced dose at 30–50 mL/min. [[FDA-Campral]]

Practice is moving

Naltrexone

The tablet label reports cases of hepatitis and "clinically significant liver dysfunction", often with alcohol-related liver disease, viral hepatitis or other hepatotoxic drugs as contributors. In 3,285 US veterans with cirrhosis who started naltrexone, 2% had enzyme rises and no case was classed as drug-induced liver injury; a smaller cohort says "more safety data are needed" in decompensated cirrhosis. [[FDA-Naltrexone,Thompson2024JHEP,Ayyala2022]]

Transplant

Early transplant for severe hepatitis

The old rule asked for 6 months of abstinence first, yet most deaths from severe alcohol-associated hepatitis come within 2 months. Early transplant for carefully selected people now has published survival of 94% at 1 year and 84% at 3 years, with return to drinking still debated. See Liver transplant. [[Mathurin2011NEJM,Lee2018Gastro,Louvet2022Lancet]]
