Part 4 · Complications

Hepatic encephalopathy

When a scarred liver and its bypass veins let ammonia slip past, the brain is affected: from changes only a test can find, to confusion, sleepiness and coma. Follow ammonia from the gut to the brain, see the West Haven grades, and how lactulose and rifaximin are used.

Urgent careVomiting blood, black or tarry stools, new confusion or drowsiness, or fever or belly pain with a swollen abdomen need urgent care: call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department.

The West Haven grades

Overt hepatic encephalopathy (HE) affects 30–40% of people with cirrhosis at some point in their illness, and minimal or covert HE is found in 20–80%; a 2024 meta-analysis of 101 studies pooled covert HE at 40.9%. In a US cohort of 294 people without prior HE, the 1-year chance of a first overt episode was 10% in Child A and 25% in Child B. [[Vilstrup2014HE,Lv2024CHE,Tapper2020OHE]]

GradeGroupWhat it looks like (our summary of AASLD/EASL 2014)
MinimalCovertNo change a clinician can see; picked up only by psychometric or neurophysiological testing.

Grade IIOvertDrowsy or apathetic, mixed up about the date, clearly changed personality or behaviour, clumsy planned movements, and asterixis (the "liver flap", a flapping tremor). [[Lui2026Asterixis]] Bedside check for time: three or more of these five are wrong: date, weekday, month, season, year.
Grade IIIOvertVery sleepy, close to stupor but still rousable, deeply confused and behaving oddly. Also wrong about place: three or more of these are wrong: country, state or region, city, place.
Grade IVOvertComa, with no response even to pain.

Summarised in our own words from the AASLD/EASL 2014 guideline; the full wording is in the guideline itself. The guideline treats the West Haven criteria as the gold standard but notes that different observers often disagree, most of all about grade I. HE is also classified by cause (type A acute liver failure, type B mainly bypass, type C cirrhosis), course (episodic, recurrent, persistent) and whether it was precipitated; for example, a person with cirrhosis having a repeat grade 3 episode set off by a urine infection would be type C, grade 3, recurrent and precipitated. HE is diagnosed by excluding other causes of brain dysfunction. [[Vilstrup2014HE]]

How lactulose and rifaximin are used

Toggle a treatment · schematic gut

The trials · per 100 people

What the guideline recommends (AASLD/EASL 2014), in our words, not instructions:

  • Every episode of overt HE is treated actively, whether or not it had a clear trigger.
  • Lactulose is the first-choice treatment for an episode: 25 mL every 12 hours until there are at least two soft or loose bowel movements a day, then adjusted to keep two to three a day.
  • After a first episode, lactulose is recommended to prevent another.
  • After a second episode, rifaximin is added to lactulose to prevent further ones.
  • Preventive treatment before any episode is not needed, except for people with cirrhosis known to be at high risk of HE.
  • Overt HE that keeps coming back despite treatment, together with liver failure, is a reason for liver transplant.

[[Vilstrup2014HE]]

The guideline warns that too much lactulose can lead to aspiration, dehydration, high blood sodium and badly irritated skin around the anus, and that overuse can itself bring on HE. The US label (Enulose) adjusts the dose every day or two to reach 2 or 3 soft stools a day and reports blood ammonia falling by 25 to 50%. The US rifaximin label (Xifaxan) covers lowering the risk that overt HE comes back in adults, at 550 mg by mouth twice a day, with caution in severe (Child-Pugh class C) liver impairment. [[Vilstrup2014HE,FDA-Enulose,FDA-Xifaxan]]

Across 38 trials (1,828 people), lactulose and lactitol improved HE (RR 0.63) and lowered mortality in overt HE (RR 0.36). Rifaximin added to lactulose for an acute episode gave complete reversal in 76% vs 50.8% in one trial of 120. [[Gluud2016NAD,Sharma2013RifaxLact]] Early evidence In a 50-person single-centre trial, a polyethylene glycol bowel prep improved HE grade at 24 hours more often than lactulose (91% vs 52%). [[Rahimi2014HELP]]

Finding the trigger

In cirrhosis, a trigger can be found for almost every episode of HE, and the guideline says to look for it actively and treat it. Everyday life with HE

Covert HE

Driving

Drivers with minimal HE on an inhibitory control test had more crashes in the year before (17% vs 3% by transport records; relative risk 5.77) and more crashes or violations over the next year (22% vs 7%). The guideline says covert HE does not by itself make someone a dangerous driver, that doctors should talk through the risks, and that the safest advice is often to stop driving until the driving authority has formally cleared the person (summarised, not advice). [[Bajaj2009Crashes,Vilstrup2014HE]] Ask your care team whether driving is safe for you.

For carers

Spotting early signs

Early changes can be small: the grade I signs include a disturbed sleep pattern, a shorter attention span, a lifted or anxious mood, and slips with simple sums. [[Vilstrup2014HE]] The LiverHope programme teaches carers to look for early signs of HE and infection and to call the nurse or go to the emergency department; HE education about giving and adjusting medicines can ease the load on carers, whose burden is higher after HE. [[Fabrellas2020LiverHope,Rogal2022AASLD,Bajaj2011Burden]] New confusion or drowsiness needs urgent care.

Food

Protein is not cut

In a trial of 30 people admitted with HE, normal protein did as well as a low-protein diet, which caused more protein breakdown. EASL 2019: "Avoid protein restriction in patients with HE"; AASLD 2021: 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day "is safe, does not worsen HE" (quoted, not instructions). Muscle is a second place the body can clear ammonia, which is why muscle loss raises HE risk. [[Cordoba2004,EASL2019Nutrition,Lai2021AASLD,Rose2020]] More in Nutrition, muscle & medicine safety.

Tests

Is a blood ammonia level needed?

The guideline says that in chronic liver disease a high blood ammonia on its own does not help diagnose HE, grade it or predict its course. A normal level in someone with overt HE puts the diagnosis "in question". [[Vilstrup2014HE]]

Falls

Falls and medicines

40% of outpatients with minimal HE reported a fall in the prior year, against 12.9% without (adjusted OR 2.91). Opioids (HE in 3.3% with none, 6.3% with chronic prescriptions), long-acting or high-dose benzodiazepines and proton pump inhibitors (adjusted HR 1.36) have all been linked to HE. [[Roman2011Falls,Moon2020Opioids,Lee2014BZD,Dam2016PPI]]

Early evidence

Fully reversible?

HE has been seen as fully reversible after transplant, but evidence of neuronal cell death and lasting deficits after overt episodes "fundamentally changes our understanding of HE as a reversible syndrome". [[Rose2020]]

Outlook

What the numbers mean

After a first acute episode, 42% of one Barcelona cohort were alive at 1 year and 23% at 3 years, and the authors said such people "should be considered as potential candidates" for transplant. In a Danish cohort with alcohol-related cirrhosis, 1-year mortality was 64% after HE vs 17% without complications. These are population statistics from older cohorts, not predictions for one person. [[Bustamante1999,Jepsen2010]] See Liver transplant.

Support

Palliative care alongside treatment

AASLD describes palliative care as multidisciplinary specialised care for the physical, spiritual and psychosocial needs of people with serious illness and their carers, which can be considered at any stage of cirrhosis alongside treatment of the liver disease. Near the end of life, HE treatment can be shaped around the person's goals. [[Rogal2022AASLD]] See Living with cirrhosis.

Doses and guideline statements on this page are reported as published (AASLD/EASL, US labels), never as instructions. HE can happen in cirrhosis of any cause: MASLD, alcohol-related liver disease, viral hepatitis, autoimmune and genetic diseases.
