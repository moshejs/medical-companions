Myasthenia gravis study guide, explained

In myasthenia gravis the nerves and the muscles are healthy, but the message between them is under attack. Here it is in moving pictures: one nerve impulse in slow motion, the antibodies that block it, why weakness grows through the day, how each treatment works, and what to watch for in a crisis.

Myasthenia gravis at a glance

What it is
Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disease of the nerve-muscle junction: muscles tire with use and recover with rest. Antibodies attack proteins on the muscle side of the junction, so the nerves themselves are healthy, and sensation, reflexes and pupils stay normal. 276,367
Who gets it
MedlinePlus gives about 20 people per 100,000. 345 Before age 40, women outnumber men about 3 to 1; after 50, men slightly outnumber women. 107
Main symptoms
About two-thirds of patients start with eye symptoms. 128 In a Japanese survey, a drooping eyelid was a first symptom in 74.9% and double vision in 47.4%. 567 Weakness is generally worse later in the day. 276
How it is diagnosed
A blood test finds acetylcholine receptor antibodies in about 85% of generalized MG but only about half of eye-only MG. Single-fibre EMG, a nerve and muscle test, is the most sensitive test. 1,461,564
Main treatments
The international consensus says pyridostigmine, a tablet that eases symptoms, should be part of first treatment in most patients, followed by steroids or other immune drugs if goals are not met. 449,476 In the MGTX trial, removing the thymus improved strength and lowered steroid needs over 3 years in people with receptor antibodies. 78,228,381,552 Newer drugs either block complement, part of the immune attack, or lower antibody levels; guidelines differ on when to use them. 200,381,549
Outlook
Myasthenic crisis, when the breathing muscles need a ventilator, has a lifetime risk of 15 to 20% in receptor-antibody MG. 107,367 Deaths from MG are now under 5%, with near-normal life expectancy; before modern immune treatment they were about 30%. 276
