# C. Diagnosis, classification & the differential
This pass covers how myasthenia gravis (MG) is confirmed: antibody assays, electrodiagnosis, bedside and pharmacological tests, and thymus imaging. It also covers the order of steps that published reviews give, the MGFA clinical classification and post-intervention status, the MG-ADL, QMG, MG Composite and MG-QOL15r scales, and the conditions most often confused with MG, as input for a conditions x features matrix. Numbers come from abstracts read through Europe PMC/PubMed, and from open-access full texts of Rousseff 2021 (PMC8073361), Yoganathan 2022 (PMC9119875), Sanders 2016 (PMC4977114), Finsterer 2019 (PMC6390566), Krenn 2020 (PMC7457047), Safa 2019 (PMC6868691) and the CDC botulism guideline (PMC8112830). The MGFA classification, MGFA-PIS and MG-ADL wording come from the MGFA's own PDFs (myasthenia.org). The MG Composite weights and MG-ADL instructions come from MGNet V1.0 forms (rarediseasesnetwork.org). What I could not access: the full texts of Jaretzki 2000 (Neurology, no abstract), Gilhus 2019 (Nature paywall), Benatar 2006 (only the abstract, and its pooled numbers as quoted by Rousseff 2021) and Titulaer 2011 Lancet Neurol (abstract only). Where a pooled number is only available second-hand, the bullet names the review that quotes it. I found no diagnostic-accuracy study for "enhanced ptosis" (curtain sign); this is flagged below.

## Facts

### Antibody tests: AChR binding, modulating, blocking, clustered AChR (cell-based assay)
- In the original 153 validated cases, AChR binding antibody (radioimmunoassay with human AChR) was undetectable in 26/153 (17%). A modified antigen cut this to 12%, so the assay was positive in about 88% of people with clinical MG. Anti-AChR without clinical MG was found in 1 of 3 monozygotic twins and 1 of 30 other first-degree relatives [Vincent1985].
- Review anchor for the calculator: radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) binding antibody is abnormal in up to 85% of adults with generalized MG but in only about 50% of ocular MG [Rousseff2021].
- A second review gives AChR antibody sensitivity in adults as 85-90% (generalized MG) and 50-70% (ocular MG). It is about 50% in childhood-onset MG and "nearly 100%" in thymoma-associated MG. Specificity is 95-100% [Yoganathan2022].
- Range across studies (Yoganathan table): AChR antibody sensitivity 50-97% in all MG, 73-94% in generalized and 38-75% in ocular; specificity 95-100% [Yoganathan2022].
- Controversy on specificity: the AChR assay is called "very high[ly]" specific and diagnostic in the right clinical setting. Yet Rousseff notes that authoritative sources recently reported specificities of 90-95%, with dozens of false positives in large series, possibly because the test is used for screening rather than confirmation [Rousseff2021].
- Binding and modulating antibodies were each found in about 86% of 349 MG patients. Adding modulating antibodies when binding was negative raised the total yield to 90%: in 27 patients (8%) only one of the two tests was positive. Blocking antibodies were positive in 52% and were never the only positive test [Howard1987].
- In a laboratory series of 41,180 sera (4,740 AChR-antibody positive), binding antibody was positive in 88% of positive sera, modulating in 70% and blocking in 65% [Yoganathan2022].
- Rousseff 2021 says modulating and blocking assays add "little diagnostic value", while noting that others have recently emphasized their significance [Rousseff2021].
- In selected seronegative cases, AChR antibody testing may be repeated within 6-12 months. Delayed seroconversion is well described but rare [Rousseff2021].
- ELISA for binding AChR antibody is generally considered less sensitive and less specific than RIPA [Rousseff2021; Habib2025].
- Cell-based assay (CBA) for clustered AChR (rapsyn co-expression): 66% (25/38) of sera negative for AChR binding in solution were positive. These antibodies were mainly IgG1 and could activate complement [Leite2008].
- In routine practice in Oxford (138 tested, 42 with final MG), the clustered-AChR CBA found antibodies in 38.1% (16/42) of RIPA-negative MG, with 100% specificity. CBA-only patients had more prepubertal onset (62.5% vs 11.5%), more ocular MG (62.5% vs 42.3%) and remission in 50.0% vs 8.3% [RodriguezCruz2015].
- Disagreement on added yield: Rousseff gives about 15% of RIPA-seronegative MG as clustered-AChR positive. The table range across studies is 13-66% sensitivity with 100% specificity [Rousseff2021; Yoganathan2022].

### Antibody tests: MuSK, LRP4, double seronegative
- In the discovery report, 70% of AChR-antibody-seronegative generalized MG patients had MuSK antibodies; no AChR-positive patient did [Hoch2001].
- Later estimates of MuSK share vary widely: 0-10% of all generalized MG [Higuchi2011]; 5-10% of all MG [Pevzner2012]; about 6-8% of all MG cases [Rousseff2021]; and 5-70% of AChR-negative generalized MG, with MuSK generally not detected in purely ocular MG [Yoganathan2022]. Rousseff attributes the variation to geography and genetic predisposition [Rousseff2021].
- MuSK antibody specificity is 94-100% [Yoganathan2022]. MuSK antibodies are mainly IgG4 [Yoganathan2022; Zisimopoulou2014].
- Yoganathan's scheme: if AChR binding antibody is negative, or the titre is <0.02 nmol/L, test MuSK next. Some clinicians order both at the first visit [Yoganathan2022].
- Double seronegative MG (neither AChR nor MuSK) is about 6-10% of MG [Yoganathan2022]. Maccora 2025 gives seronegative MG as about 10% of MG cases [Maccora2025].
- LRP4 in double seronegative MG: in about 800 sera from 10 countries (635 double seronegative), the frequency was 18.7%, ranging from 7% to 32.7% by population. 27% of ocular double-seronegative MG was LRP4 positive. No healthy control (0/56) was positive, but 4/110 with other neuroimmune diseases were. LRP4 antibodies were mainly IgG1/IgG2, and 81% of patients had MGFA class I or II at onset [Zisimopoulou2014].
- In 13 double-seronegative generalized MG sera, about 50% bound LRP4-transfected cells, and 4/13 inhibited agrin-induced AChR clustering by more than 50% [Pevzner2012].
- LRP4 antibody is not a clean marker. It may be pathogenic in about 2% of MG, but it is present in up to 23% of ALS patients and up to 20% of MuSK-MG. Rousseff says it should be interpreted strictly in the clinical context [Rousseff2021]. The range across studies is 1-50% [Yoganathan2022].
- Rousseff reports "double seropositive" MG (AChR plus MuSK) as up to 12.5% of patients, seemingly more frequent in East Asian populations [Rousseff2021].

### Antibody tests: striational (titin, RyR) and the thymoma link
- Striational antibodies react with titin and ryanodine receptor (RyR). They are found mainly in thymoma and late-onset MG, may correlate with severity, and may predict a poorer outcome after thymectomy. Titin/RyR assays carry more specific information than immunofluorescent striational staining [Romi2005].
- Diagnostic sensitivity for MG is 20-40% for titin antibodies and about 15% for RyR antibodies. The table range across studies is 20-74% (titin) and 15-74% (RyR) [Yoganathan2022].
- In one 19-patient thymoma-MG study, RyR antibodies correlated with myositis (p=0.03) [Yoganathan2022].
- A four-antibody panel (AChR, acetylcholinesterase, titin, RyR) in 89 MG patients gave 94% sensitivity and 84% specificity. This is not standard practice [Yoganathan2022].
- In the presence of striational antibodies, thymoma is "very likely". In MuSK-MG, thymoma is seen in single cases only [Rousseff2021].

### Antibody false positives and look-alikes (for the calculator's "positive without MG" branch)
- Unexplained positive AChR binding results were previously documented in 5% of LEMS and ALS patients. In the Mayo series they were seen in 2 of 22 ALS patients and 0 of 427 with miscellaneous neurological disease. Recent general anaesthesia with muscle relaxants gave false positives in the blocking and modulating assays [Howard1987].
- A false-positive AChR immunoassay in ALS was reported as early as 1980 [Mittag1980].
- AChR antibody may be positive in thymoma without clinical MG, so electrodiagnosis may be needed in doubtful cases. Up to one-third of people with thymoma develop MG [Rousseff2021].
- Some people have raised AChR titres years before clinical onset (found in stored sera). This may explain some apparent false positives [Rousseff2021].
- Two of 8 chronic MG mimics in one series (misdiagnosed and treated as refractory MG for years) were AChR antibody positive [Harrison2023].
- VGCC (P/Q) antibodies, the LEMS marker, appear at lower titres in 23% of sporadic ALS, 24% of cancer patients without neurological disease, and less than 3% of MG, epilepsy or scleroderma patients [Lennon1995].

### Electrodiagnosis: repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS)
- The validated criterion: at 2-5 Hz, a 10% decrement in amplitude from the 1st to the 4th or 5th response in the train is valid for diagnosing MG. For LEMS the increment needed is at least 25%, but it is most accurate when greater than 100%. RNS remains the preferred initial electrodiagnostic test [AAEM2001].
- Animation anchor (shape of the train): in MG the drop is largest between the 1st and 2nd responses, reaches a nadir at the 4th-5th, then stabilizes or partly recovers. This gives the "U" or staircase shape typical of postsynaptic disease. Low rates are usually 2 or 3 Hz [Rousseff2021].
- Abnormality limit: decrement >10% of initial amplitude or area. A recent study found a 7% cut-off raises sensitivity significantly with little loss of specificity [Rousseff2021]. Cut-offs of 7% (frontalis) and 8% (nasalis) raised sensitivity by 6-11% with specificity kept at 95-96%. The 10% cut-off "has never been validated" [Yoganathan2022].
- Post-activation sequence: after brief maximal voluntary contraction (10-60 s) or 30-50 Hz stimulation, CMAPs immediately after exertion rise (post-exercise facilitation). This is followed by worsening decrement (post-exercise exhaustion) lasting 2-5 min. In MG, post-exercise testing adds only a few percent of diagnostic yield [Rousseff2021].
- Retesting nasalis 1 min after muscle activation raised RNS sensitivity by only 0-2%, compared with 9-15% for testing a second muscle at rest [Yoganathan2022].
- Muscle choice anchors: in generalized MG, distal-muscle RNS is abnormal in 30-35% and proximal-muscle RNS in 60-70%. In ocular or ocular-onset MG, distal-muscle RNS is about 10% sensitive and facial-muscle RNS 35-38% [Rousseff2021].
- RNS specificity is about 97% in generalized MG and 94% in ocular MG. Rousseff cites this from the Benatar systematic review, and it reaches 100% when six pairs of muscles are studied [Rousseff2021; Benatar2006].
- Frontalis RNS sensitivity (≥10% cut-off) was 46% in generalized and 15% in ocular MG; nasalis was 35% and 19% (122 MG, 182 controls). Exploring 12 muscles bilaterally raised overall sensitivity to 82% with 100% specificity: 89% generalized, 86% oculobulbar, 67% ocular [Yoganathan2022].
- Nasalis RNS was 100% sensitive in oculobulbar MG versus 20% for hypothenar muscles. In generalized MG the two had similar yield [Yoganathan2022].
- In 37 prospectively diagnosed untreated MG patients, RNS was abnormal in only 33% of ocular MG but in at least one muscle in every bulbar and axial patient. Trapezius was most sensitive in limb-axial MG, anconeus and nasalis in bulbar MG. The authors concluded RNS is not sensitive in ocular MG and jitter should be done in facial muscles [Costa2004].
- Head-to-head with LEMS: abductor digiti quinti RNS was abnormal in 54% of 140 MG patients, and low-rate decrement was present in 46% [Oh2017].
- Cross-study ranges: RNS sensitivity 14-94% and specificity 73-100% overall; ocular MG 14-67%, generalized 32-94% [Yoganathan2022].
- High jitter (>100 µs) and decrement >10% are associated with more severe disease, while serum AChR antibody concentration does not track clinical severity [Yoganathan2022].

### Electrodiagnosis: single-fibre EMG and concentric-needle jitter
- Single-fibre EMG (SFEMG) is more sensitive than RNS for neuromuscular transmission disorders, but it may be less specific and may not be available. Abnormal jitter or impulse blocking are the diagnostic criteria [AAEM2001].
- Jitter measured with concentric-needle electrodes (CNE) is abnormal in 98-100% of generalized MG and in more than 90% of ocular MG. A normal jitter study in a weak muscle, or in three well-chosen muscles, carries a negative predictive value of about 98% [Rousseff2021].
- Low specificity: increased jitter also occurs in other synaptic disorders, denervation and reinnervation (neuropathies), some myopathies and particularly motor neuron disease. Cosmetic or therapeutic botulinum toxin use must be excluded [Rousseff2021].
- Jitter is abnormal after botulinum toxin injections even in muscles far from the injection site, and this can persist for 6 months or more. CNE and SFEMG have "similar and very high" sensitivity. Fibre density cannot be measured with CNE, so conventional EMG is needed to look for neurogenic or myopathic change [Sanders2022].
- Ocular MG with ptosis (155 referrals, 102 MG): stimulated orbicularis oculi SFEMG had sensitivity 94% (95% CI 89-98) and specificity 79% (68-90) [Giannoccaro2020].
- Other SFEMG anchors: in 348 tested (108 MG), sensitivity was 78% overall (73% ocular, 85% generalized) with specificity 91%. Orbicularis oculi SFEMG in another study had 98% sensitivity, 70% specificity and NPV 97%. In Katzberg's series, RNS was abnormal in 19% versus SFEMG in 95% (n=121) [Yoganathan2022].
- Cross-study ranges: SFEMG sensitivity 64-100% and specificity 22-100%. Yoganathan concludes SFEMG has the highest sensitivity and positive AChR antibodies the highest specificity [Yoganathan2022].
- Normal and abnormal jitter, SFEMG: normal mean consecutive difference (MCD) has been given as 10-60 µs. Abnormal is variably defined as mean jitter >40 µs, or >10% of pairs with blocking or jitter >54 µs. Limits vary by muscle and age, and about 20 fibre pairs are traditionally analysed [Yoganathan2022].
- CNE reference limits (multicentre, healthy controls), voluntary activation, as mean-MCD and individual-pair outlier limits: orbicularis oculi 31 and 45 µs; frontalis 28 and 38 µs; extensor digitorum 30 and 43 µs. Electrical stimulation: orbicularis oculi 27 and 36 µs; frontalis 21 and 28 µs; extensor digitorum 24 and 35 µs [Stalberg2016].

### Bedside and pharmacological tests
- Ice-pack test, single best study: in 155 consecutive ptosis referrals (102 ocular MG), sensitivity was 86% (95% CI 79-93) and specificity 79% (68-90). Combined with SFEMG, sensitivity was 98% and specificity 66% if either test was positive, and 82% and 92% if both were. Negativity of both had an NPV of 94% [Giannoccaro2020].
- Ice-pack systematic review (20 studies, 1,264 participants): sensitivity ranged from 38.5% to 100%. Specificity was >95% in six studies, and the lowest was 62.5% once two outliers (25%, 31.3%) were excluded. The commonest method was ice on both eyelids with a >2 mm change (ruler-measured) as positive. No adverse effects were reported [Proudman2023].
- Ice-pack technique and positivity: ice over the symptomatic eye for 2-5 min. The test is positive with ≥2 mm improvement of ptosis, a cut-off Rousseff calls "somewhat arbitrary", since heat produced similar changes in some patients. Some report specificity as low as about 25% [Rousseff2021].
- Ice, rest and heat anchors: mean ptosis improvement was 2.3 (±1.5) mm with ice, 1.3 (±1.1) mm with rest and 0.33 (±1.4) mm with heat (2-min tests). Median improvement was about 2 mm with rest versus 4.5 mm with ice. Repeating the ice test raised sensitivity by 35% [Yoganathan2022].
- Sustained upgaze (Simpson test): ptosis that increases on sustained upgaze shows levator fatigability. In one study, ice-pack alone was 28% sensitive and 100% specific, versus 73% and 97% when combined with 2 min of sustained upgaze [Yoganathan2022].
- Rest (sleep) test: ptosis or ophthalmoparesis resolves immediately after a 30-min sleep and reappears over the next 30 s to 5 min. The authors describe it as safe, "moderately sensitive, and specific" (no numbers in abstract) [Odel1991].
- Cogan's lid twitch: sensitivity 50-99% and specificity 75-100% across 7 studies [Pietris2023]. In a 35-patient prospective ptosis series, sensitivity and specificity were "relatively low" [VanStavern2007]. The forced eyelid closure test (excess upward lid overshoot after 5-10 s squeeze) had sensitivity 94% and specificity 91% in 48 patients [Apinyawasisuk2017].
- Enhanced ptosis (curtain sign; the opposite lid drops when the more ptotic lid is lifted) is described clinically. I found no diagnostic-accuracy study for it, so no sensitivity or specificity can be cited (unverified). It appears in a 2025 case report only as an examination finding.
- Edrophonium (Tensilon) test: onset within 30 s and duration about 5 min. Given IV in fractions up to 10 mg (e.g., 2 mg then 8 mg within 45 s), with atropine 0.6 mg at hand. Serious adverse events occur in <0.2% but include bradyarrhythmia to asystole, bronchospasm, seizures and TIA. The test may worsen MuSK-MG [Rousseff2021].
- Why edrophonium is now rarely used: since 2018 the FDA has discontinued edrophonium as a test for MG and it is no longer marketed in the US for this purpose. Yoganathan cites a high false-positive rate [Yoganathan2022]. British guidance notes that mimics can give positive results and cites cardiac risks, and Rousseff says the test has not been practised in the UK in recent years [Rousseff2021].
- Edrophonium numbers: one cohort reported sensitivity 92% (ocular) and 88% (generalized), with specificity 97% [Yoganathan2022]. A 2026 Vienna case-control study (182 MG, 324 controls, 1991-2024) found sensitivity 83.5% and specificity 87.7%. MuSK-MG had lower odds of a positive test (OR 0.08), and adverse events occurred in 11.5% (91.4% mild) [Keritam2026].
- Neostigmine test (IM, onset 5-15 min, lasts hours) is the main alternative. In one 47-patient study, sensitivity was 83% and specificity 97% [Yoganathan2022]. Italian recommendations place it as a third-line test in seronegative patients with normal electrodiagnosis [Rousseff2021].
- Benatar's systematic review (20 studies) pooled ice, rest, Tensilon, AChR antibody, RNS and SFEMG accuracy separately for ocular and generalized MG. It concluded that antibody and SFEMG studies were of better quality and that all estimates should be interpreted with caution [Benatar2006].

### Thymus imaging
- Thymoma is present in up to 15% of MG patients, mostly AChR-antibody positive. No clinical or serological feature reliably excludes it, so thymus imaging is advised in all confirmed or strongly suspected MG. CT is preferred for screening [Rousseff2021].
- All MG patients, whatever the subtype, should undergo chest imaging (CT or MRI) to screen for thymoma [Habib2025].
- In ICI-related MG, CT was negative for thymoma [Safa2019].

### Diagnostic pathway, step order as published
- Gilhus: diagnosis is straightforward in most patients with typical symptoms and a positive antibody test. Detailed clinical and neurophysiological examination matters in antibody-negative patients. Patients are grouped by antibody, symptoms, age at onset and thymus pathology [Gilhus2019].
- Habib and Punga's order: 1) clinical examination for fatigable weakness (ocular, bulbar, limb-girdle); 2) antibody testing (RIA most sensitive for AChR and MuSK, then clustered-receptor CBA, with ELISA less specific); 3) RNS, with SFEMG if RNS is inconclusive; 4) chest CT or MRI in all. The ice-pack test is commonly used for ptosis, and an AChE-inhibitor response may support diagnosis [Habib2025].
- Rousseff's suggested order: Step 1 is AChR and MuSK antibodies in anyone with the clinical picture; a positive result is diagnostic per some authors. Step 2 (if seropositive) is thymus imaging and thyroid function tests. Step 3 (if seronegative) is RNS of clinically affected muscles, as many as tolerated, then jitter if RNS is negative; a confirmed postsynaptic disorder makes the diagnosis definite. Step 4 is a pharmacological test as a second-line alternative where available, especially in ocular MG, with the ice-pack "recommended by some" at this step. Step 5: if everything is negative but there is clearly fluctuating asymmetric ptosis and diplopia, ocular MG is "probable", with observation and retesting; UK guidance suggests brain imaging to exclude structural mimics [Rousseff2021].
- Patients negative on all objective tests but still symptomatic are unlikely to have MG. Such cases, undiagnosed after 2 years at a specialized MG clinic, may be over 10% of referrals [Rousseff2021].
- Yoganathan's adult testing scheme: AChR antibody; then MuSK if AChR is negative or <0.02 nmol/L; then LRP4 and striational antibodies in double seronegative patients; neurophysiology (RNS, then SFEMG) and CMS genetics in selected cases [Yoganathan2022].
- Misdiagnosis cost: among 61 patients reviewed after SFEMG was introduced locally, 8 had been wrongly diagnosed and treated as refractory MG. The mean duration of misdiagnosis was 9 years (SD 5.2), and the 8 included two thymectomies. Mimics should be suspected when disease is static and treatment-refractory, and functional mimics when flares are frequent [Harrison2023].
- Of 80 patients diagnosed with double-seronegative MG at two Italian centres (2012-2024), MG was confirmed in 64% (51). The other 36% (29) received alternative diagnoses, and RNS and SFEMG were "highly accurate" in identifying MG [Maccora2025].

### MGFA clinical classification (Jaretzki 2000; MGFA wording)
- Class I: "Any ocular muscle weakness; may have weakness of eye closure. All other muscle strength is normal." [Jaretzki2000; MGFAClass2016]
- Class II: "Mild weakness affecting muscles other than ocular muscles; may also have ocular muscle weakness of any severity." IIa: "Predominantly affecting limb, axial muscles, or both. May also have lesser involvement of oropharyngeal muscles." IIb: "Predominantly affecting oropharyngeal, respiratory muscles, or both. May also have lesser or equal involvement of limb, axial muscles, or both." [Jaretzki2000; MGFAClass2016]
- Class III: "Moderate weakness affecting muscles other than ocular muscles; may also have ocular muscle weakness of any severity." IIIa and IIIb use the same "a" (limb/axial predominant) and "b" (oropharyngeal/respiratory predominant) wording as IIa/IIb [Jaretzki2000; MGFAClass2016].
- Class IV: "Severe weakness affecting muscles other than ocular muscles; may also have ocular muscle weakness of any severity." IVa and IVb use the same a/b wording [Jaretzki2000; MGFAClass2016].
- Class V: "Defined as intubation, with or without mechanical ventilation, except when employed during routine postoperative management. The use of a feeding tube without intubation places the patient in class IVb." [Jaretzki2000; MGFAClass2016]
- Definitional difference for the app: the 2016 consensus defines manifest myasthenic crisis as MGFA Class V meaning "worsening of myasthenic weakness requiring intubation or noninvasive ventilation to avoid intubation". This adds non-invasive ventilation, which the original Class V wording does not include [Sanders2016; MGFAClass2016].
- The 2016 consensus defines ocular MG as MGFA Class I at a specified point in time, not dependent on disease duration. It adds that some patients report fatigue with normal strength testing and that clinicians should use judgment before attributing fatigue to generalized MG [Sanders2016].
- The MGFA clinical classification describes disease at that examination and should be updated every 3-6 months to reflect current status [Ruzhansky2025].

### MGFA post-intervention status (MGFA-PIS)
- Complete stable remission (CSR): no symptoms or signs of MG for at least 1 year and no MG therapy during that time. There is no weakness of any muscle on careful examination by someone skilled in neuromuscular evaluation, though isolated weakness of eyelid closure is accepted [Jaretzki2000; MGFAPIS2016].
- Pharmacologic remission (PR): the same criteria as CSR except the patient continues some form of MG therapy. Patients taking cholinesterase inhibitors are excluded, because their use suggests weakness [Jaretzki2000; MGFAPIS2016].
- Minimal manifestations (MM): no symptoms of functional limitation from MG but some weakness on examination of some muscles [Jaretzki2000; MGFAPIS2016]. Subgroups by treatment:
  - MM-0: no MG treatment for at least 1 year.
  - MM-1: continuing immunosuppression but no cholinesterase inhibitors or other symptomatic therapy.
  - MM-2: only low-dose cholinesterase inhibitors (<120 mg pyridostigmine/day) for at least 1 year.
  - MM-3: cholinesterase inhibitors or other symptomatic therapy plus some immunosuppression during the past year [MGFAPIS2016].
- Change in status [MGFAPIS2016]:
  - Improved (I): a substantial decrease in pretreatment manifestations, or a sustained substantial reduction in MG medications; in prospective studies, a specific decrease in QMG score.
  - Unchanged (U): no substantial change.
  - Worse (W): a substantial increase in manifestations or in MG medications.
  - Exacerbation (E): a patient who met CSR, PR or MM criteria then developed findings beyond those criteria.
  - Died of MG (D of MG): death from MG, from complications of therapy, or within 30 days after thymectomy.
- The 2016 consensus defines the treatment goal as MGFA-PIS minimal manifestation status or better, with no more than CTCAE grade 1 medication side effects. Its remission definition excludes patients taking ChEIs every day with reasonable evidence of symptomatic benefit. Refractory MG is PIS unchanged or worse after corticosteroids and at least 2 other immunosuppressive agents at adequate dose and duration [Sanders2016].
- The 2025 MGFA task force: MGFA-PIS is the evaluator's overall clinical judgement and does not require a defined change on any outcome measure [Ruzhansky2025].

### MG-ADL (Wolfe 1999): 8 items, anchors 0/1/2/3
- Talking: 0 Normal; 1 Intermittent slurring or nasal speech; 2 Constant slurring or nasal, but can be understood; 3 Difficult to understand speech [Wolfe1999; MGADLForm].
- Chewing: 0 Normal; 1 Fatigue with solid food; 2 Fatigue with soft food; 3 Gastric tube [Wolfe1999; MGADLForm].
- Swallowing: 0 Normal; 1 Rare episode of choking; 2 Frequent choking necessitating changes in diet; 3 Gastric tube [Wolfe1999; MGADLForm].
- Breathing: 0 Normal; 1 Shortness of breath with exertion; 2 Shortness of breath at rest; 3 Ventilator dependence [Wolfe1999; MGADLForm].
- Impairment of ability to brush teeth or comb hair: 0 None; 1 Extra effort, but no rest periods needed; 2 Rest periods needed; 3 Cannot do one of these functions [Wolfe1999; MGADLForm].
- Impairment of ability to arise from a chair: 0 None; 1 Mild, sometimes uses arms; 2 Moderate, always uses arms; 3 Severe, requires assistance [Wolfe1999; MGADLForm].
- Double vision: 0 None; 1 Occurs, but not daily; 2 Daily, but not constant; 3 Constant [Wolfe1999; MGADLForm].
- Eyelid droop: 0 None; 1 Occurs, but not daily; 2 Daily, but not constant; 3 Constant [Wolfe1999; MGADLForm].
- Total = sum of items 1-8, range 0-24. The items cover 2 ocular, 3 oropharyngeal, 1 respiratory and 2 extremity symptoms. Current MGNet instructions: self-completed by the patient, rating average function over the last 7 days, taking current MG medicines into account and counting only MG-related symptoms. If torn between two scores, record the higher [MGNetADL2022].
- Original validation: 254 consecutive encounters, mean MG-ADL 4.89 ± 3.63 versus mean QMG 10.80 ± 5.70, with Pearson r=0.583 (p<0.001) [Wolfe1999].
- Clinically meaningful change: a 2-point improvement in MG-ADL indicates clinical improvement. In 87 patients, the correlation with MG Composite was r=0.85 [Muppidi2011].
- Other MG-ADL thresholds collected by MGNet: "minimal symptom expression" is an MG-ADL of 0 or 1, used as a trial endpoint; the patient-acceptable symptom state (PASS) threshold is 2; and the scale has a floor effect [MGNetSummaries2023].

### QMG, MG Composite, MG-QOL15r
- QMG has 13 clinician-administered items, each graded 0 (none) to 3 (severe), for a total range of 0-39. The items are diplopia on lateral gaze, ptosis on upward gaze, facial muscles, swallowing, speech, right and left arm outstretched, FVC, right and left hand grip, head lift, and right and left leg outstretched. It takes about 20-30 min and needs a spirometer, stopwatch and dynamometer [MGNetSummaries2023; MGNetQMGR2022].
- QMG meaningful change: MCID cut-offs were below a QMG change of 3.0 in an IVIg vs placebo trial [Katzberg2014]. The MGNet summary gives these thresholds: ≥2 points if baseline QMG is 0-16 and ≥3 points if >16 (Katzberg), ≥2.3 (Bedlack) and 2.6 (Barohn). The individual minimum detectable change of 4.3 may exceed the MCID [MGNetSummaries2023; Bedlack2005; Barohn1998].
- QMG responsiveness and longitudinal construct validity were confirmed in 53 patients [Bedlack2005].
- MG Composite has 10 items and weighted scoring, maximum 50 [Burns2008; MGNetMGC2022]:
  - Ptosis on upgaze: >45 s 0; 11-45 s 1; 1-10 s 2; immediate 3.
  - Diplopia on lateral gaze: >45 s 0; 11-45 s 1; 1-10 s 3; immediate 4.
  - Eye closure: normal 0; mild (forced open with effort) 0; moderate (forced open easily) 1; severe (cannot keep closed) 2.
  - Talking: 0/2/4/6.
  - Chewing: 0/2/4/6.
  - Swallowing: 0/2/5/6.
  - Breathing: 0/2/4/9.
  - Neck flexion or extension: 0/1/3/4.
  - Shoulder abduction: 0/2/4/5.
  - Hip flexion: 0/2/4/5.
  - Mild = MRC 4+, moderate = MRC 4 to 4-, severe = MRC 3 or less.
- MG Composite validation: 175 patients at 11 centres. A 3-point improvement was optimal for signifying clinical improvement, and test-retest reliability was 98% (lower 95% CI 97%) [Burns2010].
- MG-QOL15 was revised to the MG-QOL15r after Rasch analysis of >1,300 surveys: 3 items were reworded and a 3-response format performed best. The MG-QOL15r is the preferred HRQOL instrument [Burns2016]. With 15 items at 3 response levels (0-2), the total range is 0-30; this range is inferred from the item structure, not stated in the abstract.
- The MG-QOL15r MCID, anchored to MG-ADL in ADAPT (n=157) and MyRealWorld-MG (n=92), ranged from 1.6 to 3.0. The authors suggest 2- and 3-point thresholds [Ben2026].

### Differential: Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS)
- Antibody: P/Q-type VGCC antibodies were found in 100% (32/32) of LEMS with cancer and 91% of 33 LEMS without cancer. N-type antibodies were found in 49% [Lennon1995].
- Tumour share: about half of LEMS patients have small-cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) [Titulaer2011; Titulaer2011b]. Schoser gives up to 60% as paraneoplastic [Schoser2017].
- SCLC risk score (DELTA-P), 1 point each at or within 3 months of onset: age ≥50, smoking, weight loss ≥5%, bulbar involvement, erectile dysfunction, Karnofsky <70. A score of 0-1 means 0-2.6% SCLC; 4, 5 and 6 mean 93.5%, 96.6% and 100%. The AUC was 94.4-94.6% [Titulaer2011b].
- Clinical triad: proximal weakness, autonomic dysfunction and areflexia [Kesner2018]. Proximal leg weakness and dry mouth were early symptoms in both SCLC and non-tumour LEMS (97 patients). Cerebellar symptoms in 9% were almost exclusively in SCLC-LEMS [Titulaer2008].
- Electrodiagnosis, distinguishing feature vs MG: low baseline CMAP plus increment. With a 100% increment limit (the old "gold standard") after exercise or high-rate stimulation, 85% of 34 LEMS were confirmed. With a 60% limit, sensitivity was 97% and specificity versus MG 99% (an increment ≥60% occurred in only 4 of 538 MG tests by high-rate stimulation and none after exercise) [Oh2005].
- RNS was abnormal in 100% of LEMS versus 54% of MG. Hallmarks: LEMS shows low CMAP, low-rate decrement and high-rate/post-exercise increment; MG shows normal CMAP and low-rate decrement [Oh2017].
- Exercise duration: post-exercise increment was highest after 5-10 s of exercise (244%-243%) and lowest after 30 s (84%), so a 10-s exercise is recommended [Hatanaka2008].
- Hypermagnesaemia produces a LEMS-like presynaptic picture that tends to spare ocular muscles [Krenn2020].

### Differential: botulism
- Pattern: cranial nerve palsies followed by bilateral, symmetric, descending flaccid paralysis, proximal before distal, which may progress to respiratory failure. Patients are alert. It is a recognized mimic of MG and GBS [Rao2021].
- Chart frequencies in confirmed botulism: afebrile 99%, descending paralysis 93%, dysphagia 85%, ptosis 81%, diplopia 75%, dry mouth 63%, extraocular palsy 60%, dilated pupils 37%, abnormally reactive pupils 24%, sensory deficits 17%, altered mental status 8% [Rao2021].
- The CDC early-recognition tool requires all three: 1) afebrile; 2) acute onset of a bulbar or visual symptom; 3) a sign such as ptosis, extraocular palsy, facial paresis, fixed pupils or descending paralysis [Rao2021].
- Tests: the edrophonium test is usually negative and CSF is typically normal. RNS at 30-50 Hz classically shows an increment, with reduced CMAP amplitude and otherwise normal nerve conduction, but early studies may be normal. MFS electrodiagnostic findings can look similar [Rao2021].
- Time course anchors: median incubation 1 day and median 2 days from onset to admission. 42% had respiratory involvement at admission, and 87% of intubated patients were intubated on hospital day 1 or 2 [ChathamStephens2017].
- Asymmetry occurs in 6-15% of case series patients, so asymmetry does not exclude botulism [Rao2021].

### Differential: ALS and bulbar ALS
- Decrement on RNS can occur in denervating and reinnervating muscles, especially in ALS, but the distribution and pattern differ. None of 85 ALS patients had significant facial-muscle decrement, and ALS lacked the U-shaped partial recovery at the 5th-6th responses [Rousseff2021].
- Area decrement was abnormal more often than amplitude decrement in 421 ALS patients [Zhang2024].
- Jitter is abnormal in motor neuron disease, which is one reason SFEMG is not specific for MG [Rousseff2021; Sanders2022].
- Antibody overlap: false-positive AChR binding results in 2/22 ALS [Howard1987]; LRP4 antibodies in up to 23% of ALS [Rousseff2021]; P/Q VGCC antibodies at low titre in 23% of sporadic ALS [Lennon1995].
- Motor neuron disorders are listed among the main MG differentials [Habib2025].

### Differential: CPEO/mitochondrial myopathy and oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy (OPMD)
- Hereditary muscle diseases such as CPEO and OPMD usually cause ophthalmoparesis without diplopia as a leading feature, often affecting the superior rectus. Orbital imaging commonly shows fat replacement of extraocular muscles in CPEO. Fluctuation is the hallmark of acquired synaptic disease such as MG [Keene2022].
- Mitochondrial myopathy is one of the main differentials listed for MG [Habib2025]. CPEO, OPMD and a possible mitochondrial cytopathy were among the 8 chronic mimics misdiagnosed as refractory MG [Harrison2023].
- OPMD: autosomal dominant, usually presenting in the sixth decade with progressive dysphagia, ptosis and proximal limb weakness. It is caused by expansion of a (GCG)6 repeat in PABP2 (now PABPN1) to (GCG)8-13 [Brais1998; Reintjes2026].
- OPMD was the second most frequent alternative diagnosis, after functional disorders, among patients first labelled double-seronegative MG [Maccora2025].

### Differential: thyroid eye disease (TED) and coexistence with MG
- In acquired orbital disease such as Graves orbitopathy, ophthalmoparesis is often asymmetric with proptosis and pain, and imaging shows muscle inflammation and enlargement [Keene2022].
- Coexistence frequency: of 6,576 TED patients, 15 (0.23%) had concurrent MG and 12 (0.18%) isolated ocular MG. In these patients, ptosis (60%) and exotropia (40%, unusual for TED) were clues [Abumanhal2026].
- In the coexisting cases, MG with TED had lower AChR titres and less thymic hyperplasia than MG without hyperthyroidism, and diplopia was hard to treat [Chen2024].
- When ocular MG and TED coexist, a single positive test may not be enough, and a multimodal approach (clinical, serological, imaging, electrodiagnostic) is needed [Claytor2021]. Thyroid function tests are advised in all definite or probable MG [Rousseff2021].
- Basedow-Graves ophthalmopathy was one of the alternative diagnoses in patients first labelled seronegative MG [Maccora2025].

### Differential: brainstem lesions, Miller Fisher syndrome/GBS
- Nystagmus points to a central nervous system lesion rather than MG [Keene2022].
- A brainstem mass in a 38-year-old mimicked MG clinically and showed a decremental RNS response. Both resolved after surgery [Akkaya2005]. Brain imaging can help exclude brainstem stroke in suspected botulism or MG [Rao2021].
- Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS, 466 patients; ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia, clear consciousness): anti-GQ1b IgG was positive in 83%, CSF albuminocytological dissociation in 76%, and antecedent C. jejuni in 21%. Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis (impaired consciousness) had GQ1b in 68% and lies on the same spectrum [Ito2008].
- GBS typically raises CSF protein, especially by the second week, which separates it from botulism (and from MG, where CSF is not part of the work-up) [Rao2021].
- Autoimmune demyelinating polyneuropathy and cranial nerve disorders (Horner syndrome, third nerve palsy) are listed MG differentials [Habib2025].

### Differential: congenital myasthenic syndromes (CMS)
- 32 genes have been described (8 presynaptic, 4 synaptic, 15 postsynaptic, 5 glycosylation). The commonest are CHAT, COLQ, RAPSN, CHRNE, DOK7 and GFPT1. CHRNE may account for up to half of CMS [Finsterer2019].
- Suspect CMS when: fatigable or permanent weakness begins from birth to childhood; there is a family history; the picture suggests MG but AChR, MuSK and LRP4 antibodies are negative; RNS shows >10% decrement or SFEMG increased jitter; and immunosuppression gives no improvement [Finsterer2019].
- Onset is mostly congenital, infantile or childhood. Slow-channel CMS usually starts after adolescence, and DOK7 CMS can begin from birth into adulthood [Finsterer2019].
- Drug response is the key differentiator. Most CMS respond to AChE inhibitors, 3,4-DAP, salbutamol, albuterol, ephedrine or fluoxetine. But AChE inhibitors may worsen COLQ, LAMB2, DOK7, MUSK and LRP4 CMS, fluoxetine helps slow-channel CMS but worsened MYO9A and RAPSN CMS, and 3,4-DAP may be ineffective in CHRNE or MUSK CMS [Finsterer2019]. A drug that helps one CMS can harm another [Engel2015].
- A double (repetitive) CMAP after a single stimulus is a clue in COLQ, slow-channel and AChE-deficiency CMS [Finsterer2019].
- CMS prevalence has been estimated at about one-tenth that of MG. A British study of under-18s found 9.2 per million [Finsterer2019].

### Differential: drug-induced and checkpoint-inhibitor MG
- D-penicillamine induces true AChR-antibody MG in up to 7% of treated patients. It is mostly mild and ocular, starts 2-12 months after the drug is begun, and often remits within 1 year of stopping [Krenn2020].
- Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI): MG incidence is about 0.5%, mostly with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, at a median 28 days after start. AChR antibodies are found in 66%, overlap with myositis and myocarditis is common, and overall mortality is 20-30% [Krenn2020].
- ICI-MG case series (65 patients): median age 73 and median onset 4 weeks (range 1-16) after the first ICI dose. 63% had MGFA III-V; myositis occurred in 37%, myocarditis in 8% and respiratory failure in 45%. AChR antibodies were positive in 37/56 (66%), striational antibodies in 12/18, and CK was raised in 84% (41/49). Death occurred in 38%, 23% from MG [Safa2019].
- Drugs that aggravate or unmask MG include telithromycin (symptoms within 2 h of the first dose; withdrawn), aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, class Ia antiarrhythmics, beta blockers, parenteral magnesium and botulinum toxin A. Statins have been linked to oculo-bulbar worsening 1 week to 4 months after starting [Krenn2020].
- Allogeneic stem cell transplantation can trigger MG (incidence <1%) as chronic GVHD, 3-100 months after transplant [Krenn2020].

### Differential: functional (and other) mimics
- Functional neurological disorder was the most frequent alternative diagnosis (45%) among patients first labelled double-seronegative MG. These mimics had longer diagnostic delays and more psychiatric comorbidity. Benign essential blepharospasm and refraction defects were also found [Maccora2025].
- In the Harrison series, 4 of 8 chronic mimics were functional (one with superimposed thyroid myopathy and orbitopathy). All 8 had received anticholinesterase therapy, 3 immunotherapy and 2 thymectomy [Harrison2023].

### Matrix summary rows (condition: distinguishing feature)
- MG (AChR): fluctuating, fatigable ocular, bulbar and proximal weakness; normal CMAP; low-rate decrement with U shape; AChR binding antibody positive in about 85% (generalized) and 50% (ocular) [Rousseff2021; Oh2017].
- LEMS: proximal leg weakness, dry mouth and autonomic signs, areflexia; low CMAP with ≥60-100% increment after 10 s of exercise or 50 Hz; P/Q VGCC antibodies in 91-100%; SCLC in about half [Kesner2018; Oh2005; Lennon1995; Titulaer2011].
- Botulism: acute, descending, symmetric; afebrile, alert; pupils abnormal in a minority (dilated 37%); 30-50 Hz increment; edrophonium usually negative [Rao2021].
- ALS (bulbar): decrement without facial involvement or U shape; jitter abnormal; upper motor neuron signs; possible false-positive AChR, LRP4 or VGCC antibodies [Rousseff2021; Howard1987; Lennon1995].
- CPEO/mitochondrial: slowly progressive, symmetric ophthalmoparesis without much diplopia; no fluctuation; extraocular muscle fat replacement on imaging [Keene2022].
- OPMD: sixth-decade ptosis and dysphagia, autosomal dominant, PABPN1 GCG expansion [Brais1998].
- TED: proptosis, pain, restrictive asymmetric ophthalmoparesis, enlarged and inflamed extraocular muscles; coexists with MG in about 0.2% of TED [Keene2022; Abumanhal2026].
- Brainstem lesion: nystagmus or other central signs; abnormal MRI; decrement possible [Keene2022; Akkaya2005].
- MFS/GBS: acute ophthalmoplegia with ataxia and areflexia; anti-GQ1b in 83%; CSF albuminocytological dissociation [Ito2008].
- CMS: onset from birth or childhood; family history; antibody-negative; no response to immunotherapy; gene-specific drug response [Finsterer2019].
- Drug/ICI-induced: temporal link to a drug (ICI median 4 weeks); myositis and myocarditis overlap; raised CK [Safa2019; Krenn2020].
- Functional: frequent flares; normal objective tests (RNS/SFEMG normal); long diagnostic delay [Harrison2023; Maccora2025].
