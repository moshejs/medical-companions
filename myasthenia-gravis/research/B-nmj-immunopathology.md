# B. The neuromuscular junction & immunopathology

This pass covers normal neuromuscular transmission at the human endplate step by step with the numbers an animator can anchor to (quantal content, ACh molecules per quantum, cleft width, receptor density, endplate-potential amplitude, threshold, safety factor), the mechanism of rundown during a train and how a reduced safety factor converts it into transmission failure, the three antibody mechanisms in AChR MG, complement at the endplate, MuSK/LRP4/clustered-AChR serology and the agrin–LRP4–MuSK–Dok-7–rapsyn pathway, the thymus as the site of tolerance breakdown, the two founding animal experiments, FcRn and IgG recycling as mechanism, and susceptibility genetics. Everything here is mechanism and measurement; nothing is phrased as advice. Access notes: the Gilhus 2019 Nature Reviews Disease Primers full text is paywalled (nature.com redirects to an identity provider), so [Gilhus2019] is cited only for statements that are in its abstract or that are corroborated by a primary source cited alongside it. Several classic papers (Fambrough 1973 Science; Engel 1977 Mayo Clin Proc; Elmqvist 1964 J Physiol; Wood & Slater 2001 Prog Neurobiol; Compston 1980 Brain) are abstract-only or abstract-less in PubMed/Europe PMC, so facts drawn from them are limited to what the abstract or a quoting open-access source states; the one place a widely repeated number could not be verified is flagged "(unverified)". Figures from Slater 2017 and Ruff & Lennon 2008 are read from the open-access full texts.

## Facts

### Step 1 — nerve action potential and calcium entry
- The motor nerve terminal is fine (about 0.1 µm) and forms varicosities ("boutons") usually 1–5 µm across, from which transmitter is released [Slater2017].
- The terminal contains synaptic vesicles about 50 nm in diameter, clustered around electron-dense active zones in the presynaptic membrane that are the sites of evoked release [Slater2017].
- Active-zone protein density gives an animator a scale for the release machinery: Bassoon/Piccolo puncta at about 10 per µm² in mouse, and SNAP25 at about 15 per µm² by dSTORM, with roughly 15 µm² of SNAP25 per NMJ in both human and mouse [Jones2017].
- Depolarisation of the terminal by the nerve action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels, raising intracellular free calcium and triggering near-simultaneous release of a number of quanta [Slater2017].
- At the normal human NMJ the channel carrying that calcium is P/Q-type (Ca_V2.1): funnel-web toxin and ω-agatoxin-IVA (P-type blockers) blocked nerve-elicited muscle action potentials and evoked transmission, whereas nitrendipine (L-type) and ω-conotoxin-GVIA (N-type) did not [Protti1996].
- Vertebrate active zones are normally aligned with the openings of the underlying postsynaptic folds, so quanta are released opposite the receptor-rich crests [Slater2017].

### Step 2 — vesicle fusion, quantal content, ACh per quantum
- Each vesicle holds a packet ("quantum") of about 10,000 ACh molecules in vertebrates [Slater2017].
- The independent iontophoretic estimate puts an upper bound on the same quantity: a quantum consists of fewer than 10,000 ACh molecules; 6,000 molecules suffice to depolarise the subsynaptic membrane by 1 mV, and each released ACh molecule yields a minimum net flow of about 3,000 univalent ions [Kuffler1975].
- Quantal content (quanta released per nerve impulse) at human NMJs is only about 20–30, against typically 50–100 at mouse NMJs — human junctions release the smallest number of quanta known among vertebrates studied [Slater2017].
- Across vertebrates, quantal content scales with contact area at roughly 0.15 quanta per µm² of synaptic area during about 1 Hz stimulation, and scales inversely with the extent of postsynaptic folding [Slater2017].
- Spontaneous single-vesicle fusion occurs at a rate of about 1 Hz, producing the miniature endplate potential (mEPP) [Slater2017].

### Step 3 — the cleft, the receptor, and two ACh per receptor
- The synaptic cleft between bouton and muscle surface is about 50–100 nm wide and contains extracellular matrix [Slater2017].
- Muscle AChRs are heteropentamers of α, β and δ subunits plus either the fetal γ subunit or the adult ε subunit; subunit stoichiometry is α2βγδ (fetal) or α2βδε (adult) [Sine2012].
- The γ→ε switch is what distinguishes fetal from adult receptor: channels assembled from α, β, γ, δ versus α, β, δ, ε mRNAs differ in conductance and gating, and the developmental change in muscle mRNA content matches the functional change [Mishina1986].
- The receptor has two agonist binding sites, formed at the α–δ interface and at either the α–ε (adult) or α–γ (fetal) interface, so two ACh molecules are involved in activation; dose–response Hill plots have a limiting slope of two, which could not be explained by a single binding step [Sine2012].
- The two sites are not equivalent: ACh dissociation rate constants from the two sites differ by about 100-fold, and the channel opening rate constant for adult (frog endplate) receptors was about 31,000 s⁻¹ with maximum open probability 0.97 [Sine2012].
- The main immunogenic region (MIR) sits at the top (extracellular apex) of the α1 subunit; the structure homologous to the MIR begins at the end of strand β2 at the top of the subunit [Sine2012].
- Once opened, AChR channels stay open typically for about 1 ms, admitting enough cations to depolarise the fibre by about 1 mV per quantum from a resting potential of about −75 mV [Slater2017].

### Step 4 — receptor density on fold crests, Na_V1.4 in the troughs
- AChRs form a near-crystalline array at a density of about 10,000 AChRs per µm² of crest membrane, appearing in the electron microscope as regions of high electron density [Slater2017].
- Developmental counts give the same order: AChRs are diffusely distributed on embryonic myotubes but become concentrated at approximately 10,000 per µm² in the postsynaptic membrane [Gautam1995].
- A second, higher figure is sometimes quoted from electron-microscope autoradiography: α-bungarotoxin binding sites at mouse endplates were measured at 46,000 ± 27% sites per µm², but that value is expressed relative to the presynaptic axonal membrane as a reference structure rather than per µm² of folded postsynaptic membrane [Fertuck1976].
- AChRs are concentrated at the crests of the postsynaptic folds, directly facing the nerve; the membrane in the depths (troughs) of the folds carries a high density of the voltage-gated sodium channel Na_V1.4, which initiates the muscle action potential [Slater2017].
- Immunogold electron microscopy shows this as two interspersed domains: AChR and the 43 kDa protein (rapsyn) at the crests, coextensive with the subsynaptic density; Na⁺ channels and ankyrin in the membranes of the troughs and in perijunctional membrane [Flucher1989].
- Human NMJs have the most deeply infolded postsynaptic membranes known, with folds extending up to about 1 µm into the cytoplasm and folding that increases local membrane area roughly 8-fold; in extreme cases folds are separated by cytoplasmic sheets under 0.2 µm thick [Slater2017].
- Human NMJs are smaller, less complex and more fragmented than mouse NMJs, with "nummular" coin-shaped endplate patches, and are remarkably stable across the adult lifespan (ages 34–92) with no age-related fragmentation [Jones2017].
- Rapsyn (the 43 kDa protein) is required for the clustering itself: rapsyn-deficient mice form no AChR clusters, although synapse-specific transcription of AChR subunit genes still proceeds [Gautam1995].

### Step 5 — acetylcholinesterase, ColQ, and ACh lifetime in the cleft
- ACh action ends when ACh unbinds from the receptor and is then rapidly hydrolysed by acetylcholinesterase bound to the basal lamina in the cleft; the choline is taken back into the terminal by a specific uptake protein and resynthesised into transmitter [Slater2017].
- ACh is removed from the cleft in less than a millisecond, by enzymatic hydrolysis and diffusion, while the postsynaptic depolarisation decays over several additional milliseconds [Sine2012].
- The collagenous tail ColQ is what anchors asymmetric acetylcholinesterase to the synaptic basal lamina: ColQ-knockout mice completely lack asymmetric AChE in skeletal and cardiac muscle and brain, with no detectable AChE at the NMJ by histochemical, immunohistochemical, toxicological or electrophysiological assay [Feng1999].
- ColQ mutations in humans cause prolonged ACh action in the cleft, and the resulting Ca²⁺ entry produces local cytotoxicity including structural disruption of the postsynaptic membrane — the mechanistic mirror of what excess cholinesterase inhibition can do [Slater2017].
- With cholinesterase fully active each quantum is functionally isolated from its neighbours; when the enzyme is inhibited, two or more quanta act on partially overlapping membrane areas and potentiate each other, prolonging the synaptic current [Hartzell1976].

### Step 6 — mEPP, EPP, threshold and the safety factor (animation anchors)
- A single quantum gives a miniature endplate potential of about 1 mV; a nerve impulse releasing about 50 quanta (mouse) gives an endplate potential of about 35 mV [Slater2017].
- In mammals the quantal content released per nerve impulse at low frequency (0.1–1 Hz) is normally 2–4 times greater than needed to trigger a muscle action potential — this excess is the safety factor [Slater2017].
- A review of safety factor across species puts it at generally 3–5 in normal adult mammals, and notes two distinct strategies: frogs rely on releasing a large amount of transmitter, humans on elaborate postsynaptic specialisations that amplify the response to transmitter [Wood2001].
- A clinical electrophysiology review states the quantal release of ACh is four to five times greater than required to generate a suprathreshold EPP [Kouyoumdjian2023].
- The safety factor can be written SF = EPP / E_AP, where E_AP is the voltage difference between resting potential and action-potential threshold; the high endplate concentration of Na⁺ channels raises SF by lowering that threshold [Ruff2008].
- Measured in human intercostal muscle (type IIb fibres): the depolarisation needed to trigger an action potential at the endplate was 13.5 mV in controls versus 21.6 mV in MG; the threshold reduction at the endplate border relative to extrajunctional membrane was 11.8 mV in controls [Ruff2008].
- In the same study, MG MEPPs were 53% and EPPs 58% of control values, and the safety factor fell from 2.98 in controls to 1.09 in MG — 37% of control, with Na⁺-current loss accounting for 40% of the reduction [Ruff2008].
- Endplate Na⁺ current was reduced to about 60% of control in MG patients and passive-transfer rats, whereas MEPPs were reduced to less than 30% of control — AChR loss exceeds Na⁺ channel loss [Ruff1998].
- The Na⁺ channel loss is a consequence of complement-mediated loss of endplate membrane, not a direct antibody effect: the monoclonal myasthenogenic IgG that produces passive-transfer MG did not alter Na⁺ channel open time or opening probability [Ruff2008].
- Human endplates in MG show the EPP directly, because many EPPs fail to reach threshold: subthreshold EPPs are visible between the near-constant-amplitude muscle action potentials evoked by those EPPs that do reach threshold [Slater2017].

### Rundown during a train, and why it becomes failure in MG
- During higher-frequency activity (10–100 Hz), as occurs in vivo, quantal content and EPP amplitude decline, but normally remain adequate to activate the muscle cell [Slater2017].
- The mechanism is depletion of the immediately available vesicle store: synaptic vesicles are progressively depleted from the primary (readily releasable) compartment during low-frequency repetitive stimulation, and because of the safety factor the EPP nonetheless stays above threshold in healthy muscle [Kouyoumdjian2023].
- After 1–2 seconds, ACh mobilises from the secondary compartment to replenish the primary one, which is why the decrement curve recovers and takes a characteristic "U" shape across a train [Kouyoumdjian2023].
- This is the basis of the repetitive-nerve-stimulation decrement: with a 5–10 stimulus train at 2–5 Hz, an abnormal decrement is a reproducible 10% fall comparing the first CMAP amplitude to the fourth or fifth, with most of the decrement occurring between the first and second responses [Kouyoumdjian2023].
- Sensitivity of low-frequency RNS is about 90% in generalised MG and 0–25% in ocular MG, and in an abnormal study the tenth response never recovers to the amplitude of the first [Kouyoumdjian2023].
- The decrement plateau is reached earlier in proximal than distal muscles: decrement completion ratios at the second and third response were significantly higher in deltoid than abductor pollicis brevis, consistent with a lower safety factor in proximal muscles; early completion (e.g. at the second response) is not a technical artefact [Ueta2024].
- Myasthenia is in the end the failure of the EPP to reach the action-potential threshold, which is what "compromised safety margin" means mechanically [Sine2012].
- Presynaptic release is not passive in MG: in muscle from MG patients and from rats with experimental autoimmune MG, mean quantal content was considerably increased over control, and at each endplate the increase in quantal content correlated with the reduction of mEPP amplitude — a retrograde (muscle-to-nerve) compensatory upregulation [Plomp1995].
- That homeostatic upregulation is presumably maximal in MG, and the signalling factors behind it are still incompletely identified [Plomp2017].
- The compensation is absent in MuSK MG: in mice given patient MuSK IgG4, the normal compensatory transmitter-release upregulation seen in AChR MG did not occur, and presynaptic depression during high-rate activity was exaggerated — extra vulnerability on top of reduced postsynaptic ACh sensitivity [Klooster2012].

### AChR antibodies: subclass, epitope, three mechanisms
- AChR antibodies are predominantly IgG1 and IgG3: IgG1, IgG2, IgG3 and IgG4 anti-AChR were present in 100%, 33%, 64% and 39% of pyridostigmine-treated patients respectively, and IgG1 concentration rose significantly with Osserman severity grade (r_s = 0.37, P < 0.05) [Rodgaard1987].
- The main immunogenic region on the α1 subunit is the target of half or more of the autoantibodies in human MG and rat EAMG; it is conformation-dependent and sits at the extracellular apex of the α1 subunit [Luo2009].
- The MIR was first localised with monoclonal antibodies as a small region on the α subunit dominating the immunogenicity of the native receptor, and a monoclonal against it transferred passive EAMG in rats [Tzartos1980].
- Structurally the MIR epitope is discontinuous: loop α1 67–76 combined with the N-terminal α-helix α1 1–14 confers high-affinity binding for most rat monoclonals, while a patient-derived human monoclonal additionally required α1 15–32 [Luo2009].
- The MIR is myasthenogenic in its own right: human α1 MIR sequences grafted into Aplysia ACh-binding protein potently induced EAMG, while the unmodified binding protein did so much less potently [Lindstrom2012].
- Mechanism 1 — complement: IgG and C3 are localised on segments of the postsynaptic membrane and on fragments of degenerating junctional folds shed into the synaptic space, with a linear correlation between the length of immune-complex-bearing postsynaptic membrane and mEPP amplitude [Engel1977].
- Mechanism 1 (continued) — the terminal lytic component C9 is found on short segments of postsynaptic membrane, on degenerated fold fragments shed into the cleft, and on disintegrating folds, with an inverse relationship between fold structural integrity and local C9 abundance; destruction of folds by the membrane attack complex is a cause of the AChR deficiency [Sahashi1980].
- Membrane attack complex at the endplate is essentially universal in MG biopsies: all endplates of all 30 patients immunostained for MAC, while inflammatory cells were present at fewer than 10% of endplates and never penetrated the fibre — confirming the effector response is humoral, not cell-mediated [Nakano1993].
- Fold simplification and endplate remodelling are quantifiable: in chronic EAMG there were significant decreases in postsynaptic membrane length, postsynaptic membrane density and the postsynaptic-to-presynaptic membrane length ratio, with degeneration concentrated at the tips of the folds where AChR is concentrated [Engel1976].
- The time course of complement injury in passive transfer is fast: IgG and C3 localise on terminal expansions of junctional folds by 6 hours, AChR-rich fold segments coated with IgG and C3 are shed into the cleft by 24 hours, many sensitised postsynaptic regions are destroyed by macrophages by day 2, effective nerve–muscle contacts are re-established by day 5, animals are clinically recovered by day 10 while endplates remain abnormal, and postsynaptic regions are still reduced in size at day 54 [Engel1979].
- Mechanism 2 — antigenic modulation by cross-linking: myasthenic IgG or its divalent F(ab')₂ accelerated AChR degradation about threefold, monovalent Fab had no effect despite binding, and adding a second "piggyback" cross-linking antibody to Fab:receptor complexes restored the threefold increase [Drachman1978].
- The rate change quantified: degradation rose to a mean of 8.51 ± 0.44% per hour with myasthenic immunoglobulin versus a control rate of 3.97 ± 0.14% per hour (P < 0.001) [Kao1977].
- Freeze-fracture electron microscopy shows the intermediate step: myasthenic IgG induces clustering of AChR particles within "hot spots" and an increase in cluster size, with large (>100 nm) endocytotic pits occurring preferentially in hot spots, roughly half closely associated with AChR clusters; monovalent Fab had no effect unless cross-linked [Pumplin1983].
- A dissenting result on the cross-linking requirement: passive transfer of F(ab')₂ hybrid molecules carrying only one AChR-specific Fab reduced endplate channel number much like intact myasthenic IgG, and myasthenic Fab also reduced channel number, suggesting divalent cross-linking is not an absolute requirement; the Hill coefficient and apparent dissociation constant of the remaining receptors were unchanged [Sterz1986].
- Mechanism 3 — direct blockade, a minority mechanism: myasthenic serum globulin inhibited ¹²⁵I-α-bungarotoxin binding to the receptor in at least 5 and possibly 11 of 15 patients [Almon1974], and in a 349-patient series blocking antibodies were positive in 52% versus 86% for binding and 86% for modulating antibodies, with blocking never the only positive test [Howard1987].
- Receptor loss measured: junctional AChRs were reduced in myasthenic muscle compared with controls by ¹²⁵I-α-bungarotoxin binding in the original eight-patient/five-control series [Fambrough1973]; the widely quoted percentage reduction in that paper could not be read from the abstract (unverified).
- A larger series quantified it: mean AChRs per NMJ in deltoid was 0.7 ± 0.1 × 10⁷ in 76 MG patients versus 2.1 ± 0.2 × 10⁷ in 49 controls (P < 0.001); no control muscle had fewer than 0.9 × 10⁷ and 75% of MG muscles were below that; receptor loss correlated with clinical severity (P < 0.02), and patients with focal weakness had reduced receptors even in clinically strong muscles [Pestronk1985].
- Titre and severity correlate poorly across patients: antibody presence or titre did not correlate with age, sex, steroid therapy or symptom duration; ocular-only patients had lower titres and thymoma patients mostly above the group median [Lindstrom1976].
- The paradox stated plainly: some patients with severe weakness had low titres, and some in remission or with ocular-only disease had high titres, attributed to heterogeneity in antibody specificity, affinity and isotype [Howard1987].
- Within a patient over time the correlation is also weak: AChR antibody levels fell in 92% of patients who improved but also in 63% who did not; a fall had positive predictive value 83% but negative predictive value only 59% for improvement [Sanders2014].
- Assay sensitivity sets the baseline for "seronegative": standard radioimmunoassay was negative in 26/153 (17%) of validated MG sera, reduced to 12% with a mixed normal/denervated AChR protocol, making the test positive in about 88% of patients with clinical MG [Vincent1985].

### Complement: pathway, deposits, and why C5
- In AChR-antibody-positive MG the classical pathway is initiated when IgG1 or IgG3 (less so IgG2) bound to AChR binds C1q; C1q binding to the Fc domain autoactivates C1r, which activates C1s, which cleaves C4; C14b2a3b is the C5 convertase; C5b combines with C6, C7, C8 and C9 to form C5b-9, the terminal complement complex, and its formation produces focal lysis of the junction with loss of AChR and postsynaptic folds [Albazli2020].
- C5 is the chosen target because it is the last common step before the lytic complex: eculizumab inhibits cleavage of C5 and so prevents MAC formation while leaving upstream C3 opsonisation intact [Howard2018].
- Animal evidence that the terminal complex specifically is required: C6-deficient rats develop neither weakness nor terminal complement complex on the postsynaptic membrane after AChR antibody administration, and exogenous C6 restores both; C3 and C4 knockout mice show reduced weakness and preserved AChR density [Albazli2020].
- Intrinsic complement regulators matter at the endplate: CD55 (decay-accelerating factor) dissociates C3 and C5 convertases and CD59 blocks terminal complex formation, both localise to the NMJ, and mice deficient in DAF and CD59 go into crisis in experimental MG [Albazli2020].
- Regulator expression is lower in extraocular muscle, a candidate explanation for its susceptibility: by qPCR, extraocular muscle showed lower mRNA for DAF, CD59 and Crry, and immunoreactivity for all three was intense at diaphragm junctions but less intense or absent at extraocular junctions [Kaminski2004].
- Indirect in vivo evidence of complement consumption: MG patients with AChR antibody concentrations above the median (11.2 nmol/L) had significantly lower mean serum C3 and C4 than those below it, and this was unrelated to MG severity (grades 4–5 had C3 and C4 similar to grades 1–2) [Romi2005b].
- Complement deposition is not confined to AChR MG: in seven LRP4-antibody-positive patients, C5b-9 deposition was found at NMJs in all biopsies, affecting 6–96% of identified endplates, always co-localised with IgG1, with C1QC and C3 transcripts higher than controls and reduced, shortened postsynaptic clefts in 3 of 4 evaluable patients [Hoffmann2026].

### MuSK antibodies: IgG4, Fab-arm exchange, monovalency
- MuSK antibodies were found in 70% of AChR-antibody-negative MG patients and in no AChR-positive patients; they were specific for the extracellular domains of MuSK and strongly inhibited MuSK function in cultured myotubes [Hoch2001].
- MuSK antibodies are mainly IgG4, a subclass unable to activate complement with low affinity for Fcγ receptors, and therefore usually considered anti-inflammatory [Huijbers2018].
- IgG4 exchanges half-molecules with unrelated IgG4 in blood ("Fab-arm exchange"), producing antibodies with two different binding sites — functionally monovalent for each antigen and unable to cross-link it [Vergoossen2021].
- At least 50% of patients had IgG4 (but not IgG1-3) MuSK antibodies that could undergo Fab-arm exchange in vitro, and endogenous hybrid κ/λ IgG4 MuSK antibodies were isolated, confirming exchange occurs in vivo [Koneczny2017].
- Monovalency is what makes them pathogenic: monovalent anti-MuSK IgG4 caused rapid, severe myasthenic weakness in mice while the same antibodies in parental bivalent form were less potent or caused no phenotype; about 99% of circulating IgG4 MuSK antibodies are rendered bispecific and functionally monovalent by Fab-arm exchange [Vergoossen2021].
- Mechanistically monovalent and bivalent forms act in opposite directions: monovalent MuSK IgG4 abolished agrin-induced MuSK phosphorylation (maximum inhibition at 7.7 nM) and AChR clustering, whereas bivalent anti-MuSK IgG4 partially activated MuSK phosphorylation and clustering independent of agrin [Vergoossen2021].
- The blocked step is the MuSK–LRP4 interaction: pathogenic IgG4 binds a structural epitope in the first Ig-like domain of MuSK, prevents MuSK–LRP4 binding and inhibits agrin-stimulated MuSK phosphorylation, with no direct effect on MuSK dimerisation or internalisation [Huijbers2013].
- Patient IgG4 alone is myasthenogenic: purified IgG4 (but not IgG1-3) from MuSK MG patients bound mouse NMJs and, injected into immunodeficient mice, caused paralysis, reduced AChR density and fragmented AChR area, with severely reduced postsynaptic ACh sensitivity [Klooster2012].
- IgG1-3 MuSK antibodies are not inert: they did not inhibit LRP4–MuSK binding yet still inhibited agrin-induced clustering, and both IgG4 and IgG1-3 dispersed preformed agrin-independent AChR clusters in Dok-7-overexpressing myotubes [Koneczny2013].
- The clustering pathway being disrupted: neural agrin binds LRP4, which forms a complex with and activates MuSK [Kim2008; Zhang2008]; MuSK signals through the cytoplasmic adaptor Dok-7, without which mice form neither AChR clusters nor neuromuscular synapses [Okada2006]; and rapsyn tethers the receptors into high-density clusters [Gautam1995].
- Clinically, cholinesterase inhibition helps less in MuSK MG: edrophonium or neostigmine was equivocal or negative in 11 of 37 MuSK-positive patients (29.7%), and the response to oral pyridostigmine ranged from mild benefit to overt intolerance [Evoli2003].
- Hyperexcitability is documented: a MuSK-antibody-positive patient on cholinesterase inhibitors showed extra repetitive discharges after the compound motor action potential at low-frequency stimulation, indicating abnormal sensitivity to ACh [Punga2006].
- There is a mechanism for pyridostigmine making it worse: in MuSK passive-transfer mice, pyridostigmine at therapeutically relevant levels (days 7–14) exacerbated AChR loss and endplate potential reduction and precipitated generalised weakness in mice given sub-symptomatic antibody doses, with no such effect in control-IgG mice, while 3,4-diaminopyridine instead enhanced transmission — attributed to prolonged ACh action in the cleft potentiating anti-MuSK-induced AChR loss [Morsch2013].
- Rituximab works especially well in MuSK MG: in a multicentre blinded prospective review, 58% (14/24) of rituximab-treated MuSK patients reached a favourable outcome versus 16% (5/31) of controls (P = 0.002, number needed to treat 2.4), with 29% versus 74% still on prednisone (mean 4.5 vs 13 mg/day) [Hehir2017].
- A long-term series found all 6 MuSK patients reached remission (4/6) or minimal manifestations (2/6) with no reinfusions needed, against 10/11 AChR patients improving but 6 needing reinfusion; MuSK antibody titres went negative in 3 and fell over 80% in the other 3 [DiazManera2012].
- One explanation for relapse after B-cell depletion: autoantibody-expressing CD27+ B cells appear in the reconstituted repertoire during relapse but not remission, and CD27hiCD38hi plasmablasts contribute to MuSK autoantibody production, with hallmarks of antigen-driven affinity maturation [Stathopoulos2017].

### LRP4, clustered-AChR antibodies, seronegative MG
- LRP4 antibodies were found in a proportion of AChR-antibody-negative patients, inhibit binding of LRP4 to its ligand, and are predominantly IgG1 — a complement activator [Higuchi2011].
- Reported frequencies disagree sharply by cohort: 9.2% (11/120) of double-seronegative patients in one 217-patient series, against 2% and about 50% in two contemporaneous studies from different geographic locations [Zhang2012]; another series found about 50% of 13 double-seronegative sera bound LRP4-transfected cells and 4/13 inhibited agrin-induced AChR aggregation by more than 50% [Pevzner2012].
- Clustered-AChR cell-based assays recover a substantial share of "seronegative" patients: expressing AChR subunits with rapsyn in HEK cells detected AChR antibodies in 66% (25/38) of sera previously negative in solution-phase assay; these were mainly IgG1 and activated complement, and serum binding to clustered AChR correlated with complement deposition on thymic myoid cells [Leite2008].
- In routine practice the yield is lower but highly specific: the clustered-AChR cell-based assay detected antibodies in 38.1% (16/42) of radioimmunoprecipitation-negative MG patients with 100% specificity; all 21 tested seronegative patients were LRP4-negative [RodriguezCruz2015].
- Clustered-AChR-only patients form a distinct phenotype: frequent prepubertal onset (62.5%, median age 6 years, versus 11.5% and median 38 years in seronegative MG), high prevalence of ocular MG (62.5% vs 42.3%), less bulbar involvement (25.0% vs 46.2%), no respiratory symptoms (0% vs 23.1%), less thymectomy (6.3% vs 19.2%) and more remission (50.0% vs 8.3%) [RodriguezCruz2015].
- The serological map as of the late 2010s: 10–20% of patients lack AChR antibodies, MuSK is the commonest alternative, and cell-based assays extend testing to clustered AChR, LRP4 and agrin; intracellular targets (cortactin, titin, ryanodine receptor) serve as biomarkers in some patients [Vincent2018].
- MuSK antibodies are clearly pathogenic but coexisting IgG1, IgG2 and IgG3 antibodies have effects that question IgG4 as the sole pathogenic factor in MuSK MG [Vincent2018].

### The thymus as origin
- Thymic muscle-like (myoid) cells exist and bear AChR: dissociated rat and human thymus cultures yielded cells identical to skeletal muscle in morphology, contractility, electrophysiology and the presence of AChR, strategically placed to initiate the anti-AChR response [Kao1977b].
- The hyperplastic early-onset MG thymus has all the features of a tertiary lymphoid organ: neoangiogenesis with high endothelial venules and lymphatic vessels, chemokine overexpression recruiting peripheral cells, and ectopic germinal centre development, with thymic epithelial cells or myoid cells supplying AChR for local antigen presentation to recruited B cells and T follicular helper cells; interferon-β is overexpressed in MG thymus and may orchestrate the change [Cron2018].
- Thymic roles are subtype-specific: inflammatory, neoplastic and age-related thymic changes are pivotal for initiating anti-AChR autoimmunity in early-onset MG, thymoma MG and probably late-onset MG respectively, whereas the thymus is presumably unrelated to MuSK MG [Marx2013].
- MuSK MG thymi are largely unremarkable: only 4 of 14 MuSK-positive thymi had rare small germinal centres and overall they resembled age-matched controls, while about 75% of MuSK-negative seronegative samples had lymph-node-type infiltrates like AChR-positive patients [Leite2005].
- Thymic regulatory T cells are functionally defective in MG: CD4+CD25+ thymocyte numbers were normal but regulatory activity was severely impaired with decreased Foxp3, and the defect was not explained by contamination with activated effector cells [Balandina2005].
- AIRE expression is lost in thymoma: in a large series about 95% of thymoma patients were "chimeric", with AIRE and major AIRE-dependent autoantigens undetectable in the tumour but maintained in remnant thymus and lymph nodes; neutralising anti-type-I-interferon antibodies were present in about 60% of thymoma patients [Strobel2007].
- AIRE also controls expression of the AChR α subunit gene itself: AIRE and an IRF8-binding CHRNA1 promoter variant both modulate CHRNA1 mRNA in human medullary thymic epithelial cells, and the variant (which prevents IRF8 binding and abrogates promoter activity) was associated with early disease onset in two independent populations [Giraud2007].
- The thymoma mechanism bundle: MG occurs in at least 30% of thymoma patients, with inefficient tolerance attributed to defective epithelial AIRE and/or MHC class II expression, absence of myoid cells, failure to generate FOXP3+ regulatory T cells, and T-cell-signalling polymorphisms; autoreactive CD4+ effector generation plus defective regulatory T-cell production inside thymomas is found in >90% of MG-positive thymomas [Marx2010; Strobel2008].
- Frequency figures differ between sources: one review states about one half of cortical thymoma patients develop MG and 15% of MG patients have thymomas [Romi2011], against "at least 30% of all patients with thymomas" [Marx2010].
- Thymoma brings autoimmunity to intracellular striational antigens: titin and ryanodine receptor antibodies are found in 95% of thymoma MG and 50% of late-onset MG (onset ≥50 years) and are associated with severe disease [Romi2011].
- Titin antibodies bind near the A/I junction and in the central I-band and correlate with myopathy; ryanodine receptor antibodies recognise a region near the N-terminus important for channel regulation and inhibit Ca²⁺ release in vitro — but in vivo pathogenicity of antibodies to these intracellular antigens is still unproven [Skeie2006].
- Striational antibodies occur mainly in thymoma and late-onset MG, may correlate with severity, and may predict an unsatisfactory outcome after thymectomy; titin and ryanodine receptor assays give more specific clinical information than immunofluorescent striational staining [Romi2005].
- Experimental autoimmune MG, the founding demonstration that the receptor is the antigen: rabbits injected with AChR purified from electric eel electric organ in complete Freund's adjuvant produced precipitating anti-AChR antibody and, after the second injection, developed flaccid paralysis and abnormal electromyography characteristic of neuromuscular blockade, dramatically relieved by edrophonium or neostigmine [Patrick1973].
- Passive transfer, the founding demonstration that the antibody is sufficient: daily injection of an immunoglobulin fraction from MG patient serum into mice for up to 14 days reduced mEPP amplitudes and NMJ AChR numbers, and some mice showed typical decremental responses on repetitive nerve stimulation that were reversed by neostigmine [Toyka1975].

### FcRn and IgG recycling (mechanism)
- FcRn protects IgG from degradation throughout life, which is why IgG has a long serum half-life, and recent interest has been in reducing the half-life of pathogenic antibodies by interfering with the FcRn–Fc interaction [Roopenian2007].
- The numbers: human IgG and albumin have a half-life of around 19–21 days, while most other serum proteins such as IgA have a half-life of at most about 5–7 days [Pyzik2019].
- The mechanism is pH-dependent rescue: FcRn binds IgG with high affinity at mildly acidic pH 5.0–6.5 and essentially not at neutral pH, so IgG taken up into sorting endosomes at pH about 6 binds FcRn and is diverted into Rab4/Rab11a recycling endosomes and returned to the surface, while IgG that cannot bind FcRn is sorted to lysosomes [Pyzik2019].
- FcRn-mediated rescue is saturable, which is why high-dose IgG accelerates clearance of endogenous IgG [Pyzik2019].
- Blocking FcRn therefore lowers circulating IgG: in a first-in-human randomised placebo-controlled study in 62 healthy volunteers, a single dose of the FcRn antagonist efgartigimod reduced IgG by up to 50% and multiple dosing lowered IgG on average by 75% of baseline, with IgG returning to baseline about 8 weeks after the last dose and no effect on albumin or on immunoglobulins other than IgG [Ulrichts2018].
- FcRn blockade also clears IgG immune complexes, not only monomeric IgG, and inhibits IgG-IC-driven inflammatory pathways [Blumberg2019].

### Genetics of susceptibility (brief)
- The most reproducible association in typical early-onset MG with thymic hyperplasia is the A1-B8-DR3 ("8.1") MHC haplotype, though strong linkage disequilibrium has prevented identification of the causative polymorphism; the CHRNA1 promoter variant and the PTPN22 coding variant are two functional candidates outside the strongest MHC signal [Giraud2008].
- PTPN22*R620W: the 620W risk allele was increased in 293 non-thymoma patients without anti-titin antibodies (OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.32–2.97, P = 0.00059) but not in non-thymoma patients with anti-titin antibodies or in thymoma patients [Vandiedonck2006].
- A genome-wide association study of 1,032 AChR-positive cases and 1,998 controls found signals at CTLA4 (rs231770, P = 3.98×10⁻⁸, OR 1.37), HLA-DQA1 (rs9271871, P = 1.08×10⁻⁸, OR 2.31) and TNFRSF11A (rs4263037, P = 1.60×10⁻⁹, OR 1.41), with CTLA4 and HLA-DQA1 replicating in an Italian cohort; early- and late-onset disease had distinct but overlapping loci, and in late-onset cases HLA-DQA1 reached P = 7.02×10⁻¹⁸ (OR 4.27) [Renton2015].
- A larger GWAS (1,873 AChR-positive cases, 36,370 controls) found a signal within CHRNA1 itself and a transcriptome-wide association with CHRNB1 in skeletal muscle, plus new loci on 10p14 and 11q21, confirming PTPN22, HLA-DQA1/HLA-B and TNFRSF11A, and showed genetic correlation with hypothyroidism, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis and type 1 diabetes [Chia2022].
- The largest meta-analysis to date (5,708 cases, 432,028 controls, plus a 23andMe replication of 3,989 cases and 226,643 controls) identified 12 independent genome-wide significant hits across 11 loci, two of them specific to early-onset disease [Braun2024].

### Framing fact for the app
- MG is defined mechanistically as an autoimmune disease caused by antibodies against the AChR, MuSK or other AChR-related proteins in the postsynaptic muscle membrane, with localised or general muscle weakness as the predominant symptom induced by those antibodies; patients are grouped by antibody status, symptoms, age at onset and thymus pathology [Gilhus2019].
