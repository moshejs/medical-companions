# G. Myasthenic crisis & drugs that worsen MG
Scope: what myasthenic crisis is (definition, how often it happens, triggers, death rates, time on the ventilator, extubation), how breathing is monitored at the bedside, how crisis is managed in published studies (NIV, intubation, PLEX/IVIG, steroids, complement/FcRn agents), myasthenic vs cholinergic crisis, and a drug-by-drug evidence table for a "drugs that can worsen MG" checker. Sources: PubMed/Europe PMC abstracts and open full texts (Sheikh 2021, Krenn 2020, Wendell 2011 via PMC, Sanders 2016 via PMC, Narayanaswami 2021 via PMC), current US labels on DailyMed (grepped from the SPL XML on 2026-10-20), the 2007 Ketek label (FDA accessdata), the MGFA "cautionary drugs" web page, and an archived copy of the 2016 FDA fluoroquinolone press release. Not accessed: Table 1 of the 2020 consensus update ("drugs to avoid or use with caution") is an image in the PMC version and could not be read, so its wording is not quoted here; the MGFA PDF list could not be text-extracted (the HTML page was used); Claytor 2023, Neumann 2020 and Thomas 1997 were read as abstracts only, so their trigger percentages beyond the abstract are not given; the primary FDA 2011 safety communication on fluoroquinolones and MG was not found on fda.gov (404) and the February 2011 date comes from FDA's own 2016 press release (archived copy). Gilhus2019 was not opened and is not cited. Several items the brief asked about have no citable number in what was read; these are marked "(unverified)" or "no citable source found".

## Facts

### Definitions
- The international consensus defines impending crisis as rapid clinical worsening of MG that, in the opinion of the treating physician, could lead to crisis within days to weeks [Sanders2016].
- Manifest myasthenic crisis is defined as worsening of MG severe enough to need intubation or noninvasive ventilation to avoid intubation. This is MGFA Class V, except when intubation is part of routine postoperative care [Sanders2016; Jaretzki2000].
- Crisis is respiratory insufficiency needing invasive or noninvasive ventilation. It can come from weak breathing muscles or from bulbar weakness with upper-airway collapse [Claytor2023].
- Crisis usually builds up rather than starting suddenly, which "provides a window of opportunity for prevention" [Claytor2023].

### How often, and when
- Lifetime risk: 15-20% of people with MG have at least one crisis [Wendell2011; Claytor2023]. Other reviews give different figures: one-fifth to one-third of generalized MG [Chaudhuri2009], and 10-20% [Huan2026].
- Crisis usually happens within the first 2-3 years of the disease [Claytor2023]. Median time from first symptoms to first crisis was 8 months (Columbia series, 73 episodes) [Thomas1997]. Wendell gives 8-12 months, and says crisis is the first sign of MG in about one-fifth of patients [Wendell2011].
- In an Indian cohort of 64 patients, 21.9% had a crisis, 1-120 months (median 8.5) after onset. Half of those had more than one crisis (2-4 attacks) [Kalita2014].
- Independent predictors of crisis or exacerbation in 815 German patients: severity at diagnosis (QMG or MGFA class), thymoma, and MuSK antibodies. Reaching minimal manifestation status by 12 months lowered the risk. The time from diagnosis to starting immunosuppression did not affect risk [Nelke2022].
- Other high-risk features listed in a review: a previous crisis, severe disease, oropharyngeal (bulbar) weakness, MuSK antibodies and thymoma [Claytor2023].
- People with thymoma-associated MG reached crisis sooner than those without thymoma (17.95 vs 51.31 months from onset) [Huan2023].

### Triggers (with frequencies)
- Columbia series, 73 crises: infection 38% (usually pneumonia or upper respiratory infection), no obvious cause 30%, aspiration 10% [Thomas1997].
- No trigger is found in 30-40% of patients. Respiratory infection is the most common trigger [Claytor2023]. Wendell says one-third to one-half have no obvious cause [Wendell2011].
- Kalita (14 crises): infection 6, surgery 5, drug taper 2, iodinated contrast 1 [Kalita2014].
- Crises triggered by infection had worse outcomes in Nelke's cohort [Nelke2022].
- Triggers in an eculizumab emergency case series (5 episodes): COVID-19 (2), puerperium (1), PD-1 inhibitor (1), other infection (1) [Huang2026].
- Steroid start: crisis after starting steroids is reported in 9-18% of patients [Wendell2011]. See also "Corticosteroid initiation" below.
- Pregnancy/postpartum and surgery as trigger percentages in large crisis cohorts: no citable percentage found in the abstracts read (unverified).

### Mortality: then and now
- Death rate per crisis fell from 42% (early 1960s) to 6% (late 1970s). Overall MG mortality fell from 12% to 3.3% over the same 20 years [Cohen1981].
- Columbia 1983-1994: 4% of crises were fatal (3/73), and 4 more patients died after extubation. All 7 deaths were from overwhelming medical comorbidity [Thomas1997].
- US hospital data, 2000-2005: in-hospital mortality was 2.2% for all MG admissions and 4.47% for crisis. Older age (aOR 9.28) and respiratory failure (aOR 3.58) predicted death [Alshekhlee2009]. Mortality in US cohorts is under 5% [Claytor2023].
- German multicenter study, 2006-2015 (250 ventilated crises, median age 72): in-hospital mortality was 12%, linked to more than 3 comorbidities, ARDS and resuscitation. The main cause of death was multiorgan failure, mostly from sepsis [Neumann2020].
- Chinese prospective cohort (277 patients): in-hospital mortality 5.05%. All-cause mortality over about 29 months was 15.16%, mostly septic shock (35.7%) and multiorgan failure (21.4%). 79.8% had a favorable outcome at 1 year [Huan2026].
- A review gives 5-12% mortality for crisis [Vianello2022].
- Controversy: the low US figures (about 4%) come from younger or administrative cohorts. The German ventilated cohort was older and had 12% mortality, which the authors called "comparable to previous reports" given its age and disease burden [Neumann2020; Alshekhlee2009].

### ICU course, ventilation time, prolonged ventilation (timeline anchors)
- Extubation anchors (Columbia): 25% extubated by day 7, 50% by day 13, 75% by day 31. The longest crisis lasted more than 5 months [Thomas1997].
- Median mechanical ventilation was 12 days in the German cohort. 20.5% of survivors still needed ventilation at hospital discharge [Neumann2020].
- Mayo NIV study: mean ventilation was 10.4 days. Longer ventilation was linked to intubation and atelectasis [Seneviratne2008].
- Predictors of intubation longer than 2 weeks (Columbia): pre-intubation bicarbonate ≥30 (relative hazard 4.5), peak vital capacity <25 mL/kg on days 1-6 (RH 2.9), and age >50 (RH 2.4). Share intubated more than 2 weeks by number of risk factors: 0% with none, 21% with one, 46% with two, 88% with three [Thomas1997].
- Predictors of ventilation longer than 15 days (Germany): older age, late-onset MG, MGFA IVb before crisis, more than 3 comorbidities (OR 2.99), pneumonia (OR 3.13) and resuscitation (OR 9.15). Early-onset MG, thymic hyperplasia and a successful NIV trial predicted shorter ventilation [Neumann2020].
- MuSK-only crises lasted longer than AChR crises: ventilation 43.0 vs 17.4 days, ICU stay 45.3 vs 21.2 days, hospital stay 55.9 vs 28.8 days [Konig2021].
- Tracheostomy was done in 49.8% of ventilated crises. Ventilation lasted 34.4 days with tracheostomy vs 7.9 days without. Early tracheostomy (≤day 10) was linked to about 2 weeks less ventilation and ICU stay [Angstwurm2022].
- Intubated patients had a median hospital stay of 17 days [Wendell2011].

### Weaning and extubation failure
- Mayo: extubation failure in 44% (20/46 episodes), and reintubation in 26% (9/35). Atelectasis was the strongest predictor. Others were male sex, a previous crisis, and intubation for more than 10 days [Seneviratne2008b].
- Earlier series: extubation failure in 27% (7/26). Median time to reintubation was 36 hours. Older age and atelectasis were linked to failure [Rabinstein2005].
- German cohort: weaning failure in 64.2% and extubation failure in 43.0% (58/135) after the first attempt. Extubation was most likely to succeed between day 7 and day 12 after intubation [Neumann2024].
- Weaning failure was more common with first-line IVIG (OR 2.53) and less common with first-line PLEX or immunoadsorption (OR 0.57, p=0.07) [Neumann2024].
- Extubation failure is common in MG, and early tracheostomy may help [McKenzie2024].

### Bedside respiratory monitoring
- The "20/30/40" thresholds come from Guillain-Barré syndrome, not MG. In 114 severe GBS patients, progression to ventilation was linked to vital capacity <20 mL/kg, maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP/NIF) <30 cmH2O, maximal expiratory pressure (MEP) <40 cmH2O, or a fall of more than 30% in any of these [Lawn2001].
- A neuromuscular review notes that the "20/30/40 rule" was proposed to spot GBS patients at risk of respiratory failure [Vianello2022]. No MG-specific validation of the full 20/30/40 cut-offs was found in the sources read.
- MG-oriented thresholds: a VC under 1 L (or under 20-25 mL/kg) or an NIF weaker than 20 cmH2O signals significant respiratory weakness. Expiratory pressure below 40 cmH2O may signal crisis [Wendell2011]. These differ from the GBS-derived 20/30/40 figures.
- No single tool predicts neuromuscular respiratory failure well. A combined assessment is preferred: history, single breath count, neck flexion strength, bulbar weakness, paradoxical breathing and pulmonary function tests, with serial measurements to show the trend [McKenzie2024].
- Single breath count: take a deep breath, then count at 2 counts per second, sitting upright, in a normal voice. A count of ≥25 fits normal respiratory muscle function. The count correlates moderately with FVC and has been used by telephone [Dishnica2023].
- Difficulty counting to 20 in one breath signals expiratory muscle weakness [Wendell2011].
- In 31 AChR+ patients, single breath count correlated with FVC, NIF and neck flexor strength. The authors call breath count and neck flexor testing useful bedside tools [Elsheikh2016].
- Improving neck flexor strength tracks with bulbar and respiratory improvement during recovery [Wendell2011].
- Pulse oximetry can look normal while CO2 is high. In a neuromuscular disease audit, 54% of "other NMD" patients with high CO2 had normal oxygen saturation (≥95%) [Gray2024]. The claim that SpO2 and blood gases are "late signs" in MG crisis specifically: no MG-specific source with numbers was found in what was read (unverified). The Gray data come from chronic, not acute, respiratory failure.
- A PaCO2 above 45 mmHg when BiPAP was started was the only predictor of BiPAP failure [Seneviratne2008]. A PaCO2 above 50 mmHg predicted failure in the earlier series [Rabinstein2002].
- Bulbar weakness and aspiration: aspiration triggered 10% of Columbia crises [Thomas1997]. Most crises in the 1960s-1970s series came from aspiration pneumonia or happened in people with severe dysphagia [Cohen1981]. NIV may raise aspiration risk when bulbar weakness is present [Wendell2011].
- Worse outcomes in Nelke's cohort were linked to lower vital capacity before and at admission [Nelke2022].

### Management as published: ventilation
- Mayo, 60 episodes: BiPAP was the first support in 24, and intubation was avoided in 14 of them (about 58%). Mean BiPAP time in those 14 was 4.3 days [Seneviratne2008].
- First BiPAP series: intubation was avoided in 7 of 11 trials (64%) [Rabinstein2002].
- German cohort: NIV was enough in 38% of 92 trials and was linked to shorter ventilation and ICU stay [Neumann2020].
- NIV-first patients needed support for a median of 4 days vs 9 days for patients intubated first. NIV after extubation prevented reintubation in about 70% in two studies [Wendell2011].
- Reviews describe myasthenic crisis as the classic neuromuscular condition best treated with NIV. Unlike in GBS, NIV is not considered dangerous in MG [Rabinstein2016; McKenzie2024].
- Pyridostigmine during ventilation: the 1981 series found cholinergic drugs "not useful during crisis" and suggested stopping them during ventilation [Cohen1981]. A review says they are usually stopped to cut secretions and avoid confusing the assessment, then restarted at a low dose around extubation [Wendell2011].
- Consensus wording: cholinesterase inhibitors increase airway secretions, which may make breathing harder [Sanders2016].

### Management as published: PLEX vs IVIG
- Consensus: trials suggest PLEX and IVIG work equally well in impending or manifest crisis, but "expert consensus suggests that PLEX is more effective and works more quickly." The choice depends on the patient: PLEX is avoided in sepsis, and IVIG in hypercoagulable states, renal failure or immunoglobulin hypersensitivity [Sanders2016].
- Onset: PLEX acts in about 2 days and IVIG in about 4-5 days. Regimens cited: IVIG 400 mg/kg/day for 5 days, PLEX 5 exchanges over 10 days [Wendell2011].
- RCT in MG exacerbation (n=87): 3 PLEX vs IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day for 3 or 5 days. Myasthenic muscle score change at day 15 was similar (+18 vs +15.5). Side effects: 8 patients with PLEX vs 1 with IVIG [Gajdos1997].
- RCT in moderate-to-severe worsening MG (n=84): IVIG 1 g/kg/day × 2 days was as effective as PLEX × 5 exchanges at day 14. 69% improved with IVIG and 65% with PLEX [Barth2011].
- Meta-analysis of RCTs: QMG mean difference -2.8 favouring PLEX (95% CI -5.6 to 0.1, not significant), with a trend to faster effect with PLEX in the first 2 weeks [Pavlekovics2023].
- Retrospective crisis series (54 episodes): PLEX gave better ventilatory status at 2 weeks and better 1-month function, but more complications (13 vs 5 episodes) [Qureshi1999].
- US inpatient sample: outcomes with IVIG and PLEX were similar, and IVIG had possible economic advantages [Mandawat2010].
- German ventilated cohort: patients given PLEX or immunoadsorption first needed invasive ventilation less often [Neumann2020]. Nelke found no outcome difference between IVIG, PLEX, or both [Nelke2022]. The sources disagree.
- Practice guidance from a crisis centre favours PLEX as the main acute treatment [JaniAcsadi2007]. A 2023 review says plasmapheresis "is preferred" over IVIG [Claytor2023].

### Management as published: steroids, infection, newer agents
- Consensus: corticosteroids may cause transient worsening, so it may be reasonable to let PLEX or IVIG take effect for several days before starting them [Sanders2016].
- High-dose prednisone (60-100 mg/day) can be started with IVIG or PLEX, ideally in hospital. Improvement typically begins around day 13, and 85% improve within 3 weeks [Wendell2011].
- Infection: the authors of a large cohort suggest more intensive monitoring with a focus on preventing infectious complications [Nelke2022]. Sepsis was the leading cause of death in two cohorts [Neumann2020; Huan2026].
- Complement C5 inhibitors in crises not responding to standard therapy (17 cases, median age 77; 12 eculizumab, 5 ravulizumab): 82% left ICU/IMC within 6 weeks, 2 died of bacterial sepsis, and no meningococcal infection occurred [Gerischer2026].
- Emergency eculizumab (4 patients, 5 episodes): QMG improved from 28.2 to 15.0 and MG-ADL from 16.8 to 6.0 within 48 h. One patient was extubated at 17 h [Huang2026].
- Efgartigimod, prospective single-arm (16 patients with impending crisis): 75% reached the MG-ADL endpoint at week 8. Median QMG fell from 21 to 13. There was no control arm [Shelly2026].
- Efgartigimod as first-line or add-on therapy in 10 crises: all were weaned from ventilation, after 10.44 ± 4.30 days [Song2024].
- Evidence level for complement and FcRn agents in crisis: case series and single-arm studies only. No RCT in crisis was found [Gerischer2026; Huang2026; Shelly2026].

### Myasthenic vs cholinergic crisis
- Cholinergic crisis mixes muscarinic signs (sweating, tearing, salivation, nausea, diarrhoea, bradycardia) with nicotinic signs (fasciculations) [Wendell2011].
- The pyridostigmine label says myasthenic crisis and cholinergic crisis "may be difficult to distinguish" by symptoms. Cholinergic crisis calls for prompt withdrawal of anticholinesterase drugs [FDAMestinon2026].
- How rare it is: consensus says cholinergic crises are now rare, but too much cholinesterase inhibitor cannot always be ruled out as a cause of worsening [Sanders2016]. One crisis series found no cholinergic cases [Wendell2011].
- Miosis as a distinguishing sign: no source read gave it explicitly (unverified).
- Historically, edrophonium (Enlon) was labelled for evaluating emergency treatment in myasthenic crises. The label requires atropine 1 mg to be ready, and warns to watch a patient in crisis for bradycardia or cardiac standstill [FDAEnlon2026].
- Safety in diagnostic use: adverse events occurred in 11.5% of 506 tested patients, 91.4% of them mild. Sensitivity was 83.5% and specificity 87.7% [Keritam2026].
- Why it is now avoided in crisis: the consensus documents read do not recommend edrophonium testing in crisis. The rationale given here (cardiac and cholinergic risks) comes from the label warnings above. No guideline sentence saying "avoid" was found (unverified).

### Drugs that can worsen MG: general framing
- Sheikh's review divides drugs into those that induce de novo MG through immune effects (checkpoint inhibitors, D-penicillamine, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, interferons) and those that impair neuromuscular transmission (antibiotics, antiarrhythmics, anaesthetics, neuromuscular blockers) [Sheikh2021].
- The MGFA list says these associations "do not necessarily mean" a drug should not be prescribed. Some drugs may be necessary and "should not be deemed 'off limits'" [MGFA2024].
- Sheikh: although infections must be treated aggressively in MG, macrolides, aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones should be avoided if possible. Cephalosporins, sulfa drugs, clindamycin, tetracyclines, polymyxin B and nitrofurantoin can be given safely [Sheikh2021].
- Real-world size of risk: exacerbation followed 2.4% of ciprofloxacin, 1.6% of levofloxacin and 1.5% of azithromycin courses, vs 1.3% of amoxicillin courses (p=0.68). Infection was a confounder in 88.2% of these exacerbations [Uysal2025].
- Electronic-alert overrides (70 orders, 55 encounters): exacerbation in 12.7% of encounters, all after IV magnesium or IV labetalol. In 5 of 7 events another risk factor was present [Petrucelli2024].
- French claims (14,459 MG patients): 89.6% received a "contraindicated" drug during follow-up. Exposure was not linked to ICU admission for MG (HR 1.04) [Sole2026].
- The 2020 consensus update has a table of drugs to avoid or use with caution. It was not readable in the version accessed [Narayanaswami2021].

### Drug-by-drug evidence (for the checker)
- Telithromycin (ketolide): contraindicated in MG and boxed warning on the 2007 label, citing fatal and life-threatening respiratory failure, sometimes within a few hours of the first dose [FDAKetek2007]. In French pharmacovigilance, 7 of 10 cases began within 2 hours of the first dose [Perrot2006]. No longer marketed in the US [Sheikh2021]. Evidence: label contraindication plus boxed warning.
- Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, others): FDA boxed warning. Myasthenia gravis was added to the class boxed warning in February 2011 [FDAFluoroquinolone2016].
- Current fluoroquinolone labels: the boxed warning title includes "exacerbation of myasthenia gravis", and the labels say to avoid the drug in patients with known MG [FDACiprofloxacin2026; FDALevofloxacin2026].
- FAERS review of fluoroquinolones: 37 cases. Onset was a median of 1 day after exposure; 30% needed ventilation, 5% died, and 16% worsened again on rechallenge [Jones2011]. Sheikh rates this association "probable" [Sheikh2021].
- Macrolides: label warnings (not boxed) for azithromycin, clarithromycin and erythromycin. Each label reports worsening of MG and new myasthenic syndrome [FDAZithromax2026; FDAClarithromycin2026; FDAErythromycin2026]. Sheikh rates the association "definite" and suggests avoiding macrolides if there is an alternative [Sheikh2021]. MGFA: "use cautiously, if at all" [MGFA2024].
- Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, neomycin, tobramycin): label warning to use with caution in MG because of curare-like effects at the neuromuscular junction [FDAGentamicin2026]. Sheikh rates this "definite", with the main risk at high doses, in kidney failure, or with neuromuscular blockers [Sheikh2021]. MGFA: use only if there is no alternative [MGFA2024].
- Polymyxins/colistin: the colistimethate label warns of neuromuscular blockade and apnoea, especially in renal impairment, and of interaction with aminoglycosides and neuromuscular blockers. It does not name MG [FDAColistimethate2026]. Sheikh found no clinical reports of MG worsening with polymyxin B [Sheikh2021]. Evidence: mechanism and label (general), no MG case reports.
- IV magnesium: the magnesium sulfate injection label describes blocking of neuromuscular transmission and a risk of respiratory paralysis. The label read does not name MG [FDAMagnesiumSulfate2026]. Case reports include crisis after IV magnesium for atrial fibrillation [Jessop2022] and crises after magnesium for pre-eclampsia [Sheikh2021]. MGFA: potentially dangerous IV, use only if necessary with monitoring [MGFA2024].
- Magnesium sulfate in pre-eclampsia: the consensus says magnesium sulfate is not recommended for eclampsia in MG and suggests barbiturates or phenytoin instead [Sanders2016].
- Beta-blockers, including timolol eye drops: timolol ophthalmic labels warn that it can increase muscle weakness in MG [FDATimolol2026]. A case report describes worsening MG with timolol eye drops [Verkijk1985]. Sheikh rates beta-blockers "possible": they can be used in stable MG with close monitoring early on [Sheikh2021]. IV labetalol was linked to exacerbation in alert-override data [Petrucelli2024].
- Calcium channel blockers: Sheikh rates L-type blockers "possible", usable in stable MG with monitoring [Sheikh2021]. The verapamil label notes it may potentiate neuromuscular blocking agents and does not name MG [FDAVerapamil2026]. Evidence: case reports and mechanism only.
- Procainamide: label warning that MG may worsen and that use "may be hazardous" without adjusting anticholinesterase therapy [FDAProcainamide2026].
- Quinidine: label warning to monitor for worsening in MG [FDAQuinidine2026]. Sheikh rates class Ia antiarrhythmics "definite" and suggests avoiding them if there is an alternative [Sheikh2021].
- Quinine (Qualaquin): label contraindication in MG [FDAQualaquin2026]. MGFA notes US use is restricted to malaria [MGFA2024].
- Chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine: case reports of both onset and worsening of MG [Sheikh2021]. The US chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil) labels read contained no MG statement (grep of the DailyMed SPL) [FDAPlaquenil2026]. MGFA: use with caution [MGFA2024]. Evidence: case reports only, no US label warning found.
- Botulinum toxin: boxed warning for distant spread of toxin effect (all botulinum toxin products). The label also says people with neuromuscular junction disorders such as MG should be monitored, and lists MG worsening after blepharospasm treatment [FDABotox2026].
- Sheikh rates botulinum toxin "definite": avoid if possible, though it may be offered with cautious dose titration in mild or stable MG with dystonia or blepharospasm [Sheikh2021]. MGFA: avoid [MGFA2024].
- Non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockers: the rocuronium label warns that small doses may have profound effects in MG [FDARocuronium2026]. People with MG are more sensitive to these drugs, and the block can be prolonged and unpredictable [Sheikh2021].
- Succinylcholine: people with MG are more resistant to IV succinylcholine because they have fewer AChRs, but it is not absolutely contraindicated [Sheikh2021]. The US succinylcholine and sugammadex labels read had no MG-specific text.
- Sugammadex: a systematic review (24 studies) found fast reversal (train-of-four recovery 79.7 s to 10 min), a low rate of postoperative crisis, and no deaths [Kaye2025]. A Japanese study cited by Sheikh found postoperative crisis in 4.3% with sugammadex vs 8.7% without [Sheikh2021].
- Iodinated contrast (debated): pooled data from 3 studies showed more symptoms after contrast in 6.1% (23/374) vs 3.5% (11/313) after unenhanced CT. The guideline estimates a risk under 5% within 24 h, and says gadolinium is safe [Geenen2024].
- One contrast study found more exacerbations within 1 day of contrast CT (6.3% vs 0.6%, p=0.01) [Somashekar2013]. Another found delayed worsening in 12.3%, but alternative causes were more likely; it says contrast should not be withheld if indicated [Rath2017]. MGFA: newer agents appear safer, use cautiously [MGFA2024].
- Statins: risk of new-onset MG in the first 6-12 months after starting (pooled IRR 2.66 for days 0-179, 1.41 for days 180-364, about 1.0 after 1 year), higher with high-intensity statins [Yan2026].
- Statin start and crisis: crisis risk was raised only in the first 30 days after starting (IRR 3.60) [Yoo2026]. The French data suggest that for statins, long-term mortality benefit likely outweighs short-term MG risk [Sole2026]. Sheikh rates statins "probable" [Sheikh2021].
- D-penicillamine (induces MG): label warning lists MG among fatal associations. Myasthenic symptoms usually go away after the drug is withdrawn [FDAPenicillamine2026].
- D-penicillamine MG affects about 1-7% of patients. Onset is usually 6-7 months after starting (range 1 month to 8 years), and 70% remit 6-10 months after stopping [Sheikh2021]. A systematic review gives onset 2-12 months after starting, with mainly ocular symptoms [Antos2023].
- Immune checkpoint inhibitors: label warning listing myasthenic syndrome/MG (including exacerbation) and myocarditis-myositis-MG overlap syndrome [FDAKeytruda2026].
- Checkpoint-inhibitor MG incidence: 0.12% with nivolumab (12/9,869), none with ipilimumab (0/408) [Suzuki2017]. Another study found 0.24% (14/5,898) [Sheikh2021].
- Checkpoint-inhibitor MG course (65 patients): median onset 4 weeks. 37% also had myositis, 45% had respiratory failure, 38% died (23% from MG). IVIG or PLEX first gave better results than steroids alone (95% vs 63% improved) [Safa2019].
- An earlier review found 30.4% MG-related mortality and onset within about 6 weeks [Makarious2017]. In-hospital mortality for triple-M overlap is close to 40% [Furlepa2025].
- Consensus 2020 on checkpoint inhibitors: existing MG is not an absolute contraindication, and avoiding anti-CTLA-4 plus anti-PD-1/PD-L1 combinations may be prudent [Narayanaswami2021].
- Interferons: Sheikh rates them "possible", not contraindicated, association rarely reported [Sheikh2021]. Krenn cites cases of MG triggered or worsened by interferon alpha, including pegylated interferon [Krenn2020]. Evidence: case reports only.
- Corticosteroid initiation (transient worsening): 42% of 55 patients given prednisone 40-80 mg worsened within 4 weeks. Predictors were older age, severe bulbar symptoms and more severe MG [Bae2006].
- Published rates of steroid-induced worsening range from 25% to 75%, mostly in the first 2 weeks [Sheikh2021]. Krenn's table gives 8.6% severe worsening needing ventilation [Krenn2020]. The prednisone label warns that anticholinesterases plus corticosteroids can cause severe weakness in MG [FDAPrednisone2026].
- Giving IVIG before full-dose prednisone was followed by worsening in only 2.2% of 45 patients, vs 42% in a historical series [DiezPorras2020].
- Lithium: Sheikh rates it "probable", usable because worsening is rarely reported [Sheikh2021]. A case report describes recurrent acute myasthenia on lithium that resolved after stopping it, twice [Yao2025]. Evidence: case reports.
- Phenytoin: one case of MG on long-term phenytoin is reported [Sheikh2021]. Consensus names phenytoin as an alternative to magnesium for eclampsia in MG [Sanders2016]. Evidence: a single case report; the label read had no MG statement.
- Others on the MGFA list: desferrioxamine. Also discussed by Sheikh: tyrosine kinase inhibitors ("doubtful", probable with tandutinib), antipsychotics ("possible"), and penicillins ("probable", but worsening is rare) [MGFA2024; Sheikh2021].
