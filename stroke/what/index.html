Part 1 · Understanding stroke

What a stroke is

A stroke happens when part of the brain loses its blood supply, or when a blood vessel in or around the brain bleeds. Brain cells then get hurt or die 875. Follow the three kinds below, then explore how common stroke is and what it leaves behind.

Step 1 · healthy

Blood reaches the brain

Arteries carry blood to the brain. In 1971 the WHO described stroke as "A sudden onset of disturbance of focal brain function due to the blockage or rupture of blood vessels" 280. Blockage or rupture: that is the first split to learn. Stroke broadly includes ischaemic stroke, intracerebral haemorrhage and subarachnoid haemorrhage 875.

What counts as a stroke: the clock and the tissue

The World Health Organization (WHO) wording of 1980 used a clock. The 2013 AHA/ASA statement said the classic definition is mainly clinical and does not account for advances in science and technology, and it combined clinical and tissue criteria 875. Pick a situation and compare the two rules.

This is where the two rules disagree. The clock calls it short. The tissue definition counts infarction accompanied by overt symptoms as ischaemic stroke, even if symptoms are brief 875.

What comes after: death, disability, a second stroke

Move through time since the stroke. Each mark appears only where a study reports a figure; the lines between marks are straight joins, not data. Cohorts differ in era, age and case mix, so read the gap between them as a warning about comparing studies 391,635.

Cumulative deaths. ARIC: 10.5% at 30 days, 21.2% at 1 year, 39.8% at 5 years, 58.4% at 24 years 516,635. Perth: 36.5% in the first year, 58% by 5 years, 79% at 10 years 391,400. The older Perth cohort sits far above ARIC, whose participants were recruited as middle-aged adults. ICH is shown as 100 minus the published pooled survival of 46% and 29% 806.

In plain wordsMany people live for years after stroke: almost 94 million people are alive today after having one 306. A second stroke is a real risk, which is why follow-up care matters. About 1 person in 9 has another stroke within a year 682. Numbers describe groups. They cannot say what will happen to one person 682.

By type, at 30 days and after

Sweden (22,929 people): 30-day mortality 11.1% for ischaemic stroke and 30.7% for ICH. Among 30-day survivors, 5-year mortality did not differ 910.

ICH has not budged at one month

One-month case fatality: median 40.4% in studies to 2008 996; pooled 35.5% (32.3-38.9) in studies from 2008 to 2023, with no time trend. Good function after 3-12 months: 31.2% 1061.

Falling, unevenly

Greater Cincinnati, adjusted 5-year mortality, 1993-94 to 2015: ischaemic stroke 53% to 48.3%; ICH 64.4% to 69.2%, no significant change 849. South London: 30-day case fatality after first ischaemic stroke fell 38% from 2000 to 2015 1012.

SAH

Crude incidence fell from 10.2 per 100,000 person-years in 1980 to 6.1 in 2010 288. Case fatality fell 17% between 1973 and 2002 while mean age rose from 52 to 62 years 736.

Dementia

About 10% had dementia before a first stroke, 10% developed it soon after a first stroke, and more than a third had it after recurrent stroke 780.

A second stroke is deadlier

Perth: 30-day case fatality was 41% after first recurrence and 22% after first-ever stroke 401. Recurrence has fallen over time: pooled 5-year risk from 32% to 16.2% across studies 682, and in South London from 18% (1995-1999) to 12% (2000-2005), with no improvement since 319.

Exam hookPooled recurrence: 11.1% at 1 year, 26.4% at 5 years, 39.2% at 10 years 682. ICH survival: less than half at 1 year (46%), less than a third at 5 years (29%) 806.

Established

Younger and older diverge

Across 50 studies in 20 high-income countries, every study with data showed a less favourable trend under 55 than at 55 and over (pooled relative temporal rate ratio 1.57, 1.42-1.74). Absolute trends were heterogeneous: the firm finding is divergence, not a universal rise 907.

Established

Oxford, 2002-2010 against 2010-2018

2429 strokes. Incidence rose under 55 (IRR 1.67, 1.31-2.14) and fell at 55 and over (0.85, 0.78-0.92), independent of sex, severity, subtype and changes in investigation 567.

Hypothesis

Why? Sources disagree

US inpatient data point to rising traditional risk factors 350. In Oxford the rise persisted in people without diabetes, hypertension, smoking or obesity, so its cause is unexplained 567.

Young does not mean spared

Dutch cohort, age 18-50: 20-year mortality among 30-day survivors was 26.8% after ischaemic stroke and 13.7% after ICH, 3.9 times expected 869. In high-income countries, 1-year recurrence up to age 65 was unchanged after 2000 (RR 1.04, 0.78-1.39) 729.

GBD rounds also disagree here: for people under 70, GBD 2019 reported incidence up 15% for 1990-2019 302, while GBD 2021 reports 4.1% for 1990-2021 305.

Sex and gender

Rates are higher in men

Age-adjusted incidence per 100,000: men 160, women 125. Age-adjusted death rate: men 103, women 75 306.

SAH is the exception

About 51% of incident SAH occur in women 306. Pooled SAH risk ratio for women: 1.3 (0.98-1.7) 288.

Crude against adjusted

INSTRUCT (16,957 people): crude mortality rate ratio for women 1.35 at 1 year; adjusted 0.81. Age, prestroke function, severity and atrial fibrillation explained the reversal 792. In pooled trials women had higher survival (OR 0.82) and more disability (OR 1.20) 165.

Who has more strokes?

Age-specific rates are higher in men, but women have more stroke events because they live longer 831. Globally, 47.4% of incident strokes are in females 305. In the US, females accounted for 56.6% of stroke deaths in 2022, against 47.9% globally 305,635.

Recognition

Women are more likely to present with non-traditional symptoms and stroke mimics and can be misdiagnosed. There was no substantial difference in thrombolysis or thrombectomy outcome 150.

Unequal burden

Race in these studies is a social and recorded category, and socioeconomic adjustment changed several estimates 345,444,849.

United States, Israel and the cost

United States

  • About 795,000 new or recurrent strokes a year: about 610,000 first and 185,000 recurrent. These, and the 87/10/3 split, trace back to 1999 Greater Cincinnati data 635.
  • 9.4 million adults self-report a stroke, a prevalence of 3.3% 635.
  • 165,393 deaths in 2022, about 1 in 20 US deaths; fifth among causes 635. About 65% occurred outside an acute care hospital 635.
  • The 2026 AHA report exists, but its abstract states no stroke numbers and its full text could not be read, so these are from the 2025 edition 635,766.

Israel

  • GBD 2021: 8000 incident strokes, 3000 deaths, 92,000 prevalent cases. Age-standardised death rate fell 67.2% from 1990 to 2021 305.
  • A registry-based paper states around 18,000 strokes a year 923. Registry reports count stroke or TIA in at least some cases 826; the gap was not reconciled.
  • Age-adjusted rates were significantly higher in Arab than in Jewish people 826. Overall acute treatment and outcomes were similar 923.
  • By 2016-2017, t-PA use had stabilised at 9.2%, from 0.5% in a 2004 survey 826,959.

Plans

The Commission built its plan on four pillars: surveillance, prevention, acute care and rehabilitation 304. The European plan targets 90% or more of patients in a stroke unit by 2030 743; in 2023, 43.5% of 52 European nations had a national stroke plan 187.
