Part 2 · Recognition & assessment

Recognising stroke

Most strokes announce themselves in a few plain signs, and some do not. Step through balance, eyes, face, arm, speech and time, then test how well the published checklists perform, what they miss and what looks like a stroke but is not.

EmergencyIf a face droops, an arm is weak or speech goes wrong, even for a short time, treat it as an emergency and call your local emergency number. Sudden loss of balance or a sudden change in sight can also be a stroke [[Harbison2003,Ekundayo2013,Aroor2017]].

Exam hookCPSS items are arm, speech and face [[Kothari1999]]. ROSIER is positive above 0, on a range of -2 to +5 [[Nor2005]]. The published LVO thresholds are RACE of 5 or more and LAMS of 4 or more [[PerezDeLaOssa2014,Nazliel2008]].
In plain wordsPeople who arrive by ambulance tend to be scanned and treated faster than people who arrive another way [[Ekundayo2013]]. Seeing a family doctor first can make it more likely that someone reaches hospital late [[Fladt2019]].

Established

No perfect LVO scale

An AHA evidence review of 36 studies found C statistics mostly 0.70 to 0.85 and no threshold with both high sensitivity and high specificity. A positive test gave an LVO probability of perhaps 50 to 60%; a negative test could still leave 10% or more [[Smith2018]].

The price of missing fewer

In 1004 people within 6 hours (32.7% with LVO), the best accuracy was 79% for NIHSS of 11 or more and RACE of 5 or more, with over 25% false negatives. Reaching 10% or fewer needed NIHSS 5, RACE 1 or CPSS severity 0, which would send nearly everyone to a thrombectomy centre [[Turc2016]].

Field performance varies

RACE of 5 or more reached 85% sensitivity in one comprehensive centre's series [[PerezDeLaOssa2014]]. Seven scales reached only 38 to 62% in 2007 regional stroke codes, with specificity 80 to 93%, and paramedics completed them in 78 to 88% of codes [[Nguyen2021]]. Spectrum and prevalence differ between the studies.

What the scales share

Across tools published 2015 to 2018, LVO sensitivity ranged 23 to 99% and specificity 24 to 97%. Tools combining cortical signs and motor function did best. Gaze deviation, aphasia and neglect were significant indicators [[Antipova2019]].

Area under the curve for anterior LVO, PRESTO

Netherlands, 1039 FAST-positive people, 12% with anterior LVO [[Duvekot2021]]. RACE 95% CI 0.79 to 0.86; clinician NIHSS in the emergency department 0.83 to 0.89.

Mimics: what looks like a stroke but is not

About 3 in 10 people checked for a suspected stroke turn out to have something else. It is still right to get checked quickly [[Merino2013]]. The share and the mix depend on where you count.

Points towardsFeatures, as published
A strokeExact onset time, definite focal symptoms, abnormal vascular findings, neurological signs, lateralising signs, and being able to classify the stroke clinically [[Hand2006]]
A mimicCognitive impairment and abnormal signs in other systems [[Hand2006]]. More likely in younger people, women, people without vascular risk factors, those arriving by personal vehicle and those whose symptoms began as inpatients [[Merino2013]]

Treating a mimic by mistake

Thrombolysis in mimics was 1.8% (95% CI 1.5 to 2.2) of 5581 treated people, with symptomatic haemorrhage 1.0% against 7.9% in ischaemic stroke [[Zinkstok2013]]. Pooled in 392 people: symptomatic haemorrhage 0.5%, orolingual oedema 0.3%; risk ratio against stroke 0.33 (0.14 to 0.77) [[Tsivgoulis2015]].

Low blood sugar and seizures

Medical mimics make up 50 to 80% of cases in most large series, and the systemic list includes hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia [[Buck2021]]. Hypoglycaemia or seizure at presentation went with door-to-needle times 34 minutes longer [[Kamal2017]]. A sourced percentage for hypoglycaemia alone was not found in this research pass.

Missed strokes and chameleons

A stroke chameleon is a stroke that looks like something else. Minor, transient and atypical presentations are thought most prone to delayed diagnosis Early evidence [[Wallace2021]]. A meta-analysis of 23 studies (15,721 people) put emergency department sensitivity at 91.3%, so roughly 9% of cerebrovascular events are missed at first presentation, concentrated in milder, nonspecific or transient cases [[Tarnutzer2017]].

Top three pairs from a meta-analysis [[Tarnutzer2017]]. Bottom pair from two US hospitals, where 103 of 465 ischaemic strokes (22%) were initially misdiagnosed, a third of them arriving within 3 hours [[Arch2016]].

These estimates measure different things and should not be merged. The 9% comes from accuracy studies at first presentation [[Tarnutzer2017]]. The 1.2 to 12.7% (2243 probable and 23,809 potential missed strokes among US stroke admissions) comes from treat-and-release visits in the prior 30 days [[NewmanToker2014]].

Who is missed more

Odds of a probable miss fell with age against 18 to 44 years (45 to 64: 0.43; 65 to 74: 0.28; 75 and over: 0.19) and were lower in men (0.75). They were higher in Black (1.18), Asian or Pacific Islander (1.29) and Hispanic (1.30) people, and in non-teaching (1.45) and low-volume (1.57) hospitals [[NewmanToker2014]].

Younger adults

In a registry of 57 adults aged 16 to 50, 8 (14%) were misdiagnosed and 7 of those were first sent home from the emergency department. All 8 were first seen at hospitals not certified as primary stroke centres [[Kuruvilla2011]].

The classic posterior signs are rare

Horner's syndrome, crossed deficits, quadrantanopia and oculomotor palsy had PPVs of 92 to 100% but sensitivity of only 1.3 to 4.0%, in 1174 MRI-confirmed infarcts. Hemiplegia was present in 53.6% of posterior and 74.9% of anterior infarcts [[Tao2012]].

HINTS, in the right people

In 101 high-risk people with acute vestibular syndrome: 100% sensitive, 96% specific, with first DWI falsely negative in 12% [[Kattah2009]]. Pooled over 11 studies (n=1286): sensitivity 0.97, specificity 0.81, lower than the original [[Xu2026,Kattah2009]].

Women and men: the same focal signs, more nonfocal ones

Pooled data from 21 articles [[Shajahan2023]]. Dots are labelled W for women and M for men.

The two main reviews disagree on some single symptoms. Dysarthria was less common in women in one (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.82 to 0.95) [[Shajahan2023]] and more common in the other (OR 1.14, 1.04 to 1.24) [[Ali2022]]. Both agree on more nonfocal symptoms in women. The second (60 studies, n=582,844) found more headache and changes in consciousness, and overall focal symptoms did not differ (pooled OR 1.03); most studies carried possible bias [[Ali2022]].

Exam hookRoughly 9% of cerebrovascular events are missed at first emergency presentation, and dizziness carries a false-negative rate of 39.4% [[Tarnutzer2017]]. Posterior stroke is misdiagnosed about twice as often as anterior stroke, 37% vs 16% [[Arch2016]].
In plain wordsWomen can have the same weakness and speech problems as men, and they are also more likely to have confusion or feel generally weak [[Shajahan2023]]. Young people have strokes too, and these are more likely to be missed [[Kuruvilla2011,NewmanToker2014]].

Why people arrive late

Delay has barely moved

Over two decades, median onset-to-door times and the share arriving before 3 hours improved little, despite better door-to-needle times [[Pulvers2017]]. The AHA has described patient delay as the major factor limiting definitive treatment [[Moser2006]].

Who you call matters

Of 336 people with MRI-confirmed stroke in Basel, 42% arrived after more than 4.5 hours. A face-to-face family doctor visit was the main modifiable risk (adjusted OR 4.19, 95% CI 1.85 to 9.46) and ambulance transport lowered it (0.41, 0.24 to 0.71) [[Fladt2019]].

The ambulance effect

EMS arrival was independently associated with onset-to-door of 3 hours or less (adjusted OR 2.00), door-to-imaging of 25 minutes or less (1.89) and door-to-needle of 60 minutes or less (1.44) [[Ekundayo2013]]. Prenotified arrivals had door-to-imaging of 26 vs 31 minutes and door-to-needle of 78 vs 80 minutes [[Lin2012]].

Campaigns: remembered, not acted on

Of 10 campaign evaluations only 3 were controlled before-and-after studies, and public-only campaigns did little for awareness of the need for an emergency response [[Lecouturier2010Media]]. An Act FAST leaflet trial raised recall (75.7% vs 68.2%) without changing response to scenarios [[Dombrowski2015]]. In Ireland, emergency attendance rose after F.A.S.T. but the effect was not sustained [[Mellon2014]].

Knowing is not recognising

In a UK review, knowledge of the commonest symptoms looked good, yet fewer than half of people recognised they had had a stroke, and most contacted a general practitioner first [[Lecouturier2010Response]]. Among older adults in Ireland, slurred speech (54%) was the only established warning sign identified by more than half [[Hickey2009]].

Bringing treatment sooner: mobile units and direct transport

Two ideas try to close the gap. A mobile stroke unit brings a scanner and the drug to the person. Direct transport takes someone with a suspected large vessel occlusion straight past the nearest hospital to a thrombectomy centre. Choose one, then scrub the time.
