Part 1 · Understanding stroke

Bleeding strokes

A bleeding stroke (haemorrhagic stroke, US spelling hemorrhagic) has two main forms. Blood can burst into the brain itself, or spread over its surface from a burst aneurysm. Scrub through both, then try the rules doctors use to spot them.

A sudden headache that is the worst of your life, peaking in moments, is an emergency [[Claassen2022]]. Stroke signs of any kind: call emergency services.

The Ottawa SAH Rule

The rule applies to alert people over 15 with a new severe nontraumatic headache that peaks within 1 hour. If any of six items is present, it says investigate [[Hoh2023,Perry2013]]. Tick items to see how it reads.

Teaching tool only. It shows how a published rule is scored. It is never for an individual's diagnosis or care.

The 6-hour CT rule

CT sensitivity was higher within 6 hours of onset than overall [[Perry2011]]. Move the time since the headache began, and add a new neurological deficit, to see which route the 2023 AHA/ASA guideline describes [[Hoh2023]].

Teaching tool only, never for an individual's diagnosis or care. Sensitivity axis starts at 85%.

Disagreement on the early scan

Within 6 hours, one cohort found 100% sensitivity [[Perry2011]]. A meta-analysis gave 98.7%, with 13 missed SAH, 1.46 per 1000 [[Dubosh2016]]. A review quotes 95.5% for an implementation study of 3672 people, so SAH might be missed in 5% [[Claassen2022]]. The figures are not reconciled here. The first study's conditions were third-generation scanners read by a qualified radiologist [[Perry2011]].

After CT

After lumbar puncture in 1739 alert patients, fewer than 2000 x 106/L red cells plus no xanthochromia excluded aneurysmal SAH: sensitivity 100% (74.7 to 100), specificity 91.2%, based on only 15 cases [[Perry2015]]. If CT angiography is negative or inconclusive with high concern for an aneurysm, AHA/ASA 2023 says digital subtraction angiography is indicated (Class 1, B-NR) [[Hoh2023]].

Causes of ICH: SMASH-U and where bleeds sit

SMASH-U sorted 1013 consecutive ICH cases in Helsinki into six groups [[Meretoja2012]]. Pick a group. Deep bleeds point to hypertensive disease and lobar bleeds to amyloid angiopathy [[Magid2022,An2017]].

Schematic. Bleed sizes only hint at the cited medians. Percentage shares by exact site could not be verified, so none is given.

High blood pressure

Long-standing hypertension degenerates the media and smooth muscle of the responsible small arteries. Lipohyalinosis is most often found in non-lobar ICH [[An2017]]. Bleeds occur in the putamen, caudate, thalamus, brainstem and deep cerebellar nuclei [[Magid2022]]. The most common site is the putamen [[An2017]].

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy

Amyloid builds up in arterial walls with loss of smooth muscle cells. It occurs mainly in lobar locations or the subarachnoid space over the cortical convexities, with microbleeds or superficial siderosis [[Magid2022]].

Anticoagulants

In Greater Cincinnati, anticoagulant-associated ICH rose from 5% of ICH in 1988 to 17% in 1999, as US per-capita warfarin distribution quadrupled [[Flaherty2007]]. Warfarin did not raise baseline volume, but raised the risk of expansion (OR 6.2, 1.7 to 22.9) [[Flibotte2004]].

Other risk factors

One review lists current smoking, excess alcohol, low cholesterol and drugs, plus older age, male sex, Asian ethnicity, chronic kidney disease and microbleeds [[An2017]].

Boston criteria version 2.0 for amyloid angiopathy

These criteria grade how sure one can be of cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). Set the MRI findings below. This covers the two MRI-based levels. Definite and pathology-supported CAA need tissue [[Charidimou2022]].

LevelWhat it needs [[Charidimou2022]]
DefiniteFull post-mortem: a presentation with spontaneous ICH, transient focal neurological episodes, convexity SAH or cognitive impairment or dementia; severe CAA with vasculopathy; no other diagnostic lesion.
Probable with supporting pathologyClinical data plus evacuated haematoma or cortical biopsy showing some degree of CAA, and no other diagnostic lesion.
ProbableAge 50 or over; spontaneous ICH, transient focal neurological episodes or cognitive impairment or dementia; at least 2 strictly lobar haemorrhagic lesions on T2*-weighted MRI (ICH, microbleed, cortical superficial siderosis or convexity SAH foci), OR 1 lobar lesion plus 1 white matter feature (severe visible perivascular spaces in the centrum semiovale, or multispot white matter hyperintensities); no deep haemorrhagic lesion; no other cause.
PossibleSame age and presentation, with a single strictly lobar haemorrhagic lesion OR a single white matter feature; no deep haemorrhagic lesion; no other cause. A cerebellar lesion counts as neither lobar nor deep.

Causes to exclude include head trauma, haemorrhagic transformation of an ischaemic stroke, arteriovenous malformation, haemorrhagic tumour and CNS vasculitis [[Charidimou2022]]. In the autopsy group, probable CAA by v2.0 had sensitivity 74.5% and specificity 95.0% (AUC 0.848), against 64.5% and 95.0% (AUC 0.798) for the earlier criteria [[Charidimou2022]].

Disputed outside ICH In a community and memory clinic cohort of 54, v2.0 probable CAA had sensitivity 28.6% and specificity 65.3% (AUC 0.47), and was not superior to v1.5 [[Switzer2024]].

Exam hookProbable CAA in Boston v2.0 adds white matter features, and needs age 50 or over [[Charidimou2022]]. ABC/2 estimates ICH volume: A greatest diameter, B the diameter at 90 degrees to A, C slices with haemorrhage times slice thickness [[Kothari1996]].

WFNS and Hunt and Hess describe how ill the person is. AHA/ASA 2023 recommends clinical scales like these to set initial severity and predict outcome (Class 1, B-NR) [[Hoh2023]]. The Fisher family describes the blood on CT, which predicts spasm. In the original Fisher work, no detected or diffuse blood was almost never followed by severe vasospasm (1 of 18), while thick clots or layers almost always were (23 of 24) [[Fisher1980]]. In 1355 placebo-arm patients, 33% developed symptomatic vasospasm, and the modified scale's crude ORs against grade 0 to 1 were 1.6, 1.6 and 2.2 for grades 2, 3 and 4 [[Frontera2006]].

Unruptured aneurysms

3.2%

Prevalence (1.9 to 5.2) in a population with no comorbidity, mean age 50, 50% men. Prevalence ratio 6.9 with polycystic kidney disease, 3.4 with a family history, 1.61 in women [[Vlak2011]].

3.4%

Rupture at 5 years in PHASES (8382 people, 1.4% at 1 year). The range ran from 0.25% to over 15%. The predictors are region, hypertension, age, size, earlier SAH and site [[Greving2014]].

3.6 and 2.8

Times the rupture risk in Finnish and Japanese people against North American and other European populations [[Greving2014]]. Prevalence was similar, so it points to higher rupture risk [[Vlak2011]].
