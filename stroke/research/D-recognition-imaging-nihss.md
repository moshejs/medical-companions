## Findings

### signs · FAST and BE-FAST
- FAST checks face, arm and speech, with time as the prompt to act. In a 6-month audit of 487 people admitted with suspected stroke (356 with stroke or TIA), paramedics using the Face Arm Speech Test were correct in 144 of 183 people with stroke (79%) [[Harbison2003]].
- In the same audit the share of non-stroke admissions was similar by route: 23% ambulance, 29% primary care doctors, 29% emergency room [[Harbison2003]].
- Paramedics admitted 46% of people within 3 hours of onset, against 12% for primary care doctors (P<0.01) [[Harbison2003]].
- Paramedics referred more total anterior circulation strokes than primary care doctors (39% vs 14%, P<0.0001) and fewer lacunar strokes (14% vs 31%, P<0.001) [[Harbison2003]].
- In 736 people with ischaemic stroke at one US centre (records from 2014), 14.1% had no FAST symptom at presentation. Of these, 42% had gait imbalance or leg weakness, 40% had visual symptoms and 70% had either [[Aroor2017]].
- Adding balance and eyes (BE-FAST) reduced the share of people with stroke not identified from 14.1% to 4.4% (P<0.0001) [[Aroor2017]].
- That study was retrospective, included only people with confirmed ischaemic stroke, and excluded people who were comatose or intubated. It cannot measure specificity. The authors made any change to public education conditional on prospective validation [[Aroor2017]].
- In 164 people with MRI-confirmed infarcts, 46 had posterior circulation stroke. Sensitivity for posterior circulation stroke was 97.8% for BE-FAST and 58.7% for FAST, and specificity was 10.0% and 39.8%. FAST alone would have missed 39.1% (18 of 46) [[Tanglay2024]].

### signs · Accuracy of stroke recognition scales
- The Cochrane review included 23 studies: CPSS 11, ROSIER 8, FAST 5, LAPSS 5, MASS 3, and one each for OPSST, MedPACS and PreHAST [[Zhelev2019]].
- Twelve of those studies were at high risk of bias for patient selection. The authors call their findings provisional hypotheses that need better-designed studies [[Zhelev2019]].
- ROSIER in the emergency room: pooled sensitivity 0.88 (95% CI 0.84 to 0.91), prediction interval about 0.75 to 0.95. This means about 12% of people with stroke or TIA are missed, with a plausible range of 5% to 25%. No reliable pooled specificity could be obtained because of extreme heterogeneity [[Zhelev2019]].
- LAPSS prehospital: pooled sensitivity 0.83 (95% CI 0.75 to 0.89), specificity 0.93 (0.88 to 0.96). The authors were uncertain of these results because of risk of bias [[Zhelev2019]].
- The review's conclusion: in the field, CPSS had consistently the highest sensitivity. In the emergency room, ROSIER should be the test of choice, in part because it had been evaluated in more studies than FAST [[Zhelev2019]].
- CPSS is 3 items: arm weakness, speech, facial droop. Any one abnormal item, as judged by a physician, gave sensitivity 66% and specificity 87% for stroke, rising to sensitivity 88% for anterior circulation stroke. Paramedic and EMT reproducibility for the total score: intraclass correlation 0.89. This was a convenience sample of 171 people, 49 with stroke or TIA [[Kothari1999]].
- ROSIER has 7 items and scores from -2 to +5. History: loss of consciousness, convulsive fits. Signs: face, arm or leg weakness, speech disturbance, visual field defect. Positive means a score above 0 [[Nor2005]].
- ROSIER prospective validation in 173 referrals: sensitivity 93% (95% CI 89 to 97), specificity 83% (77 to 89), PPV 90%, NPV 88% [[Nor2005]].
- LAPSS paramedic field validation: sensitivity 91% (95% CI 76 to 98), specificity 97% (93 to 99), PPV 86%, NPV 98% [[Kidwell2000]].
- Specificity disagreements are large. ROSIER specificity was 83% in its derivation team's validation [[Nor2005]] and 44% (95% CI 34 to 53) in an independent UK cohort of 356 people, where FAST was 39% (30 to 48) [[Whiteley2011]].
- ROSIER sensitivity in that cohort was 83% (78 to 87) against FAST 81% (76 to 86), with no detectable difference. The authors concluded that the simpler FAST could replace ROSIER in the emergency department [[Whiteley2011]].
- In UK ambulance use, ROSIER was no better than FAST, and both showed poor specificity. Consultants confirmed 64% of strokes and 78% of non-strokes identified by ambulance clinicians using ROSIER [[Fothergill2013]].
- Scales for separating mimics had sensitivity of 44 to 91% and specificity of 27 to 98%. FABS performed best (90% and 91%) [[Antipova2019]].
- A review table lists CPSS as "gaze, arm weakness, level of consciousness" [[Budincevic2022]], which does not match the original scale of arm, speech and face [[Kothari1999]]. Use the original.

### signs · Large vessel occlusion (LVO) scales
- RACE items: facial palsy 0 to 2, arm motor 0 to 2, leg motor 0 to 2, gaze 0 to 1, aphasia or agnosia 0 to 2. In 357 people in the field, LVO prevalence was 21%. RACE of 5 or more: sensitivity 0.85, specificity 0.68, PPV 0.42, NPV 0.94. AUC 0.82, against 0.85 for the NIHSS [[PerezDeLaOssa2014]].
- LAMS scores facial weakness, arm strength and grip, total 0 to 5 [[Llanes2004]]. At 4 or more: sensitivity 0.81, specificity 0.89, accuracy 0.85, positive likelihood ratio 7.36, negative likelihood ratio 0.21, in 119 people of whom 62% had persisting LVO [[Nazliel2008]].
- VAN asks about vision, aphasia and neglect, without a score. In a pilot of 62 code strokes with 14 LVOs, sensitivity was 100% and specificity 90%, against 74% for NIHSS of 6 or more. PPV was 74% against 58%. The authors call for a larger validation [[Teleb2017]].
- PRESTO (Netherlands, 1039 FAST-positive people, 12% with anterior LVO) AUCs: RACE 0.83 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.86), G-FAST 0.80, CG-FAST 0.80, LAMS 0.79, CPSS 0.79, PASS 0.76, C-STAT 0.75, FAST-PLUS 0.72. Clinician NIHSS in the emergency department was 0.86 (0.83 to 0.89) [[Duvekot2021]].
- In 2007 stroke codes in a large Dutch urban centre (7.9% with anterior LVO), 7 scales had sensitivity of only 38 to 62% and specificity of 80 to 93%. Paramedics could complete them in 78 to 88% of codes [[Nguyen2021]].
- Sensitivity varies widely between field studies: 85% for RACE of 5 or more in one comprehensive centre's series [[PerezDeLaOssa2014]], and 38 to 62% across scales in a regional stroke-code cohort [[Nguyen2021]]. Spectrum and prevalence differ between the studies.
- AHA evidence review of 36 studies: C statistic mostly 0.70 to 0.85. No threshold on any instrument gave both high sensitivity and high specificity. A positive test gave an LVO probability of perhaps 50 to 60%; a negative test could still leave 10% or more [[Smith2018]].
- In 1004 people within 6 hours (32.7% with LVO), the best accuracy was 79% (95% CI 77 to 82) for NIHSS of 11 or more and RACE of 5 or more, but false negatives exceeded 25%. Reaching 10% or fewer false negatives needed NIHSS 5, RACE 1 or CPSS severity 0, which would send nearly everyone to a thrombectomy centre [[Turc2016]].
- Across tools published 2015 to 2018, LVO sensitivity ranged 23 to 99% and specificity 24 to 97%. Tools combining cortical signs and motor function (FAST-ED, NIHSS, RACE) did best. Gaze deviation, aphasia and neglect were significant indicators of LVO [[Antipova2019]].

### signs · Misdiagnosis and the posterior circulation
- Meta-analysis of 23 studies (15,721 people): overall emergency department sensitivity 91.3% (95% CI 90.7 to 92.0), specificity 92.7% (91.7 to 93.7), so roughly 9% of cerebrovascular events are missed at first presentation [[Tarnutzer2017]].
- Misses concentrate in milder, nonspecific or transient presentations, with a range of 24 to 60% [[Tarnutzer2017]]:
  - dizziness vs motor findings: false-negative rate 39.4% vs 4.4% (OR 14.22, 95% CI 9.76 to 20.74)
  - SAH with normal vs abnormal mental state: 23.8% vs 4.2% (OR 7.03, 4.80 to 10.31)
  - TIA vs ischaemic stroke: false-discovery rate 59.7% vs 11.7% (OR 11.21, 6.66 to 18.89)
- US linked records from nine states: treat-and-release emergency visits in the prior 30 days gave 23,809 potential (12.7%) and 2243 probable (1.2%) missed strokes among stroke admissions. Estimated 15,000 to 165,000 misdiagnosed events each year in US emergency departments [[NewmanToker2014]].
- Odds of a probable miss were lower in men (OR 0.75) and fell with age against 18 to 44 years (45 to 64: 0.43; 65 to 74: 0.28; 75 and over: 0.19). They were higher in Black (1.18), Asian or Pacific Islander (1.29) and Hispanic (1.30) people, and in non-teaching (1.45) and low-volume (1.57) hospitals [[NewmanToker2014]].
- These two estimates measure different things and should not be merged: 9% comes from accuracy studies at first presentation [[Tarnutzer2017]], and 1.2 to 12.7% comes from returns to hospital after discharge [[NewmanToker2014]].
- At two US hospitals, 103 of 465 ischaemic strokes (22%) were initially misdiagnosed. A third of those missed had arrived within 3 hours and a further 11% between 3 and 6 hours [[Arch2016]].
- 37% of posterior strokes were initially misdiagnosed, against 16% of anterior strokes (P<0.001). Independent predictors were nausea or vomiting (OR 4.02, 95% CI 1.60 to 10.1), dizziness (OR 1.99, 1.03 to 3.84) and previous stroke (OR 2.40, 1.30 to 4.42) [[Arch2016]].
- Typical posterior signs are uncommon. Horner's syndrome (4.0%), crossed sensory deficits (3.0%), quadrantanopia (1.3%), oculomotor palsy (4.0%) and crossed motor deficits (4.0%) had PPVs of 92 to 100% but sensitivity of only 1.3 to 4.0%, in 1174 MRI-confirmed infarcts [[Tao2012]].
- The most common deficits were shared. Hemiplegia was present in 53.6% of posterior and 74.9% of anterior infarcts, and hemisensory deficit in 36.4% and 34.2% [[Tao2012]]. Bedside signs alone often mislocalise.
- HINTS in 101 high-risk people with acute vestibular syndrome: normal head impulse, direction-changing nystagmus or skew was 100% sensitive and 96% specific for stroke. First MRI DWI was falsely negative in 12%, all within 48 hours [[Kattah2009]].
- Pooled HINTS (11 studies, n=1286): sensitivity 0.97 (95% CI 0.94 to 0.99), specificity 0.81 (0.72 to 0.88). This specificity is lower than the original 96% [[Xu2026,Kattah2009]].

### signs · Chameleons: women and younger people
- A stroke chameleon is a stroke that looks like something else. Minor, transient and atypical presentations are thought most prone to delayed diagnosis in outpatient settings (Early evidence) [[Wallace2021]].
- A review names young people, women, minorities, and people with nonspecific, transient or posterior symptoms as at higher risk of misdiagnosis [[Bakradze2018]].
- Pooled data from 21 articles: women and men had a similar share of motor deficit (56% vs 56%) and speech deficit (41% vs 40%). Women more often had generalised nonspecific weakness (49% vs 36%), mental status change (31% vs 21%) and confusion (37% vs 28%). Men more often had ataxia (44% vs 30%) and dysarthria (32% vs 27%) [[Shajahan2023]].
- A second meta-analysis (60 studies, n=582,844) found women had more headache (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.39), changes in consciousness or mental status (OR 1.38, 1.19 to 1.61) and coma or stupor (OR 1.39, 1.25 to 1.55). Overall focal symptoms did not differ (pooled OR 1.03). Most studies carried possible bias [[Ali2022]].
- The two reviews disagree on some single symptoms. Dysarthria was less common in women in one (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.82 to 0.95) [[Shajahan2023]] and more common in the other (OR 1.14, 1.04 to 1.24) [[Ali2022]]. Both agree on more nonfocal symptoms in women.
- Earlier single-population studies: nontraditional symptoms in 28% of women vs 19% of men (OR 1.62, 95% CI 1.2 to 2.2) [[Labiche2002]]; 51.8% vs 43.9% (P=0.09), with mental status change 23.2% vs 15.2% (P=0.03) [[Lisabeth2009]].
- In a registry of 57 adults aged 16 to 50, 8 (14%) were misdiagnosed and 7 of those 8 were first sent home from the emergency department. Age under 35 (P=.05) and posterior circulation stroke (P=.006) were associated with being misdiagnosed. All 8 were first seen at hospitals not certified as primary stroke centres [[Kuruvilla2011]].

### signs · Mimics: how many, and what they are
- Share of suspected strokes that are mimics or non-stroke, by source:
  - 19% in an emergency department series of 411 people [[Libman1995]]
  - 26% implied by 74% confirmed stroke (95% CI 66 to 83) across 29 studies (8839 people, with significant heterogeneity) [[Gibson2013]]
  - 30% of 8187 people seen by a stroke team over 10 years [[Merino2013]]
  - 31% of 350 presentations, including 44 labelled "possible stroke or TIA" [[Hand2006]]
  - 23 to 29% by referral route [[Harbison2003]]
- The five most frequent non-stroke diagnoses across studies were seizure, syncope, sepsis, migraine and brain tumours [[Gibson2013]].
- In ROSIER derivation, common mimics were seizures (23%), syncope (23%) and sepsis (10%) [[Nor2005]].
- Among 429 mimics given thrombolysis in a registry of 10,436 (4.1%), the commonest were functional disorder (30.8%), migraine (17.5%) and seizures (14.2%), as reported in a review [[Buck2021]].
- Medical mimics make up 50 to 80% of cases in most large series, and the review's list of systemic mimics includes hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia [[Buck2021]]. A sourced percentage for hypoglycaemia alone was not found in this pass.
- Mix depends on setting: in 419 nursing home residents with suspected stroke, 66% were mimics, led by infections (24%) and seizures (20%) [[Buck2021]].
- Hypoglycaemia or seizure at presentation was associated with door-to-needle times 34 minutes longer, in US registry data [[Kamal2017]].
- Features pointing to a mimic: cognitive impairment and abnormal signs in other systems. Features pointing to stroke: exact onset time, definite focal symptoms, abnormal vascular findings, neurological signs, lateralising signs, and being able to classify the stroke clinically [[Hand2006]].
- Mimics were more likely in younger people, women, people without vascular risk factors, those arriving by personal vehicle and those whose symptoms began as inpatients [[Merino2013]].
- Thrombolysis in mimics is uncommon and has a low complication rate. Frequency was 1.8% (95% CI 1.5 to 2.2) of 5581 treated people, with symptomatic haemorrhage 1.0% (0.0 to 5.0) vs 7.9% (7.2 to 8.7) in ischaemic stroke [[Zinkstok2013]].
- Pooled data: symptomatic haemorrhage 0.5% (95% CI 0 to 2) and orolingual oedema 0.3% (0 to 2) among 392 people with mimics given thrombolysis. Risk ratio for symptomatic haemorrhage compared with stroke was 0.33 (0.14 to 0.77) [[Tsivgoulis2015]].

### signs · Public knowledge and delay
- Patient delay was described by the AHA as the major factor limiting definitive treatment, and most people still do not seek care in time despite decades of campaigns [[Moser2006]].
- Over two decades, median onset-to-door times and the share of people arriving before 3 hours improved little, despite improvements in door-to-needle time [[Pulvers2017]].
- Of 336 people with MRI-confirmed stroke in Basel, 42% arrived more than 4.5 hours after onset. A face-to-face visit to a family doctor was the main modifiable risk (adjusted OR 4.19, 95% CI 1.85 to 9.46), and ambulance transport lowered the risk (adjusted OR 0.41, 0.24 to 0.71) [[Fladt2019]].
- In a UK review, knowledge of the two commonest symptoms and of the need for an emergency response looked good. Even so, fewer than half of people recognised they had had a stroke, recognition did not shorten time to presentation, and most contacted a general practitioner first [[Lecouturier2010Response]].
- In a survey of older adults in Ireland, fewer than half identified established warning signs such as weakness and headache from a list, with slurred speech (54%) the exception [[Hickey2009]].
- Campaigns raise awareness more than they change behaviour. Of 10 evaluations, only 3 were controlled before-and-after studies. Public-only campaigns raised awareness of symptoms but had little effect on awareness of the need for an emergency response [[Lecouturier2010Media]].
- A randomised leaflet study of UK Act FAST: recall rose (75.7% vs 68.2%, P=0.003) and more FAST elements were named (66.1% vs 45.3%, P<0.001), with no effect on recognition or response to scenarios [[Dombrowski2015]].
- Most interviewed patients and witnesses knew the campaign but reported that it had not changed what they did [[Dombrowski2013]].
- In Ireland, emergency attendance rose after F.A.S.T. (β=0.84, 95% CI 0.43 to 1.24) but the effect was not sustained [[Mellon2014]].

### signs · Ambulance use and prenotification
- 63.7% of 204,591 people in US registry data arrived by ambulance. More than a third did not use EMS. Older people, those with Medicaid or Medicare, and people with severe stroke were more likely to use it; minority groups and rural residents were less likely [[Ekundayo2013]].
- EMS arrival was independently associated with [[Ekundayo2013]]:
  - onset-to-door of 3 hours or less: adjusted OR 2.00 (95% CI 1.93 to 2.08)
  - door-to-imaging of 25 minutes or less: OR 1.89 (1.78 to 2.00)
  - door-to-needle of 60 minutes or less: OR 1.44 (1.28 to 1.63)
- Among 371,988 people arriving by EMS, 67.0% were prenotified. Prenotification went with door-to-imaging of 26 vs 31 minutes and door-to-needle of 78 vs 80 minutes (both P<0.0001) [[Lin2012]].
- Time scale of the urgency: in a typical large vessel ischaemic stroke, 1.9 million neurons, 14 billion synapses and 12 km of myelinated fibres are lost each minute. This is a modelled estimate from a final volume of 54 mL evolving over 10 hours [[Saver2006]].

### signs · Mobile stroke units and direct-to-thrombectomy
- BEST-MSU (alternating weeks, 1515 enrolled, 1047 eligible for t-PA): median onset-to-t-PA 72 vs 108 minutes. 97.1% vs 79.5% of eligible people received t-PA [[Grotta2021]].
- BEST-MSU outcomes: utility-weighted mRS 0.72 vs 0.66 (adjusted OR for a score of 0.91 or more, 2.43, 95% CI 1.75 to 3.36). mRS 0 to 1 at 90 days in 55.0% vs 44.4%. Mortality at 90 days 8.9% vs 11.9% [[Grotta2021]].
- B_PROUD (Berlin, nonrandomised, 1543 people): median mRS at 3 months 1 vs 2 (common OR for worse mRS, 0.71, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.86) [[Ebinger2021]].
- PHANTOM-S (Berlin, randomised weeks): alarm-to-treatment fell by 15 minutes across the catchment and by 25 minutes when the unit was deployed. Thrombolysis rates were 29% and 33% vs 21% [[Ebinger2014]].
- Meta-analysis: excellent outcome adjusted OR 1.64 (95% CI 1.27 to 2.13; 5 studies, n=3228). Onset-to-thrombolysis was shorter by a median of 31 minutes (23 to 39). No increase in mortality or symptomatic haemorrhage [[Turc2022]].
- Limits of this evidence: BEST-MSU was an alternating-week trial [[Grotta2021]] and B_PROUD was nonrandomised [[Ebinger2021]]. Neither was individually randomised.
- RACECAT (Catalonia, nonurban, cluster-randomised; suspected LVO; 1401 people) was stopped for futility. In the 949 with target ischaemic stroke, median mRS was 3 in both arms (adjusted common OR 1.03, 95% CI 0.82 to 1.29) [[Perez2022]].
- In RACECAT, direct transport lowered intravenous thrombolysis (47.5% vs 60.4%) and raised thrombectomy (48.8% vs 39.4%). 90-day mortality was 27.3% vs 27.2% [[Perez2022]].
- A model of treatment times finds that drip-and-ship rarely predicts the best outcome at current times, and becomes more favourable if door-to-needle falls to 30 minutes. It is sensitive to local times at both centres [[Holodinsky2018]].
- ESO-ESMINT 2019 asked for further randomised trials on mothership vs drip-and-ship approaches [[Turc2019ESO]].

### imaging · CT versus MRI
- Prospective comparison in 356 people with suspected stroke (217 with a final diagnosis of stroke). For any acute stroke, sensitivity was 83% for MRI (95% CI 78 to 88) and 26% for CT (20 to 32) [[Chalela2007]].
- Acute ischaemic stroke was detected in 46% of people by MRI and 10% by CT. In those scanned within 3 hours, 46% vs 7% (6 of 90) [[Chalela2007]].
- Cochrane found DWI sensitivity 0.99 (95% CI 0.23 to 1.00) and CT 0.39 (0.16 to 0.69), with specificity 0.92 and 1.00. It warned of narrow patient spectrum, small samples and incorporation bias [[Brazzelli2009]]. The very wide CI shows how uncertain this is.
- For acute haemorrhage MRI and CT were equivalent (96% concordance), and MRI found far more chronic haemorrhage (mostly microbleeds; 49 people) [[Kidwell2004]]. MRI was similar to CT for acute intracranial haemorrhage in the Chalela series too [[Chalela2007]].
- CT matched MRI for acute haemorrhage but was far less sensitive for acute ischaemia, so a normal CT does not rule out ischaemic stroke [[Chalela2007]].
- For posterior fossa infarcts, non-contrast CT sensitivity was 41.8% (95% CI 30.1 to 54.4), in 67 people with restricted diffusion on MRI [[Hwang2012]].
- An AAN guideline (since retired) rated DWI as more useful than non-contrast CT within 12 hours (Level A), adding that DWI is not perfectly sensitive [[Schellinger2010]].

### imaging · Early ischaemic signs and hyperdense artery
- Systematic review: mean sensitivity for early infarction signs 66% (range 20 to 87%), specificity 87% (56 to 100%). Interobserver kappa ranged from 0.14 to 0.78 [[Wardlaw2005]].
- Any early sign raised the risk of poor outcome (OR 3.11, 95% CI 2.77 to 3.49; 3468 people). Two studies found no evidence that thrombolysis in the presence of early signs worsened outcome beyond the effect of the signs themselves [[Wardlaw2005]].
- Experience improved detection; knowing the symptoms did not [[Wardlaw2005]].
- In 258 readers, being a neuroradiologist, slower reading, more pronounced signs and later CT all improved detection. Rating scales and slower reading are recommended to improve it [[Wardlaw2010]].
- Hyperdense MCA sign: pooled risk ratio for poor outcome after thrombolysis 1.56 (95% CI 1.50 to 1.62) in 11,818 people, with no significant effect on symptomatic haemorrhage [[Sun2020]].
- A second meta-analysis found RR 1.43 (1.30 to 1.57) after reperfusion therapy, but called its prognostic sensitivity and specificity poor [[Shi2022]].
- Thrombus length: in 138 people, every one of the 62 who recanalised with thrombolysis had a thrombus of 8 mm or less, and most thrombi in non-recanalising people exceeded 8 mm [[Riedel2011]].

### imaging · ASPECTS
- ASPECTS divides the MCA territory into 10 regions, with one point subtracted for each region showing early ischaemic change. 10 is a normal scan; 0 means diffuse involvement of the whole MCA territory [[Barber2000,Pexman2001]].
- The 10 regions are caudate (C), lentiform (L), internal capsule (IC), insular ribbon (I), and M1 to M6. M1 is anterior MCA cortex, M2 is cortex lateral to the insular ribbon, M3 is posterior MCA cortex. M4, M5 and M6 lie about 2 cm above M1, M2 and M3, rostral to the basal ganglia [[Pexman2001]].
- Dependence and death rise sharply at an ASPECTS of 7 or less [[Pexman2001]].
- Derivation in 156 people with anterior ischaemia treated with alteplase within 3 hours: ASPECTS correlated inversely with NIHSS (r=-0.56). Sensitivity and specificity were 0.78 and 0.96 for functional outcome, and 0.90 and 0.62 for symptomatic haemorrhage. Kappa 0.71 to 0.89 when the affected hemisphere was known [[Barber2000]].
- ASPECTS was not a hard stop in later trials. SELECT2 enrolled ASPECTS 3 to 5 or core of at least 50 mL: thrombectomy gave generalised OR 1.51 (95% CI 1.20 to 1.89), functional independence 20% vs 7% [[Sarraj2023]].
- RESCUE-Japan LIMIT, in a large ischaemic region: mRS 0 to 3 in 31.0% vs 12.7% (RR 2.43, 95% CI 1.35 to 4.37), any intracranial haemorrhage 58.0% vs 31.4% [[Yoshimura2022]].

### imaging · CT angiography, collaterals and multiphase CTA
- CT angiography clot burden score above 6 predicted good outcome with AUC 0.75, sensitivity 73.0, specificity 64.6, in 85 people. Higher collateral and clot burden scores (meaning less clot) went with smaller final infarcts [[Tan2009]].
- Multiphase CTA gives excellent interrater reliability (κ=0.81). It predicted outcome modestly but better than single-phase CTA or perfusion CT in model comparison, in 147 people [[Menon2015]].
- ESCAPE excluded a large infarct core or poor collaterals on CT and CTA. Functional independence 53.0% vs 29.3%; mortality 10.4% vs 19.0% [[Goyal2015]].
- Collateral grade and recanalisation: complete revascularisation in 14.1% with poor, 25.2% with good and 41.5% with excellent collaterals. In 222 people, revascularisation brought no observed benefit in those with poor collaterals [[Bang2011]].
- Clinical scales are not good enough to replace arterial imaging: a study of 13 scales concluded intracranial arterial imaging should be done in all people with acute ischaemic stroke within 6 hours [[Turc2016]].

### imaging · CT perfusion thresholds and mismatch trials
- Core: relative CBF performed best (AUC 0.79), with an optimal threshold of less than 31% of contralateral CBF, in 54 people. No single threshold avoids false positives in white matter, particularly with leukoaraiosis or poor bolus [[Campbell2011CBF]].
- Penumbra: Tmax above 6 s predicted salvage better than above 2 s. A threshold between 4 and 6 s appeared optimal, in 33 people imaged at 3 to 6 hours [[Olivot2009]].
- Working definitions in DEFUSE 3 software output: CBF below 30% of normal tissue for early infarct core, and perfusion delay above 6 s for hypoperfused tissue [[Albers2018]].
- DEFUSE 3 (6 to 16 hours) entry: ICA or proximal MCA occlusion, core below 70 mL, ratio of ischaemic tissue to core of 1.8 or more, penumbra volume of 15 mL or more [[Albers2018]].
- DEFUSE 3 result: mRS shift OR 2.77 (P<0.001), functional independence 45% vs 17%, mortality 14% vs 26% (P=0.05), symptomatic haemorrhage 7% vs 4% [[Albers2018]].
- DAWN (6 to 24 hours) selected on a mismatch of deficit and infarct, by age [[Nogueira2018]]. As quoted in a 2026 review: age 80 or over with NIHSS of 10 or more and core below 21 mL; age under 80 with NIHSS of 10 or more and core below 31 mL, or NIHSS of 20 or more and core below 51 mL [[Ordonez2026]].
- DAWN result: functional independence 49% vs 13% (adjusted difference 33 percentage points, credible interval 24 to 44). Symptomatic haemorrhage 6% vs 3%; mortality 19% vs 18% [[Nogueira2018]].
- EXTEND-IA used core below 70 mL plus salvageable tissue on CTP. Reperfusion at 24 hours was median 100% vs 37%, and functional independence 71% vs 40%. Stopped early at 70 people [[Campbell2015]].
- Thrombolysis on perfusion: EXTEND at 4.5 to 9 hours or on waking: mRS 0 to 1 in 35.4% vs 29.5% (adjusted RR 1.44, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.06), symptomatic haemorrhage 6.2% vs 0.9% [[Ma2019]].
- Pooled at 4.5 to 9 hours or wake-up: excellent outcome 36% vs 29% (adjusted OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.99); symptomatic haemorrhage 5% vs under 1% [[Campbell2019]].
- Visual estimation of core is imperfect: applying DAWN criteria to raters' visual estimates, rather than software measurements, changed eligibility in a study reviewed in [[AlAhmed2025]].

### imaging · DWI-FLAIR mismatch and unknown onset
- The idea: a lesion visible on DWI but not on FLAIR suggests onset within about 4.5 hours. In 543 people it identified that window with sensitivity 62% (95% CI 57 to 67), specificity 78% (72 to 84), PPV 83% (79 to 88), NPV 54% (48 to 60). Interobserver κ was 0.569 [[Thomalla2011]].
- WAKE-UP (unknown onset, DWI-FLAIR mismatch, thrombectomy planned excluded; stopped early at 503 of a planned 800): mRS 0 to 1 in 53.3% vs 41.8% (adjusted OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.09 to 2.36) [[Thomalla2018]].
- WAKE-UP median mRS 1 vs 2. Numerically more deaths (4.1% vs 1.2%, P=0.07) and symptomatic haemorrhage (2.0% vs 0.4%, P=0.15) [[Thomalla2018]].
- Pooled four trials with DWI-FLAIR or perfusion mismatch (843 people): favourable outcome 47% vs 39% (adjusted OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.03). Death 6% vs 3% (adjusted OR 2.06, 1.03 to 4.09). Symptomatic haemorrhage 3% vs under 1% (adjusted OR 5.58, 1.22 to 25.50) [[Thomalla2020]].

### imaging · Door-to-imaging and door-to-needle
- Registry benchmark: door-to-imaging of 25 minutes or less, used as the quality measure in Get With The Guidelines analyses [[Ekundayo2013,Lin2012]]. A survey of national guidelines found the USA and Qatar put weight on aiming for under 20 minutes [[Wijeratne2022]].
- Baseline: only 26.6% of 25,504 people treated within 3 hours had door-to-needle of 60 minutes or less (2003 to 2009), varying by hospital from 0% to 79.2%. In-hospital mortality was lower at 60 minutes or less (adjusted OR 0.78, 95% CI 0.69 to 0.90) [[Fonarow2011]].
- Target: Stroke phase I: median door-to-needle 77 to 67 minutes. Share within 60 minutes 26.5% to 41.3%, reaching 53.3% in the final quarter [[Fonarow2014]].
- Phase I outcomes: in-hospital mortality 9.93% vs 8.25% (adjusted OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.94); symptomatic haemorrhage 5.68% vs 4.68%; discharge home 37.6% vs 42.7% [[Fonarow2014]].
- Second survey (888 hospitals, 16,901 people): median door-to-needle 56 minutes, with 59.3% within 60 and 30.4% within 45 minutes. Direct transport to the scanner, premixing alteplase, starting it in the imaging suite and feedback to EMS were used less often [[Xian2017]].
- Phases I and II (154,221 people): median 78, then 66, then 50 minutes. Within 60 minutes: 26.4%, 42.7%, 68.6%. Within 45 minutes: 10.1%, 17.7%, 41.4% [[Xian2022]].
- Phase II vs preintervention: alteplase use 11.7% vs 5.6%, in-hospital mortality 6.0% vs 10.0% (adjusted OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.73), bleeding 3.4% vs 5.5%, discharge home 49.6% vs 35.7% [[Xian2022]].
- One-year outcomes in 61,426 people (median age 80): door-to-needle above 60 vs within 60 minutes: mortality 35.8% vs 32.1% (adjusted HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.14). Above 45 vs within 45 minutes: 35.0% vs 30.8% (adjusted HR 1.13, 1.09 to 1.18) [[Man2020JAMA]].
- Target: Stroke was associated with lower readmission at one year, but its mortality benefit dissipated after adjustment (adjusted HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.02) [[Man2020CQO]]. This is a useful caution for the in-hospital mortality figures above.
- Speed still matters when thrombectomy follows. Each 15 minutes of door-to-needle delay in people having both treatments went with higher odds of never returning home in a year (adjusted OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.19) [[Man2023]].
- Time to treatment: in 58,353 people, each 15 minutes faster was associated with lower in-hospital mortality (OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.95 to 0.98) and symptomatic haemorrhage (OR 0.96), and more independent walking at discharge (OR 1.04) [[Saver2013]].
- Trial meta-analysis: good outcome within 3 hours 32.9% vs 23.1% (OR 1.75, 95% CI 1.35 to 2.27); 3 to 4.5 hours 35.3% vs 30.1% (OR 1.26, 1.05 to 1.51); beyond 4.5 hours 32.6% vs 30.6% (OR 1.15, 0.95 to 1.40) [[Emberson2014]].
- Thrombectomy: mRS shift cOR 2.79 at 3 hours and 1.98 at 6 hours, significant through 7 hours 18 minutes in the early-window trials [[Saver2016]]. Pooled benefit across 5 trials: adjusted cOR 2.49 (95% CI 1.76 to 3.53), number needed to treat 2.6 for one level on the mRS [[Goyal2016]].
- Value of a minute: each minute saved on onset-to-treatment gave on average 1.8 days of healthy life (95% prediction interval 0.9 to 2.7) in a model [[Meretoja2014]].

### imaging · The Helsinki model
- Helsinki cut median door-to-needle from 105 minutes in 1998 to 60 in 2003 and 20 in 2011. In 2011, 31% of admitted people with ischaemic stroke had tPA, 94% of those within 60 minutes [[Meretoja2012]].
- Its central rule: do as little as possible after arrival and as much as possible before it, while the person is in transit. Angiography or perfusion imaging doubled in-hospital delay [[Meretoja2012]].
- Melbourne adopted it within 4 months: in-hours door-to-needle 43 to 25 minutes (p=0.009), with out-of-hours unchanged at 67 and 62 minutes [[Meretoja2013]].
- Christchurch: overall median 87 to 40 minutes, in-hours 34, door-to-CT 49 to 19 minutes, CT-to-needle 32 to 20 minutes [[Wu2018]].

### imaging · AI imaging (Early evidence)
- Cluster-randomised stepped-wedge trial (243 people): AI LVO detection with phone alerts cut door-to-groin by 11.2 minutes (95% CI -18.22 to -4.2). No difference in functional independence (OR 1.3, 0.42 to 4.0), and the trial was small [[MartinezGutierrez2023]].
- Observational hub-and-spoke study: CTA-to-team-notification 7 vs 26 minutes; door-to-puncture for transferred people 141 vs 185 minutes [[Elijovich2022]].
- England (observational, 2019 to 2023): thrombectomy rate 2.3% to 4.6% in 26 evaluation sites and 1.6% to 2.6% in others (interaction OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.43). Patient-level OR 1.57 (1.33 to 1.86). Funded through NHS England; not randomised [[Nagaratnam2025]].
- A 2020 systematic review of 20 machine learning studies could not compare algorithms because performance standards differed [[Murray2020]].

### imaging · Guidelines
- The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline replaces the 2018 guideline and its 2019 update. Its stated key updates cover thrombolytic choice and eligibility, thrombectomy eligibility, hyperglycaemia, dysphagia, paediatric stroke and thrombolysis contraindications [[Prabhakaran2026,Powers2019]].
- As reported in a 2026 review (not read in the guideline itself): AHA/ASA recommends thrombectomy to 16 hours (Class I) and considers it reasonable to 24 hours (Class IIa) in people meeting DAWN or DEFUSE 3 criteria [[Ordonez2026]].
- ESO-ESMINT 2019: high-quality evidence to recommend thrombectomy with best medical care within 6 hours for LVO, and moderate-quality evidence at 6 to 24 hours for people meeting trial criteria [[Turc2019ESO]].
- A recent review describes imaging strategy as evolving as treatment eligibility expands [[Yogendrakumar2026]].

### nihss · Origin and reliability
- Designed in 1989 as a 15-item examination for acute stroke trials. In 24 people interrater reliability averaged kappa 0.69. Test-retest kappa was 0.66 to 0.77 and did not differ among a neurologist, a resident, a neurology nurse and an emergency nurse [[Brott1989]].
- Validity in 65 people: correlation with infarct size at 1 week r=0.68 and with outcome at 3 months r=0.79 [[Brott1989]].
- The most reliable original item (pupillary response) had low validity, whereas less reliable motor items were more valid [[Brott1989]].
- Video training of 162 investigators gave moderate to excellent agreement on most items (unweighted kappa above 0.60). Facial paresis and ataxia had poor agreement (below 0.40) [[Lyden1994]].
- Non-neurologists can use it after training: intraclass correlation 0.93 and 0.95 for intraobserver and interobserver reliability [[Goldstein1997]].
- A 2025 animated certification system: overall intraclass correlation 0.979, while item kappas still ranged from 0.25 to 0.90 across the 15 items [[Anderson2025]].
- Factor analysis finds left and right brain factors, with a 4-factor fit separating cortical and motor function on each side [[Lyden1999,Lyden2004]].
- The modified NIHSS removes level of consciousness, face weakness, ataxia and dysarthria, and collapses sensory to 2 responses. Items with poor kappa fell from 8 to 3 [[Lyden2001]].
- Retrospective scoring from notes works in some settings. Agreement with prospective scores r²=0.94 [[Williams2000]] and R²=0.991 in severe stroke [[Lindsell2005]], but interrater agreement fell to 0.48 at a hospital without neurologist assessment, with 62% of items missing [[Bushnell2001]].

Teaching notes for item feedback:
- Facial palsy and ataxia: historically the hardest items to agree on (kappa below 0.40) [[Lyden1994]].
- Items with a high LVO signal: level of consciousness questions, gaze, motor leg and neglect independently predicted central occlusion [[Fischer2005]].
- Language adds points that right-sided signs do not. Left hemisphere strokes score higher for the same volume (see limitations) [[Woo1999,Lyden2004]].
- Motor: limb paresis explained most of the variance in quality of life at 90 days (17.5 to 24.1% of EQ-5D utility), far more than neglect (8.9%), aphasia (10.0%) or hemianopia (10.8%) [[vanderEnde2023]].
- Not on the scale: truncal ataxia, the commonest sign in people who scored 0 with an infarct on DWI [[MartinSchild2011]], and isolated hand or finger palsy [[Siniscalchi2018]].

### nihss · Severity bands
- A commonly used banding is minor 1 to 4, moderate 5 to 15, moderate to severe 16 to 20, severe 21 to 42 [[Mwamba2026]].
- These bands are a convention, not validated thresholds. The Medicare analysis below derived different categories from the data (0 to 7, 8 to 13, 14 to 21, 22 to 42) [[Fonarow2012]].
- "Mild" has no single definition. A 2015 analysis found about a third of people with mild stroke were not functionally independent at 90 days, irrespective of treatment or definition used, including NIHSS 0 to 5 [[Spokoyny2015]].
- PRISMS (minor nondisabling deficits, median NIHSS 2): favourable outcome 78.2% with alteplase vs 81.5% with aspirin (adjusted risk difference -1.1%, 95% CI -9.4 to 7.3), symptomatic haemorrhage 3.2% vs 0. Stopped very early, so not definitive [[Khatri2018]].

### nihss · NIHSS and outcome
- TOAST (1281 people): each extra point lowered the likelihood of an excellent outcome by 24% at 7 days and 17% at 3 months [[Adams1999]].
- TOAST: excellent outcome at 3 months in 46% with NIHSS 7 to 10 and 23% with 11 to 15. A score of 16 or more forecasts a high probability of death or severe disability; 6 or less forecasts a good recovery [[Adams1999]].
- Lacunar subtype was the only one predicting outcome independently of NIHSS (OR 3.1, 95% CI 1.5 to 6.4) [[Adams1999]].
- 30-day mortality in 33,102 Medicare beneficiaries: NIHSS 0 to 7, 4.2%; 8 to 13, 13.9%; 14 to 21, 31.6%; 22 to 42, 53.5%. NIHSS alone gave c-statistic 0.82 [[Fonarow2012]].
- Compared with other scales, NIHSS gave the most prognostic information: sensitivity to poor outcome 0.71, specificity 0.90, accuracy 0.83. A cut-point of 13 best predicted 3-month outcome [[Muir1996]].
- NIHSS explained 11.3 to 25.0% of variation in outcome models against 0.7 to 4.0% for a comorbidity index [[Rost2016]].
- In clinical practice NIHSS is useful for early prognosis and serial assessment [[Kasner2006]].
- 24-hour NIHSS and 90-day mRS gave the same trial conclusion in 61 of 73 trials (83.6%; κ=0.64) [[Rinkel2025]].

### nihss · NIHSS and LVO probability
- At a score of 10 or more, PPV for arterial occlusion was 97% in carotid and 96% in vertebrobasilar stroke. At 12 or more, PPV for central occlusion was 91%. These are selected people having arteriography, median NIHSS 14 [[Fischer2005]].
- In 2152 people, 48.5% had an occlusion. Best anterior cut-offs were 9 or more within 3 hours (PPV 86.4%) and 7 or more at 3 to 6 hours (PPV 84.4%) [[Heldner2013]].
- Only 5% of people with central occlusion within 3 hours had NIHSS under 4. In the posterior circulation, and after 6 hours, prediction was poor [[Heldner2013]].
- Median NIHSS with occlusion: 7 anterior, 3 posterior [[Heldner2013]].
- Disagreement on the usable threshold: 10 to 12 gives high PPV in selected cohorts [[Fischer2005]], while unselected data show that 11 or more misses over 25% of LVOs and that keeping misses at or below 10% requires a cut-off of 5 [[Turc2016]].

### nihss · Limitations
- Left hemisphere bias: for the same NIHSS band below 20, median right hemisphere infarct volume was about double the left. At NIHSS 16 to 20, median 133 mL right vs 48 mL left (P<0.001) [[Woo1999]].
- In large strokes, median NIHSS was 19 for left and 15 for right, with median volumes of 37.8 mL and 56.2 mL. Left-sided strokes scored about 4 points higher despite smaller volume [[Lyden2004]].
- With NIHSS 0 to 5, DWI volume was 8.8 cm³ right vs 3.2 cm³ left (P=0.04). Among lesions above the median of 9 cm³, 8 of 37 right-sided and 1 of 39 left-sided strokes scored 0 to 5 (P=0.01) [[Fink2002Stroke]].
- Posterior circulation: total baseline score is lower, and only ataxia and visual subscores are higher. Best cut-off for favourable outcome was 5 or less posterior vs 8 or less anterior [[Sato2008]].
- In 1569 people, median NIHSS was 2 posterior vs 7 anterior. 71% of those with posterior stroke scored 4 or less, and 15% of them had a poor outcome at 3 months [[Inoa2014]].
- The authors of that series argue thrombolysis should not be withheld on the NIHSS alone in posterior stroke [[Inoa2014]].
- A score of zero does not exclude stroke. 20 people in one series had NIHSS 0 with a DWI infarct, 0.76% of those admitted to the stroke service. Common symptoms were headache, vertigo and nausea, mostly posterior. 30% had a cardioembolic cause, which matters for prevention [[MartinSchild2011]].
- The e-NIHSS adds posterior elements while keeping the same total and item count. It raised posterior scores by an average of 2 points in early testing (Early evidence) [[Olivato2016]].
- Cranial nerves are not tested in detail, and the scale underestimates brainstem or cerebellar severity [[Budincevic2022]].

### nihss · Modified Rankin Scale
- mRS in the 1988 interobserver study has six grades, 0 to 5 [[vanSwieten1988]]. Modern usage is a 7-level scale that adds 6 for death [[Saver2021,Thomalla2018]]. It descends from Rankin's 1957 work [[Rankin1957]].
- Definitions, as listed in a 2026 clinical study [[Luo2026]]:

| mRS | Definition (verbatim from source) | Standard label |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | No symptoms at all | |
| 1 | No significant disability despite symptoms; able to carry out all usual duties and activities | Symptomatic but nondisabled; excellent [[Saver2021]] |
| 2 | Slight disability; unable to carry out all previous activities but able to look after own affairs without assistance | |
| 3 | Moderate disability; requires some help, but able to walk without assistance | |
| 4 | Moderately severe disability; unable to walk without assistance and unable to attend to own bodily needs without help | |
| 5 | Severe disability; bedridden, incontinent, and requiring constant nursing care and attention | Requires constant care; very poor [[Saver2021]] |
| 6 | Death | |

- The Stroke Therapy Academic Industry Roundtable recommends uniform names, because trials had used "a bewildering variety" of labels [[Saver2021]].
- Original agreement: same grade in 65 of 100 people, one grade apart in 32, two grades in 3. Kappa 0.56, weighted kappa 0.91 [[vanSwieten1988]].
- Reliability disagrees across studies. A systematic review found weighted kappa from 0.25 to 0.95, overall kappa 0.46 [[Quinn2009]]. Another synthesis reports moderate inter-rater reliability improved by structured interviews (kappa 0.56 vs 0.78) [[Banks2007]].
- Structured interview: agreement 57% vs 78%, weighted kappa 0.78 vs 0.93 [[Wilson2002]]. Across multiple raters: agreement 43% vs 81%, kappa 0.25 vs 0.74 [[Wilson2005]].
- A simplified questionnaire took 1.67 minutes on average, with kappa 0.72 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.86) [[Bruno2010]].
- mRS at 3 months or later is the preferred primary outcome for acute trials [[Lees2012]]. Comorbidities and socioeconomic factors affect how it should be read [[Banks2007]].
- Common dichotomies in the trials cited here: 0 to 1 (WAKE-UP, EXTEND), 0 to 2 (DAWN, DEFUSE 3, ESCAPE, EXTEND-IA), 0 to 3 (RESCUE-Japan LIMIT), and the ordinal shift across the scale [[Thomalla2018,Ma2019,Nogueira2018,Albers2018,Goyal2015,Campbell2015,Yoshimura2022]].

### Exam hooks
- FAST misses about 14% of ischaemic strokes; adding balance and eyes reduced this to 4.4% in a retrospective study [[Aroor2017]].
- CPSS items are arm, speech and face [[Kothari1999]].
- ROSIER positive is a score above 0, range -2 to +5 [[Nor2005]].
- RACE of 5 or more and LAMS of 4 or more are the published LVO thresholds [[PerezDeLaOssa2014,Nazliel2008]].
- No clinical scale reliably identifies LVO with both high sensitivity and high specificity [[Smith2018]].
- Roughly 9% of cerebrovascular events are missed at first emergency presentation, and dizziness carries a false-negative rate of 39.4% [[Tarnutzer2017]].
- Posterior stroke is misdiagnosed about twice as often as anterior stroke (37% vs 16%) [[Arch2016]].
- About 30% of people seen by a stroke team have a mimic [[Merino2013]].
- Symptomatic haemorrhage after thrombolysis in mimics is about 0.5 to 1% [[Tsivgoulis2015,Zinkstok2013]].
- Non-contrast CT has low sensitivity for early ischaemia: 26% vs 83% for MRI for any acute stroke [[Chalela2007]]. It is 41.8% in the posterior fossa [[Hwang2012]].
- ASPECTS: 10 regions, 10 is normal, subtract 1 per region; poor outcome rises sharply at 7 or less [[Pexman2001]].
- DEFUSE 3 criteria: core below 70 mL, mismatch ratio of 1.8 or more, penumbra of 15 mL or more; 6 to 16 hours [[Albers2018]].
- DWI-positive and FLAIR-negative suggests onset within about 4.5 hours [[Thomalla2011,Thomalla2018]].
- Helsinki principle: do as much as possible before arrival; median 20 minutes achieved [[Meretoja2012]].
- NIHSS: 15 elements, maximum 42. Left hemisphere bias and poor capture of posterior strokes are the two named weaknesses [[Budincevic2022,Woo1999,Sato2008]].
- NIHSS 16 or more forecasts death or severe disability; 6 or less forecasts good recovery [[Adams1999]].
- mRS 0 to 6, where 6 is death. Grade 3 walks unaided with some help; grade 4 cannot walk unaided [[Luo2026]].

### In plain words
- If a face droops, an arm is weak or speech goes wrong, even for a short time, treat it as an emergency and call an ambulance [[Harbison2003,Ekundayo2013]].
- Sudden loss of balance or a sudden change in sight can also be a stroke, even if the face, arm and speech seem fine [[Aroor2017]].
- People who arrive by ambulance tend to be scanned and treated faster than people who arrive another way [[Ekundayo2013]].
- Seeing your family doctor first can make it more likely you reach hospital late [[Fladt2019]].
- Women can have the same weakness and speech problems as men, and they are also more likely to have confusion or feel generally weak [[Shajahan2023]].
- Young people have strokes too, and these are more likely to be missed [[Kuruvilla2011,NewmanToker2014]].
- About 3 in 10 people checked for a suspected stroke turn out to have something else. It is still right to get checked quickly [[Merino2013]].
- A normal first CT scan does not rule out a stroke. CT shows bleeding well but can miss early stroke damage [[Chalela2007]].
- If you wake up with stroke symptoms, a scan may still help doctors judge whether treatment could help [[Thomalla2018]].
- The NIHSS is a set of short bedside checks. A higher number means a more severe stroke now. It is not a promise about the future [[Adams1999,Kasner2006]].
- A low score does not always mean a small stroke, especially with problems of balance or on the right side of the brain [[MartinSchild2011,Fink2002Stroke]].
- The mRS is a 0 to 6 scale of how much help a person needs in daily life [[Luo2026]].

## Open questions and controversies
- FAST or BE-FAST for the public: BE-FAST finds more strokes [[Aroor2017]] and more posterior strokes, at the cost of specificity (10.0% vs 39.8%) [[Tanglay2024]]; prospective data on public behaviour are lacking, and FAST campaigns themselves change behaviour little [[Lecouturier2010Media,Dombrowski2015]]. Unresolved.
- ROSIER or FAST: Cochrane prefers ROSIER in the emergency room [[Zhelev2019]]; an independent cohort found no difference and favoured the simpler FAST [[Whiteley2011]].
- Bypass to a thrombectomy centre: RACECAT found no outcome difference in nonurban Catalonia [[Perez2022]]; modelling suggests the answer depends on local times [[Holodinsky2018]]. Results may not transfer elsewhere.
- Mobile stroke units: consistent benefit in nonrandomised designs [[Grotta2021,Ebinger2021,Turc2022]]; cost and generalisation outside urban systems are outside the sources read here.
- Who should get MRI first: Chalela argues MRI should be preferred [[Chalela2007]]; Helsinki found that added vascular or perfusion imaging doubled in-hospital delay [[Meretoja2012]]; Cochrane questions the generalisability of the DWI data [[Brazzelli2009]].
- CTP thresholds: 30 to 31% rCBF and Tmax above 6 s are common working numbers [[Campbell2011CBF,Albers2018]], but no single threshold avoids false positive white matter regions [[Campbell2011CBF]]. Olivot's data support a range of 4 to 6 s [[Olivot2009]].
- Low ASPECTS: the original score identified people unlikely to recover independently despite thrombolysis [[Barber2000]]; recent thrombectomy trials in large core still show benefit, with low absolute rates of independence and more haemorrhage in one [[Sarraj2023,Yoshimura2022]].
- AI imaging: faster workflows are supported by one small cluster trial, with no proven functional gain [[MartinezGutierrez2023]]; national uptake data are observational [[Nagaratnam2025]].
- A better NIHSS: e-NIHSS is an early attempt to cover the posterior circulation [[Olivato2016]]; the original remains standard for comparability [[Lyden2001,Lees2012]].
