# A · Epidemiology, burden, outcomes and history of stroke

Pass date: 2026-11-08. Modules served: what, history.

## Findings

### what · Definitions: the clinical WHO definition
- WHO 1971 wording: "A sudden onset of disturbance of focal brain function due to the blockage or rupture of blood vessels" (the source prints "bran", a typographical error) [[Engelhardt2017]].
- WHO 1980 wording: "Rapidly developing clinical signs of focal (or global) disturbance of cerebral function, lasting more than 24 hours or leading to death, with no apparent cause other than that of vascular origin" [[Engelhardt2017]].
- The WHO work grew from community stroke registers. A pilot with 15 centres in 10 countries of Africa, Asia and Europe recorded 6395 new strokes from May 1971 to September 1974 [[Hatano1976]].
- The fuller collaborative report covered 17 centres in developing and developed countries from 1971 to 1974. Age-adjusted incidence showed moderate geographical variation, and hypertension control seemed insufficient in most countries [[Aho1980]].

### what · Definitions: the AHA/ASA 2013 tissue-based definition
- The 2013 AHA/ASA statement said the classic definition is mainly clinical and does not account for advances in science and technology. It combined clinical and tissue criteria [[Sacco2013]].
- CNS infarction is brain, spinal cord or retinal cell death attributable to ischaemia, based on neuropathological, neuroimaging and/or clinical evidence of permanent injury [[Sacco2013]].
- Ischaemic stroke is CNS infarction accompanied by overt symptoms. Silent infarction by definition causes no known symptoms [[Sacco2013]].
- Stroke also broadly includes intracerebral haemorrhage and subarachnoid haemorrhage [[Sacco2013]].
- Ischaemic stroke wording: "An episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal cerebral, spinal, or retinal infarction" [[Engelhardt2017]].
- Intracerebral haemorrhage wording: "Rapidly developing clinical signs of neurological dysfunction attributable to a focal collection of blood within the brain parenchyma or ventricular system that is not caused by trauma" [[Engelhardt2017]].
- Subarachnoid haemorrhage wording: "Rapidly developing signs of neurological dysfunction and/or headache because of bleeding into the subarachnoid space, which is not caused by trauma" [[Engelhardt2017]].
- Stroke not otherwise specified keeps the time rule: "An episode of acute neurological dysfunction presumed to be caused by ischemia or hemorrhage, persisting ≥ 24 hours or until death, but without sufficient evidence to be classified as one of the above" [[Engelhardt2017]].
- TIA has a matching tissue-based definition: "a transient episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal brain, spinal cord, or retinal ischemia, without acute infarction" [[Easton2009]].

### what · Ischaemic and haemorrhagic shares
- Global, GBD 2021, share of incident strokes: ischaemic 65.3% (95% UI 62.4-67.7), intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) 28.8% (28.3-28.8), subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH) 5.8% (5.7-6.0) [[Feigin2024]].
- Counts behind those shares in 2021: 7,804,000 ischaemic strokes, 3,444,000 ICH and 697,000 SAH [[Feigin2025]].
- High-income countries: ischaemic 74.9% (72.3-84.1), ICH 17.8% (17.3-17.9), SAH 7.3% (7.2-8.3) [[Feigin2024,Feigin2025]].
- All low-income and middle-income countries combined: ischaemic 63.4% (53.6-73.7), ICH 31.1% (30.2-31.3), SAH 5.5% (5.4-5.7) [[Feigin2024,Feigin2025]].
- ICH share is highest in low-income countries, at 36.9% (36.5-37.1) [[Feigin2024]].
- Disagreement on shares: GBD 2019 reported ischaemic 62.4%, ICH 27.9% and SAH 9.7% globally [[Feigin2021]]; GBD 2021 reported 65.3%, 28.8% and 5.8% [[Feigin2024]]. The SAH share fell by about 40% between two modelling rounds, so these are model revisions rather than a real change.
- US shares reported by the AHA: 87% ischaemic, 10% ICH, 3% SAH. These rest on Greater Cincinnati 1999 data in an unpublished NHLBI tabulation [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- Lifetime risk from age 25, global 2016: ischaemic 18.3%, haemorrhagic 8.2% [[Feigin2018]].

### what · Global burden 2021 (GBD)
- Stroke was the third most common GBD level 3 cause of death in 2021, after ischaemic heart disease and COVID-19: 7.3 million deaths (95% UI 6.6-7.8), 10.7% of all deaths (9.8-11.3) [[Feigin2024]].
- Stroke was the fourth most common cause of DALYs: 160.5 million (147.8-171.6), 5.6% of all DALYs (5.0-6.1) [[Feigin2024]].
- In 2021 there were 11.9 million incident strokes (10.7-13.2) and 93.8 million prevalent strokes (89.0-99.3) [[Feigin2024]].
- Age-standardised rates per 100,000 per year: incidence 142 (128-156), prevalence 1099 (1044-1162), deaths 87 (79-94), DALYs 1886 (1739-2018) [[Feigin2025]].
- Age-standardised rates by type, incidence then death per 100,000: ischaemic 92.4 and 44.2; ICH 40.8 and 39.1; SAH 8.3 and 4.2 [[Feigin2024]].
- Worldwide, 84.1% (77.8-88.8) of stroke DALYs in 2021 were attributed to the 23 risk factors analysed [[Feigin2024]].
- Risk clusters as a share of stroke burden: metabolic 68.8% (57.6-77.5), environmental 36.7% (29.0-44.2), behavioural 35.2% (26.9-44.7). They overlap, so they sum to more than 100% [[Feigin2025]].
- Largest single attributable risk: high systolic blood pressure, 56.8% (42.5-68.0) of stroke DALYs [[Feigin2025]].
- Risk factors whose attributable DALYs grew most from 1990 to 2021: high BMI 88.2% (53.4-117.7), high ambient temperature 72.4% (51.1-179.5), high fasting plasma glucose 32.1% (26.7-38.1) [[Feigin2024]].

### what · Trends: counts rise, rates fall
- 1990 to 2021, absolute counts rose: new strokes +70.2% (65.9-74.6), people living after stroke +86.1% (83.0-89.4), deaths +44.1% (32.3-56.0), DALYs +32.2% (21.7-42.7) [[Feigin2024]].
- Over the same period age-standardised rates fell: incidence -21.8% (-23.7 to -19.8), prevalence -8.5% (-9.7 to -7.3), deaths -39.4% (-44.0 to -34.6), DALYs -38.7% (-43.4 to -34.0) [[Feigin2024]].
- GBD 2021 found stagnation in the fall of incidence from 2015 onwards, with some increases in southeast Asia, east Asia and Oceania, in countries with lower SDI, and in people younger than 70 [[Feigin2024]].
- High-SDI countries had much larger falls than low-SDI countries in age-standardised deaths: -59.4% (-61.6 to -57.7) against -26.5% (-33.6 to -18.1) [[Feigin2024]].
- A 2009 review of 56 population-based studies found incidence fell 42% in high-income countries and more than doubled in low-income and middle-income countries over four decades. In 2000-08, incidence in low-income and middle-income countries exceeded high-income countries by 20% [[Feigin2009]].
- The WSO-Lancet Neurology Commission projected deaths rising 50%, from 6.6 million (6.0-7.1) in 2020 to 9.7 million (8.0-11.6) in 2050, and DALYs from 144.8 million (133.9-156.9) to 189.3 million (161.8-224.9) [[Feigin2023]].
- In the EU, a separate projection for 2017 to 2047 gave incident strokes +3%, prevalent cases +27%, deaths -17% and DALYs -33% [[Wafa2020Stroke]].
- US decline: mortality has been falling since the early 20th century, from both lower incidence and lower case fatality. An AHA/ASA statement judged hypertension control, started in the 1970s, to have had the most substantial influence [[Lackland2014]].
- US ARIC cohort 1987-2011 (14,357 people): stroke incidence fell (age-adjusted IRR per 10 years 0.76, 95% CI 0.66-0.87), but only at age 65 and over (0.69) and not under 65 (0.97, 0.76-1.25) [[Koton2014]].
- The US decline has stalled: the age-adjusted death rate rose 7.0% from 2012 to 2022 (36.9 to 39.5 per 100,000), and deaths rose 28.7% (128,546 to 165,393) [[Martin2025HDSS]].

### what · Regional and national variation
- Highest burden across age-standardised measures in 2021 was in east Asia, central Asia and sub-Saharan regions; lowest was in high-income North America, Australasia and Latin America [[Feigin2024]].
- Age-standardised incidence ranged from 57.7 per 100,000 (Luxembourg) to 355.0 (Solomon Islands) [[Feigin2024]].
- Age-standardised death rate ranged from 14.2 per 100,000 (Singapore) to 277.4 (North Macedonia) [[Feigin2024]].
- Age-standardised DALY rate ranged from 333.3 per 100,000 (Switzerland) to 6100.0 (Nauru) [[Feigin2024]].
- Age-standardised prevalence ranged from 521.5 per 100,000 (Cyprus) to 2045.8 (Ghana) [[Feigin2024]].
- Counts in 2021: East Asia 4,220,000 incident strokes and 2,664,000 deaths; South Asia 1,697,000 and 1,067,000; sub-Saharan Africa 867,000 and 484,000; Western Europe 717,000 and 326,000; high-income North America 466,000 and 209,000 [[Feigin2024]].
- Lower-income and lower-middle-income countries carried 87.2% of stroke deaths and 89.4% of stroke DALYs [[Feigin2025]].
- Lifetime risk varied by region: East Asia 38.8%, Central Europe 31.7%, Eastern Europe 31.6%, eastern sub-Saharan Africa 11.8% [[Feigin2018]].

### what · Lifetime risk
- Global lifetime risk of first stroke from age 25 in 2016 was 24.9% (95% UI 23.5-26.2), this is the "1 in 4" figure. Men 24.7% (23.3-26.0), women 25.1% (23.7-26.5) [[Feigin2018]].
- By SDI: high 23.5%, high-middle 31.1% (highest), low 13.2% (lowest) [[Feigin2018]].
- Global lifetime risk rose from 22.8% in 1990 to 24.9% in 2016, a relative rise of 8.9% (6.2-11.5). The calculation accounts for the competing risk of dying of other causes [[Feigin2018]].
- Low lifetime risk in poorer regions is not low danger: competing deaths shorten exposure, which is why the method matters [[Feigin2018]].
- Framingham (4897 people free of stroke at 55, up to 51 years of follow-up): lifetime risk about 1 in 5 for women and 1 in 6 for men, similar at ages 55, 65 and 75. Normal blood pressure carried about half the lifetime risk of high blood pressure [[Seshadri2006]].
- Disagreement: Framingham reports a clear female excess, attributed to longer life expectancy [[Seshadri2006]]; GBD 2016 reports almost no sex difference (25.1% against 24.7%) [[Feigin2018]]. Starting age and population differ.

### what · United States statistics
- The newest edition is the 2026 AHA report, which adds a chapter on cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome. Its abstract states no stroke numbers and its full text was not accessible, so the figures below are from the 2025 edition [[Palaniappan2026,Martin2025HDSS]].
- About 795,000 people have a new or recurrent stroke each year: about 610,000 first and 185,000 recurrent [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- An estimated 9.4 million Americans aged 20 or over self-report a stroke (NHANES 2017-2020), a prevalence of 3.3% [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- Stroke prevalence is projected to rise by 3.4 million adults by 2030, to 3.9% of adults, a 20.5% rise from 2012 [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- In 2022, 165,393 deaths had stroke as the underlying cause, about 1 in 20 US deaths, one every 3 minutes 11 seconds. Stroke ranked fifth among causes of death [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- About 65% of US stroke deaths occurred outside an acute care hospital [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- CDC reports the stroke death rate fell from 39.5 to 39.0 per 100,000 between 2022 and 2023, and someone has a stroke every 40 seconds [[CDC-StrokeFacts]].
- Caveat: the 795,000, 610,000 and 185,000 figures and the 87/10/3 split trace back to 1999 Greater Cincinnati data in unpublished NHLBI estimates, compiled in 2008 [[Martin2025HDSS]].

### what · Stroke in younger people
- GBD 2021, people aged 15-49: 1,762,000 incident strokes (about 15% of all), 20,319,000 prevalent (22%), 373,000 deaths (about 5%), 21,367,000 DALYs (13%) [[Feigin2025]].
- GBD 2021, people under 70: 6,307,000 incident strokes (about 53%), 61,049,000 prevalent (65%), 2,409,000 deaths (33%), 88,143,000 DALYs (55%) [[Feigin2025]].
- By type, 15-49 year olds make up over 12% of ischaemic strokes, about 18% of ICH and over 34% of SAH [[Feigin2025]].
- From 1990 to 2021 in people under 70, all-age incidence rose 4.1% (0.9-7.6) and prevalence 14.8% (13.1-16.8), while death and DALY rates fell 17.4% and 19.0% [[Feigin2024]].
- Disagreement between GBD rounds: for 1990-2019, GBD 2019 reported incidence up 15% (12-18) and prevalence up 22% (21-24) under 70 [[Feigin2021]]; GBD 2021 reports 4.1% and 14.8% for 1990-2021 [[Feigin2024]].
- Meta-analysis of 50 studies in 20 high-income countries: every study with data showed a less favourable trend under 55 than at 55 and over (pooled relative temporal rate ratio 1.57, 95% CI 1.42-1.74). It was highest in population-based studies whose latest period began after 2010 (1.87, 1.55-2.27) (Established) [[Scott2022]].
- That review also found absolute incidence trends in young people were heterogeneous. The firm finding is divergence by age, not a universal rise (Established) [[Scott2022]].
- Oxford Vascular Study, 2429 strokes, 2002-2010 against 2010-2018: incidence rose under 55 (IRR 1.67, 1.31-2.14) and fell at 55 and over (0.85, 0.78-0.92). The rise was independent of sex, severity, subtype and changes in investigation (Established) [[Li2022]].
- In the same study the rise was also seen for TIA (1.87) but not for myocardial infarction and other major vascular events (0.73). It persisted in people without diabetes, hypertension, smoking or obesity, so its cause is unexplained (Hypothesis) [[Li2022]].
- US National Inpatient Sample: ischaemic stroke hospitalisation almost doubled in men aged 18-34 and 35-44 since 1995-96. Having 3 to 5 risk factors rose from 19% to 35% of men aged 35-44 between 2003-04 and 2011-12 [[George2017]].
- Disagreement on mechanism: George reports rising traditional risk factors as the likely driver [[George2017]]; Li reports the rise persisting in people without those factors and greatest in professional or managerial occupations (IRR 2.52) [[Li2022]].
- Greater Cincinnati: incidence per 100,000 person-years in ages 20-54 rose 1993-94 to 2005 from 83 to 128 in Black adults and from 26 to 48 in White adults. Strokes under 55 rose from 12.9% to 18.6% of all strokes [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- Denmark, ages 15-30: first hospitalisation for stroke rose from 11.97 to 16.77 per 100,000 person-years between 1994 and 2012 (annual change 1.83%) [[Tibaek2016]].
- Young adults with ischaemic stroke have long life expectancy and are usually left out of trials, so optimal management is unknown [[Ekker2018]]. Causes are more varied than in older adults [[Boot2020]].
- Dutch FUTURE cohort, age 18-50: 20-year mortality among 30-day survivors was 26.8% (21.9-31.8) after ischaemic stroke and 13.7% (3.6-23.9) after ICH. Mortality was 3.9 times expected for both types [[RuttenJacobs2013]].
- In high-income countries, case fatality in people up to age 65 has fallen (RR 0.74, 0.68-0.81, about 3.2% a year), but 1-year recurrence after 2000 was unchanged (RR 1.04, 0.78-1.39) [[Nehme2026]].

### what · Sex differences
- GBD 2021 share by sex (male then female): incident strokes 52.6% and 47.4%, prevalent 51.0% and 49.0%, deaths 52.1% and 47.9%, DALYs 55.0% and 45.0% [[Feigin2024]].
- Age-adjusted incidence per 100,000: men 160 (144-178), women 125 (113-137). Age-adjusted death rate: men 103 (93-113), women 75 (66-82) [[Feigin2025]].
- SAH is the exception: about 51% of incident SAH occur in women [[Feigin2025]]. Pooled SAH risk ratio for women is 1.3 (0.98-1.7), and it was 2.5 (1.8-3.4) in Japanese women over 75 against men aged 45-54 [[Etminan2019]].
- Review of 98 articles: mean age at first stroke 68.6 years in men and 72.9 in women. Male incidence was 33% higher and prevalence 41% higher. One-month case fatality was 24.7% in women and 19.7% in men [[Appelros2009]].
- Age-specific rates are higher in men, but women have more stroke events because they live longer and have much higher incidence at older ages [[Reeves2008]].
- Disagreement on who bears more strokes: Reeves says women have more events overall [[Reeves2008]]; GBD 2021 gives 47.4% of incident strokes in females globally [[Feigin2024]]. The first reflects older, high-income populations.
- In the US, females accounted for 56.6% of stroke deaths in 2022, against 47.9% globally [[Martin2025HDSS,Feigin2024]].
- Ontario, 9.2 million adults: females had a lower overall hazard of stroke or TIA (HR 0.82), except SAH (1.29). Hazard was higher in females at age 30 or under (1.26) and lowest at 50-69 (0.69) [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- Crude mortality is worse in women, adjusted mortality is better. INSTRUCT (16,957 people): crude rate ratio 1.35 at 1 year and 1.24 at 5 years; adjusted 0.81 and 0.76. Age, prestroke function, severity and atrial fibrillation explained the reversal [[Phan2017]].
- Pooled trials (19,652 people, 40% women): women with ischaemic stroke had higher survival at 3-6 months (OR 0.82), more disability (OR 1.20) and worse quality of life [[Carcel2019]].
- Women are more likely to present with non-traditional symptoms and stroke mimics and can be misdiagnosed. There was no substantial difference in thrombolysis or thrombectomy outcome [[Bushnell2018]].
- Sex and sociocultural gender both shape risk factors, assessment, treatment and outcomes, including female-specific risk factors [[Rexrode2022]].

### what · Survival by time
- ARIC (US, 1987-2011): cumulative all-cause mortality after a first stroke was 10.5% at 30 days, 21.2% at 1 year, 39.8% at 5 years and 58.4% at 24 years [[Martin2025HDSS,Koton2014]].
- ARIC mortality to end of follow-up was higher after haemorrhagic (68%) than ischaemic stroke (57%) [[Koton2014]].
- Perth (Australia, 1989-90): risk of death in the first year was 36.5% (31.5-41.4), 10 times expected. By 5 years 58% had died, about 10% a year after year 1, twice expected [[Hankey2000]].
- Perth at 10 years: 79% (74-84) had died. Average annual case fatality in 1-year survivors was 4.8%, 2.3 times the general population [[Hardie2003]].
- Disagreement: Perth's older cohort shows much higher 1-year (36.5%) and 5-year (58%) mortality [[Hankey2000]] than ARIC's 21.2% and 39.8% [[Martin2025HDSS]]. Era, age and case mix differ; ARIC participants were recruited as middle-aged adults.
- Greater Cincinnati 5-year mortality, adjusted, 1993-94 to 2015: ischaemic stroke 53% to 48.3% (p=0.009); ICH 64.4% to 69.2%, no significant change (p=0.32) [[Robinson2024]].
- Sweden (Riksstroke, 22,929 people): 30-day mortality was 11.1% for ischaemic stroke and 30.7% for ICH. Among 30-day survivors, 5-year mortality did not differ [[Sennfalt2019]].
- ICH pooled survival: 46% (43-49) at 1 year and 29% (26-33) at 5 years [[Poon2014]].
- ICH 1-month case fatality: median 40.4% (range 13.1-61.0) in studies to 2008 [[vanAsch2010]]; pooled 35.5% (32.3-38.9) in studies 2008-2023, with no time trend [[Wolsink2025]].
- ICH incidence: 24.6 per 100,000 person-years (19.7-30.7) [[vanAsch2010]]; 29.2 (23.3-36.4) in the update [[Wolsink2025]]. Good functional outcome after 3-12 months was 31.2% (24.7-38.6) [[Wolsink2025]].
- SAH: crude incidence 7.9 per 100,000 person-years (6.9-9.0), falling from 10.2 in 1980 to 6.1 in 2010 [[Etminan2019]]. Case fatality fell 17% between 1973 and 2002 while mean age rose from 52 to 62 years [[Nieuwkamp2009]].
- South London, first ischaemic stroke 2000-2015: adjusted mortality fell 24%, and 30-day and 1-year case fatality fell 38% and 37% [[Wafa2020PLoSMed]].

### what · Disability and dementia
- Oxford Vascular Study (748 strokes): disabled (mRS above 2) rose from 21% before stroke to 43% of survivors at 1 month. At 5 years 39% of survivors were disabled [[LuengoFernandez2013]].
- At 5 years, 70% of people with stroke and 48% of people with TIA were dead or disabled. Five-year risk of care home placement was 19% after stroke and 11% after TIA [[LuengoFernandez2013]].
- Sweden at 5 years: death or dependency (mRS 3 or above) in 70.6% after ischaemic stroke and 79% after ICH [[Sennfalt2019]].
- Perth at 10 years in 30-day survivors: death or new institutionalisation 79% (73-85), death or new disability 87% (81-92) [[Hardie2004]].
- Dementia: about 10% had dementia before a first stroke, 10% developed it soon after a first stroke, and more than a third had it after recurrent stroke [[Pendlebury2009]].

### what · Recurrence by time
- Pooled cumulative recurrence (13 studies, 9115 survivors): 3.1% (1.7-4.4) at 30 days, 11.1% (9.0-13.3) at 1 year, 26.4% (20.1-32.8) at 5 years, 39.2% (27.2-51.2) at 10 years. Heterogeneity was substantial [[Mohan2011]].
- South London (2874 people): 7.1% at 1 year, 16.2% at 5 years, 24.5% at 10 years. Atrial fibrillation predicted recurrence at all three times [[Mohan2009]].
- Perth: 43% (34-51) at 10 years, about 4% a year after year 1. Thirty-day case fatality was 41% after first recurrence and 22% after first-ever stroke [[Hardie2004]].
- Rotterdam (1701 people): 18.0% (16.2-19.8) at 10 years overall, falling from 21.4% (1990-2000) to 11.0% (2010-2020) [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- Disagreement: 10-year figures range from 18.0% [[Martin2025HDSS]] and 24.5% [[Mohan2009]] to 39.2% [[Mohan2011]] and 43% [[Hardie2004]]. Era, definitions and competing deaths explain part of this.
- Time trend: pooled 5-year risk fell from 32% to 16.2% across studies [[Mohan2011]]. In South London it fell from 18% (1995-1999) to 12% (2000-2005), with no improvement since [[Flach2020]].

### what · Cost
- Global direct and indirect cost is estimated at over US$891 billion a year: $315 billion in health care and $576 billion in lost income, in 2017 [[Feigin2023]].
- WSO states over US$890 billion, 0.66% of global GDP, projected almost to double by 2050 [[Feigin2025]].
- Europe, 32 countries, 2017: EUR 60 billion in total. Health care EUR 27 billion (45%, 1.7% of health spending), social care EUR 5 billion, productivity losses EUR 12 billion, informal care EUR 16 billion (1.3 billion hours) [[LuengoFernandez2020]].
- Care cost per citizen in Europe varied from EUR 11 in Bulgaria to EUR 140 in Finland, average EUR 59 [[LuengoFernandez2020]].
- US, 2020-2021 annual average: direct medical cost of ischaemic stroke and TIA $25.0 billion, indirect cost of stroke mortality $24.0 billion, mean direct expense $7034 per patient [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- CDC reports nearly $56.2 billion in stroke-related cost between 2019 and 2020 [[CDC-StrokeFacts]].
- Per-patient figures, 46 studies: highest yearly cost in the US ($59,900), Sweden ($52,725) and Spain ($41,950). Highest lifetime cost in Australia ($232,100). Methods were too heterogeneous for comparison, and few studies came from South America or Africa [[Strilciuc2021]].
- Oxford: average 5-year cost per person placed in institutional care was $125,359 (SD 91,121) after stroke [[LuengoFernandez2013]].

### what · World Stroke Organization and policy
- The WSO Global Stroke Fact Sheet 2025 draws mainly on GBD 2021. Its statistics were approved by the WSO Executive Committee and GBD leaders, and it plans updates every 1-2 years [[Feigin2025]].
- WSO counts stroke as the second leading cause of death among non-communicable disorders [[Feigin2025]]. GBD 2021 ranks it third among all causes because COVID-19 ranked second [[Feigin2024]].
- The WSO-Lancet Neurology Commission built its plan on four pillars: surveillance, prevention, acute care and rehabilitation [[Feigin2023]].
- The Commission warned that on these trends the Sustainable Development Goal 3.4 target will not be met [[Feigin2023]].
- European Action Plan targets for 2030: 10% fewer strokes in absolute number, 90% or more of patients treated in a stroke unit as first level of care, national plans covering the whole chain of care, and full national public health strategies [[Norrving2018]].
- In 2023 data from 52 European nations, 43.5% of nations had a national stroke plan and inequity persisted [[Christensen2026]].

### what · Disparities by ancestry and income
- REGARDS (27,744 US adults aged 45 or over, 40.4% Black, 460 strokes): age-sex-adjusted Black to White incidence rate ratio 1.51 (1.26-1.81). It was 4.02 (1.23-13.11) at 45-54 and 0.86 (0.33-2.20) at 85 and over [[HowardVJ2011]].
- REGARDS mediation (25,714 people): relative risk 2.90 at age 45 and 1.66 at 65. Traditional risk factors explained 40-45% of the excess, about half of that through systolic blood pressure. Adding socioeconomic factors explained 47-53% [[HowardG2011]].
- About half of the excess remained unexplained, leaving differential impact of risk factors, residual confounding or nontraditional factors as possibilities (Hypothesis) [[HowardG2011]].
- REGARDS (29,681 people): the Black to White mortality hazard ratio was about 4.0 at 45, falling to about 1.0 at 85. It was driven by incidence, not 30-day case fatality [[Howard2016]].
- Deaths from stroke fell 75% over 54 years, with virtually no reduction in the relative Black to White disparity or the Stroke Belt excess [[Howard2024]].
- 2022 US age-adjusted death rates per 100,000: Black 57.2, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander 52.7, White 38.3, Hispanic 35.3, Asian 30.3, American Indian or Alaska Native 30.3 [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- Stroke Belt mortality has historically been about 30% higher than the rest of the US, and the Stroke Buckle about 40% higher [[Martin2025HDSS]].
- Disagreement on geography: REGARDS incidence ratios for the Belt (1.06, 0.87-1.29) and Buckle (1.19, 0.96-1.47) did not reach significance [[HowardVJ2011]], while mortality differences are long established [[Howard2024]].
- Greater Cincinnati per 100,000, 1993-94 to 2015: White 215 to 170, Black 349 to 311. Disparities did not narrow in 22 years, especially in younger adults [[Madsen2024]].
- Long-term survival: Black patients had higher 5-year mortality after ischaemic stroke (OR 1.23, 1.08-1.39), unchanged over time [[Robinson2024]].
- Disagreement on case fatality: REGARDS finds no corresponding disparity at 30 days [[Howard2016]], while Greater Cincinnati finds a 5-year survival gap [[Robinson2024]]. They measure different time horizons.
- Mexican Americans in Corpus Christi had higher ischaemic stroke incidence than non-Hispanic White residents at 45-59 (RR 2.04, 1.55-2.69) and 60-74 (1.58), and more ICH (1.63) [[Morgenstern2004]].
- Northern Manhattan: incidence per 1000 person-years was 13 in Black, 10 in Hispanic and 9 in White participants. The Hispanic excess (HR 1.48) was largely explained by education and insurance (HR 1.17, 0.85-1.62) [[Gardener2020]].
- Income: stroke mortality and DALY rates were 3.6 (3.5-3.8) and 3.7 (3.5-3.9) times higher in World Bank low-income than high-income countries in 2019 [[Feigin2021]].
- Within and between populations, higher incidence and mortality are generally seen in lower socioeconomic groups, who are less likely to receive evidence-based services, though results are inconsistent [[Addo2012]].
- Almost half of stroke deaths were attributable to poorly managed modifiable risk factors in a GBD 2017 ecological analysis [[Avan2019]].
- Race in these studies is a social and recorded category; Greater Cincinnati took it from the medical record, and socioeconomic adjustment changed several estimates [[Robinson2024,HowardG2011,Gardener2020]].

### what · Israel
- GBD 2021 estimates for Israel: 8000 incident strokes (7000-9000), 3000 deaths (2000-3000), 92,000 prevalent cases (89,000-95,000), 54,000 DALYs (48,000-59,000). Age-standardised death rate fell 67.2% from 1990 to 2021 [[Feigin2024]].
- Israeli National Stroke Registry 2014-2017: average annual incidence of cerebrovascular events (stroke or TIA) was 3.2 per 1000. Age-adjusted rates were significantly higher in Arab than in Jewish people [[Ram2019]].
- Disagreement: a NASIS-based paper states around 18,000 strokes annually [[Simaan2021]], against GBD's 8000 [[Feigin2024]]. The registry counts include TIA in at least some reports, and modelled and hospital counts differ; this was not reconciled.
- National survey in all 28 admitting hospitals (Feb-March 2004, 2174 admissions): 7% of neurology admissions were to a designated stroke unit, thrombolysis was 0.5%, and 3-month mortality was 14% for ischaemic stroke and 34% for ICH [[Tanne2006]].
- By 2016-2017, t-PA use had stabilised at 9.2%, arrival by ambulance reached 49.0%, and vascular imaging 72.5% [[Ram2019]].
- NASIS-REVASC (1432 revascularised patients in 2014-2016): Arab patients were younger (66 against 73) with more smoking (31% against 18%) and diabetes (57% against 34%). They received less systemic thrombolysis (48% against 59%) but similar overall treatment and outcomes [[Simaan2021]].
- Single-centre Galilee data (499 patients, moderate or greater vessel occlusion, 2023-2025): in-hospital mortality was 11.1% in Jewish and 4.8% in Arab patients, with Jewish patients older. Adjusted OR 2.05 (0.97-4.34). The authors call this exploratory (Early evidence) [[Ryder2026]].
- Small exploratory work suggests Arab subgroups differ in stroke patterns, for example large vessel stroke in 30% of Christian and 9.9% of Druze patients (Early evidence) [[Ryder2024]].
- First ischaemic stroke, 41,639 patients 2014-2018: 44.5% had diabetes, linked to higher mortality in the first year and long term but not the first month [[Zamir2021]].
- NASIS 2016, 3-month outcome in survivors: mRS 0-1 in 43%, 2-3 in 29%, 4-5 in 28% [[Miaary2019]].
- Stroke was described as the third commonest cause of death in Israel in 2008, with mortality falling [[Koton2008]].

### history · Antiquity to the Renaissance: apoplexy
- "Apoplexia" means struck down with violence. The picture was known to Greek physicians at least 2500 years ago [[Engelhardt2017]].
- The first recorded appearance of the term is in the Hippocratic Corpus. Aphorisms II-42: "It is impossible to remove a strong attack of apoplexy, and not easy to remove a weak attack" [[Engelhardt2017]].
- Galen: "Apoplexy is a privation of sense and motion in all the nerves" [[Engelhardt2017]].
- Understanding rested on Galen and humoral pathology for centuries, and Islamic scholars expanded the knowledge and addressed contradictions [[Yilmaz2025]]. Medieval authors added no novel concepts, by another account [[Engelhardt2017]].
- Apoplexy was an umbrella term covering non-vascular and even non-cerebral events such as myocardial infarction and pulmonary embolism [[Engelhardt2017]].

### history · The 17th century: Wepfer and Willis
- Johann Jakob Wepfer (1658) distinguished non-traumatic intracranial bleeding, including subarachnoid and cerebral haemorrhage, splitting the unitary concept for the first time [[Engelhardt2017]].
- Wepfer is credited with showing apoplexy results from brain haemorrhage or vessel occlusion [[Karenberg1998]].
- Caveat: a re-reading of his text shows his thinking stayed rooted in Galenic theory and iatrochemistry, so "first modern" overstates it [[Karenberg1998]].
- Thomas Willis published Cerebri anatome in London (350th anniversary in 2014, so 1664). He explained the pathological and functional meaning of the circular arterial anastomosis named after him, and coined "neurologie" [[ArraezAybar2015]].
- William Cole first used "stroke" for apoplexy in English medical writing in 1689 [[Engelhardt2017]].

### history · 18th and 19th centuries: softening, thrombosis, embolism
- Morgagni (1761) separated "sanguineous", "serous" and "neither" apoplexy [[Engelhardt2017]].
- Rochoux (1814) and Rostan (1819) defined haemorrhagic apoplexy and brain softening, the basic haemorrhagic and ischaemic division still used [[Engelhardt2017]].
- Dechambre introduced the word "lacune" in 1838, well before Fisher [[Engelhardt2017]].
- Rudolf Virchow (1852) created the terms "thrombosis" and "embolism" and described embolic "apoplexia ischaemica" [[Engelhardt2017,Bagot2008]].
- Caveat: review of the historical literature casts doubt on a triad described by Virchow in its usual form. He was routinely credited with it only 100 years after his work [[Bagot2008]].
- Cohnheim (1872) introduced "infarct" after embolic obstruction of end arteries [[Engelhardt2017]].
- "Apoplexy" left the international death lists from the 5th revision (1938). "Stroke" appeared in ICD-9 [[Engelhardt2017]].

### history · 20th century: seeing the vessels and the brain
- Egas Moniz showed the first cerebral angiogram on 28 June 1927, of a pituitary tumour in a young man, and presented it in Paris on 7 July 1927 [[Artico2017]].
- His 1949 Nobel Prize was for prefrontal leucotomy, now unacceptable, not for angiography [[Artico2017]].
- Moniz described the first carotid occlusion in 1936, and some features of carotid dissection [[Oliveira2018]].
- Carotid occlusions were thoroughly described in English only with Miller Fisher's 1951 paper [[Oliveira2018,Fisher1951]].
- Fisher's 1965 paper described lacunes as small deep cerebral infarcts [[Fisher1965]]. Through serial sections at necropsy he linked them to penetrating artery disease, naming lipohyalinosis and fibrinoid degeneration (a 1968 Fisher paper is the cited source for these terms) [[Caplan2015]].
- The first CT scan of a live patient was on 1 October 1971 at Atkinson Morley Hospital, under James Ambrose, in a woman with a suspected frontal tumour, later a cystic astrocytoma [[Schulz2021]].
- Godfrey Hounsfield of EMI built the system, and Allan Cormack, with earlier reconstruction work, shared the Nobel Prize with him [[Schulz2021]]. The 1973 papers describe the system and clinical use [[Hounsfield1973,Ambrose1973]].
- Dates differ: one review says CT entered clinical practice in 1975 [[Artico2017]]; the first patient scan was 1971 [[Schulz2021]].

### history · The penumbra
- The penumbra was discovered in four experiments in September 1975, in Lindsay Symon's London laboratory, where Symon first said the word [[Astrup2025]].
- Astrup 1977, baboon cortex: electrical failure below about 15 ml/100 g per minute (about 35% of control), and massive potassium release at a lower flow, median 6 ml/100 g per minute (the abstract writes the limits as 18 and 2). This established a dual threshold [[Astrup1977]].
- The term was set out in Astrup, Siesjö and Symon, 1981 [[Astrup1981]].
- The perfusion range between the two thresholds is the penumbra; flow restored above the functional threshold can reverse deficits without permanent damage [[Heiss2011]].
- The lower the flow, the shorter the time for useful reperfusion, so infarction spreads from the core outward [[Heiss2011]].

### history · Thrombolysis
- The development of thrombolysis is traced to the 1930s [[Yilmaz2025]].
- Early trials were held back by high rates of ICH [[NINDS1995]].
- NINDS 1995 (624 patients, t-PA within 3 hours): global OR for favourable outcome 1.7 (1.2-2.6). Patients were at least 30% more likely to have minimal or no disability at 3 months [[NINDS1995]].
- NINDS symptomatic ICH was 6.4% against 0.6%. Mortality at 3 months was 17% against 21% (P=0.30) [[NINDS1995]].
- ECASS (1995, 620 patients, 1.1 mg/kg, within 6 hours) had no difference in primary end points in the intention-to-treat analysis, and its authors could not recommend use in an unselected population [[Hacke1995]].
- ECASS III (2008, 821 patients, 0.9 mg/kg, 3 to 4.5 hours): favourable outcome 52.4% against 45.2% (OR 1.34, 1.02-1.76), symptomatic ICH 2.4% against 0.2% [[Hacke2008]].
- Meta-analysis of 6756 patients: within 3 hours OR 1.75 (32.9% against 23.1%); 3 to 4.5 hours OR 1.26 (35.3% against 30.1%); beyond 4.5 hours OR 1.15 (0.95-1.40) [[Emberson2014]].
- "Time is brain" quantified: a typical large vessel stroke loses 1.9 million neurons, 14 billion synapses and 12 km of myelinated fibres each minute [[Saver2006]].

### history · Stroke units
- Langhorne 1993 (10 trials, 1586 patients): odds of death within 4 months 0.72 (0.56-0.92), a 28% reduction, and 0.79 (0.63-0.99) at 12 months. This overturned the view that units did not affect mortality [[Langhorne1993]].
- Cochrane 2020 (29 trials, 5902 people), compared with an alternative service: poor outcome OR 0.77 (0.69-0.87), death OR 0.76 (0.66-0.88) at a median of 1 year. Benefits did not depend on age, sex, severity or stroke type [[Langhorne2020]].

### history · Thrombectomy: failure, then the 2015 wave
- In 2013 three NEJM trials were neutral: IMS III (656 patients; 40.8% against 38.7%, stopped for futility) [[Broderick2013]], SYNTHESIS (362 patients; 30.4% against 34.8%) [[Ciccone2013]] and MR RESCUE (118 patients; mRS 3.9 against 3.9) [[Kidwell2013]].
- MR CLEAN (2015, 500 patients at 16 Dutch centres, proximal anterior occlusion, within 6 hours): adjusted common OR 1.67 (1.21-2.30). Functional independence 32.6% against 19.1%. Retrievable stents were used in 81.5% of the intervention group [[Berkhemer2015]].
- ESCAPE (316 patients): functional independence 53.0% against 29.3%, mortality 10.4% against 19.0% [[Goyal2015]].
- EXTEND IA (70 patients): functional independence 71% against 40% [[Campbell2015]].
- SWIFT PRIME (196 patients): functional independence 60% against 35% [[Saver2015]].
- REVASCAT (206 patients): functional independence 43.7% against 28.2% [[Jovin2015]].
- HERMES pooled the five (1287 patients): adjusted common OR 2.49 (1.76-3.53), number needed to treat 2.6 to reduce disability by at least one mRS level. No heterogeneity across subgroups, including age 80 or over [[Goyal2016]].
- DAWN (2018, 206 patients, 6 to 24 hours, clinical-core mismatch): functional independence 49% against 13% [[Nogueira2018]].
- DEFUSE 3 (2018, 182 patients, 6 to 16 hours, perfusion selection): functional independence 45% against 17%, mortality 14% against 26% (P=0.05) [[Albers2018]].
- These two trials moved selection from clock time toward tissue, the clinical use of the penumbra idea [[Yilmaz2025,Heiss2011]].

### Exam hooks
- Classic WHO definition: focal (or global) deficit lasting more than 24 hours or leading to death, with no apparent cause other than vascular [[Engelhardt2017]].
- AHA/ASA 2013: imaging or pathology evidence of infarction counts, even if symptoms are brief; silent infarction has no known symptoms [[Sacco2013]].
- TIA is defined by absence of acute infarction, not by a 24-hour clock [[Easton2009]].
- Global shares: about 65% ischaemic, 29% ICH, 6% SAH [[Feigin2024]].
- GBD 2021: 11.9 million new strokes, 93.8 million living after stroke, 7.3 million deaths, 160.5 million DALYs [[Feigin2024]].
- Lifetime risk from age 25: about 25%, or 1 in 4 [[Feigin2018]].
- About 90% of burden sits in lower-income and lower-middle-income countries (87.2% of deaths, 89.4% of DALYs) [[Feigin2025]].
- Counts up, rates down: new strokes +70.2%, age-standardised incidence -21.8%, 1990 to 2021 [[Feigin2024]].
- Biggest single risk: high systolic blood pressure, 56.8% of stroke DALYs [[Feigin2025]].
- SAH is the type where women are not in the minority [[Feigin2025,Etminan2019]].
- Pooled recurrence: 11.1% at 1 year, 26.4% at 5 years, 39.2% at 10 years [[Mohan2011]].
- ICH survival: less than half at 1 year (46%), less than a third at 5 years (29%) [[Poon2014]].
- REGARDS: Black to White disparity is largest in middle age and driven by incidence rather than 30-day case fatality [[Howard2016]].
- Women: higher crude mortality, lower adjusted mortality, more disability [[Phan2017,Carcel2019]].
- Who coined what: Dechambre "lacune" 1838; Virchow "thrombosis" and "embolism"; Cole "stroke" 1689 in English; Symon "penumbra" [[Engelhardt2017,Astrup2025]].
- Moniz's Nobel was for leucotomy, not angiography [[Artico2017]].
- MR CLEAN absolute difference in independence: 13.5 percentage points [[Berkhemer2015]]. HERMES number needed to treat: 2.6 [[Goyal2016]].
- NINDS: symptomatic ICH 6.4% against 0.6%, no mortality penalty at 3 months [[NINDS1995]].
- Stroke unit benefit applies across age, sex, severity and stroke type [[Langhorne2020]].

### In plain words
- A stroke happens when part of the brain loses its blood supply, or when a blood vessel in or around the brain bleeds. Brain cells then get hurt or die [[Sacco2013]].
- Most strokes, about 2 in 3, come from a blocked artery. Roughly 1 in 3 are bleeds [[Feigin2024]].
- About 1 in 4 adults will have a stroke at some point in life [[Feigin2018]].
- Stroke is not only a disease of old age. About 1 in 7 strokes worldwide happen in people aged 15 to 49 [[Feigin2025]].
- Most of the stroke burden is linked to things that can be changed, and high blood pressure matters most [[Feigin2024,Feigin2025]].
- Many people live for years after stroke. Almost 94 million people are alive today after having one [[Feigin2025]].
- A second stroke is a real risk, which is why follow-up care matters. About 1 person in 9 has another stroke within a year [[Mohan2011]].
- Numbers describe groups. They cannot say what will happen to one person [[Mohan2011]].
- Stroke does not reach everyone equally. Where people live, how much money they have and their access to care all affect risk and recovery [[Addo2012,Howard2024]].
- A short spell of symptoms that goes away can still be a warning sign, and doctors now use scans to help tell the difference [[Easton2009]].
- For centuries people called stroke "apoplexy", meaning struck down. Treatment that opens the blocked artery is only about 30 years old [[Engelhardt2017,NINDS1995]].
- Doctors once saw stroke as something they could do little about. Clot-busting drugs, stroke units and clot-removal procedures changed that [[NINDS1995,Langhorne2020,Goyal2016]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Is stroke in young adults truly rising? Divergence by age is consistent in high-income countries [[Scott2022]] and the Oxford rise was independent of investigation changes [[Li2022]]; absolute trends are heterogeneous, and earlier work raised the concern that more imaging explains part of it [[Scott2022,George2017]]. Status: divergence established, absolute rise varies by setting.
- What drives it? Rising traditional risk factors [[George2017]]; unexplained rise in people without them, especially cryptogenic events [[Li2022]]. Status: unresolved (Hypothesis).
- Are the latest GBD figures stable? GBD 2019 gave 101 million prevalent strokes, 12.2 million incident and a 9.7% SAH share [[Feigin2021]]; GBD 2021 gives 93.8 million, 11.9 million and 5.8% [[Feigin2024]]. Differences between rounds are largely methodological; a page should give one round and say so.
- Is the US number current? 795,000 is widely repeated but rests on 1999 data extrapolated by NHLBI [[Martin2025HDSS]]. Status: no fresher population estimate found in this pass.
- Rank of stroke: WSO says second among non-communicable disorders [[Feigin2025]], while GBD 2021 says third among all causes behind ischaemic heart disease and COVID-19 [[Feigin2024]]. Both are true for their own denominators.
- Does the tissue definition improve on 24 hours? The AHA/ASA statement argues it fits modern science [[Sacco2013]]; "stroke not otherwise specified" still uses 24 hours and many epidemiological sources predate imaging [[Engelhardt2017,Aho1980]]. Status: definitions coexist, so counts are not fully comparable across eras.
- Ethnic differences in Israel: higher age-adjusted rates in Arab people nationally [[Ram2019]]; acute treatment outcomes comparable [[Simaan2021]]; single-centre mortality lower in Arab patients, not significant after adjustment [[Ryder2026]]. Status: limited and partly exploratory data.
- Attribution in history: Wepfer as the first modern [[Engelhardt2017]] against his pre-modern thinking [[Karenberg1998]]; Virchow's triad [[Bagot2008]]. Status: textbook shorthand simplifies the record.
