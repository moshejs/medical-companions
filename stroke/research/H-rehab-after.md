# H · Rehabilitation, recovery and life after stroke

Pass date: 2026-11-08. Modules served: rehab, after.

Access notes:
- The 2026 AHA/ASA rehabilitation guideline (Richards2026) replaces the 2016 guideline (Winstein2016). Treat Richards2026 as the current AHA/ASA position and Winstein2016 as history. Class (COR) and level (LOE) are quoted as published.
- Everything here is teaching material recorded as published. No dose, threshold or rule below is advice for an individual.

## Findings

### rehab · The guidelines and what changed in 2026
- Richards2026 states that it replaces the 2016 guideline. Its literature search ran from June to October 2025. Key updates named in the abstract: medical comorbidities, fracture risk, fall prevention, technology-enabled rehabilitation, caregiver support, participation, and safe return to work and driving [[Richards2026]].
- Take-home messages in Richards2026 include: rehabilitation should begin in the acute phase; it combines restitution of body structure and function with learning compensatory strategies; "uncertainty remains regarding the best treatment approaches, their timing, and dosing" [[Richards2026]].
- After 24 hours poststroke, Richards2026 says moderate to high-intensity exercise and task practice should be performed [[Richards2026]].
- Periodic reassessment and re-engagement in therapy are named as important to prevent functional decline, and care partners should receive education and training [[Richards2026]].
- Winstein2016 described rehabilitation as a sustained, coordinated team effort (the person and their goals, family, nurses, therapists, psychologists and others), and warned that post-acute care is often treated as a cost to trim without recognising its clinical impact [[Winstein2016]].
- Organisation in Richards2026: organised, coordinated, interprofessional care for candidates for post-acute rehabilitation (COR 1, LOE A). Inpatient rehabilitation facility in preference to skilled nursing facility for those who qualify (COR 1, LOE B-NR) [[Richards2026]].
- Early supported discharge with multidisciplinary services for mild to moderate activity limitation (COR 1, LOE B-R). Early discharge with family-led home-based rehabilitation "has no impact on outcome" compared with early discharge alone (COR 3: No Benefit, LOE B-R) [[Richards2026]].
- UK guidance, NICE NG236 (published 18 October 2023): offer needs-based rehabilitation "for at least 3 hours a day, on at least 5 days of the week" covering a range of multidisciplinary therapy (rec 1.2.16). Where the person is unable or does not wish to do this, offer needed therapy for a minimum of 5 days per week (rec 1.2.17) [[NICE-NG236]].
- NICE NG236 also says care and rehabilitation should continue after leaving hospital "for as long as it continues to help them achieve their treatment goals" (rec 1.1.8) [[NICE-NG236]].
- Canadian best practice (sixth update) covers transitions, driving, vocational roles, leisure, relationships and sexuality, and long-term care, so people do not fall "through the cracks" between care stages [[Mountain2020]].
- Stroke rehabilitation overview: evidence supports rehabilitation in coordinated multidisciplinary stroke units or through early supported discharge teams [[Langhorne2011]]. Several large motor recovery trials were neutral, with both groups improving to a similar extent. This may reflect no added benefit or the difficulty of running such trials (Established) [[Stinear2020]].

### rehab · Early supported discharge
- Cochrane, 17 trials, 2422 participants. Hospital stay about 6 days shorter (MD -5.5 days, 95% CI -3 to -8). Death or dependency at median 6 months OR 0.80 (0.67 to 0.95), which equates to five fewer adverse outcomes per 100 patients [[Langhorne2017]].
- Death alone OR 1.04 (0.77 to 1.40). Death or institutional care OR 0.75 (0.59 to 0.96). No adverse impact on mood or carer health status, and readmission similar (OR 1.09, 0.79 to 1.51) [[Langhorne2017]].
- Benefits were greatest with a coordinated team and with mild to moderate disability (subgroup interaction P=0.06 and P=0.04) [[Langhorne2017]].

### rehab · The recovery time course
- Copenhagen Stroke Study: 1197 consecutive people with acute stroke from a catchment of 239,886, examined weekly. Functional recovery was complete within 12.5 weeks (95% CI 11.6 to 13.4) in 95%, and 80% reached best ADL function within 6 weeks (5.3 to 6.7) [[Jorgensen1995]].
- Best ADL function by initial severity: within 8.5 weeks (mild), 13 weeks (moderate), 17 weeks (severe), 20 weeks (very severe). 80% reached it within 3, 7, and 11.5 weeks (mild, moderate, severe and very severe respectively) [[Jorgensen1995]].
- Neurological recovery ran ahead of functional recovery by 2 weeks on average [[Jorgensen1995]].
- The Copenhagen authors concluded that recovery "should not be expected after the first 5 months", a statement later data challenge (see Open questions) [[Jorgensen1995]].
- Initial severity in the same cohort: very severe 19%, severe 14%, moderate 26%, mild 41%. In-hospital death 21%, nursing home 15%, own home 64% [[Jorgensen1995Part1]].
- After rehabilitation, among survivors: 46% no ADL disability, 26% mild, 8% moderate, 20% severe or very severe [[Jorgensen1995Part1]].
- The Copenhagen authors warned that their tables should not be used to select who gets rehabilitation, because "even the most severe cases regularly experience meaningful improvement" [[Jorgensen1995Part1]].
- Walking (804 people): at onset 51% could not walk, 12% walked with help, 37% walked independently. At end of rehabilitation 21% had died, 18% could not walk, 11% walked with help, 50% walked independently. Recovery of walking occurred in 95% within 11 weeks [[Jorgensen1995Walking]].
- Arm (421 people): best arm function reached by 80% within 3 weeks and 95% within 9 weeks. Mild paresis 3 and 6 weeks. Severe paresis 6 and 11 weeks. Full function reached by 79% with mild and only 18% with severe paresis [[Nakayama1994]].
- Language (881 people): aphasia in 38% at admission and 18% at discharge. 95% reached stationary language within 2 weeks (mild), 6 weeks (moderate), 10 weeks (severe). Initial severity was the only clinically relevant predictor [[Pedersen1995]].
- Time alone explained 16% to 42% of observed improvement in the first 6 to 10 weeks in 101 people with first middle cerebral artery infarcts: 42% of Barthel change, 22% of Functional Ambulation Categories, 19% of Action Research Arm Test, about 25 to 26% for arm motor scores, 33 to 39% for leg motor scores, 16% for inattention [[Kwakkel2006]].
- Decline is real too. In the Swedish Stroke Register (64,746 people; 35,064 followed-up survivors), ADL dependency rose from 16.2% at 3 months to 28.3% at 12 months, challenging the belief that function is stable beyond 3 months [[Ullberg2015]].

### rehab · Proportional recovery: the rule and its critics
- Original finding: in 41 people with ischaemic stroke, clinical variables explained 47% of variance in arm recovery. With a set of severe, poorly recovering outliers removed, this rose to 89% and recovery approximated 0.70 x initial impairment [[Prabhakaran2008]].
- Replication formula on the Fugl-Meyer Upper Extremity scale (0 to 66): predicted change = 0.7 x (66 - initial score) + 0.4. In 211 people, about 70% (n=146) showed recovery of about 78%, and 65 did worse than predicted [[Winters2015]].
- Predictors of not fitting: no finger extension, facial palsy, more severe leg paresis, and more severe Bamford stroke type [[Winters2015]].
- Critique: headline correlations are "likely inflated". Correlations between baseline and recovery become extreme when outcomes vary less than baselines, regardless of the true relationship [[Hope2019]].
- Rejoinder: existing data are "largely consistent" with the rule as a population-level model for arm motor recovery, and reports of its demise "are exaggerated" because they focus on individual prediction. About 30% with severe paresis do not follow it [[Kundert2019]].
- Hawe and colleagues published a further critique under the title "Taking Proportional Out of Stroke Recovery". The record has no abstract, so its content is not summarised here (unverified) [[Hawe2019]].
- PREP2 (207 people recruited within 3 days) combines arm impairment, age, motor evoked potentials on TMS, and MRI lesion load or NIHSS to predict arm function at 3 months. Correct for 75%, and TMS is needed for one third [[Stinear2017]].

### rehab · Plasticity: why timing might matter
- Neuroplasticity is defined as the nervous system's ability to respond to intrinsic or extrinsic stimuli by reorganising structure, function and connections. Common themes across conditions: experience dependence, time sensitivity, and the importance of motivation and attention (Established) [[Cramer2011]].
- Animal evidence suggests a time-limited window of neuroplasticity opens after stroke, with the greatest gains during it. Mechanisms include activity-dependent rewiring and synapse strengthening (Established in animal models) [[Murphy2009]].
- Rats given 5 weeks of enriched rehabilitation starting at 5 days improved markedly, at 14 days somewhat less, and at 30 days no better than social housing. Dendritic growth in undamaged motor cortex tracked this [[Biernaskie2004]].
- Human randomised test (CPASS): 20 extra hours of self-selected task-specific therapy. By 1 year, Action Research Arm Test difference versus controls was +6.87 ± 2.63 (P=0.009) when begun at 2 to 3 months, +5.25 ± 2.59 (P=0.043) at 30 days or less, and +2.41 ± 2.25 (P=0.29) at 6 months or later (Early evidence: phase II) [[Dromerick2021]].
- A counterpoint from the chronic phase: Queen Square, 90 hours over 3 weeks in 224 people (median 18 months after stroke). Median scores on admission and at 6 months: Fugl-Meyer Upper Limb 26 to 37, ARAT 18 to 27, CAHAI 40 to 52, ArmA-A 8 to 3, ArmA-B 38 to 19. Gains continued during follow-up. No control group is described in the abstract [[Ward2019]].

### rehab · The modified Rankin Scale at 3 months
- The mRS was reported with six grades (0 to 5), and interobserver testing gave kappa 0.56 and weighted kappa 0.91 [[vanSwieten1988]].
- Modern trials use 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death), with 3 to 6 counted as dead or dependent [[ATTEND2017]] and 0 to 2 as a favourable outcome at 3 months [[Bernhardt2015]].
- Inter-rater reliability is moderate and improves with structured interviews (kappa 0.56 versus 0.78). Test-retest reliability kappa 0.81 to 0.95 [[Banks2007]].
- Young adults show the gap between mRS and real life: among 362 people aged 18 to 50 in Singapore, 87.8% reached mRS 0 to 2 at 90 days but 68.8% had returned to work [[Kwok2025]].

### rehab · Dose: how much, how early
- AVERT, 2104 people at 56 stroke units in five countries. Favourable outcome at 3 months 46% (very early, higher dose mobilisation) versus 50% (usual care), adjusted OR 0.73 (0.59 to 0.90; p=0.004). Deaths 8% versus 7%, OR 1.34 (0.93 to 1.93). Mobilised within 24 hours: 92% versus 59% [[Bernhardt2015]].
- AVERT dose-response (observational within the trial, Class III): each extra out-of-bed session per day OR 1.13 (1.09 to 1.18) for a favourable outcome, while more minutes per day OR 0.94 (0.91 to 0.97). Shorter, more frequent sessions looked better [[Bernhardt2016]].
- Guideline after AVERT: graduated mobilisation protocol commencing 24 to 48 hours after stroke onset (COR 1, LOE A). High-dose mobilisation within the first 24 hours "should not be performed" (COR 3: Harm, LOE A) [[Richards2026]].
- AVERT-DOSE is a phase III adaptive trial of mobility regimens begun within 48 hours in mild (NIHSS below 7) and moderate (NIHSS 8 to 16) ischaemic stroke, planned 1300 and 1400 participants, about 50 hospitals in seven countries. This pass found only its protocol, no results [[Bernhardt2023]].
- Meta-analysis of more versus less scheduled therapy: g=0.35 (0.26 to 0.45), with a positive relationship between scheduled time and outcome, controlling for time after stroke [[Lohse2014]].
- At least 16 hours more therapy in the first 6 months was needed for a significant ADL difference (20 studies, 2686 people). No significant effect in the 3 chronic-phase studies, and none for dexterity [[Kwakkel2004]].
- Extra rehabilitation of the same content improved activity (SMD 0.39, 0.07 to 0.71). Doubling or more gave SMD 0.59 (0.23 to 0.94). At least an extra 240% was needed for a significant likelihood of benefit [[Schneider2016]].
- Cochrane: physical rehabilitation versus no treatment improved independence in ADL (SMD 0.78, 0.58 to 0.97; 27 studies, 3423 participants). A dose of 30 to 60 minutes a day, 5 to 7 days a week was effective, though evidence on dose "does not support robust conclusions" and no named approach was better than another [[Pollock2014]].
- 467 physical therapy RCTs (N=25,373): strong evidence favours intensive, highly repetitive, task-specific training in all phases, with effects mostly limited to what was trained. Neurological treatment approaches were equal or worse [[Veerbeek2014]].
- Against simple dose-response: 85 people at least 6 months after stroke got 3200, 6400, 9600 or individualised maximum repetitions over 8 weeks. Overall effects were small and there was no evidence of a dose-response effect [[Lang2016]].
- Early high dose may harm: in VECTORS (52 people), high-intensity constraint therapy gave less improvement at day 90 than dose-matched constraint or traditional therapy [[Dromerick2009]].
- Review: compiled data show a positive, moderate dose-response relationship, confounded by time. Longer scheduled therapy may not help in the first hours, days or weeks [[Lang2015]].

### rehab · Arm and hand
- EXCITE (222 people, 3 to 9 months after stroke, with 2 weeks of constraint-induced movement therapy): Wolf Motor Function Test time fell 52% versus 26% at 12 months (between-group difference 34%, 12% to 51%). Motor Activity Log Amount of Use rose 1.21 to 2.13 versus 1.15 to 1.65 [[Wolf2006]].
- Constraint therapy Cochrane review (42 studies, 1453 participants): arm motor function SMD 0.34 (0.12 to 0.55), but disability SMD 0.24 (-0.05 to 0.52, not significant) [[Corbetta2015]]. Lancet Neurology review: original and modified versions help, whereas constraint alone has no evidence of efficacy [[Kwakkel2015]].
- ICARE (361 people, mean 46 days after stroke): structured task-oriented training was no better than dose-equivalent or monitored usual occupational therapy. Log WMFT change 0.82, 0.84 and 0.75. Between-group P values 0.16, 0.94 and 0.15 [[Winstein2016JAMA]].
- RATULS (770 people): ARAT success at 3 months 44% with robot, 50% with enhanced therapy, 42% with usual care. Robot versus usual care aOR 1.17 (98.3% CI 0.70 to 1.96). Enhanced therapy versus usual care aOR 1.51 (0.90 to 2.51). Neither improved function [[Rodgers2019]].
- VA ROBOTICS (127 people, at least 6 months after stroke): at 12 weeks, Fugl-Meyer difference versus usual care 2.17 (-0.23 to 4.58), not significant. At 36 weeks, 2.88 (0.57 to 5.18) versus usual care, but not better than intensive comparison therapy [[Lo2010]].
- Cochrane: robot-assisted arm training improved ADL (SMD 0.31, 0.09 to 0.52), arm function (SMD 0.32, 0.18 to 0.46) and arm strength (SMD 0.46, 0.16 to 0.77), with caution due to trial variation [[Mehrholz2018]].
- FAST INdiCATE compared functional strength training with movement performance therapy (288 people, 2 to 60 days after stroke). ARAT change 9.70 versus 7.90, difference 1.35 (-1.20 to 3.90; p=0.298). Baseline neural measures did not predict response. This trial is an upper-limb trial, not a walking trial [[Pomeroy2018]].
- VNS-REHAB (108 people, at least 9 months after ischaemic stroke, moderate to severe arm weakness, all implanted): Fugl-Meyer rose 5.0 versus 2.4, difference 2.6 (1.0 to 4.2; p=0.0014). Clinically meaningful response at 90 days 47% versus 24%. Funded by MicroTransponder [[Dawson2021]].
- Mirror therapy (62 studies, 1982 participants): motor function SMD 0.47 (0.27 to 0.67), ADL SMD 0.48 (0.30 to 0.65), with small studies and uncertain quality [[Thieme2018]].
- Repetitive task training (33 trials, 1853 participants): arm function SMD 0.25 (0.01 to 0.49), hand function SMD 0.25 (0.00 to 0.51), low quality. Sustained up to 6 months [[French2016]].
- Richards2026 arm recommendations: repetitive, graded practice of functional tasks including constraint therapy (COR 1, LOE A). Robotic-aided rehabilitation in addition to standard treatment for moderate to severe hemiparesis to deliver more practice (2a, A). Mirror therapy (2a, B-R). VR (2a, B-R). VNS paired with high-dose task training (2b, B-R). Bobath not recommended (3: No Benefit, B-R) [[Richards2026]].
- Richards2026 summarises that superiority of robotic therapy "has not been demonstrated", but it creates more possibilities for practice [[Richards2026]].

### rehab · Walking
- LEAPS (408 people, 2 months after stroke): 52.0% improved functional walking at 1 year. Early locomotor training versus home exercise OR 0.83 (0.50 to 1.39). Late versus home OR 1.19 (0.72 to 1.99). Multiple falls were more common with early training in those with severe impairment (P=0.02) [[Duncan2011]].
- Treadmill Cochrane (56 trials, 3105 participants): no increased chance of independent walking (RD 0.00, -0.02 to 0.02). Walking speed MD 0.06 m/s (0.03 to 0.09). Benefit was mainly in people already walking [[Mehrholz2017]].
- Electromechanical gait training with physiotherapy (62 trials, 2440 participants): independent walking OR 2.01 (1.51 to 2.69), walking speed MD 0.06 m/s (0.02 to 0.10). Eight need treatment to prevent one dependency in walking. Those who are non-ambulatory and in the first 3 months seem to benefit most, on post hoc analysis [[Mehrholz2020]].
- PHYS-STROKE (200 people, days 5 to 45, median NIHSS 8): aerobic treadmill training was not superior to relaxation for walking speed (0.1 m/s, 0.0 to 0.2) or Barthel (0, -5 to 5). Serious adverse events IRR 1.81 (0.97 to 3.36) [[Nave2019]].
- Fitness training Cochrane (75 studies, 3017 mostly ambulatory people): disability improved with cardiorespiratory training (SMD 0.52, 0.19 to 0.84) and mixed training (SMD 0.23, 0.03 to 0.42). VO2 peak MD 3.40 mL/kg/min (2.98 to 3.83). No serious adverse events [[Saunders2020]].
- Richards2026 gait: task-specific overground walking, stepping and balance (1, A). At least 4 to 8 weeks of high-dose therapy, 1 to 2 hour sessions 5 times per week (2a, B-R). Robot gait training or light to moderate treadmill training not recommended over overground training in ambulatory people (3: No Benefit, A). Bobath not recommended (3: No Benefit, B-R) [[Richards2026]].

### rehab · Aphasia
- Median frequency in mixed stroke 30% (acute) and 34% (rehabilitation). Lowest in acute subarachnoid haemorrhage (9%), highest in acute ischaemic stroke arriving within 3 hours (62%). Aphasia at 1 year was 2% to 12% lower than baseline [[Flowers2016]].
- Aphasia raises in-hospital death (OR 2.7, 2.4 to 3.1) and disability from 28 days (OR 1.5, 1.3 to 1.7) to 2 years (OR 1.7, 1.6 to 2.0) [[Flowers2016]].
- In the VERSE trial screen, 25% of 13,654 people with acute stroke had aphasia [[Godecke2021]].
- Cochrane (57 RCTs, 3002 participants): therapy versus none improved functional communication (SMD 0.28, 0.06 to 0.49), reading, writing and expressive language, but benefits were not evident at follow-up. High intensity or dose helped, with more dropouts [[Brady2016]].
- RELEASE regimen analysis (959 participants, 25 trials): greatest gains with more than 20 to 50 hours total (18.37 WAB-AQ points, 10.58 to 26.16), 2 to 4 and 9 or more hours per week, 3 to 5 or more days per week, tailored receptive-expressive therapy and home practice. No comprehension gains with 20 hours or less, under 3 hours per week, or 3 days per week or fewer. Findings are exploratory [[RELEASE2022]].
- RELEASE predictors: entering before 1 month gave +19.1 WAB-AQ points (13.9 to 24.4), and under age 55 gave +15.4 (10.0 to 20.9). Recovery continued, less, beyond 6 months [[RELEASE2021]].
- VERSE (246 randomised, from within 14 days): early intensive therapy did not beat usual care at 12 weeks. Communication recovery 50.3% versus 52.1% (difference -1.8, -8.7 to 5.0) [[Godecke2021]].
- FCET2EC (156 people aged 70 or under, aphasia for at least 6 months): 3 weeks of at least 10 hours per week. ANELT change 2.61 versus -0.03, Cohen's d 0.58, p=0.0004 [[Breitenstein2017]].
- Big CACTUS (278 people, at least 4 months after stroke): daily computerised therapy improved word finding by 16.2% more than usual care (12.7 to 19.6), but not conversation (-0.03, -0.21 to 0.14) [[Palmer2019]].
- Richards2026: a variety of speech and language treatment approaches (1, A). Supported communication training (1, B-NR). Intensive treatment (2a, B-R). Telerehabilitation when face to face is impractical (2a, A). Computerised treatment as a supplement (2b, B-R) [[Richards2026]].

### rehab · Swallowing recovery
- Dysphagia incidence by method: cursory screening 37% to 45%, clinical testing 51% to 55%, instrumental testing 64% to 78%. Pneumonia RR 3.17 (2.07 to 4.87) with dysphagia and 11.56 (3.36 to 39.77) with aspiration [[Martino2005]].
- Population register: dysphagia 44.7%, upper limb weakness 77.4%, urinary incontinence 48.2%, impaired cognition 43.9% at maximum deficit [[Lawrence2001]].
- 128 people with first stroke: abnormality at baseline 51% clinically, 64% on videofluoroscopy. At 6 months 87% of survivors were back on their prestroke diet, yet 50% still had clinical abnormality, and videofluoroscopy in 67 showed penetration in 34 and aspiration in 17. Chest infection 20% [[Mann1999]].
- FOOD: early tube feeding absolute risk reduction in death 5.8% (-0.8 to 12.5). PEG versus nasogastric raised death or poor outcome by 7.8% (0.0 to 15.5, p=0.05). Data "do not support a policy of early initiation of PEG" [[Dennis2005]].
- Cochrane 2026 update (181 trials, 11,500 participants): behavioural interventions may reduce swallowing impairment (SMD -1.17, -1.60 to -0.74, very low certainty) and chest infection or pneumonia (OR 0.45, 0.27 to 0.76, low certainty). Conclusions are limited by small trials at high or unclear risk of bias [[Wilkinson2026]].
- Richards2026: screening as soon as possible, within 24 hours of admission and before eating, drinking or oral medication (1, B-R). Instrumented studies over bedside studies with suspected dysphagia (1, B-NR). Behavioural swallowing treatment (1, B-NR). PES may be considered (2b, B-R). Gastrostomy preferred to nasogastric tubes with chronic inability to swallow safely (1, B-NR) [[Richards2026]].

### rehab · Spasticity
- Prevalence ranged from 4% to 42.6%, disabling spasticity 2% to 13%. 4% to 27% at 1 to 4 weeks, 19% to 26.7% at 1 to 3 months, 17% to 42.6% beyond 3 months. Risk factors: lower Barthel, severe paresis, stroke-related pain, sensory deficits [[Wissel2013]].
- Botulinum toxin for the arm (40 trials, 2718 people): robust evidence for reduced resistance to passive movement and better self-care, and robust evidence for no effect on arm-hand capacity at follow-up. Also reduced involuntary movements, pain and caregiver burden [[Andringa2019]].
- BoNT-A versus placebo (22 studies, 1804 people, stroke or traumatic brain injury): muscle tone SMD -0.98 at week 4 (-1.28 to -0.68) and -0.67 at week 12 (-0.88 to -0.46). Adverse events RR 1.36 (0.82 to 2.27) [[Dong2017]].
- Cochrane found at best low-level evidence for outpatient rehabilitation after toxin (3 RCTs, 91 people) [[Demetrios2013]]. Evidence is insufficient that systemic antispasmodics improve function, and adverse events are higher (RR 1.65, 1.12 to 2.42) [[Lindsay2016]].
- Richards2026: targeted botulinum toxin for arm spasticity and for leg spasticity interfering with gait (1, A). Instrumented guidance (1, B-R). Oral agents for generalised spasticity (2b, A). Intrathecal baclofen for severe spasticity (2a, B-R). Splints and taping not recommended for preventing wrist and finger spasticity (3: No Benefit, B-NR) [[Richards2026]].

### rehab · Vision and neglect
- 915 stroke survivors referred with suspected visual problems: 92% had confirmed impairment. Field loss 52%, ocular motility disorders 68%, strabismus 16%, inattention 15%. This is a referred sample, so not a population prevalence [[Rowe2017]].
- At follow-up of field loss: 7.5% full recovery, 39% improvement, 52% no recovery [[Rowe2013]].
- Cochrane on field defects: scanning training improved quality of life (VFQ-25 MD 9.36, 3.10 to 15.62) but not the visual field. Prisms showed no effect on ADL, reading, falls or quality of life, with more minor adverse events such as headache [[Pollock2019]].
- Neglect: 29% overall (38% after right and 18% after left brain damage, 6324 people in 41 articles), 53% with ecological assessment versus 24% with other tests, falling from acute to chronic stages [[Esposito2021]].
- Cochrane on neglect (65 RCTs, 1951 participants): effectiveness "remains unproven" and no approach can be supported or refuted. Body awareness interventions on persisting ADL SMD 0.61 (0.24 to 0.97), very uncertain [[Longley2021]].
- Richards2026: visual eye movement training for neglect (2a, A). Mirror therapy with conventional therapy (2a, B-R). Prism adaptation with shift over 10 degrees (2b, B-R). For field cuts, prisms (2a, B-R) and scanning training (2a, B-R) [[Richards2026]].

### rehab · Shoulder pain, bladder and falls
- Hemiplegic shoulder pain, population-based: 10% at baseline, 21% at 4 and 12 months, 29% overall during follow-up. Peak onset and severity at 4 months, after most rehabilitation stays end. Passive range testing predicted later pain (crude RR 3.22, 1.01 to 10.27) [[AdeyWakeling2015]].
- Richards2026 shoulder: clinical assessment and caregiver education (1, B-NR each). Slings and positioning devices to prevent worsening subluxation (2a, B-R). Botulinum toxin for spastic shoulder muscles (2a, B-R). Overhead pulley exercises not recommended (3: Harm, C-EO) [[Richards2026]].
- Urinary incontinence Cochrane (20 trials, 1338 participants): insufficient evidence to guide continence care in rehabilitation [[Thomas2019]].
- Richards2026 falls: formal fall prevention programme (1, B-NR). Balance exercises, tai chi and dual-task training (2a, B-R each) [[Richards2026]].

### rehab · Fatigue
- Pooled prevalence using a Fatigue Severity Scale cut-off of 4 or more: 48% (95% CI 42% to 53%), 35 studies, N=6851. Time of assessment, stroke type and geography explain variation [[Alghamdi2021]].
- Richards2026 gives ">40%" and defines fatigue as not relieved by rest. CBT for fatigue and sleep (2a, B-R), light therapy (2b, C-LD), fluoxetine not recommended to treat fatigue (3: No Benefit, B-R) [[Richards2026]].
- Cochrane on treatments (12 trials, 703 participants): insufficient evidence on any treatment or prevention. The benefit seen overall (SMD -1.07, -1.93 to -0.21) disappeared in trials with adequate allocation concealment (SMD -0.38, -0.80 to 0.04) [[Wu2015]].

### rehab · Fluoxetine: a promising signal that large trials did not confirm
- FLAME (2011, 118 people, moderate to severe motor deficit): Fugl-Meyer improvement at day 90 was 34.0 points with fluoxetine 20 mg versus 24.3 with placebo (p=0.003) [[Chollet2011]].
- FOCUS (UK, n=3127, 20 mg for 6 months): mRS common OR 0.951 (0.839 to 1.079). New depression 13.43% versus 17.21%. Fractures 2.88% versus 1.47% [[Dennis2019]].
- AFFINITY (Australia, New Zealand, Vietnam, n=1280): mRS common OR 0.94 (0.76 to 1.15). Falls 3% versus 1%, fractures 3% versus 1%, seizures 2% versus under 1% [[Hankey2020]].
- EFFECTS (Sweden, n=1500): mRS common OR 0.94 (0.78 to 1.13). New depression 7% versus 11%. Fractures 4% versus 2%. Hyponatraemia 1% versus under 1% [[Lundstrom2020]].
- Cochrane 2021 (76 studies, 13,029 participants): in low risk of bias studies, no difference in disability (SMD -0.0, -0.05 to 0.05) or independence (RR 0.98, 0.93 to 1.03). Less depression (RR 0.75, 0.65 to 0.86). More fractures (RR 2.35, 1.62 to 3.41) and seizures (RR 1.40, 1.00 to 1.98). "When all studies, irrespective of risk of bias, were included", SSRIs reduced disability scores but not the proportion independent [[Legg2021]].
- Richards2026 cites a meta-analysis of the three trials (n=5907): no effect on mRS, less new depression, more seizures, falls with injury, fractures and hyponatraemia with very low absolute risks [[Richards2026]].
- The lesson: FOCUS, AFFINITY and EFFECTS were designed collaboratively with the same basic protocol, so their agreement is strong evidence (Established) [[Mead2020,Dennis2019,Hankey2020,Lundstrom2020]].

### rehab · Technology and home delivery
- Home telerehabilitation versus in-clinic therapy (124 people, 36 sessions of 70 minutes): Fugl-Meyer change 7.86 versus 8.36, adjusted difference 0.06 (-2.14 to 2.26), within the 2.47 margin, so not inferior [[Cramer2019]].
- Cochrane (22 trials, 1937 participants): no difference in ADL versus usual care after discharge (SMD -0.00, -0.15 to 0.15) or versus in-person programmes (SMD 0.03, -0.43 to 0.48). Evidence low to moderate [[Laver2020]].
- Richards2026: telerehabilitation is a reasonable alternative after mild to moderate stroke when in-person access is limited or absent (2a, B-R) [[Richards2026]].

### after · Depression
- Pooled frequency 31% (95% CI 28% to 35%), 61 studies and 25,488 people. Unchanged from 33% in the 2005 review. 25% between 1 and 5 years, 23% at 5 years [[Hackett2014]].
- Separate synthesis: prevalence 29% (25 to 32), stable up to 10 years. Cumulative incidence 39% to 52% within 5 years. Recovery at 1 year in 15% to 57% of those depressed a few months after stroke [[Ayerbe2013]].
- The two reviews disagree on the course: Hackett reports lower frequency with time [[Hackett2014]]; Ayerbe reports stable prevalence [[Ayerbe2013]]. At 15 years in South London, depression was 39.1% among survivors, though with a small, selected group [[Crichton2016]].
- Predictors: pre-stroke depression, more severe deficit, physical disability. Depression worsens functional outcome, a reciprocal relationship [[Kutlubaev2014]]. AHA statement: PSD affects approximately one third at any one time after stroke and is linked with poor recovery, recurrent vascular events, and mortality [[Towfighi2017]].
- Robinson and Jorge argue that merging rigorously diagnosed mood disorders with scale cut-offs misses findings, such as cognitive impairment with major but not minor depression. They propose inflammation, genetic variation, white matter disease and altered neuroplasticity as mechanisms (Hypothesis) [[Robinson2016]].
- Treatment Cochrane (65 trials, 5831 participants, all very low certainty): drugs RR 0.70 (0.55 to 0.88) for meeting depression criteria, psychological therapy RR 0.77 (0.62 to 0.95), non-invasive brain stimulation RR 0.67 (0.39 to 1.14) [[Allida2023]].
- Richards2026: early screening from 3 days to 3 months (1, A). Periodic screening at 3 and 6 months and throughout the lifespan (1, B-NR). Antidepressants for PSD (2a, A). CBT (2a, B-R). Exercise at least 3 times per week (2a, A). Prophylactic antidepressants not recommended (3: No Benefit, A) [[Richards2026]].
- Disagreement: Robinson and Jorge reported that antidepressants significantly decrease incident PSD and may improve recovery and survival [[Robinson2016]]; Richards2026 does not recommend routine prophylaxis after the large trials [[Richards2026]].

### after · Anxiety, PTSD and emotionalism
- Pooled anxiety prevalence in the first year 29.3% (24.8% to 33.8%), with high heterogeneity. 36.7% at 0 to 2 weeks, 24.1% from 2 weeks to 3 months, 23.8% from 3 to 12 months [[Rafsten2018]].
- PTSD after stroke or TIA (9 studies, N=1138): 23% (16% to 33%) within 1 year, 11% (8% to 14%) after 1 year [[Edmondson2013]].
- Pseudobulbar affect occurs in 11% to 37.8% according to Richards2026. Dextromethorphan/quinidine (1, A), noting its pivotal trials did not include people with stroke. Regular benzodiazepines potentially harmful in anxiety (3: Harm, B-NR) [[Richards2026]].

### after · Cognition and dementia
- 10% had dementia before first stroke, 10% developed it soon after first stroke, and more than a third had it after recurrent stroke. Range 7.4% (population-based, first-ever, pre-stroke dementia excluded) to 41.3% (hospital-based, recurrent, included) [[Pendlebury2009]].
- STROKOG (3146 people, 13 studies, 8 countries): 44% impaired in global cognition and 30% to 35% in individual domains at 2 to 6 months. Diabetes and prior stroke were strongly associated [[Lo2019]].
- REGARDS (23,572 people, 515 with incident stroke): acute drop in global cognition (0.10 points), then faster decline (0.06 points per year faster) and faster onset of impairment (OR 1.23 per year, 1.10 to 1.38) [[Levine2015]].
- Stroke characteristics raising risk include severity, lesion volume, location, and recurrence. Lack of harmonised definition and timing is the main challenge [[Rost2022]].
- Richards2026: supervised technology-supported cognitive training (1, B-R). Cognitive training plus physical activity (2a, B-R). Exercise to optimise cognitive health (1, A) [[Richards2026]].

### after · Pain
- Central post-stroke pain pooled prevalence 11% (7% to 18%), exceeding 50% with medullary or thalamic stroke. Onset 26% at stroke, 31% within 1 month, 41% between 1 and 12 months, 5% after 12 months [[Liampas2020]].
- CPSP is pain with sensory abnormalities in the body parts matching the injured brain territory. Exact prevalence is unknown partly because it is hard to separate from other pain types [[Klit2009]].
- Other syndromes include complex regional pain, shoulder subluxation, spasticity-related pain and post-stroke headache. Pain is linked to depression, cognitive dysfunction and poor quality of life, and is underdiagnosed [[Harrison2015]].
- Richards2026 lists duloxetine, lamotrigine or amitriptyline as reasonable first-line (2a, B-R), and pregabalin or gabapentin as second-line (2b, B-NR). This differs from Liampas, which calls lamotrigine best supported [[Richards2026,Liampas2020]].

### after · Sex and intimacy
- Richards2026: sexual dysfunction is reported by more than 50% and is under-addressed. It "may be reasonable" for providers to discuss sexual function (2b, B-NR) [[Richards2026]].
- Deterioration in sexual life was reported in 5% to 76% of working-age adults [[Daniel2009]]. Dysfunction can last months or as long as 2 years. Reviewed participants were predominantly men, with few women or younger people studied [[Dusenbury2017]].
- Psychological factors play a significant part, and the role of lesion location remains inconclusive [[Grenier2017]]. Cause is multifactorial and post-stroke dysfunction is frequently neglected by patients and clinicians [[Park2015]].
- Eight intervention studies: a 30 to 45 minute structured programme, sexual counselling, pelvic floor training, and a clinician interview script raised discussion from 0 to 80% within 10 months [[Auger2021]].

### after · Work and driving
- Return to work: median 41% at 0 to 6 months, 53% at 1 year, 56% at 1.5 years, 66% at 2 to 4 years. Predictors: ADL independence, fewer deficits, better cognition [[Edwards2018]]. Earlier reports ranged from 0% to 100% because of methods [[Daniel2009]].
- Odds of return: haemorrhagic stroke OR 0.53 (0.45 to 0.60), aphasia OR 0.37 (0.20 to 0.69), white-collar work OR 1.84 (1.64 to 2.06), ADL independence OR 3.99 (1.73 to 9.23) [[Orange2024]]. Pooled in young ischaemic stroke 63.2% (56.0 to 69.9) [[Kwok2025]].
- Richards2026: early tailored vocational rehabilitation (2a, B-R), modified duties (2a, B-R), fatigue management and early employer engagement (2a, B-R) [[Richards2026]].
- UK DVLA rules as published (page updated 7 November 2025). Group 1 car and motorcycle: must not drive, may resume after 1 month with satisfactory clinical recovery, and need not notify unless residual neurological deficit remains at 1 month (such as visual field defects, cognitive defects, impaired limb function). TIA: 1 month off driving after each episode [[DVLA-Neuro]].
- Group 2 bus and lorry: must notify, and the licence is refused or revoked for 1 year after stroke or TIA. Relicensing is considered with no debarring residual impairment and no other significant risk factors [[DVLA-Neuro]]. Rules differ by country.
- Predicting fitness: cognitive tests had best reproducibility, such as Trail Making A and B, Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure and Useful Field of View, across 17 studies [[Marshall2007]]. Richards2026 says no test or battery precisely identifies who needs on-road evaluation [[Richards2026]].
- Cochrane (4 trials, 245 participants): no clear evidence of improved on-road scores [[George2014]]. Richards2026: on-road test for those with relevant impairments (1, C-LD), simulator may be considered (2b, B-NR) [[Richards2026]].

### after · Carers
- Most stroke survivors are cared for at home by a family caregiver, and caregiver stress is a leading cause of institutionalisation [[Bakas2014]].
- Physical disability correlated with burden in all 7 longitudinal studies reviewed [[Shamoun2022]]. Caregiver depression in developing countries pooled 48.75% (38.64% to 58.86%), ranging 17.2% to 76%, with long caregiving the only significant factor [[Alemayehu2024]].
- TRACS (928 dyads, 36 stroke units): structured carer training did not change patient activity (difference -0.2, -3.0 to 2.5) or burden (0.5, -1.7 to 2.7) at 6 months [[Forster2013]].
- Caregiver-mediated exercises (9 trials): no significant effect on basic ADL (SMD 0.21, -0.02 to 0.44), and burden did not increase. Standing balance improved (SMD 0.53, 0.19 to 0.87) [[Vloothuis2016]].
- Review of 18 newer studies: small samples and conflicting results, with burden the most common significant carer outcome. Firm conclusions were difficult [[Bakas2022]].
- Richards2026: engage carers early and throughout (2a, B-R). Teach-back training (2a, B-R). Tailored multicomponent psychosocial intervention after discharge (2a, B-R) [[Richards2026]]. Note the tension with neutral TRACS and ATTEND [[Forster2013,ATTEND2017]].

### after · Long-term needs and aftercare
- UK survey (1251 people, 1 to 5 years after stroke): 51% had no unmet needs. Among the rest the median was 3. 54% had an unmet need for stroke information, 52% lost work activities, 18% lost income, 31% had higher expenses [[McKevitt2011]].
- Systematic review (32 studies): 15.08% to 97.59% had unmet needs, median 67.20%, 62.14% at 6 months and 81.37% at 2 years. Main concerns: information, physical function, mental health [[Lin2021]].
- Post-Stroke Checklist 11 areas: secondary prevention, ADL, mobility, spasticity, pain, incontinence, communication, mood, cognition, life after stroke, relationship with caregiver [[Philp2013]].
- South London at 15 years: reported as 262 (21%) of 2625 people with first-ever stroke surviving to 15 years. The record states these figures, though 262 of 2625 is 10%, so the 21% probably uses another denominator (unverified). 87% lived at home. Disability mild 33.8%, moderate 14.3%, severe 15.0%. Cognitive impairment 30.0%, depression 39.1%, anxiety 34.9% [[Crichton2016]].
- Self-management (14 trials, 1863 participants): quality of life SMD 0.34 (0.05 to 0.62), self-efficacy SMD 0.33 (0.04 to 0.61), no superior effect on ADL, participation or mood [[Fryer2016]].
- Discharge planning (2a, B-R), community or home rehabilitation (1, A), community exercise (2a, C-LD), social health screening (1, B-NR) in Richards2026 [[Richards2026]].

### after · Young adults and pregnancy
- Average age of onset for ischaemic stroke is decreasing as incidence rises under age 50, with traditional risk factors increasingly important [[Maaijwee2014]].
- FUTURE (722 people aged 18 to 50, mean 9.1 years follow-up): poor outcome (mRS over 2) 32.0% overall, 36.5% ischaemic stroke, 49.3% intracerebral haemorrhage, 16.8% TIA. One of 8 survivors is dependent at 10 years [[Synhaeve2014]].
- FUTURE mortality (959 people): 20-year risk among 30-day survivors 24.9% TIA, 26.8% ischaemic stroke, 13.7% intracerebral haemorrhage. SMR 2.6, 3.9 and 3.9 versus the general population [[RuttenJacobs2013]].
- US pregnancy-related hospitalisations: 16,694 of 37,360,772 (0.045%, 1 in 2222) had acute stroke. In-hospital mortality 42.1 versus 0.11 per 1000, falling among those with stroke from 5.5% in 2007 to 2.7% in 2015 [[Elgendy2020]].
- Pregnancy after stroke (441 French women aged 15 to 40, 187 subsequent pregnancies): recurrence 1% within 1 year, 2.3% within 5 years. Post-partum risk ratio 9.7 (1.2 to 78.9) versus pregnancy 2.2 (0.3 to 17.5). Outcome of pregnancies resembled the general population. "A previous ischemic stroke is not a contraindication" [[Lamy2000]].
- Canadian consensus: decisions rest on what would be done if the woman was not pregnant and if she had not had a stroke, since maternal health is vital for fetal wellbeing. Evidence is limited, hence consensus [[Swartz2018,Ladhani2018]].

### after · Long-term survival, recurrence and global burden
- Australia and New Zealand (313,162 people, 2008 to 2017): survival 79.4% at 3 months, 73.0% at 1 year, 52.8% at 5 years, 36.4% at 10 years. Recurrence 7.8%, 11.0%, 19.8% and 26.8%. Loss of 5.5 years of life expectancy (32.7% of predicted), 7.4 years with haemorrhagic stroke [[Peng2022]].
- Pooled recurrence 3.1% at 30 days, 11.1% at 1 year, 26.4% at 5 years, 39.2% at 10 years. Five-year risk fell from 32% to 16.2% across studies [[Mohan2011]].
- Perth (1989 cohort): 10-year recurrence 43% (34 to 51). 30-day case fatality 41% after recurrent stroke versus 22% after first [[Hardie2004]].
- Sweden (22,929 people): 5-year death or dependency 70.6% ischaemic, 79% haemorrhagic [[Sennfalt2019]]. Oxford: 70% dead or disabled at 5 years, 39% of survivors disabled, 19% care-home risk [[LuengoFernandez2013]].
- South London: 20% to 30% have poor outcomes up to 10 years, with disability around 110 per 1000 from 3 months to 10 years [[Wolfe2011]].
- GBD 2021: 7.3 million deaths (third cause), 160.5 million DALYs (fourth), 93.8 million prevalent, 11.9 million incident strokes [[Feigin2024]]. GBD 2019: 6.55 million deaths (second cause), 12.2 million incident, 101 million prevalent, age-standardised mortality 3.6 times higher in low-income countries [[Feigin2021]].
- WSO 2025: cost over US$890 billion, 87% of deaths and 89% of DALYs in lower-income and lower-middle-income countries [[Feigin2025]].

### after · Advance care planning and palliative care
- AHA 2025 update: palliative care needs "are insufficiently addressed by current models of care" and should be addressed throughout the illness, including communication about prognosis and goals [[Creutzfeldt2025]]. The 2014 statement listed nine expectations, including estimating prognosis, setting goals of care, and bereavement resources [[Holloway2014]].
- Corpus Christi cohort (148 people): 18% had no advance care planning, 44% informal conversations only, 38% formal documents. Informal conversations (HR 3.55, 1.35 to 9.33) and formal documents (HR 2.85, 1.05 to 7.72) were linked with earlier transition to comfort measures. Formal gave no extra association beyond informal (HR 0.80, 0.40 to 1.63) [[Lank2021]].

### after · Physical activity for life
- Richards2026: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, 3 to 5 days of aerobic and 2 to 3 days of strength and balance training (1, A). Tailored programme (1, A). Behaviour change techniques (2a, B-R) [[Richards2026]].
- AHA statement: low to moderate-intensity aerobic activity, muscle strengthening and less sedentary behaviour, customised for adherence [[Billinger2014]].
- BUST-stroke (19 people): 3-minute breaks every 30 minutes for 8 hours did not change glucose or insulin [[English2018]].

### after · Rehabilitation worldwide and mobile delivery
- 2.41 billion people globally had conditions that would benefit from rehabilitation in 2019, up 63% since 1990, at least one in three people [[Cieza2021]].
- ATTEND (India, 1250 people, 14 hospitals): most people with stroke in India have no access to organised rehabilitation. Family-led rehabilitation did not change death or dependency at 6 months, 47% versus 47% (OR 0.98, 0.78 to 1.23) [[ATTEND2017]].
- Scoping review of low-resource community rehabilitation (25 studies, 11 countries, 16 RCTs): mostly middle-income, one low-income study (Uganda). 10 RCTs positive, 5 not. No cost-effectiveness data [[Hardianto2026]].
- mHealth apps (11 trials, 264 survivors): 6 of 7 studies showed better physical function, most robustly for the arm. "Promising", fair quality (Early evidence) [[Rintala2023]].
- WalkWithMe in Benin (9 adults, unsupervised, 10 weeks): adherence over 70% for most, Barthel 85 to 95, usability problems noted. Pilot only (Early evidence) [[Noukpo2024]].
- Smartphone-guided home functional electrical stimulation (31 adults, retrospective comparison): adherence 87.3% to 90.3%, no adverse events, preliminary (Early evidence) [[Purohit2026]].
- mHealth and medication adherence (10 RCTs, 2151 people): OR 2.81 (1.35 to 5.85) [[Zeng2022]].

### Exam hooks
- 80% reach best ADL function by 6 weeks and 95% by 12.5 weeks [[Jorgensen1995]].
- Proportional recovery: about 0.7 of initial impairment, except a subgroup with severe impairment [[Prabhakaran2008,Winters2015]]. Statistical coupling may inflate it [[Hope2019]].
- AVERT: higher dose mobilisation within 24 hours reduced favourable outcome (46% versus 50%) [[Bernhardt2015]]. Shorter, more frequent sessions looked better [[Bernhardt2016]].
- 2026 AHA/ASA: mobilise from 24 to 48 hours (COR 1), no high-dose mobilisation in first 24 hours (COR 3: Harm) [[Richards2026]].
- CPASS: extra therapy worked best at 2 to 3 months [[Dromerick2021]].
- EXCITE positive, ICARE and RATULS neutral [[Wolf2006,Winstein2016JAMA,Rodgers2019]]. LEAPS: treadmill with body-weight support no better than home exercise [[Duncan2011]].
- Aphasia about 30% acutely [[Flowers2016]]. FCET2EC showed benefit in chronic aphasia [[Breitenstein2017]]; VERSE showed no benefit of early intensive therapy [[Godecke2021]].
- Fluoxetine: no functional benefit, less depression, more fractures [[Dennis2019,Hankey2020,Lundstrom2020,Legg2021]].
- Depression about 31% [[Hackett2014]], anxiety about 29% [[Rafsten2018]], fatigue about 48% [[Alghamdi2021]], central pain about 11% [[Liampas2020]], hemiplegic shoulder pain about 29% [[AdeyWakeling2015]], neglect about 30% [[Esposito2021]].
- 10% dementia soon after first stroke, more than a third after recurrent stroke [[Pendlebury2009]].
- Botulinum toxin reduces resistance to passive movement and aids self-care, but not arm capacity [[Andringa2019]].
- DVLA Group 1: 1 month off driving. Group 2: 1 year [[DVLA-Neuro]].
- PEG is not supported early [[Dennis2005]].
- Five-year recurrence 26.4% [[Mohan2011]]; 5-year survival 52.8% [[Peng2022]].

### In plain words
- Most recovery happens in the first weeks and months, and it is fastest early on. Bigger strokes take longer [[Jorgensen1995]].
- Getting better can continue after that with the right practice, even long after the stroke. Not everyone gains the same amount [[Ward2019,Prabhakaran2008]].
- After a stroke, your brain can rewire itself. Practice helps it do this [[Cramer2011,Murphy2009]].
- In hospital, short and frequent times out of bed seem better than long sessions very early on [[Bernhardt2016]].
- Doing the real task, again and again, is the core of arm and walking therapy [[Veerbeek2014]].
- Trouble finding words is called aphasia. Speech therapy helps, even long after a stroke [[Brady2016,Breitenstein2017]].
- Feeling very tired is common after a stroke, and rest does not always fix it. It is worth telling your team [[Alghamdi2021,Richards2026]].
- Low mood and worry are common, can last for years, and can be treated. They are part of the stroke, not a weakness [[Hackett2014,Rafsten2018,Richards2026]].
- Fluoxetine is an antidepressant. It was once hoped to help movement recover, but big trials found it did not and it raised the chance of broken bones [[Dennis2019]].
- Sex and intimacy can change after a stroke. You can raise this with your team, as many people have the same worries [[Richards2026]].
- Many people go back to work, but it can take a year or more [[Edwards2018]].
- Driving rules depend on where you live and what you drive. In the UK a car driver stops for at least a month [[DVLA-Neuro]].
- Carers often get worn out or low too. They need support of their own [[Alemayehu2024,Bakas2014]].
- A second stroke is a real risk for years, so preventing one is lifelong work [[Mohan2011,Maaijwee2014]].
- Talking with family about what matters to you, before you are unwell, can guide later choices [[Lank2021,Creutzfeldt2025]].

## Open questions and controversies
- When does recovery stop? Position A: neurological and functional recovery "should not be expected after the first 5 months" [[Jorgensen1995]]. Position B: recovery continues, less, beyond 6 months in language [[RELEASE2021]], and intensive chronic-phase arm therapy gives gains [[Ward2019,Dawson2021,Breitenstein2017]]. Status: unresolved. One reading is that the apparent plateau partly reflects the therapy dose delivered as well as biology (Hypothesis).
- Is proportional recovery biology or arithmetic? Position A: mechanism present in non-severe paresis [[Prabhakaran2008,Winters2015]]. Position B: inflated by mathematical coupling [[Hope2019]]. Position C: valid at population level but not for an individual [[Kundert2019]]. Status: unresolved, and not a tool for individual prognosis.
- More therapy or better therapy? Position A: more time helps [[Lohse2014,Schneider2016,Kwakkel2004]], and NICE sets 3 hours a day [[NICE-NG236]]. Position B: controlled dose trials are neutral or negative [[Lang2016,Winstein2016JAMA,Dromerick2009]]. Status: Richards2026 states that uncertainty remains on dosing [[Richards2026]].
- Early or later intensive aphasia therapy? Early intensive therapy gave no gain in VERSE [[Godecke2021]], yet RELEASE links starting before 1 month to the largest gains [[RELEASE2021]]. These are not the same comparison: VERSE compares regimens in early patients, whereas RELEASE reflects natural recovery as well.
- Robots: RATULS negative [[Rodgers2019]], Cochrane positive [[Mehrholz2018]], Richards2026 COR 2a for extra practice with superiority not shown [[Richards2026]].
- Prisms for field loss: Richards2026 COR 2a [[Richards2026]]; Cochrane low-quality evidence of no effect on most outcomes and more minor harms [[Pollock2019]].
- Neglect: Richards2026 gives COR 2a for visual training [[Richards2026]], whereas Cochrane says no approach can be supported or refuted [[Longley2021]].
- Carers and family-led therapy: Richards2026 supports carer engagement at 2a [[Richards2026]], but TRACS and ATTEND were neutral [[Forster2013,ATTEND2017]]. ATTEND authors did not support new task-shifting services without new evidence, which matters in low-resource settings [[ATTEND2017]].
- Burden numbers differ across WSO and GBD papers. WSO 2022 gives prevalent strokes +102% and DALYs +143% for 1990 to 2019 [[Feigin2022]]; GBD 2019 gives +85% and +32% for the same period [[Feigin2021]]; WSO 2025 gives +86% and +32% for 1990 to 2021 [[Feigin2025]]. Rank also differs: GBD 2021 puts stroke third for deaths (behind COVID-19 and ischaemic heart disease) [[Feigin2024]], while WSO 2025 says second among non-communicable disorders [[Feigin2025]]. Use the GBD paper directly and name the year.
- Mobile rehabilitation in low-resource settings: promising but thin, with no cost data in low-resource community studies [[Hardianto2026,Rintala2023]]. Status: (Early evidence).
