## Findings

### vessels · Circle of Willis
- The anterior circulation arises from the internal carotid arteries and the posterior circulation from the vertebral arteries, and the Circle of Willis joins them as an anastomosis [[Rosner2023]].
- In 150 volunteers on 1.5T MR angiography, the anterior part of the circle was complete in 74%, the posterior part in 52% and the entire circle in 42%; completeness was slightly higher in younger people and in women [[KrabbeHartkamp1998]].
- In the Tromso population study (N=1,864, mean age 65.4, 3T time-of-flight MRA, 1 mm vessel threshold), 47 variants were found, five of them made up 68.5%, and the circle was complete in only 11.9% [[Hindenes2020]].
- The commonest pattern in that study (27.8%) lacked both posterior communicating arteries; missing segments increased with age (P=.0147) and were not associated with sex (P=.0526) [[Hindenes2020]].
- In 976 people with atherosclerosis followed for 9.2 years, 3.1% had a stroke; an incomplete anterior circle gave a hazard ratio of 2.8 (95% CI 1.3-6.3) for anterior circulation stroke, and 7.0 (1.3-38.2) when combined with a one-sided incomplete posterior circle [[vanSeeters2015]].

### vessels · Brain energy and blood flow
- The brain receives 15-20% of cardiac output, a figure one group calls "widely accepted dogma"; in their 139 subjects the share of cardiac output reaching the brain fell 1.3% per decade and was higher in women [[Xing2017]].
- The brain is cited as about 2% of body weight while receiving 15-20% of cardiac output (background statement in a rodent hypoperfusion paper) [[Cox2026]].
- In rodent grey matter signalling energy was budgeted as action potentials 47%, postsynaptic glutamate effects 34%, resting potential 13% and glutamate recycling 3% [[Attwell2001]].
- In 34 volunteers aged 22-82 on PET, cerebral blood flow, oxygen use and blood volume fell about 0.5% per year in grey and white matter, while oxygen extraction stayed constant [[Leenders1990]].
- Normal cerebral blood flow is stated as about 50-60 mL/100 g/min; at 10-20 mL/100 g/min cellular functions are interrupted but structure is intact, and below that cell death follows within minutes [[Mangiardi2023]].
- A second source gives awake normal flow of roughly 50 mL/100 g/min, with functional impairment below approximately 22 mL/100 g/min [[Piperata2026]].
- A case report gives EEG suppression at 15-20 mL/100 g/min (30-40% of a normal 50) and membrane pump failure below 10-15 mL/100 g/min [[Berrada2025]].
- The infarct core is described at 5-10 mL/100 g/min, with persistent depolarisation [[Koukalova2024]].

### vessels · Territories and syndromes: anterior circulation
- The anterior circulation accounts for 70% of ischaemic strokes, and the middle cerebral artery (MCA) is the most commonly involved artery; accessory MCA occurs in under 4% and duplication in under 3%; the artery has segments M1 to M4, with lateral lenticulostriate branches [[Benjamin2026MCA]].
- Entire MCA syndrome: contralateral gaze palsy, hemiplegia, hemisensory loss, neglect, hemianopsia, and global aphasia if on the left [[Lo2025]].
- MCA deep syndrome: hemiplegia, hemisensory loss, transcortical aphasia and lacunar syndromes [[Lo2025]].
- MCA superior division: hemiplegia, hemisensory loss, gaze palsy, neglect, and Broca aphasia if on the left [[Lo2025]].
- MCA inferior division: hemianopsia or upper quadrantanopsia, Wernicke aphasia if on the left, constructional dyspraxia if on the right [[Lo2025]].
- MCA parasylvian: face and hand weakness, with conduction aphasia and Gerstmann syndrome if on the left [[Lo2025]].
- By segment, M1 occlusion gives contralateral hemiplegia of the face and arm with the leg to a lesser extent, homonymous hemianopia, head and eye deviation to the side of the lesion and global aphasia; M2 superior gives face and arm sensorimotor loss and Broca aphasia; M2 inferior gives superior quadrantanopia, Wernicke aphasia, and hemineglect if non-dominant [[Stebner2025]].
- Entire anterior cerebral artery (ACA) syndrome: contralateral hemiplegia, abulia, incontinence, transcortical motor aphasia and limb dyspraxia; distal ACA: weakness of leg, hip, foot and shoulder with sensory loss in the foot [[Lo2025]].
- By segment, A1 occlusion is often asymptomatic because of the anterior communicating artery; A2 and A3 give contralateral leg weakness, sensory loss, urinary incontinence and abulia [[Stebner2025]].
- ACA share of strokes differs widely by source: approximately 20% of ischaemic strokes in one review [[MatosCasano2026]], 1.8% (27 of 1,490) in the Lausanne registry, where 63% were embolic [[Bogousslavsky1990]], and 1.3% of 3,705 in another registry [[Kumral2002]].
- In 48 people with ACA infarction, left-sided lesions gave mutism, transcortical motor aphasia and leg-predominant hemiparesis; right-sided lesions gave acute confusional state, motor hemineglect and hemiparesis; bilateral lesions gave akinetic mutism and severe sphincter dysfunction [[Kumral2002]].
- In 100 Korean patients with ACA infarction, motor deficit occurred in 91, hypobulia or apathy in 43, urinary incontinence in 30 and grasp reflex in 25; local ACA atherosclerosis was found in 68, and the mechanism was ACA atherosclerosis in 61, cardiogenic in 10 and internal carotid to ACA embolism in 6 [[Kang2008]].
- Anterior choroidal artery syndrome: hemiparesis, hemisensory loss and hemianopia [[Lo2025]].
- Large MCA infarcts were 208 cases, 7.6% of infarcts in the Lausanne registry; internal carotid occlusion was present in 41% and dissection in 12%; mortality was 17% and severe disability 50%; oedema deaths (16 of 35) were in younger people (57 vs 73 years) and came sooner (5 vs 37 days) [[Heinsius1998]].
- In 55 patients with complete MCA infarction, only 12 (22%) survived; herniation occurred in 43 (78%), deterioration ran over 2-5 days, 49 were ventilated, and survivors had a mean Barthel index of 60 [[Hacke1996]].
- Arterial territory maps of the hemispheres and of the brainstem and cerebellum are published in templates suitable for reference [[Tatu1998,Tatu1996]].

### vessels · Territories and syndromes: posterior circulation
- The posterior cerebral artery (PCA) arises from the basilar in approximately 70%, from the posterior communicating artery in 20% and mixed in 10%; the artery of Percheron arises from P1 [[Benjamin2026PCA]].
- Superficial PCA syndrome: cortical blindness, homonymous or altitudinal hemianopia, quadrantanopia, colour vision loss, visual agnosia, pure alexia, prosopagnosia and memory deficits [[Lo2025]].
- By segment, P1 gives ipsilateral third nerve palsy with contralateral hemiplegia and thalamic deficits; P2 and P3 give hemianopia, memory loss, alexia without agraphia, visual agnosia, cortical blindness and Anton syndrome [[Stebner2025]].
- In 79 people with PCA territory infarcts (New England registry), causes were cardiac embolism 41%, proximal arterial disease 32%, cryptogenic 10% and intrinsic PCA disease 9%; visual abnormality occurred in 84%, motor, cognitive or ataxic signs in 25% and sensory signs in 15% [[Yamamoto1999]].
- A second PCA series reports common unilateral headache, embolism in up to half and undetermined cause in at least a quarter [[Brandt2000]].
- Thalamic infarct syndromes follow territories (anterolateral, anteromedial, lateral, including Dejerine-Roussy) [[Lo2025,Schmahmann2003]].
- Artery of Percheron infarcts fell into four imaging patterns at 43%, 38%, 14% and 5%, and the midbrain "V sign" was seen in 67% of cases with midbrain involvement [[Lazzaro2010]].
- In the New England Posterior Circulation Registry (407 people), 59% had stroke with no TIA, 24% TIA then stroke and 16% TIA only; embolism accounted for 40% (24% cardiac, 14% intra-arterial, 2% both), large-artery haemodynamic mechanisms 32%; 30-day mortality was 3.6%, and the distal territory was most often affected [[Caplan2004]].
- In that registry the leading symptoms were dizziness 47%, unilateral limb weakness 41%, dysarthria 31%, headache 28% and nausea or vomiting 27%; leading signs were weakness 38%, gait ataxia 31%, limb ataxia 30%, dysarthria 28% and nystagmus 24%; proximal territory lesions featured dysphagia and Horner syndrome, middle territory weakness and facial palsy, distal territory sensory loss, lethargy and visual field loss [[Searls2012]].
- Midbrain syndromes listed: Weber, Claude, Benedikt. Pontine syndromes listed: pure motor, ataxic hemiparesis, Foville, wrong-way eyes, Millard-Gubler, AICA lateral caudal pons, superior cerebellar artery [[Lo2025]].
- Medial medullary syndrome: contralateral arm and leg weakness, contralateral position and vibration loss, ipsilateral tongue weakness [[Lo2025]].

### vessels · Wallenberg, basilar occlusion and locked-in syndrome
- Wallenberg (lateral medullary) syndrome, from PICA or vertebral artery disease, maps structure to sign: inferior cerebellar peduncle and vestibular nuclei to ipsilateral ataxia, vertigo, nystagmus and nausea; trigeminal nucleus and tract to ipsilateral facial pain and temperature loss; spinothalamic tract to contralateral body pain and temperature loss; descending sympathetics to ipsilateral Horner syndrome; nucleus ambiguus to hoarseness and dysphagia; nucleus solitarius to ipsilateral taste loss [[Lo2025,EastonCarr2026]].
- In 130 people with pure lateral medullary infarction, vertebral artery disease was found in 67% and PICA disease in 10%; mechanisms were large vessel 50%, dissection 15%, small vessel 13% and cardiac embolism 5%; rostral lesions favoured dysphagia and facial paresis [[Kim2003]].
- Basilar occlusion: often preceding TIAs, headaches, dizziness, confusion, cranial nerve palsies, quadriparesis, locked-in state and coma [[Lo2025]]. Hallmarks are decreased consciousness, quadriparesis, pupillary and oculomotor abnormalities, dysarthria and dysphagia, with prodromal vertigo or headache [[Mattle2011]].
- Top of the basilar syndrome: abnormal pupils, abnormal vertical eye movements, hypersomnolence, confusion, hallucinations, memory loss and visual defects [[Lo2025]].
- In the BASICS registry (592 people with basilar occlusion), poor outcome (mRS 4-5 or death) occurred in 68% [[Schonewille2009]].
- Locked-in syndrome: quadriplegia and bulbar palsy from an anterior pontine lesion, with cognition, vertical eye movement, blinking and hearing preserved; classical, incomplete and total types are described [[Das2023]].
- A 1986 review of 139 locked-in cases reported mortality of 60%, with better recovery in nonvascular causes [[Patterson1986]].
- A 2023 review of 82 pontine infarcts cites a newer report of 75% acute mortality and one chronic survey with functional movement recovery in 72%, and describes a pontine warning syndrome [[Schnetzer2023]].
- Diagnosis of locked-in syndrome took on average over 2.5 months, the family was the first to realise in more than half of cases, life expectancy once stable can be several decades, and chronically affected people self-report meaningful quality of life [[Laureys2005]].

### vessels · Lacunar syndromes
- Fisher described at least 20 lacunar syndromes; small lacunes were tied to lipohyalinosis, and larger ones to atheromatous or embolic mechanisms [[Fisher1982]].
- Lacunar infarcts are about one quarter of ischaemic strokes, with five classic syndromes and good short-term but worse mid and long-term outcome [[Arboix2009,Norrving2003]]. Another review says "nearly one-third" [[Regenhardt2018]].
- Size is given as under 15 mm classically, with a range of 2-3 to 15-20 mm; the vessels are end arteries; silent lesions occur in 20-50% of older adults; at 5 years 25% had died, 20% had recurrence and 30% were impaired [[Gore2026]].
- Penetrating arteries measure 400-900 micrometres; sites are lenticulostriate, anterior choroidal, thalamoperforating, basilar paramedian and the recurrent artery of Heubner [[Venkataraman2024]].
- Pure motor: MCA lenticulostriates to the posterior limb of the internal capsule, or basilar paramedian branches to the basis pontis [[Lo2025]].
- Pure sensory: PCA thalamogeniculate branches to the ventral posterolateral nucleus [[Lo2025]].
- Sensorimotor: lenticulostriates or PCA thalamoperforators to the thalamocapsular region, with no cortical signs [[Lo2025]].
- Ataxic hemiparesis: lenticulostriates to the corona radiata or posterior limb, or pontine perforators, leg more than arm [[Lo2025]].
- Dysarthria-clumsy hand: lenticulostriates to the anterior limb or genu, or pontine perforators [[Lo2025]].

### vessels · Oxfordshire (Bamford) classification
- In the Oxfordshire Community Stroke Project (675 first-ever strokes, 543 infarcts): total anterior circulation infarct (TACI) 17%, partial anterior (PACI) 34%, posterior (POCI) 24%, lacunar (LACI) 25% [[Bamford1991]].
- Natural history as stated qualitatively: TACI carried a negligible chance of good outcome and high mortality; PACI early recurrence; POCI later first-year recurrence but the best functional outcome; LACI many substantially handicapped [[Bamford1991]].
- Imaging concordance: in 1,012 people a visible infarct was present in 65%, and concordance was TAC 79%, PAC 71%, LAC 73%, POC 83%, overall 75% [[Mead2000]].
- In 510 people scanned within 6 hours (IST-3), early ischaemic changes were seen in TACS 79%, PACS 57%, LACS 40% and POCS 33%, and a hyperattenuated artery in TACS 54%, PACS 35%, LACS 5% and POCS 19% [[Kobayashi2009]].
- In 1,115 thrombolysed people the split was TACI 17.67%, PACI 62.78%, POCI 13.72%, LACI 5.83%; TACI carried an odds ratio of 8.80 (2.84-27.25) for symptomatic haemorrhage and 5.24 (3.19-8.62) for mortality [[Yang2016]].
- A smaller imaging correlation study (117 people) also separated TACI from the other three groups [[Pittock2003]].

### cascade · Thresholds and penumbra
- The term "ischaemic penumbra" was named by Astrup and colleagues in 1981 [[Astrup1981]].
- In awake monkeys, reversible paralysis occurred below about 23 mL/100 g/min, irreversible damage below 10-12 mL/100 g/min for 2-3 hours or 17-18 mL/100 g/min with permanent occlusion, and microscopic infarct was seen at 15-30 minutes [[Jones1981]].
- A rodent-based threshold ladder: protein synthesis stops at 0.55 mL/g/min, anaerobic glycolysis starts at 0.35, neurotransmitter release and energy metabolism are disturbed at 0.20, and anoxic depolarisation occurs below 0.15; here the penumbra is redefined as tissue with preserved energy metabolism, and it is gone after a few hours in rodents as the core expands [[Hossmann1994]].
- Reported human thresholds vary: penumbra 14.1-35.0 mL/100 g/min and core 4.8-8.4 mL/100 g/min, too heterogeneous for meta-analysis [[Bandera2006]].
- Human functional imaging supports the penumbra concept [[Heiss2000]]; the penumbra was described as short-lived [[Hakim1998]]; later work shows that core growth varies between people and that penumbra can remain salvageable up to and perhaps beyond 24 hours [[Baron2021]].
- Early after onset, penumbra may be as much as half of the lesion volume [[Woodruff2011]].

### cascade · Time is brain
- Saver's estimate: for a typical large-vessel supratentorial stroke, final infarct volume 54 mL (range 19-100), evolution 10 hours (range 6-18), out of 22 billion forebrain neurons [[Saver2006]].
- Per hour without reperfusion: 120 million neurons, 830 billion synapses and 714 km of myelinated fibres, equal to 3.6 years of normal ageing [[Saver2006]].
- Per minute: 1.9 million neurons, 14 billion synapses and 12 km of fibres [[Saver2006]].
- Each minute saved in thrombolysis was modelled as 1.8 days of healthy life (0.9-2.7), so 15 minutes equals about 1 month [[Meretoja2014]]. For thrombectomy the figure was 4.2 days per minute (2.3-5.4), so 20 minutes equals about 3 months [[Meretoja2017]].

### cascade · Pathobiology: excitotoxicity, calcium, depolarisation
- Dirnagl's framework orders the injury into excitotoxicity, peri-infarct depolarisations, inflammation and apoptosis across overlapping timescales [[Dirnagl1999]].
- Glutamate accumulates from pump and reuptake failure, overstimulates AMPA and NMDA receptors, and drives calcium, sodium and water influx; this activates proteases, lipases and nucleases, plus nNOS and PLA2, and mitochondria release calcium; spreading depolarisation is triggered by glutamate and potassium [[Moskowitz2010]].
- NMDA signalling is split: extrasynaptic GluN2B promotes death and GluN2A promotes survival, with PSD95 and nNOS coupling as a target [[Lai2014]].
- Disrupting the NMDA receptor to PSD-95 link reduced ischaemic damage in rats [[Aarts2002]]. The ESCAPE-NA1 trial of nerinetide reached mRS 0-2 in 61.4% vs 59.2%, adjusted risk ratio 1.04 (0.96-1.14), with effect modification by alteplase [[Hill2020]].
- Of 1,026 experimental treatments, the 114 that reached clinical testing were no better in animal models than the 912 that did not (31.3% vs 24.4%) [[OCollins2006]]. Translational targets continue to be reviewed [[Chamorro2016]].
- Persistent depolarisation produces cytotoxic oedema and the diffusion-weighted core, while transient spreading depolarisations in the penumbra expand the lesion [[Hartings2017]]. The inverse haemodynamic response turns the normal flow rise into a fall [[Dreier2011]].
- In human injured cortex, spread was 3.3 mm/min (range 0.41-10) [[Fabricius2006]].
- In 16 people with malignant MCA infarction monitored for 1,638 hours, there were 127 cortical spreading depressions and 42 peri-infarct depolarisations, seen in all but two people [[Dohmen2008]].

### cascade · Inflammation and the blood-brain barrier
- Inflammation is involved at all stages, along with stroke-related immunodepression; extracellular ATP rises within minutes [[Iadecola2011]]. The immune response is double-edged [[Iadecola2020]].
- Matrix metalloproteinases mediate a reversible early barrier opening, followed by a delayed second opening at 24-72 hours; early opening in the thrombolysis window raises haemorrhage risk, and the hyperintense acute reperfusion marker (HARM) was found in one-third of people with ischaemic stroke [[Yang2011]]. Barrier dysfunction and recovery are reviewed elsewhere [[Jiang2018]].

### cascade · Collaterals, fast and slow progressors
- Collateral circulation and its role in tissue survival during occlusion are reviewed in these papers [[Liebeskind2003,Mangiardi2023]].
- In 94 people with untreated occlusion, time showed no correlation with infarct growth (r=-0.05); growth with poor vs good collaterals was 52.3 vs 0.9 cm3, and median penumbral salvage 0 vs 5.9 cm3 [[Vagal2018]].
- In 196 people, collaterals predicted good outcome, odds ratio 1.93 (1.06-3.34) [[Lima2010]].
- With poor collaterals, favourable outcome fell with delay: onset-to-puncture of 300 minutes or under 59% vs over 300 minutes 32%; puncture-to-reperfusion of 60 minutes or under 73% vs over 60 minutes 32%. Time had no significant effect on outcome with good collaterals [[Hwang2015]].
- In one trial imaging analysis, median final infarct volume was 54.5 mL with intra-arterial therapy vs 81.8 mL in controls [[Boers2017]].
- In the SELECT study, slow progressors were those with an early infarct growth rate under 10 mL/h (200 slow, 85 fast); fast progressors had a higher median NIHSS (19 vs 15) and shorter time from last known well to groin puncture (180 vs 266 minutes); functional independence was 61% in slow vs 35% in fast progressors, adjusted odds ratio 2.94, and odds fell 14% for every 5 mL/h [[Sarraj2021]].
- Multiphase CT angiography offers a practical collateral triage tool [[Menon2015]].
- Mapping predictions: with over 90% reperfusion, early diffusion volume correlated with final infarct at r=0.95; with under 10% reperfusion, Tmax over 6 seconds correlated at r=0.86 [[Wheeler2013]].
- Late-window results depend on this biology: DEFUSE 3 independence 45% vs 17% [[Albers2018]]; DAWN 49% vs 13% [[Nogueira2018]]. HERMES common odds ratio 2.49 (1.76-3.53), number needed to treat 2.6 [[Goyal2016]]. The general overview is in [[Campbell2019Primer]].

### cascade · Reperfusion injury and no-reflow
- Reperfusion injury is defined and no-reflow discussed [[Bai2015]]. Mechanisms include leucocyte and platelet activation, complement, hyperperfusion and barrier damage [[Pan2007]].
- "No study has quantified this effect in stroke patients" (on reperfusion injury as a share of damage) [[Nour2013]].
- Incomplete microcirculatory reperfusion is proposed as a target [[Dalkara2012]]. In 33 people with complete recanalisation, one met tissue no-reflow criteria [[TerSchiphorst2021]].
- Futile recanalisation is the gap between reopening and recovery [[Nie2018]].
- In one series haemorrhagic transformation occurred in 46.4% and symptomatic haemorrhage in 18.9%; poor collaterals with recanalisation carried an odds ratio of 2.666 for haemorrhagic transformation [[Bang2011HT]].

### causes · Classification systems
- TOAST defines five subtypes: large-artery atherosclerosis, cardioembolism, small-vessel occlusion, other determined aetiology and undetermined aetiology, each possible or probable; among 20 patients two neurologists disagreed in one, with a specific diagnosis in 11 and undetermined in 9 [[Adams1993]].
- TOAST cutoffs as restated in a later review: large-artery atherosclerosis needs stenosis over 50% in the corresponding artery; small-vessel occlusion is limited to subcortical lesions under 15 mm; larger subcortical infarcts and embolic infarcts with under 50% stenosis become undetermined, about 40% of cases in that category [[Kim2014]].
- TOAST inter-observer agreement was moderate (0.4 to 0.6 kappa) and lowest for small-vessel occlusion (0.53) and unknown cause (0.40). SSS-TOAST and CCS widened small vessel to 20 mm, and SSS-TOAST reduced undetermined to 4% [[Kim2014]].
- The Causative Classification System reached kappa 0.80-0.81 [[Arsava2010]].
- ASCOD phenotypes: atherosclerosis, small-vessel disease, cardiac pathology, other cause, dissection. Grades: 1 potentially causal, 2 causal link uncertain, 3 unlikely but disease present, 0 absent, 9 insufficient workup. The updated system lowered a stenosis cutoff from 70% to 50% [[Amarenco2013]].

### causes · Proportions by population
- Pooled over 65 population studies: cardioembolic 22% (20-23), large-artery 23% (21-25), small-artery 22% (21-24), other 3%, undetermined 26% (24-28); white populations cardioembolic 28% (26-29), Asian populations large-artery 33% (31-36). Trends per year: cardioembolic in white populations +2.4%, large-artery in Asian populations +5.7%, small-artery in white populations -4.7% [[Ornello2018]].
- German Stroke Data Bank (5,017): cardioembolic 25.6%, large-artery 20.9%, small-vessel 20.5% [[Grau2001]].
- London (1,181): large-artery 9.3%, cardioembolic 27.8%, small-vessel 27.0%, other 3.4%, undetermined 24.2%, concurrent 8.2%; black vs white incidence rate ratio 1.25 [[Hajat2011]].
- Auckland: ischaemic 81%, intracerebral 13%, subarachnoid 5%; of ischaemic strokes, cardioembolic 29%, small-vessel 21%, large-artery 15%, other 5%, undetermined 31% [[Krishnamurthi2018]].
- Erlangen incidence per 100,000: cardioembolic 30.2, small-artery 25.8, large-artery 15.3; large-artery in men 23.6 vs women 9.2 [[Kolominsky2001]].
- Rochester incidence per 100,000: large-artery 27, cardioembolic 40, lacunar 25, uncertain 52, other 4 [[Petty1999]].
- Northern Manhattan: ischaemic stroke per 100,000 was 88 in white, 149 in Hispanic and 191 in black people; black vs white rate ratios were intracranial 5.85, extracranial 3.18, lacunar 3.09 and cardioembolic 1.58 [[White2005]].
- Pooled Chinese vs white comparison: intracerebral haemorrhage 33% vs 12%, without clear evidence of different ischaemic subtypes [[Tsai2013]].
- In one TIA clinic, cardioembolic share rose from 26% to 56% between 2002 and 2012 [[Bogiatzi2014]].
- Global burden in 2019: 12.2 million incident strokes, 101 million prevalent, 6.55 million deaths [[Feigin2021]]. INTERSTROKE: 10 factors with population-attributable risk of 90.7%; hypertension OR 2.98, attributable risk 47.9% [[ODonnell2016]]. Genetics: 32 loci in 520,000 subjects [[Malik2018]].

### causes · Large-artery atherosclerosis
- Intracranial large-artery disease is called the most common subtype worldwide by one review [[Gorelick2008]]. Annual symptomatic intracranial disease incidence: 3, 13 and 15 per 100,000 in white, Hispanic and African American people (9%, 15% and 17% of strokes); 20-40 per 100,000 intracranial atherosclerosis events; autopsy prevalence 43%, 65% and 80% with increasing age [[Banerjee2017]].
- In the Rotterdam Study intracranial carotid calcification contributed to 75% of strokes, aortic arch to 45% and extracranial carotid to 25% [[Bos2014]].
- In Greater Cincinnati in 2005 (2,204 ischaemic strokes), large-vessel causes were 16.6%, internal carotid stenosis 8.0%, and occlusion and intracranial disease 3.5% each [[Flaherty2013]].
- SAMMPRIS: stenting vs aggressive medical management, stroke or death at 30 days 14.7% vs 5.8% and at 1 year 20.0% vs 12.2% [[Chimowitz2011]].

### causes · Small vessel disease
- Small vessel disease involves endothelial dysfunction and barrier leak [[Wardlaw2019,Pantoni2010]].
- A lesion over 20 mm is likely striatocapsular; lacunar lesions on diffusion imaging went on to cavitate in 28-94% [[Wardlaw2013]].

### causes · Cardioembolism and atrial fibrillation
- Framingham: AF raised stroke risk nearly fivefold; attributable risk was 1.5% at 50-59 and 23.5% at 80-89 [[Wolf1991]].
- Population-based registry: AF in 24.6% of 3,530 first-ever strokes; case fatality 32.5% at 30 days, 49.5% at 1 year [[Marini2005]].
- International survey (19 centres in 19 countries): AF in 28% of 2,144 people with ischaemic stroke; North America 35%, Europe 33%, Latin America 17%; prior AF 15%, new on ECG 10%, found with monitoring 2%; mean age 75 vs 64; 30-day mortality 10% vs 4% [[Perera2016]].
- Cardioembolism: AF affects 33 million, raises risk 3-5 fold, oral anticoagulants prevent up to 70%; PFO in 25% [[Kamel2017]].
- CRYSTAL AF (441 people, implantable monitor vs control): detection 8.9% vs 1.4% at 6 months, HR 6.4; 12.4% vs 2.0% at 12 months, HR 7.3 [[Sanna2014]]. Extended: 3.7%, 8.9%, 12.4% and 30.0% at 1, 6, 12 and 36 months, 3.0% in control at 36 months, median 8.4 months, 81% asymptomatic [[Brachmann2016]].
- EMBRACE (30-day event monitor vs 24-hour Holter): AF 16.1% vs 3.2%, number needed to screen 8; episodes of at least 2.5 minutes 9.9% vs 2.5%; anticoagulation 18.6% vs 11.1% [[Gladstone2014]].
- By sequential phases of monitoring, AF was newly found in 7.7%, 5.1%, 10.7% and 16.9%, overall 23.7% [[Sposato2015]].
- LOOP (screening with loop recorder): AF 31.8% vs 12.2%, stroke HR 0.80 (0.61-1.05), not significant [[Svendsen2021]].
- ASSERT: subclinical atrial tachyarrhythmia in 10.1%, stroke HR 2.49 [[Healey2012]]. ARTESIA: apixaban vs aspirin 0.78% vs 1.24% per year, HR 0.63; major bleeding 1.71% vs 0.94%, HR 1.80 [[Healey2024]].

### causes · ESUS and cryptogenic stroke
- ESUS was proposed for the about 25% of strokes that are cryptogenic: non-lacunar infarct without proximal stenosis or a cardioembolic source [[Hart2014]].
- ESUS accounts for 9-25% of ischaemic strokes, average 17%, mean age 65, NIHSS 5, recurrence 4.5% per year [[Hart2017]].
- NAVIGATE ESUS: rivaroxaban 5.1% vs aspirin 4.8% per year, HR 1.07; bleeding 1.8% vs 0.7%, HR 2.72 [[Hart2018]]. RE-SPECT ESUS: dabigatran 4.1% vs aspirin 4.8% per year, HR 0.85 (0.69-1.03) [[Diener2019]]. ARCADIA: apixaban 4.4% vs aspirin 4.4% per year, HR 1.00 [[Kamel2024]].
- ESUS is not a synonym for cryptogenic stroke [[Ntaios2020]]. Cryptogenic share is reported as 30-40% in one review [[Yaghi2017Cryptogenic]] and 20-40% in another [[Sanna2014]].

### causes · Young people and dissection
- Helsinki (1,008 aged 15-49): cardioembolic 20%, dissection 15%, large-artery 8%, small-vessel 14%, undetermined 33%; incidence 10.8 per 100,000; dyslipidaemia 60%, smoking 44%, hypertension 39%; silent infarcts 13% [[Putaala2009]]. Young stroke incidence is rising [[Ekker2018]].
- Dissection presents with headache, neck pain, Horner syndrome or cranial nerve palsy, and most people then have stroke or TIA [[Debette2009]].
- Population incidence 2.6 per 100,000 (carotid 1.72, vertebral 0.97); head or neck pain 80%, ischaemia 56%, Horner 25%; good outcome 92% [[Lee2006]]. CADISP compared carotid and vertebral disease in 982 people [[Debette2011]].
- Age 60 or over made up 7.4%, with less pain [[Traenka2017]].
- CADISS: stroke recurrence 2% (4 of 250), 3 of 126 vs 1 of 124 (antiplatelet vs anticoagulant); imaging failed to confirm dissection in 52 [[Markus2015]]. TREAT-CAD: composite 23% vs 15%, aspirin not shown non-inferior; ischaemic strokes 7 vs 0 [[Engelter2021]].

### causes · Patent foramen ovale
- PFO at autopsy: 27.3% of 965, falling by age from 34.3% to 25.4% to 20.2%; mean size 4.9 mm [[Hagen1984]].
- Adults under 55 with stroke: PFO 40% vs 10% in controls, and 54% in the 26 people with no identifiable cause [[Lechat1988]]. Under 55 years: PFO OR 3.10, atrial septal aneurysm 6.14, both 15.59; cryptogenic vs known cause OR 6.00 [[Overell2000]].
- RoPE: 10-point score; PFO prevalence rises from 23% to 73% across scores, attributable fraction 0% to 90%, recurrence 20% to 2% [[Kent2013]].
- CLOSE: 0 of 238 vs 14 of 235, HR 0.03; AF 4.6% vs 0.9% [[Mas2017]]. RESPECT: 0.58 vs 1.07 per 100 person-years, HR 0.55 [[Saver2017]]. REDUCE: 1.4% vs 5.4%, HR 0.23; AF 6.6% [[Sondergaard2017]].
- SCOPE pooled (3,740): PFO-associated stroke about 10% of ischaemic stroke at ages 18-60; annual stroke 1.09% vs 0.47% with closure, adjusted HR 0.41. By PASCAL category HR was unlikely 1.14, possible 0.38, probable 0.10; 2-year absolute risk reduction -0.7%, 2.1%, 2.1%; AF excess 4.41%, 1.53%, 0.65% [[Kent2021]].

### causes · Cancer, sickle cell, genetic and other
- Cancer: 279,719 matched pairs; 6-month stroke 3.0% vs 1.6%, HR 1.9; arterial thromboembolism 4.7% vs 2.2%, HR 2.2; lung cancer highest; resolves by 1 year [[Navi2017]]. Risk rises from 150 days before diagnosis, last 30 days 0.62% vs 0.11%, OR 5.63 [[Navi2019]]. Active cancer in 5-10% of ESUS [[Navi2021]].
- Sickle cell: in the Cooperative Study of Sickle Cell Disease (4,082 people), stroke prevalence in sickle cell anaemia 4.01% and incidence 0.61 per 100 person-years; 2-week mortality from haemorrhagic stroke 26% [[OheneFrempong1998]].
- STOP trial: children with transcranial Doppler velocity of 200 cm/s or more, transfusion to keep HbS under 30%; 130 children; standard care 10 infarcts plus 1 haematoma vs 1 infarct with transfusion, a 92% difference [[Adams1998]].
- TWiTCH: 143 vs 138 cm/s, hydroxycarbamide non-inferior to transfusion for TCD velocity [[Ware2016]]. SIT trial for silent infarcts: 2.0 vs 4.8 per 100 years, incidence rate ratio 0.41 [[DeBaun2014]]. Silent infarct is a risk factor for adults too [[Jordan2018]].
- ASH 2020 guideline: TCD and hydroxyurea in low-middle income settings; MRI once in early school age and once in adults [[DeBaun2020]].
- CADASIL: most common heritable cause, NOTCH3 cysteine changes [[Chabriat2009]]. In 411 people, median age at stroke 50.7 men, 52.5 women; death 64.6 and 70.7; 78% dependent [[Opherk2004]]. In 102 cases: ischaemic episodes 71% (46.1 years), migraine 38% with aura in 87%, dementia 28% [[Dichgans1998]].
- Fabry: 138 people with stroke, age 39.0 men, 45.7 women; 50% and 38.3% before diagnosis [[Sims2009]].
- Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome: 67 cases, multiple thunderclap headaches in 94% [[Ducros2007]].

### tia · Definition
- Tissue definition from the AHA/ASA 2009: "a transient episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal brain, spinal cord, or retinal ischemia, without acute infarction" [[Easton2009]]. This followed a 2002 proposal [[Albers2002]]. The stroke definition was updated in 2013 to tissue-based CNS infarction [[Sacco2013]].
- The ESO keeps a pragmatic clinical definition of under 24 hours [[Fonseca2021]], while others say the 24-hour limit should be abandoned, as up to 50% have infarct on diffusion imaging [[Nadarajan2014]].
- Pooled DWI positivity 34.3% (30.5-38.4), range 9-67% [[Brazzelli2014]]. In 87 consecutive people with TIA, 41.3% had acute infarct; in-hospital recurrent stroke or TIA was 19.4% with transient symptoms with infarction vs 1.3% with ischaemic stroke [[Ay2005]]. TIAregistry.org: 33.4% infarct [[Amarenco2016]].

### tia · Early stroke risk
- Emergency department (1,707 people): 10.5% stroke at 90 days, 91 of 180 in the first 2 days; adverse events 25.1%; OR age 1.8, diabetes 2.0, duration 2.3, weakness 1.9, speech 1.5 [[Johnston2000]].
- Oxford population: TIA 8.0% at 7 days, 11.5% at 1 month, 17.3% at 3 months; minor stroke 11.5%, 15.0%, 18.5% [[Coull2004]]. Measured from onset: 8.6% at 7 days and 12.0% at 30 days; from neurologist assessment only 1.9% and 4.4% [[Lovett2003]].
- Meta-analysis: 5.2% (3.9-6.5) at 7 days; range 0-12.8%; specialist emergency treatment 0.9% vs population without urgent treatment 11.0% [[Giles2007]].
- First hours: 1.2%, 2.1%, 5.1% at 6, 12, 24 hours; 42% of 30-day strokes within 24 hours [[Chandratheva2009]].
- Of strokes, 23% had a preceding TIA; 17% the same day, 9% the previous day, 43% within 7 days [[RothwellWarlow2005]].
- TIAregistry.org (4,789 people, 61 sites, 21 countries, 78.4% seen within 24 hours): stenosis of 50% or more 23.2%, AF 10.4%; stroke 1.5%, 2.1%, 2.8%, 3.7%, 5.1% at 2, 7, 30, 90, 365 days; composite 6.2% [[Amarenco2016]]. Five years (3,847): composite 12.9%, stroke 9.5%, 43.2% in years 2-5, death 10.6% [[Amarenco2018]].
- PERSIST meta-analysis (171,068): 5.94 per 100 person-years in the first year, 1.80 in years 2-5, 1.72 in years 6-10; cumulative 12.5% at 5 years, 19.8% at 10 years [[Khan2025]].
- Framingham: 1.19 per 1,000 person-years; 90-day stroke 16.7% (1948-85), 11.1%, 5.9% (2000-17); adjusted HR 4.37; 21.5% of strokes within 7 days, 48.5% after 1 year [[Lioutas2021]].

### tia · ABCD and ABCD2
- ABCD2 items: Age 60 or over = 1; Blood pressure 140/90 mmHg or higher = 1; Clinical features unilateral weakness = 2, speech disturbance without weakness = 1; Duration 60 minutes or more = 2, 10-59 minutes = 1; Diabetes = 1 [[Johnston2007]].
- ABCD2 risk bands (2-day stroke): 6-7 high, 21% of patients, 8.1%; 4-5 moderate, 45%, 4.1%; 0-3 low, 34%, 1.0%; c statistic 0.62-0.83 [[Johnston2007]].
- Original ABCD score: 7-day risk 0.4% under 5, 12.1% at 5, 31.4% at 6 [[Rothwell2005]].
- ABCD2 limitations: 29 studies, 13,766 people; score of 4 or more sensitivity 86.7%, specificity 35.4%; 20% under 4 had carotid stenosis over 50% or AF; 35-41% of mimics and 66% of true TIAs scored 4 or more; 45% were mimics; 52% scored 4 or more [[Wardlaw2015]].
- Meta-analysis: sensitivity 0.89, specificity 0.34, AUC 0.70; at a 5% baseline, over 3 adds 2.0% and 3 or under lowers 2.9% [[Sanders2012]].
- ABCD3-I adds dual TIA +2, carotid stenosis of 50% or more +2, DWI lesion +2; c statistic ABCD3 0.78-0.80; ABCD3-I 0.90 at 2 days and 0.92 at 7 days [[Merwick2010]].
- Canadian TIA Score (7,607): low 0.5% or less, medium 2.3%, high 5.9%; AUC 0.70 vs ABCD2 0.60 and ABCD2i 0.64 [[Perry2021]].
- CT/CTA with MRI: 510 people; recurrence 7.1%; CT/CTA abnormal HR 4.0 [[Coutts2012]].

### tia · Urgent assessment
- EXPRESS (Oxford): 90-day stroke 10.3% (32 of 310) before vs 2.1% (6 of 281) after, adjusted HR 0.20 (0.08-0.49); delay to assessment fell from 3 days to under 1 day, first prescription from 20 days to 1 day [[Rothwell2007]]. Fatal or disabling 16 of 310 vs 1 of 281; bed days 1,365 vs 427; saving GBP 624 per patient [[LuengoFernandez2009]].
- SOS-TIA (Paris, 1,085 people): 53% seen within 24 hours; 65% confirmed TIA or minor stroke, 13% possible; 74% home the same day; 90-day stroke 1.24% vs 5.96% predicted; carotid revascularisation 5%, AF anticoagulated 5% [[Lavallee2007]].
- AHA 2023 scientific statement: at least 240,000 TIA per year in the US; 90-day risk as high as 17.8%, almost half in 2 days; protocols with acute imaging [[Amin2023]].
- NICE 1.1.4: "Offer aspirin (300 mg daily), unless contraindicated, to people who have had a suspected TIA, to be started immediately. [2019]" [[NICE-NG128]].
- NICE 1.1.5: "Refer immediately people who have had a suspected TIA for specialist assessment and investigation, to be seen within 24 hours of onset of symptoms. [2019]" [[NICE-NG128]].
- NICE 1.1.6: "Do not use scoring systems, such as ABCD2, to assess risk of subsequent stroke or to inform urgency of referral for people who have had a suspected or confirmed TIA. [2019]" [[NICE-NG128]].
- NICE also says: secondary prevention as soon as possible after diagnosis is confirmed (1.1.7, 2008, amended 2019); no CT unless clinical suspicion of alternative diagnosis (1.2.1, 2019); consider MRI (diffusion-weighted and blood-sensitive sequences) after specialist assessment, on the same day (1.2.2, 2019); FAST in the community (1.1.1), exclude hypoglycaemia (1.1.2), ROSIER (1.1.3); committee notes 30-50% of suspected TIA have an alternative diagnosis [[NICE-NG128]].
- AHA 2009 considered hospitalisation reasonable within 72 hours with ABCD2 of 3 or more, and imaging within 24 hours [[Easton2009]]. ESO: high risk as ABCD2 of 4 or more, specialist review within 24 hours, strong recommendation for dual antiplatelet therapy, against prediction tools alone [[Fonseca2021]].

### tia · Aspirin and antiplatelet time course
- Rothwell's pooled analysis (15,778 people from 12 trials): 6-week recurrent ischaemic stroke HR 0.42 (0.32-0.55), disabling or fatal 0.29 (0.20-0.42) [[Rothwell2016]].
- TIA or minor stroke, weeks 0-2: 2 of 6,691 vs 23 of 5,726, HR 0.07; weeks 0-6 HR 0.19; severity OR 0.42 [[Rothwell2016]].
- No benefit after 12 weeks, OR 0.97; 13% long-term reduction; dipyridamole benefit only after 12 weeks, 0.76; major-stroke trials 40,531 people, 2-3 day HR 0.37; independent of dose [[Rothwell2016]].
- CHANCE: 8.2% vs 11.7%, HR 0.68 [[Wang2013]]. POINT: 5.0% vs 6.5%, HR 0.75; major haemorrhage 0.9% vs 0.4%, HR 2.32 [[Johnston2018]]. Pooled: 6.5% vs 9.1%, HR 0.70; first 21 days 5.2% vs 7.8%, HR 0.66 [[Pan2019]].
- THALES: 5.5% vs 6.6%, HR 0.83; severe bleeding 0.5% vs 0.1% [[Johnston2020]]. Short course RR 0.68, long course 0.89, bleeding with long course 2.42 [[Brown2021]].

### tia · Mimics
- 303 people: TIA 81.8%, mimics 18.2%; seizures 26 of 55 and migraine 13 of 55; unilateral paresis OR 0.35, memory loss OR 9.17, headache OR 3.71 [[Amort2011]].
- Up to 60% of referrals had no TIA; in 1,532 people 75% TIA, 3% minor stroke, 22% mimics of 25 diagnoses; mimics include migraine aura, seizure, syncope and functional disorders [[Nadarajan2014]].
- Pattern: TIA has negative symptoms maximal at onset, usually under 1 hour; aura 10-30 minutes and spreading; seizure seconds, up to about 5 minutes; syncope seconds; 15-30% of ischaemic strokes preceded by TIA [[Nadarajan2014]].
- Misdiagnosis: clinic 45.8%, inpatient 60.0% [[Sadighi2019]]. Non-neurologists: GP confirmed 19%, emergency department 13%; 31% and 55% noncerebrovascular [[Ferro1996]]. Stroke neurologists agree poorly [[Castle2010]].
- Hyperacute stroke unit: 24.2% of 2,305 admissions were mimics [[Dawson2016]]. Brain attack: 31% mimics [[Hand2006]]. General overview: [[Lui2026]].

### In plain words
- vessels: Your brain gets blood from four main arteries at the neck, joined by a ring at the base of the skull. Only some people have a full ring [[Hindenes2020]].
- vessels: The brain is about 2% of your weight but takes 15-20% of the blood your heart pumps [[Cox2026]].
- vessels: Where the stroke is decides what it affects. A stroke in one artery can affect speech or the leg more than the arm, for example [[Lo2025]].
- vessels: A person with locked-in syndrome can think and hear, but may move only their eyes and eyelids. Many live for decades and report meaningful quality of life [[Laureys2005]].
- cascade: When an artery blocks, the centre dies fast. Around it, tissue is struggling but can still recover if blood comes back soon [[Baron2021]].
- cascade: On average about 1.9 million brain cells can be lost each minute in a large stroke [[Saver2006]].
- cascade: Backup blood vessels buy time. This is why two people can have very different damage after the same delay [[Vagal2018]].
- causes: Many strokes come from clots made in the heart, blocked or narrowed arteries, or tiny vessel disease. In about one in four the cause stays unknown after tests [[Ornello2018]].
- causes: In younger people, a tear in a neck artery is one common cause [[Putaala2009]].
- causes: A hidden irregular heartbeat can take months of monitoring to find [[Brachmann2016]].
- tia: A TIA is a warning stroke. The symptoms go away and a scan shows no lasting damage [[Easton2009]].
- tia: The risk of a full stroke is highest in the first days, so being seen fast matters [[Giles2007]].
- tia: Fast clinics and quick treatment cut the risk of stroke at 90 days from about 10 in 100 to about 2 in 100 in one study [[Rothwell2007]].

## Open questions and controversies
- How common is a complete Circle of Willis: 42% on 1.5T with a healthy group [[KrabbeHartkamp1998]]; 11.9% on 3T at a 1 mm threshold in an older population sample [[Hindenes2020]]; differences reflect method and age, unresolved.
- How common are ACA strokes: approximately 20% in a review [[MatosCasano2026]]; 1.3% to 1.8% in registries [[Kumral2002,Bogousslavsky1990]]; registry counts are direct, the review figure is not sourced in its abstract.
- Lacunar share: about a quarter [[Arboix2009]]; "nearly one-third" [[Regenhardt2018]].
- Human penumbral thresholds: textbook numbers rest largely on animals, and reported human ranges vary too much to pool [[Bandera2006]].
- Penumbra lifespan: short hours in early models [[Hakim1998,Hossmann1994]]; up to and perhaps beyond 24 hours in selected people [[Baron2021]]; collaterals largely account for the difference [[Vagal2018]].
- How much harm is true reperfusion injury: "no study has quantified this effect in stroke patients" [[Nour2013]]; proposed as a target [[Bai2015]]; status (Hypothesis).
- No-reflow: incomplete microvascular reperfusion is proposed as common [[Dalkara2012]]; in 33 complete recanalisations only 1 met criteria [[TerSchiphorst2021]]; status (Early evidence).
- Neuroprotection: strong animal data [[Aarts2002]]; neutral overall in humans [[Hill2020]]; poor translation is a known pattern [[OCollins2006]].
- Which subtype leads in Asia: large-artery disease at 33% [[Ornello2018]]; no clear difference in ischaemic subtypes in Chinese vs white comparison [[Tsai2013]].
- Cryptogenic share varies with definition and workup: about 25% [[Hart2014]]; 30-40% [[Yaghi2017Cryptogenic]]; and ESUS 17% on average [[Hart2017]].
- Whether screening for AF prevents stroke: detection rises [[Sanna2014,Gladstone2014]]; LOOP stroke effect not significant, HR 0.80 (0.61-1.05) [[Svendsen2021]]; device-detected AF is a bleeding trade-off [[Healey2024]].
- Antithrombotic choice in dissection: CADISS found no clear difference [[Markus2015]]; TREAT-CAD did not show aspirin non-inferior [[Engelter2021]].
- TIA definition: tissue [[Easton2009]] vs pragmatic clinical definition under 24 hours [[Fonseca2021]].
- Role of ABCD2: AHA 2009 uses a score of 3 or more for hospitalisation [[Easton2009]]; ESO uses 4 or more for dual antiplatelet therapy but not alone for triage [[Fonseca2021]]; NICE says do not use it for urgency [[NICE-NG128]]; meta-analysis finds poor specificity [[Wardlaw2015]].
- TIA early risk spread: 5.2% pooled at 7 days [[Giles2007]]; 2.1% in a modern registry of urgently treated people [[Amarenco2016]]; 8.0% in an older population cohort [[Coull2004]]; setting and treatment explain much of this.

## Provenance
- All sources validated on 2026-11-08: PubMed esummary for citation records (tool=medcompanions), Europe PMC for abstracts, PMC for open full text, nice.org.uk for NG128.
- Reused keys from existing repository sources where the same PMID was present; text kept. Feigin2021 is the GBD 2019 Stroke Collaborators paper, under the key used by other passes.
- Adams1993 full text returned 403: only the five subtypes and the reliability test come from the abstract. TOAST numeric cutoffs (over 50% stenosis, under 15 mm) come from the review Kim2014, not the original. Remaining criteria items are not recorded here.
- Bamford1991 is paywalled: subtype shares and qualitative natural history come from the abstract. Numeric mortality by subtype could not be verified and is omitted. OCSP bedside criteria are also not recorded verbatim for the same reason.
- Amin2023 (AHA 2023 statement): abstract only; no class or level of recommendation is quoted.
- Astrup1981 and Albers2002 have no abstract: cited only as the naming source and the proposal.
- Kleindorfer2021 has no abstract and was not read, so it is not cited. Raichle2002 could not be retrieved and is omitted. The brain 2% of body weight figure comes from a background statement in Cox2026, a rodent paper, for lack of a better open source. Berrada2025 is a case report; its thresholds are background statements.
- Amort2011: the abstract's percentage for seizures (43.7%) is inconsistent with its count (26 of 55, which is 47.3%), so only the counts are given.
- MatosCasano2026, Benjamin2026MCA, Benjamin2026PCA, EastonCarr2026, Das2023, Gore2026, Venkataraman2024, Rosner2023 and Lui2026 are StatPearls chapters whose abstracts were read through Europe PMC; NCBI Bookshelf itself blocked automated access.
