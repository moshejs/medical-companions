Part 1 · Understanding heart attack

Ischaemia: time is muscle

When a clot blocks a coronary artery, the heart muscle it feeds is starved of oxygen (ischaemia). Within minutes to hours it begins to die, from the inner layer of the wall outward. The sooner blood flow is restored, the more muscle can be saved. [[Reimer1977,Gersh2005JAMA]]

Call nowIf you think you or someone else is having a heart attack, call your local emergency number now (911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 112 in the EU).

Schematic. The wavefront numbers come from dogs with a total proximal occlusion (Reimer and Jennings). In people the timing varies with collateral flow and preconditioning, so these hours are not a deadline for anyone. [[Reimer1977,Reimer1979]]

Every 30 minutes counts

Studies in people agree on the direction: earlier reperfusion goes with fewer deaths and smaller infarcts. The exact shape of the curve is uncertain. [[Gersh2005JAMA,Boersma1996,DeLuca2004]] Drag the time and switch between three kinds of evidence. These are population statistics from trials and cohorts, not predictions for one person.

What happens to the muscle that survives

Established

Stunning

Muscle saved by reperfusion can stay weak for a while: prolonged contractile dysfunction after ischaemia, in muscle that is alive. Ischaemia may "hit, run and stun". [[Braunwald1982]] Proposed mechanisms include oxygen free radicals and altered calcium handling; stunning has been seen after angioplasty, unstable angina, stress-induced ischaemia, thrombolysis and cardiopulmonary bypass. [[Kloner2001Stunning]]

In plain words: some muscle that stops working after a heart attack is only stunned and can recover over days to weeks. [[Braunwald1982]]

Established

Hibernation

Hibernating myocardium is weak at rest because of reduced coronary blood flow, and can improve partly or fully with revascularisation or by lowering oxygen demand. It is distinct from stunning, which follows a bout of ischaemia that has ended. [[Rahimtoola1989]] Revascularisation choices are in Stents or bypass?

No proven therapy

Reperfusion injury

Restoring flow can itself kill heart cells, and as of 2013 there was no effective therapy for it. [[Hausenloy2013JCI]] Cells die by necrosis, apoptosis, necroptosis and pyroptosis, and the small vessels are injured too. Many treatments that shrink infarcts in animals have translated poorly into better outcomes for patients. [[Heusch2020]] See Research toward better treatment.

Established

No-reflow

In dogs, after 40 minutes of ischaemia, tracers reached the damaged tissue evenly on reperfusion. After 90 minutes, parts of the inner wall were not reperfused because of severe capillary damage and cell swelling. The most severely ischaemic cells died before the capillary damage began. [[Kloner1974NoReflow]] In people, microvascular obstruction rose with delay, from 0.5% to 6.6% of the LV. [[Francone2009]]

Established

Preconditioning

Four 5-minute occlusions before a 40-minute occlusion limited infarct size to 25% of that in controls, but gave no benefit with a 3-hour occlusion. [[Murry1986]] It has been seen in "virtually every species" studied. [[Kloner2001Stunning]] Hypothesis Angina in the days before an infarct, and the angina "warm-up" phenomenon, may be human counterparts. [[Kloner2001Stunning]]

Established

Why infarct size matters

Infarct size is a major determinant of prognosis. [[Heusch2020]] Across 10 primary-PCI trials, each 5% larger infarct was associated with higher 1-year all-cause mortality (adjusted HR 1.19) and hospitalisation for heart failure (HR 1.20), but not with reinfarction.
