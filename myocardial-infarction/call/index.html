Minute 0 · symptoms start

The clock starts at the first symptom

Both people start to feel unwell at the same moment. What happens next depends largely on how soon help is called. An AHA scientific statement calls patient delay "the major factor limiting delivery of definitive treatment" for acute coronary syndromes. 309 In the international GRACE registry, the median time before reaching hospital was 2.3 hours for STEMI and 3.0 hours for NSTEMI. 166 ESC 2023 lists "prolonged chest pain (>15 min) and/or recurrent pain within 1 h" as red flags for contacting emergency services. 46

In plain wordsHeart muscle starts to suffer as soon as the artery blocks. The sooner help is called, the more muscle can be saved.
Exam hookGRACE median prehospital delay: 2.3 h for STEMI, 3.0 h for NSTEMI and unstable angina. 166

Why minutes matter

In a classic animal experiment, the share of threatened heart wall that died rose from 38% after 40 minutes of blocked flow to 57% at 3 hours, 71% at 6 hours and 85% at 24 hours, moving like a wavefront from the inner layer outward. 365 In people treated with clot-dissolving drugs, 65 lives were saved per 1000 treated in the first hour after symptoms began, against 26–29 per 1000 at 2–6 hours. 34 The time-is-muscle module animates this in detail, and Reperfusion shows how the artery is opened.

If the heart stops: links in the chain of survival

A heart attack can trigger cardiac arrest, when the heart stops pumping. The 2025 American Heart Association (AHA) basic life support guideline covers recognising cardiac arrest, activating the emergency response, high-quality CPR and using an automated external defibrillator (AED). 237 Its executive summary stresses high-quality chest compressions and early defibrillation, and its systems-of-care chapter covers lay rescuers, public-access defibrillators and the role of emergency dispatchers. 87,94 Pick a link to see what the evidence says about it.

Teaching model. The study reports an adjusted odds ratio of 0.91 for each minute of delay; the curve repeats that figure minute after minute, which assumes a constant effect. Population statistics, not a prediction for anyone. 234

3.7 minutes to the first shock

Every minute counts

In Japan's nationwide registry of witnessed arrests in ventricular fibrillation, each minute of delay to the first shock lowered the odds of surviving with good brain function (adjusted OR 0.91 per minute, 95% CI 0.89–0.92). Repeating that figure, a shock at 3.7 minutes leaves about 71% of the odds of a shock at minute 0. Areas with 4 or more public AEDs per km² had a mean time to shock of 2.2 minutes, against 3.7 minutes elsewhere. 234

In plain wordsA defibrillator close by means an earlier shock, and an earlier shock means a better chance of recovering well.
Exam hookPublic AED shock in Japan: 31.6% alive with minimal brain impairment at 1 month vs 14.4% of all such patients. 234

Calling, aspirin and the trip to hospital

Why not drive yourself

The ambulance brings the hospital forward

ESC 2023 advice on patient delay, as published: "The recommended action should be to contact the EMS rather than to self-present." 46 In a US registry, ambulance transport went with a shorter median time from symptoms to arrival (89 vs 120 minutes) and to an opened artery after arrival (63 vs 76 minutes). 281 The crew can record a 12-lead ECG and warn the hospital: with a prehospital ECG, median door-to-balloon time was 61 vs 75 minutes. 100

Quoted, not an instruction

Aspirin: what the guideline and the trial say

ESC 2023 gives the aspirin loading dose for ACS as "LD of 150–300 mg orally or 75–250 mg i.v. if oral ingestion is not possible", and in its fibrinolysis section states "The first dose of aspirin (162–325 mg) should be chewed or given i.v." These are words for treating teams. 46 In ISIS-2 (17,187 patients), a month of aspirin cut 5-week vascular death from 11.8% to 9.4%. 202 If you have symptoms, call emergency services and follow the dispatcher's advice.

When to call

Red flags in the guideline

ESC 2023 lists symptoms that should prompt contact with emergency services: "prolonged chest pain (>15 min) and/or recurrent pain within 1 h". 46 Symptoms differ from person to person and can be felt in the arm, jaw, neck, back or upper belly: see Symptoms & who presents differently. If in doubt, call.

No blame

Who waits longer, and why

In four US communities, about half of people (49.5% in 2000) took 4 hours or more to reach hospital, with no significant change from 1987. Black patients and women had longer delays, although the gaps narrowed. 286 A review of 42 studies found women and older people more likely to arrive later. 329 Delay reflects access, systems and how symptoms are taught, not only personal choices. 286

After resuscitation

Straight to the cath lab?

ESC 2023: resuscitated patients with persistent ST elevation "should, in general, undergo a PPCI strategy"; without ST elevation, "routine immediate ICA is not superior to a delayed invasive strategy". 46 In COACT, 90-day survival was 64.5% with immediate vs 67.2% with delayed angiography; in TOMAHAWK, 30-day death was 54.0% vs 46.0%. 89,257

Awareness

Most people know to call; fewer know the symptoms

In US survey data, 94.9% of adults in 2017 knew to call 9-1-1 for a heart attack, but only 50.2% knew all five common symptoms: jaw, neck or back discomfort; weakness or light-headedness; chest discomfort; arm or shoulder discomfort; and shortness of breath. 117
