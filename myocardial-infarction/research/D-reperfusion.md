# D · First minutes, emergency services & reperfusion (PCI, fibrinolysis, CABG)

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: call, reperfusion, cabg.

This pass covers what happens in the first minutes and hours of a heart attack (myocardial infarction, MI): how long people wait before seeking help, what emergency medical services (EMS) add, bystander CPR and public defibrillators when a heart attack causes cardiac arrest, and how blocked arteries are reopened. Reopening (reperfusion) is done by primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI, a balloon and stent through an artery), by clot-dissolving drugs (fibrinolysis), or by bypass surgery (coronary artery bypass grafting, CABG). Trial numbers come from PubMed abstracts. ESC 2023 guideline wording was read from the full text on the publisher's site (academic.oup.com). All survival and mortality figures here are population statistics from trials and registries. They describe groups, not any one person.

Access notes:

## Findings

### call · Why minutes matter (time is muscle)
- In a dog coronary-occlusion model, the share of the at-risk wall that died rose with the length of occlusion: 38 ± 4% after 40 min, 57 ± 7% after 3 h, 71 ± 7% after 6 h and 85 ± 2% after 24 h. Necrosis moved as a "wavefront" from the inner (subendocardial) layer outward (Established, animal data) [[Reimer1977]].
- In the same model, a subepicardial zone of ischaemic but still viable muscle could be salvaged for at least 3 and perhaps 6 hours [[Reimer1977]].
- Across 22 fibrinolysis-versus-control trials (50,246 patients), the number of lives saved per 1000 treated was 65 at 0–1 h, 37 at 1–2 h, 26 at 2–3 h and 29 at 3–6 h from symptom onset. This is the "golden hour" [[Boersma1996]].
- The proportional mortality reduction from fibrinolysis was 44% (95% CI 32–53) when treatment started within 2 h, versus 20% (15–25) later (p = 0.001) [[Boersma1996]].
- In the Fibrinolytic Therapy Trialists' overview (9 trials, 58,600 patients), absolute mortality fell by about 30 per 1000 when patients with ST elevation or bundle-branch block were treated 0–6 h after onset, about 20 per 1000 at 7–12 h, and a statistically uncertain ~10 per 1000 at 13–18 h [[FTT1994]].
- In 1791 patients treated with primary angioplasty, each 30 minutes of delay carried a relative risk of 1.075 (95% CI 1.008–1.15) for 1-year mortality after adjustment. The authors titled it "every minute of delay counts" [[DeLuca2004]].

### call · How long people wait before seeking care (patient delay)
- An AHA scientific statement calls patient delay in seeking treatment "the major factor limiting delivery of definitive treatment" for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and stroke, despite decades of public campaigns [[Moser2006]].
- In the GRACE registry (14 countries, 1999–2001), median prehospital delay was 2.3 h for ST-elevation MI (STEMI), 3.0 h for non-ST-elevation MI (NSTEMI) and 3.0 h for unstable angina. Mean delays were 4.7, 6.1 and 5.6 h [[Goldberg2002GRACE]].
- In GRACE, about 41% of patients with STEMI arrived within 2 hours of symptom onset, compared with about one third of those with NSTEMI or unstable angina [[Goldberg2002GRACE]].
- In the ARIC communities (4 US communities, 18,928 hospitalised MIs), 49.5% of people in 2000 took ≥4 hours from symptom onset to hospital arrival. This proportion did not change significantly between 1987 and 2000 [[McGinn2005]].
- In ARIC, Black patients and women consistently had longer delays than white patients and men, although the gaps narrowed over time. Delay reflects access and system factors as well as individual choices [[McGinn2005]].
- A systematic review of 42 studies (1960–2008) found that women and older people were more likely to arrive at hospital later than men and younger people [[Nguyen2010]].
- In central Massachusetts, the median prehospital delay among people aged ≥65 hospitalised with MI was 2.0 h, with no significant improvement from 2001 to 2011 [[Makam2016]].

### call · Public awareness of symptoms and calling emergency services
- In US national survey data (NHIS), the adjusted share of adults who knew all five common heart attack symptoms was 39.6% in 2008, 50.0% in 2014 and 50.2% in 2017. The five are jaw, neck or back discomfort; weakness or light-headedness; chest discomfort; arm or shoulder discomfort; and shortness of breath [[Fang2019MMWR]].
- In the same survey, the share who knew to call 9-1-1 for a heart attack rose from 91.8% (2008) to 93.4% (2014) and 94.9% (2017). Persistent disparities in symptom awareness by demographic group were reported [[Fang2019MMWR]].
- ESC 2023 advice on patient delay: "The recommended action should be to contact the EMS rather than to self-present" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ESC 2023 lists red-flag symptoms that should prompt contact with EMS: "prolonged chest pain (>15 min) and/or recurrent pain within 1 h" [[Byrne2023ESC]].

### call · What EMS adds (ambulance, prehospital ECG, cath-lab activation)
- In NRMI-2 (US, 1994–1998, 772,586 MI patients), only 53.4% of patients with MI arrived by ambulance, and the share did not change over 4 years [[Canto2002]].
- In NRMI-2, EMS use was independently linked with faster door-to-fibrinolysis (12.1 min faster) and faster door-to-urgent angioplasty (31.2 min faster) [[Canto2002]].
- In the NCDR ACTION Registry–GWTG (37,634 STEMI patients, 372 US hospitals, 2007–2009), only 60% of patients used EMS transport [[Mathews2011]].
- Compared with self-transport, EMS transport was linked with a shorter median symptom-onset-to-arrival time (89 vs 120 min), a shorter median door-to-balloon time (63 vs 76 min) and a shorter median door-to-needle time (23 vs 29 min) (all P < 0.0001) [[Mathews2011]].
- In that registry, race, income and education did not appear to be linked with mode of transport. Older patients, those living farther away and those with haemodynamic compromise were more likely to use EMS [[Mathews2011]].
- In NCDR ACTION 2007, only 27.4% of EMS-transported STEMI patients had a prehospital ECG. With a prehospital ECG, median door-to-balloon time was 61 vs 75 min and median door-to-needle time 19 vs 29 min. There was a trend toward lower in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.63–1.01) [[Diercks2009]].
- In the UK MINAP audit (288,990 EMS admissions, 2005–2009), prehospital ECG use in STEMI rose from 64% to 79% [[Quinn2014]].
- In MINAP, a prehospital ECG was linked with more STEMI reperfusion (83.5% vs 74.4%), more call-to-balloon times <90 min (27.9% vs 21.4%), more door-to-needle times <30 min (90.6% vs 83.7%) and lower 30-day mortality (7.4% vs 8.2%; adjusted OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.91–0.96) [[Quinn2014]].
- In 365 US hospitals, six strategies were linked with faster door-to-balloon time. Mean reductions: emergency physicians activating the cath lab, 8.2 min; a single call to a central page operator, 13.8 min; activating the lab while the patient is en route, 15.4 min; staff expected in the lab within 20 min of the page (vs >30 min), 19.3 min; an attending cardiologist always on site, 14.6 min; and real-time data feedback, 8.6 min [[Bradley2006]].
- ESC 2023 on the first ECG: it should be "interpreted by a qualified emergency medical technician or physician within 10 min", and "an ECG should be performed within 10 min of FMC" (first medical contact) [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ESC 2023 on systems of care: "It is recommended that a regional reperfusion strategy is established", and EMS should "transport patients with a working diagnosis of STEMI to hospitals with a 24/7 service for PCI" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- The 2025 US ACS guideline was written jointly with emergency physicians (ACEP) and EMS physicians (NAEMSP) as well as ACC, AHA and SCAI. It replaces the 2013 STEMI and 2014 NSTE-ACS guidelines and the 2015 primary PCI focused update [[Rao2025ACC]].

### call · Aspirin in the first minutes (quoted, not instructions)
- In ISIS-2 (17,187 patients randomised up to 24 h after onset, median 5 h), one month of 160 mg/day enteric-coated aspirin reduced 5-week vascular mortality from 11.8% to 9.4% (odds reduction 23%, SD 4) [[ISIS2_1988]].
- In ISIS-2, streptokinase alone reduced 5-week vascular mortality from 12.0% to 9.2% (odds reduction 25%). The two together reduced it from 13.2% to 8.0% (odds reduction 42%, 95% confidence limits 34–50%) compared with neither [[ISIS2_1988]].
- In ISIS-2, aspirin also halved non-fatal reinfarction (1.0% vs 2.0%) and non-fatal stroke (0.3% vs 0.6%), without a significant increase in cerebral haemorrhage or transfusion-requiring bleeds [[ISIS2_1988]].
- ESC 2023 aspirin loading dose for ACS, as published: "LD of 150–300 mg orally or 75–250 mg i.v. if oral ingestion is not possible" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ESC 2023, in its section on antithrombotic therapy with fibrinolysis: "The first dose of aspirin (162–325 mg) should be chewed or given i.v." Quoted as published. It is clinical guidance for treating teams, not an instruction to readers; people with symptoms should call emergency services and follow the dispatcher's advice [[Byrne2023ESC]].

### call · When a heart attack causes cardiac arrest: bystander CPR and AEDs
- In Sweden (30,381 witnessed out-of-hospital cardiac arrests, 1990–2011), bystanders gave CPR before EMS arrived in 51.1% of cases. 30-day survival was 10.5% with early CPR versus 4.0% without (P < 0.001) [[HasselqvistAx2015]].
- After propensity adjustment in that study, CPR before EMS arrival was linked with higher 30-day survival (OR 2.15, 95% CI 1.88–2.45). Survival was also linked with a shorter time from collapse to the start of CPR [[HasselqvistAx2015]].
- In the Resuscitation Outcomes Consortium (13,769 out-of-hospital arrests, 2005–2007), survival to discharge was 9% with bystander CPR but no AED, 24% when an AED was applied before EMS arrival, and 38% when the AED delivered a shock [[Weisfeldt2010]].
- In that study, AED application was linked with greater survival (adjusted OR 1.75, 95% CI 1.23–2.50). The authors extrapolated about 474 lives saved per year in the US and Canada [[Weisfeldt2010]].
- In Japan 2005–2007 (bystander-witnessed VF arrests of cardiac origin), 31.6% of those shocked with a public-access AED were alive at 1 month with minimal neurological impairment, versus 14.4% of all such patients [[Kitamura2010]].
- In that Japanese study, each 1-minute delay to the first shock lowered the odds of a good neurological outcome (adjusted OR 0.91 per minute, 95% CI 0.89–0.92) [[Kitamura2010]].
- In Japan 2005–2013 (43,762 witnessed VF arrests), public-access defibrillation rose from 1.1% to 16.5% of cases. Favourable neurological survival at 1 month was 38.5% with public-access defibrillation versus 18.2% without (adjusted OR 1.99, 95% CI 1.80–2.19) [[Kitamura2016]].
- In Denmark (2001–2012), bystander CPR rose from 66.7% to 80.6% and bystander defibrillation from 2.1% to 16.8% in arrests not witnessed by EMS. Among 30-day survivors, bystander CPR was linked with lower 1-year risk of brain damage or nursing-home admission (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.47–0.82) [[Kragholm2017]].
- The 2025 AHA Adult Basic Life Support guideline covers recognising cardiac arrest, activating the emergency response, high-quality CPR and AED use [[Kleinman2025BLS]].
- The 2025 AHA guidelines emphasise "the importance of high-quality chest compressions, early defibrillation" and are organised around the Utstein Formula for Survival [[DelRios2025AHA]].
- The 2025 AHA Systems of Care chapter covers community initiatives to promote lay rescuer response, public access defibrillation and an enhanced role for emergency telecommunicators (dispatchers) [[Dezfulian2025Systems]].
- ESC 2023: resuscitated patients with persistent ST-segment elevation "should, in general, undergo a PPCI strategy (immediate ICA and PCI if indicated)" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ESC 2023: in resuscitated patients without ST elevation, "routine immediate ICA is not superior to a delayed invasive strategy". This is based on COACT and TOMAHAWK [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- COACT (552 resuscitated patients without STEMI): 90-day survival was 64.5% with immediate angiography versus 67.2% with delayed angiography (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.62–1.27) [[Lemkes2019COACT]].
- TOMAHAWK (554 resuscitated patients without ST elevation): 30-day death was 54.0% with immediate angiography versus 46.0% with delayed or selective angiography (HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.00–1.63; P = 0.06) [[Desch2021TOMAHAWK]].

### reperfusion · Primary PCI versus fibrinolysis
- Meta-analysis of 23 randomised trials (7739 patients with STEMI eligible for fibrinolysis): primary angioplasty versus fibrinolysis gave lower short-term death (7% vs 9%; p = 0.0002), non-fatal reinfarction (3% vs 7%; p < 0.0001), stroke (1% vs 2%; p = 0.0004) and the combined endpoint (8% vs 14%; p < 0.0001) (Established) [[Keeley2003]].
- Without the SHOCK trial data, short-term death in that meta-analysis was 5% vs 7% (p = 0.0003). The benefit held at long-term follow-up, whichever fibrinolytic was used and whether or not patients were transferred [[Keeley2003]].
- In fibrinolysis trials, haemorrhagic stroke is the main risk: about 4 extra strokes per 1000 on days 0–1 [[FTT1994]]. In ISIS-2, streptokinase caused confirmed cerebral haemorrhage in 0.1% vs 0.0% with placebo [[ISIS2_1988]].
- DANAMI-2 (1572 patients): moving patients from referral hospitals for angioplasty, versus on-site fibrinolysis, lowered death, reinfarction or disabling stroke at 30 days (8.5% vs 14.2%; P = 0.002). 96% were transferred within 2 hours [[Andersen2003DANAMI2]].
- In DANAMI-2, the benefit came mainly from less reinfarction (1.6% vs 6.3%). Death (6.6% vs 7.8%) and stroke (1.1% vs 2.0%) did not differ significantly [[Andersen2003DANAMI2]].
- CAPTIM (840 patients within 6 h, managed by mobile emergency units): prehospital fibrinolysis versus primary angioplasty gave 30-day death, reinfarction or disabling stroke of 8.2% vs 6.2% (risk difference 1.96, 95% CI −1.53 to 5.46). Median onset-to-treatment was 130 vs 190 min [[Bonnefoy2002CAPTIM]].
- Using NRMI data from 192,509 patients at 645 hospitals, the mortality advantage of primary PCI over fibrinolysis shrank as PCI-related delay (door-to-balloon minus door-to-needle) grew. The break-even delay depended on age, symptom duration and infarct location [[Pinto2006]].

### reperfusion · Guideline time targets (as published)
- ESC 2023: primary PCI is preferred "provided it can be performed in a timely manner (i.e. within 120 min of the ECG-based diagnosis" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ESC 2023: "If PPCI is not feasible within 120 min, patients should undergo immediate fibrinolysis" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ESC 2023: "The goal is to start fibrinolytic therapy within 10 min of the STEMI diagnosis." [[Byrne2023ESC]]
- ESC 2023: reperfusion applies "provided the patient has presented within 12 h of symptom onset" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ESC 2023 on transfers: "a door-in to door-out time of ≤30 min is recommended to expedite reperfusion therapy" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ESC 2023 after fibrinolysis: "routine early angiography (i.e. within 2–24 h) is recommended", and "rescue PCI is indicated if fibrinolysis fails" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- Earlier US guidelines (before 2025) recommended a door-to-balloon time of 90 minutes or less, which became a national performance measure [[Menees2013,Bradley2006]].
- In MINAP, the audit benchmarks were call-to-balloon <90 min for primary PCI and door-to-needle <30 min for fibrinolysis [[Quinn2014]].

### reperfusion · Pharmaco-invasive strategy (STREAM)
- STREAM (1892 patients within 3 h of onset who could not get primary PCI within 1 hour): prehospital tenecteplase, clopidogrel and enoxaparin, then angiography, versus primary PCI. Death, shock, heart failure or reinfarction at 30 days was 12.4% vs 14.3% (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.68–1.09; P = 0.21) [[Armstrong2013STREAM]].
- In STREAM, 36.3% of the fibrinolysis group needed emergency angiography (failed fibrinolysis). The rest had angiography at a median of 17 h [[Armstrong2013STREAM]].
- Intracranial haemorrhage in STREAM was 1.0% vs 0.2% (P = 0.04). After the dose was halved for patients ≥75 years, it was 0.5% vs 0.3% (P = 0.45) [[Armstrong2013STREAM]].

### reperfusion · Time windows (12 h, 12–48 h, later)
- BRAVE-2 (365 patients 12–48 h after onset, without persistent symptoms): an invasive strategy reduced final infarct size (median 8.0% vs 13.0% of the left ventricle; P < .001). The mean difference was −6.8% (95% CI −10.2 to −3.5) [[Schomig2005BRAVE2]].
- In BRAVE-2, death, recurrent MI or stroke at 30 days was 4.4% vs 6.6% (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.27–1.62), which was not significant [[Schomig2005BRAVE2]].
- ESC 2023 cites "A small RCT in 347 STEMI patients presenting 12–48 h after symptom onset" (BRAVE-2's imaging cohort) as the basis for considering PCI in that window [[Byrne2023ESC,Schomig2005BRAVE2]].
- OAT (2166 stable patients with an occluded infarct artery 3–28 days after MI): routine PCI did not reduce death, reinfarction or class IV heart failure at 4 years (17.2% vs 15.6%; HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.92–1.45) [[Hochman2006OAT]].
- ESC 2023 addresses "routine PCI of an occluded IRA in STEMI patients presenting >48 h after onset of symptoms" in asymptomatic patients, a practice the OAT data argue against [[Byrne2023ESC,Hochman2006OAT]].

### reperfusion · Door-to-balloon trends: faster care, flat population mortality
- In CMS data, median US door-to-balloon time fell from 96 min (2005) to 64 min (first 3 quarters of 2010). The share treated <90 min rose from 44.2% to 91.4%, and the share <75 min from 27.3% to 70.4% [[Krumholz2011D2B]].
- The largest falls were in groups that had been slowest: patients >75 years (38 min), women (35 min) and Black patients (42 min) [[Krumholz2011D2B]].
- In the CathPCI Registry (96,738 primary PCIs, 2005–2009), median door-to-balloon time fell from 83 to 67 min and ≤90 min rose from 59.7% to 83.1%. Yet unadjusted in-hospital mortality did not change (4.8% vs 4.7%; P = 0.43) [[Menees2013]].
- Nallamothu and colleagues resolved this paradox in 150,116 procedures: at the individual level, each 10-minute decrease in door-to-balloon time was linked with lower in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.91–0.93). The pPCI population grew 55% and changed, raising population-level risk [[Nallamothu2015]].
- In that study, the annual median door-to-balloon time fell from 86 min (2005) to 63 min (2011), while risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality went from 4.7% to 5.3% (p = 0.06) [[Nallamothu2015]].
- In 43,801 NCDR patients (2005–2006), adjusted in-hospital mortality rose with door-to-balloon time: 3.0% at 30 min, 3.5% at 60 min, 4.3% at 90 min, 5.6% at 120 min, 7.0% at 150 min and 8.4% at 180 min [[Rathore2009]].

### reperfusion · How PCI is done: wrist versus groin
- MATRIX (8404 patients with ACS): radial (wrist) versus femoral (groin) access reduced net adverse clinical events (9.8% vs 11.7%; RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.73–0.96). This was driven by less major bleeding (1.6% vs 2.3%; RR 0.67) and lower all-cause mortality (1.6% vs 2.2%; RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.53–0.99) [[Valgimigli2015MATRIX]].
- In MATRIX, major adverse cardiovascular events were 8.8% vs 10.3% (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.74–0.99; p = 0.0307), which did not meet the prespecified α of 0.025 [[Valgimigli2015MATRIX]].
- RIVAL (7021 patients with ACS): the primary outcome did not differ between radial and femoral access (3.7% vs 4.0%; HR 0.92). Radial access had fewer large haematomas (HR 0.40) and fewer pseudoaneurysms needing closure (HR 0.30), and benefit was seen in the STEMI subgroup (HR 0.60) [[Jolly2011RIVAL]].
- ESC 2023 lists "Radial artery approach as default vascular access" among strategies to reduce PCI-related bleeding [[Byrne2023ESC]].

### reperfusion · Thrombus aspiration (clot suction): no routine benefit
- TOTAL (10,732 patients with STEMI): routine manual thrombectomy did not reduce cardiovascular death, recurrent MI, cardiogenic shock or class IV heart failure at 180 days (6.9% vs 7.0%; HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.85–1.15) [[Jolly2015TOTAL]].
- In TOTAL, stroke within 30 days was higher with thrombectomy (0.7% vs 0.3%; HR 2.06, 95% CI 1.13–3.75) [[Jolly2015TOTAL]].
- TASTE (7244 patients with STEMI, registry-based randomised trial): 30-day all-cause death was 2.8% with aspiration versus 3.0% with PCI only (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.72–1.22) [[Frobert2013TASTE]].

### reperfusion · Complete versus culprit-only revascularisation (multivessel disease)
- COMPLETE (4041 patients with STEMI and multivessel disease after successful culprit PCI): at median 3 years, complete revascularisation reduced cardiovascular death or MI (7.8% vs 10.5%; HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.60–0.91) [[Mehta2019COMPLETE]].
- In COMPLETE, cardiovascular death, MI or ischaemia-driven revascularisation fell from 16.7% to 8.9% (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.43–0.61). The benefit was similar whether nonculprit PCI was during or after the index hospitalisation [[Mehta2019COMPLETE]].
- PRAMI (465 patients): preventive PCI of non-infarct arteries reduced cardiac death, nonfatal MI or refractory angina (9 vs 23 events per 100 patients; HR 0.35, 95% CI 0.21–0.58) [[Wald2013PRAMI]].
- DANAMI-3–PRIMULTI (627 patients): FFR-guided complete revascularisation reduced the composite of death, reinfarction and ischaemia-driven revascularisation (13% vs 22%; HR 0.56). The effect came from fewer repeat revascularisations [[Engstrom2015]].
- Compare-Acute (885 patients): FFR-guided complete revascularisation in the acute setting gave 8 vs 21 events per 100 patients at 12 months (HR 0.35). This was mainly fewer revascularisations (6.1% vs 17.5%) [[Smits2017]].
- MULTISTARS AMI (840 stable patients with STEMI): immediate multivessel PCI versus staged PCI within 19–45 days gave a 1-year composite of 8.5% vs 16.3% (risk ratio 0.52, 95% CI 0.38–0.72). It was noninferior and superior [[Stahli2023MULTISTARS]].
- FIRE (1445 patients aged ≥75, median age 80, 35.2% STEMI): physiology-guided complete revascularisation reduced death, MI, stroke or revascularisation at 1 year (15.7% vs 21.0%; HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.57–0.93) [[Biscaglia2023FIRE]].

### reperfusion · Cardiogenic shock: culprit-only first
- SHOCK (302 patients): emergency revascularisation versus initial medical stabilisation gave 30-day mortality of 46.7% vs 56.0% (P = 0.11), which was not significant. 6-month mortality was 50.3% vs 63.1% (P = 0.027) [[Hochman1999SHOCK]].
- In SHOCK, 1-year survival was 46.7% vs 33.6% (absolute difference 13.2%; RR for death 0.72) [[Hochman2001SHOCK1y]]. At 6 years, survival was 32.8% vs 19.6% [[Hochman2006SHOCKlong]].
- CULPRIT-SHOCK (706 patients with MI, shock and multivessel disease): culprit-lesion-only PCI versus immediate multivessel PCI gave 30-day death or renal-replacement therapy of 45.9% vs 55.4% (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.71–0.96) [[Thiele2017CULPRIT]].
- At 1 year in CULPRIT-SHOCK, death was 50.0% vs 56.9% (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.76–1.01). Repeat revascularisation (32.3% vs 9.4%) and heart-failure rehospitalisation (5.2% vs 1.2%) were more frequent with culprit-only PCI [[Thiele2018CULPRIT1y]].
- ESC 2023: "Early revascularization with either PCI or CABG is recommended for patients with AMI complicated by CS" [[Byrne2023ESC]].

### reperfusion · NSTE-ACS: timing of the invasive strategy
- ESC 2023 definitions: an immediate invasive strategy is "emergency (i.e. as soon as possible) angiography and PCI if indicated". An early invasive strategy is "routine invasive angiography (and PCI if needed) within 24 h of presentation" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ESC 2023: "An early (i.e. within 24 h) invasive strategy should be considered when any high-risk features are present." High-risk features include a confirmed NSTEMI by ESC hs-cTn algorithms, dynamic ST/T changes, transient ST elevation and "A GRACE risk score >140" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ESC 2023 very-high-risk features (immediate invasive) include "Haemodynamic instability or CS" and "Recurrent or ongoing chest pain refractory to medical treatment" [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- FRISC II (2457 patients with unstable coronary disease): an early invasive versus non-invasive strategy reduced death or MI at 6 months (9.4% vs 12.1%; RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.62–0.98) [[FRISCII1999]].
- TIMACS (3031 patients): early (median 14 h) versus delayed (median 50 h) angiography did not significantly reduce death, MI or stroke at 6 months (9.6% vs 11.3%; HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.68–1.06) [[Mehta2009TIMACS]].
- In TIMACS, the highest-risk third did benefit (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.48–0.89), and refractory ischaemia was reduced overall (HR 0.72) [[Mehta2009TIMACS]].
- VERDICT (2147 patients): very early angiography (median 4.7 h) versus standard care (61.6 h) did not improve the overall long-term outcome (27.5% vs 29.5%; HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.78–1.08). It did improve outcome in patients with GRACE >140 (HR 0.81; interaction P = 0.023) [[Kofoed2018VERDICT]].
- A meta-analysis of 8 trials (5324 patients) found no overall mortality reduction with early versus delayed invasive strategy (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.64–1.03). Lower mortality was seen in subgroups with raised biomarkers, diabetes, GRACE >140 or age ≥75 (Early evidence: interaction tests inconclusive) [[Jobs2017]].
- A later meta-analysis of 17 RCTs (10,209 patients) found no mortality difference (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.78–1.04). Early invasive strategy reduced recurrent ischaemia (RR 0.57) and length of stay (−22 h) [[Kite2022]].

### cabg · When bypass surgery is chosen
- ESC 2023: "Emergency coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) surgery should be considered for patients" in whom PCI is not feasible or has failed (sentence quoted in part) [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- In SHOCK, the early revascularisation group was treated with angioplasty in 55% and CABG in 38% [[Hochman2001SHOCK1y]].
- In New York State (44,365 CABG patients, 1993–1996), in-hospital mortality fell the later CABG was done after an acute MI: 11.8% at <6 h, 9.5% at 6 h–1 day and 2.8% at >1 day [[Lee2001CABGtiming]].
- In that study, mortality was higher after transmural MI if CABG was done within 7 days. The authors suggested that "waiting in some may be warranted" (observational data) [[Lee2001CABGtiming]].
- Loop and colleagues compared 2306 patients with an internal-mammary-artery graft to the LAD with 3625 who had vein grafts only. Vein-only patients had 1.61 times the risk of death over 10 years [[Loop1986]].
- In the same series, 10-year survival with versus without a mammary graft was 82.6% vs 71.0% in three-vessel disease [[Loop1986]].
- ART (3102 patients): bilateral versus single internal-thoracic-artery grafting showed no significant difference in 10-year death (20.3% vs 21.2%; HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.82–1.12) [[Taggart2019ART]].

### cabg · CABG versus PCI for multivessel and left main disease (mostly stable patients)
- SYNTAX (1800 patients with three-vessel or left main disease): major adverse cardiac or cerebrovascular events at 12 months were 17.8% with PCI vs 12.4% with CABG (P = 0.002), mainly from repeat revascularisation (13.5% vs 5.9%). Stroke was higher with CABG (2.2% vs 0.6%) [[Serruys2009SYNTAX]].
- SYNTAX 10 years (SYNTAXES): all-cause death was 28% after PCI versus 24% after CABG (HR 1.19, 95% CI 0.99–1.43). It was 28% vs 21% in three-vessel disease (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.11–1.81) and 27% vs 28% in left main disease (HR 0.92). SYNTAX excluded acute MI [[Thuijs2019SYNTAXES]].
- FREEDOM (1900 patients with diabetes, 83% three-vessel disease): 5-year death, MI or stroke was 26.6% with PCI versus 18.7% with CABG (P = 0.005). Stroke was higher with CABG (5.2% vs 2.4%) [[Farkouh2012FREEDOM]].
- EXCEL (1905 patients with left main disease of low or intermediate complexity): 5-year death, stroke or MI was 22.0% PCI vs 19.2% CABG (difference 2.8 points, 95% CI −0.9 to 6.5). All-cause death was 13.0% vs 9.9% [[Stone2019EXCEL]].
- NOBLE (1201 patients with left main disease): 5-year MACCE was 28% PCI vs 19% CABG (HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.24–2.01). All-cause mortality was 9% vs 9% [[Holm2020NOBLE]].
- Pooled individual data from 11 trials (11,518 patients without acute MI): 5-year mortality was 11.2% after PCI vs 9.2% after CABG (HR 1.20). In multivessel disease with diabetes it was 15.5% vs 10.0% (HR 1.48). In left main disease it was 10.7% vs 10.5% [[Head2018]].
- Left main meta-analysis (SYNTAX, PRECOMBAT, NOBLE, EXCEL; 4394 patients): 5-year death was 11.2% PCI vs 10.2% CABG (HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.91–1.32). Spontaneous MI (6.2% vs 2.6%) and repeat revascularisation (18.3% vs 10.7%) were more common after PCI [[Sabatine2021]].

### reperfusion · Contrast case: stable coronary disease is not a heart attack (ISCHEMIA)
- ISCHEMIA (5179 patients with stable coronary disease and moderate or severe ischaemia): an initial invasive strategy did not reduce ischaemic events or death over a median 3.2 years compared with medical therapy first. 5-year event rates were 16.4% vs 18.2% (difference −1.8 points, 95% CI −4.7 to 1.0) [[Maron2020ISCHEMIA]].
- In ISCHEMIA, deaths were 145 vs 144 (HR 1.05, 95% CI 0.83–1.32). At 6 months, events were higher with the invasive strategy (5.3% vs 3.4%), largely from procedure-related MI [[Maron2020ISCHEMIA]].
- Teaching point: ISCHEMIA studied stable patients, not people having a heart attack. Its result does not apply to STEMI or high-risk NSTE-ACS, where the trials above show benefit from timely reperfusion or invasive care [[Maron2020ISCHEMIA,Keeley2003,FRISCII1999]].

### Exam hooks
- Primary PCI versus fibrinolysis (23 RCTs): short-term death 7% vs 9%, reinfarction 3% vs 7%, stroke 1% vs 2% [[Keeley2003]].
- The fibrinolysis "golden hour": 65 lives saved per 1000 treated within the first hour, versus 26–29 per 1000 at 2–6 h [[Boersma1996]].
- ESC 2023: primary PCI if it can be done within 120 min of the ECG-based diagnosis; otherwise fibrinolysis within 10 min of diagnosis [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- After fibrinolysis: routine angiography within 2–24 h, and rescue PCI if fibrinolysis fails [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- Door-in-door-out at a non-PCI hospital: ≤30 min (ESC 2023) [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ECG within 10 min of first medical contact [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- ISIS-2: aspirin and streptokinase each cut vascular death by about a quarter, and together by 42% [[ISIS2_1988]].
- STREAM: in early STEMI where PCI is not possible within 1 h, a pharmaco-invasive strategy gave outcomes similar to primary PCI, with more intracranial bleeding before the age-based dose change [[Armstrong2013STREAM]].
- Door-to-balloon time fell nationally while population mortality stayed flat. Individual-level data still show about 8% lower odds of in-hospital death per 10 min saved [[Menees2013,Nallamothu2015]].
- Radial access (MATRIX) reduced major bleeding and all-cause mortality versus femoral access [[Valgimigli2015MATRIX]].
- Routine thrombus aspiration: no benefit (TOTAL, TASTE), and more stroke in TOTAL [[Jolly2015TOTAL,Frobert2013TASTE]].
- STEMI with multivessel disease, stable: complete revascularisation beats culprit-only (COMPLETE). In cardiogenic shock: culprit-only first (CULPRIT-SHOCK) [[Mehta2019COMPLETE,Thiele2017CULPRIT]].
- Late (3–28 days) PCI of an occluded infarct artery in stable patients: no benefit (OAT) [[Hochman2006OAT]].
- NSTE-ACS: early (<24 h) invasive strategy helps mainly high-risk patients such as those with GRACE >140 (TIMACS, VERDICT) [[Mehta2009TIMACS,Kofoed2018VERDICT]].
- Diabetes plus multivessel disease: CABG beats PCI for death/MI/stroke (FREEDOM), at the cost of more stroke [[Farkouh2012FREEDOM]].
- Internal mammary artery to LAD: the survival-defining graft in CABG [[Loop1986]].
- Bystander CPR roughly doubles the odds of 30-day survival after out-of-hospital arrest (OR 2.15) [[HasselqvistAx2015]].

### In plain words
- If you think you or someone else is having a heart attack, call your local emergency number now (911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 112 in the EU).
- During a heart attack, heart muscle dies a little more with every minute the artery stays blocked. Getting help fast saves muscle and lives [[Reimer1977,Boersma1996]].
- Calling an ambulance is better than driving yourself. The crew can record your heart tracing on the way and warn the hospital, so the team is ready when you arrive [[Mathews2011,Diercks2009,Byrne2023ESC]].
- Many people wait hours before getting help. Women and older people often wait longer. Delay is common and nobody's fault, but calling early helps [[Goldberg2002GRACE,Nguyen2010]].
- Doctors open a blocked heart artery in one of two ways: a thin tube with a tiny balloon and a mesh tube (stent), usually through the wrist, or clot-dissolving medicine [[Keeley2003,Valgimigli2015MATRIX]].
- When the balloon-and-stent procedure can be done quickly, it works better than clot-dissolving medicine. When it cannot be done quickly, the medicine is given first [[Keeley2003,Byrne2023ESC]].
- Some people have narrowings in more than one artery. Fixing the other narrowings too, during the same hospital stay or soon after, lowers the chance of another heart attack [[Mehta2019COMPLETE]].
- Bypass surgery uses a blood vessel from your chest or leg to route blood around blocked arteries. It is often chosen for people with diabetes and many blocked arteries [[Farkouh2012FREEDOM,Loop1986]].
- If someone collapses and is not breathing normally, calling for help, starting CPR and using a public defibrillator (AED) can more than double their chance of surviving [[HasselqvistAx2015,Weisfeldt2010]].
- Survival numbers on this page come from large groups of people. They cannot predict what will happen to any one person.
- Scores and time targets on this page explain how hospitals work. They are not a way to decide whether to call for help. If in doubt, call.

## Open questions and controversies
- Is faster door-to-balloon time still worth chasing? Population-level studies saw no mortality fall as times dropped [[Menees2013]]. Individual-level analysis shows each 10 min saved is linked with lower mortality, and the patient mix changed [[Nallamothu2015]]. Current view: total ischaemic time, including patient delay and EMS, matters more than the in-hospital segment alone (Established for individual association; population effect debated).
- Primary PCI versus a pharmaco-invasive strategy when transfer is long: primary PCI is superior when timely [[Keeley2003,Andersen2003DANAMI2]]. Prehospital fibrinolysis with planned angiography gave similar outcomes in early presenters (CAPTIM, STREAM), with more intracranial bleeding [[Bonnefoy2002CAPTIM,Armstrong2013STREAM]]. Status: guideline-endorsed when PCI cannot be done within 120 min [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- Immediate versus staged complete revascularisation in stable STEMI: MULTISTARS AMI favoured immediate [[Stahli2023MULTISTARS]]. COMPLETE showed benefit regardless of timing [[Mehta2019COMPLETE]]. In shock, immediate multivessel PCI was harmful at 30 days [[Thiele2017CULPRIT]]. Status: timing in stable patients is still being refined.
- Complete revascularisation in older adults: FIRE showed benefit at ≥75 years [[Biscaglia2023FIRE]]. Whether that holds for frail patients and those with NSTEMI is still under study (Early evidence).
- How early is "early" in NSTE-ACS? Meta-analyses show no overall mortality benefit [[Jobs2017,Kite2022]]. Subgroups at high risk (GRACE >140) may benefit [[Kofoed2018VERDICT,Mehta2009TIMACS]]. ESC 2023 says "should be considered" within 24 h for high risk [[Byrne2023ESC]].
- Left main disease, PCI or CABG: EXCEL found no significant difference in the composite but more all-cause death with PCI [[Stone2019EXCEL]]. NOBLE favoured CABG for MACCE with similar mortality [[Holm2020NOBLE]]. The IPD meta-analysis gave an 85.7% Bayesian probability that 5-year death is higher with PCI, probably <0.2% per year [[Sabatine2021]]. Status: heart-team decision.
- Routine angiography after resuscitated cardiac arrest without ST elevation: neither COACT nor TOMAHAWK showed benefit [[Lemkes2019COACT,Desch2021TOMAHAWK]]. Status: selective approach (ESC 2023).
- Patient delay has barely changed in decades despite campaigns [[McGinn2005,Makam2016,Moser2006]]. Status: unsolved. Awareness of the need to call 9-1-1 is high (≈95%) but knowledge of all symptoms is only ≈50% [[Fang2019MMWR]].

## Provenance
- Every source above was confirmed against its PubMed esummary record (authors, title, journal, year, volume/pages, PMID, DOI, PMCID), and every number was taken from the PubMed abstract (efetch) read in this pass. ISIS-2, FTT and FRISC II have no DOI in their PubMed records, so none is given.
- ESC 2023 (Byrne2023ESC) quotations come from the publisher full text on academic.oup.com, read through a fetch tool that returned verbatim fragments of up to about 125 characters. Class and Level of Evidence were in image tables and could not be read, so none is given. The ESC sections on multivessel PCI and thrombus aspiration were not reached in the fetched text, so ESC wording on those topics is not quoted; the trial data stand on their own.
- The 2025 ACC/AHA ACS guideline (Rao2025ACC) full text returned HTTP 403 from ahajournals.org and jacc.org, and Europe PMC has no full text. Only its scope (abstract) is cited. No ACC/AHA 2025 recommendation, COR/LOE or time target is quoted. This is the main gap for the builders: the US FMC-to-device 90/120 min, door-to-needle 30 min, aspirin dose and nonculprit-PCI recommendations should be added once the text can be read.
- The 2025 AHA CPR guideline chapters (Parts 1, 4, 7) were read as abstracts only, because the full text was blocked (403) and the Europe PMC full text for Part 4 was empty. No CPR rate, depth or hands-only wording is quoted. The 2020 AHA Part 3 (PMID 33081529) has no PubMed abstract and was not cited.
- Dropped: Patel 2018 JACC research letter on awareness (no abstract in PubMed); Pinto 2006's specific break-even delay minutes (not in the abstract, only the qualitative finding is used); Gulati2021ChestPain (its abstract does not contain the advice on the word "atypical", so it is not cited here, although the page follows that advice); the "ISCHEMIA excluded recent ACS" detail (not in the abstract; the teaching point is worded as "stable patients" instead).
- Overlap notes: SHOCK/CULPRIT-SHOCK are cited here only for revascularisation strategy. Pass F owns cardiogenic shock in general. Aspirin appears here only as first-minute/ISIS-2 evidence. Pass E owns antithrombotic therapy.
