# B · Atherosclerosis, plaque rupture, thrombosis, ischaemia & risk factors

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: plaque, ischaemia, risk.

This pass covers how a heart attack (myocardial infarction, MI) happens: how atherosclerotic plaque builds up in coronary arteries over decades, how a plaque ruptures or erodes and triggers a clot (thrombus), how heart muscle dies when its blood supply stops (the ischaemic "wavefront"), why time to reperfusion matters, and the risk factors that drive the process. Risk calculators (Pooled Cohort Equations, PREVENT) are described as teaching tools only. They are never a way to decide whether to seek care.

## Findings

### plaque · How atherosclerosis starts: LDL retention and early lesions
- Atherosclerosis is the formation of fibrofatty lesions in the artery wall. It causes most myocardial infarctions and many strokes, and its development "probably requires" low-density lipoprotein (LDL), the particle that carries cholesterol in the blood [[Libby2019Primer]].
- The key initiating step in atherogenesis is the retention of apolipoprotein B (apoB)-containing lipoproteins beneath the endothelium (subendothelial retention). A chronic, maladaptive inflammatory response dominated by macrophages and T cells then drives lesion growth (Established) [[Tabas2007]].
- Populations who keep lifelong low levels of apoB lipoproteins have about a 90% lower risk of coronary artery disease [[Tabas2007]].
- AHA lesion classification, type I: the earliest lesion has more intimal macrophages and scattered lipid-filled macrophages (foam cells) [[Stary1994]].
- Type II lesions include the fatty streak, the first lesion visible to the naked eye. They consist of layers of macrophage foam cells and lipid droplets inside intimal smooth muscle cells [[Stary1994]].
- Types I and II are generally the only lesion types found in children [[Stary1994]].
- Type III (intermediate) lesions add pools of extracellular lipid. They appear in progression-prone "adaptive intimal thickenings" in young adults [[Stary1994]].
- Adaptive intimal thickenings are present at constant locations in everyone from birth. They do not narrow the lumen, and they are where lesions progress most [[Stary1995]].
- Type IV lesions (atheroma) have a confluent core of extracellular lipid and can cause symptoms. From around the fourth decade of life, lesions may add thick fibrous layers (type V, fibroatheroma) and/or fissure, haematoma and thrombus (type VI, "complicated" lesion) [[Stary1995]].
- Some type V lesions are largely calcified (type Vb). Others are mostly fibrous tissue with little lipid or calcium (type Vc) [[Stary1995]].
- Atherosclerosis is lipoprotein-driven. Plaques form at specific arterial sites through intimal inflammation, necrosis, fibrosis and calcification, and may progress quietly for decades before suddenly causing a coronary thrombosis [[Bentzon2014]].

### plaque · Atherosclerosis begins in youth
- PDAY autopsy study (2876 people aged 15–34 who died of external causes, US 1987–1994): intimal lesions were in all aortas and in more than half of the right coronary arteries in the youngest group (15–19 years). Lesions increased in prevalence and extent with age through 30–34 years [[Strong1999PDAY]].
- PDAY: fatty streaks and raised lesions "increase rapidly" between ages 15 and 34. The authors conclude that primary prevention of atherosclerosis must begin in childhood or adolescence [[Strong1999PDAY]].
- Intravascular ultrasound of 262 young donor hearts (mean donor age 33.4 years) found atherosclerosis (intimal thickness ≥0.5 mm) in 51.9% of donors [[Tuzcu2001]].
- In that study, prevalence ranged from 17% in donors under 20 years to 85% in donors aged 50 or older, or "1 of 6 teenagers" [[Tuzcu2001]].
- Prevalence of atherosclerosis was similar in men and women (52% vs 51.7%), although average intimal thickness was greater in men [[Tuzcu2001]].
- Bogalusa Heart Study autopsies (93 young people aged 2–39 with risk-factor data): the share of coronary intimal surface covered by fatty streaks rose with the number of risk factors, from 1.3% (0 factors) to 2.5% (1), 7.9% (2) and 11.0% (3 or 4) (P for trend 0.01) [[Berenson1998Bogalusa]].
- Bogalusa: coronary fibrous plaques covered 0.6%, 0.7%, 2.4% and 7.2% of the surface with 0, 1, 2 and 3–4 risk factors (P for trend 0.003) [[Berenson1998Bogalusa]].
- Bogalusa: smokers had more coronary fatty streak surface than non-smokers (8.27% vs 2.89%, P=0.04) [[Berenson1998Bogalusa]].

### plaque · The vulnerable plaque: thin-cap fibroatheroma
- The thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA) is the non-thrombosed lesion that most resembles a ruptured plaque. It has a necrotic core under a fibrous cap measuring <65 µm, with few smooth muscle cells and many macrophages [[Virmani2006]].
- In TCFAs the necrotic core is about 2–17 mm long (mean 8 mm). In more than 75% of cases the vessel is less than 75% narrowed by area (diameter stenosis <50%), and the necrotic core area is ≤3 mm² in at least 75% [[Virmani2006]].
- TCFAs are most common in proximal coronary arteries. They usually show positive (outward) remodelling rather than severe narrowing [[Virmani2006]].
- TCFAs are common in people with high total cholesterol, a high total/HDL cholesterol ratio, raised high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and in women over 50 [[Virmani2006]].
- Autopsy study of 295 coronary plaques from 213 sudden-death cases: fibrous cap thickness was the best discriminator of plaque type. Caps in ruptured plaques measured <55 µm, all stable fibroatheromas had caps >84 µm, and most TCFAs fell in the 54–84 µm range [[Narula2013]].
- In the same study, more than 75% cross-sectional area stenosis was seen in 70% of ruptures and 40% of TCFAs. Only 5% of ruptures and 10% of TCFAs were narrowed by less than 50% [[Narula2013]].
- When cap thickness was left out of the analysis, macrophage infiltration and necrotic core size were the two best discriminators of plaque type [[Narula2013]].
- Intraplaque haemorrhage: red-cell membranes (glycophorin A) and iron build up in late-necrotic-core and thin-cap plaques. They add free cholesterol and macrophages and enlarge the necrotic core, which may increase the risk of plaque destabilisation (Early evidence) [[Kolodgie2003]].
- Plaque haemorrhage can expand a plaque quickly. It can come from a cap fissure or from fragile new vessels (neovascularisation) [[Falk2013]].
- Ruptured plaques are associated with positive (expansive) remodelling, a large necrotic core, and a thin, disrupted cap infiltrated by foamy macrophages [[Falk2013]].

### plaque · Most culprit lesions were not severe narrowings
- Of 29 patients who had an angiogram before an MI and then a new occlusion, the artery that later occluded had less than 50% stenosis on the earlier angiogram in 19 (66%) and less than 70% in 28 (97%) [[Little1988]].
- In that series, only 10 of 29 MIs (34%) came from the artery that had previously held the most severe stenosis [[Little1988]].
- PROSPECT (697 patients with acute coronary syndromes, three-vessel intravascular ultrasound, median follow-up 3.4 years): the 3-year rate of major adverse cardiovascular events was 20.4%. Events were related to culprit lesions in 12.9% and to non-culprit lesions in 11.6% [[Stone2011PROSPECT]].
- Non-culprit lesions that caused later events were usually mild on angiography (mean diameter stenosis 32.3%). They were more likely to have plaque burden ≥70% (HR 5.03), minimal lumen area ≤4.0 mm² (HR 3.21), or a TCFA appearance on radiofrequency ultrasound (HR 3.35) [[Stone2011PROSPECT]].
- Research thinking on thrombotic complications has moved "beyond the 'vulnerable plaque' concept" (Established as a shift in thinking; mechanisms still debated) [[Libby2021Nature]].

### plaque · Plaque rupture, erosion and calcified nodule
- The plaque types mainly responsible for coronary thrombosis are rupture and erosion. Rupture is the most common cause of acute MI, especially in men [[Falk2013]].
- Autopsy data show that women under 50 more often have erosion. In older women, rupture becomes more common with each decade [[Falk2013]].
- Rupture exposes highly thrombogenic, red-cell-rich necrotic core material. The structural precondition is an extremely thin fibrous cap, so ruptures occur mainly in TCFAs [[Bentzon2014]].
- Erosion usually occurs on pathological intimal thickening or fibroatheromas. Its mechanisms remain "largely unknown", although coronary spasm is suspected [[Bentzon2014]].
- The calcified nodule is a rare cause of coronary thrombosis, found in highly calcified, tortuous arteries of older people [[Bentzon2014]].
- Autopsy, 50 consecutive sudden deaths from coronary thrombosis: 28 (56%) had plaque rupture and 22 (44%) had erosion of a proteoglycan-rich plaque without rupture [[Farb1996]].
- In that series, erosion cases were younger (mean 44 vs 53 years) and more often women (50% vs 18%). They had less luminal narrowing (70% vs 78%) and less calcification (23% vs 69%) [[Farb1996]].
- In that series, macrophages infiltrated the cap in 100% of ruptures vs 50% of erosions, and T cells in 75% vs 32% [[Farb1996]].
- Autopsy, 113 men with coronary disease who died suddenly: 59 had an acute thrombus, 41 from rupture (69%) and 18 from erosion (31%) [[Burke1997]].
- In that study, smoking was a risk factor in 75% of men with acute thrombosis vs 41% of men with stable plaques [[Burke1997]].
- In that study, the total-to-HDL cholesterol ratio was 8.5 in rupture deaths vs 5.0 with erosion and 5.5 with stable plaque. The authors conclude that abnormal cholesterol predisposes to rupture and smoking predisposes to acute thrombosis [[Burke1997]].
- In vivo optical coherence tomography (OCT) in 126 patients with acute coronary syndrome: culprit lesions were rupture in 43.7%, erosion in 31.0% and calcified nodule in 7.9% [[Jia2013OCT]].
- In that study, patients with erosion were younger (53.8 vs 60.6 years for rupture). They more often presented with non-ST-elevation ACS (61.5% vs 29.1%) [[Jia2013OCT]].
- OCT-erosion lesions had thicker fibrous caps than ruptures (169.3 vs 60.4 µm), less lipid, and the least severe stenosis (55.4% vs 68.8% diameter stenosis) [[Jia2013OCT]].
- Erosion lesions are often negatively remodelled, rich in smooth muscle cells and proteoglycans, and have little or no inflammation [[Falk2013]].
- Background reported in a 2010 autopsy paper: among in-hospital deaths from acute MI, erosions account for 25% of deaths, more often in women [[Kramer2010JACC]].
- In 115 culprit thrombi from sudden coronary deaths, 69% were late-stage (days to weeks old). Late-stage thrombi were more common on erosions (88%) than on ruptures (54%). So thrombus often builds in stages before death [[Kramer2010JACC]].
- EROSION study (single centre, uncontrolled): plaque erosion was found by OCT in 103 of 405 patients with ACS (25.4%). Of 60 enrolled patients treated with antithrombotic therapy without a stent, 78.3% had a >50% reduction in thrombus volume at 1 month (Early evidence) [[Jia2017EROSION]].

### plaque · From exposed plaque to clot: platelets and coagulation
- When a high-risk plaque ruptures or erodes, exposed plaque components activate tissue factor. A fibrin layer forms (coagulation cascade) while circulating platelets and inflammatory cells are recruited [[Badimon2014]].
- Exposed plaque components, platelet receptors and coagulation factors interact, leading to platelet activation, aggregation and a superimposed thrombus (atherothrombosis) that may block the artery and cause an acute ischaemic syndrome [[Badimon2014]].
- Fragile, leaky new vessels growing into the intima help enlarge the necrotic core and make a plaque more vulnerable [[Badimon2014]].
- Culprit plaque morphology ranges from thrombosis with or without total occlusion to sudden narrowing from intraplaque haemorrhage [[Falk2013]].

### plaque · Inflammation as a driver
- Atherosclerosis is recognised as an inflammatory disease in which macrophages and T lymphocytes play a dominant role. Pro-inflammatory (M1) and repair-promoting (M2) macrophage subtypes have both been described in human plaque [[Falk2013]].
- Inflammatory pathways and leukocytes link traditional and emerging risk factors to the altered behaviour of arterial wall cells [[Libby2021Nature]].
- In 27,939 healthy US women followed for about 8 years, relative risks of a first cardiovascular event across rising quintiles of C-reactive protein were 1.4, 1.6, 2.0 and 2.3. For LDL cholesterol quintiles they were 0.9, 1.1, 1.3 and 1.5 [[Ridker2002CRP]].
- In the same cohort, 77% of events occurred in women with LDL cholesterol below 160 mg/dL, and 46% below 130 mg/dL [[Ridker2002CRP]].
- CANTOS (10,061 patients with prior MI and hs-CRP ≥2 mg/L): canakinumab, an antibody against interleukin-1β, given at 150 mg every 3 months, reduced nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke or cardiovascular death vs placebo (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.74–0.98). Median follow-up was 3.7 years (Established proof of concept for the inflammatory hypothesis) [[Ridker2017CANTOS]].
- In CANTOS, primary-endpoint rates were 4.50 per 100 person-years with placebo vs 3.86 with 150 mg. Canakinumab did not lower lipids, raised the rate of fatal infection, and did not change all-cause mortality (HR 0.94) [[Ridker2017CANTOS]].
- COLCOT (4745 patients within 30 days of MI): low-dose colchicine 0.5 mg daily lowered the composite ischaemic endpoint vs placebo (5.5% vs 7.1%; HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.61–0.96) over a median 22.6 months [[Tardif2019COLCOT]].
- LoDoCo2 (5522 patients with chronic coronary disease): colchicine 0.5 mg daily lowered the primary endpoint vs placebo (6.8% vs 9.6%; HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.57–0.83). Non-cardiovascular deaths were numerically higher (HR 1.51, 95% CI 0.99–2.31) [[Nidorf2020LoDoCo2]].
- A meta-analysis of 5 colchicine trials (11,816 patients) found that colchicine reduced MI by 22% (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.64–0.94) with no difference in all-cause death (RR 1.08) [[Fiolet2021Colchicine]].
- Clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is an expanded blood-cell clone with somatic mutations. It was linked to 1.9 times the risk of coronary heart disease (95% CI 1.4–2.7) and 4.0 times the risk of early-onset MI (95% CI 2.4–6.7) (Early evidence) [[Jaiswal2017CHIP]].
- In mice prone to high cholesterol, bone marrow lacking Tet2 caused larger atherosclerotic lesions. Tet2-deficient macrophages expressed more pro-atherogenic chemokines and cytokines (Early evidence) [[Jaiswal2017CHIP]].
- CHIP is a common, age-related contributor to cardiovascular risk that was not recognised before [[Libby2021Nature]].

### ischaemia · The wavefront of cell death (animal studies)
- In dogs, myocardial injury after up to 15 minutes of total proximal coronary occlusion is reversible: the muscle cells survive [[Kloner2001Stunning]].
- After 40 minutes of occlusion in dogs, necrosis was confined to the subendocardium. With longer occlusion, irreversible injury spread as a "wavefront" toward the subepicardium [[Reimer1977]].
- Transmural extent of necrosis was 38% after 40 minutes, 57% after 3 hours, 71% after 6 hours and 85% after 24 hours of ischaemia [[Reimer1977]].
- The authors describe a subepicardial zone of ischaemic but still viable muscle that can be salvaged for at least 3 and perhaps 6 hours after occlusion in the dog [[Reimer1977]].
- Expressed as a share of the myocardium at risk, necrosis involved 28% at 40 minutes, 70% at 3 hours and 72% at 6 hours of occlusion before reperfusion, vs 79% with permanent occlusion [[Reimer1979]].
- The lateral edges of the infarct were set by the anatomical boundaries of the ischaemic bed. Bed size ranged from 29% to 48% of the left ventricle [[Reimer1979]].
- Final transmural necrosis depended on subepicardial collateral flow after 3 hours or longer (r = 0.92 at 3 hours), but not at 40 minutes. Infarct size could be predicted from flow measured soon after occlusion [[Reimer1979]].
- Preconditioning: four 5-minute occlusions before a sustained 40-minute occlusion limited infarct size to 25% of that in controls. There was no benefit with a 3-hour sustained occlusion [[Murry1986]].
- Ischaemic preconditioning has been seen in "virtually every species" studied. Episodes as short as 5 minutes protect against a later, longer occlusion [[Kloner2001Stunning]].
- Possible human counterparts of preconditioning include the protective effect of pre-infarction angina and the angina "warm-up" phenomenon (Hypothesis) [[Kloner2001Stunning]].
- No-reflow: after 40 minutes of ischaemia in dogs, tracers reached the damaged tissue evenly on reperfusion. After 90 minutes, parts of the inner myocardium were not reperfused, because of severe capillary damage and cell swelling [[Kloner1974NoReflow]].
- In that model, the most severely ischaemic muscle cells die before capillary damage and no-reflow begin [[Kloner1974NoReflow]].

### ischaemia · Time is muscle: reperfusion timing in people
- Gersh and colleagues proposed a hypothetical curve linking symptom duration to mortality reduction and myocardial salvage. Reperfusion gives a "striking benefit" in the first 2–3 hours after symptom onset. After that, the mortality benefit continues but gets smaller over time [[Gersh2005JAMA]].
- Fibrinolysis trials (22 trials, 50,246 patients): lives saved per 1000 treated were 65, 37, 26 and 29 when treatment started at 0–1, 1–2, 2–3 and 3–6 hours after symptom onset [[Boersma1996]].
- Proportional mortality reduction was 44% (95% CI 32–53) when treatment started within 2 hours vs 20% (95% CI 15–25) when it started later [[Boersma1996]].
- Primary angioplasty for STEMI (1791 patients): each 30 minutes of delay was associated with a relative risk of 1-year mortality of 1.075 (95% CI 1.008–1.15), after adjustment for age, sex, diabetes and previous revascularisation [[DeLuca2004]].
- In that cohort, longer ischaemic time was associated with age >70, female sex, diabetes and previous revascularisation [[DeLuca2004]].
- Cardiac MRI after primary PCI in 70 STEMI patients: mean infarct size rose with symptom-to-balloon time, at 8% (≤90 minutes), 11.7% (>90–150), 12.7% (>150–360) and 17.9% (>360 minutes) of the left ventricle [[Francone2009]].
- In the same groups, salvaged myocardium fell from 8.5% to 3.2%, 2.4% and 2.1%. It was "markedly reduced" once reperfusion took more than 90 minutes [[Francone2009]].
- Microvascular obstruction rose with delay: 0.5%, 1.5%, 3.7% and 6.6% across the same time groups [[Francone2009]].

### ischaemia · Stunning, hibernation and reperfusion injury
- Stunned myocardium is prolonged contractile dysfunction after ischaemia, in muscle that has been salvaged by reperfusion. Ischaemia may "hit, run and stun" [[Braunwald1982]].
- Repeated stunning may lead to chronic post-ischaemic left ventricular dysfunction (Hypothesis in the original paper) [[Braunwald1982]].
- Mechanisms of stunning include oxygen free radicals, altered calcium handling and possibly changes in contractile proteins. Stunning has been seen after angioplasty, unstable angina, stress-induced ischaemia, thrombolysis and cardiopulmonary bypass [[Kloner2001Stunning]].
- Hibernating myocardium is resting left ventricular dysfunction caused by reduced coronary blood flow that can be partly or fully reversed by revascularisation or by lowering oxygen demand. It is distinct from stunning [[Rahimtoola1989]].
- Myocardial reperfusion injury: reperfusion itself can cause cardiomyocyte death, and as of 2013 there was no effective therapy for it [[Hausenloy2013JCI]].
- Cell death in ischaemia-reperfusion includes necrosis, apoptosis, necroptosis and pyroptosis. The coronary microcirculation is also injured (microvascular obstruction) [[Heusch2020]].
- Many interventions reduce infarct size in preclinical studies but have translated poorly into better clinical outcomes (Established gap) [[Heusch2020]].

### ischaemia · Infarct size and outcomes
- Infarct size is a major determinant of prognosis after acute MI [[Heusch2020]].
- Pooled patient-level data from 10 primary-PCI trials (2632 patients, infarct size by cardiac MRI or SPECT at a median 4 days): median infarct size was 17.9% of left ventricular mass [[Stone2016InfarctSize]].
- In that analysis, each 5% increase in infarct size was associated with higher 1-year all-cause mortality (adjusted HR 1.19, 95% CI 1.18–1.20) and hospitalisation for heart failure (HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.19–1.21). Infarct size was not related to reinfarction [[Stone2016InfarctSize]].
- Kaplan-Meier 1-year rates in that pooled cohort were 2.2% for death, 2.5% for reinfarction and 2.6% for heart failure hospitalisation (trial populations) [[Stone2016InfarctSize]].

### risk · INTERHEART: nine modifiable risk factors
- INTERHEART was a case-control study of first acute MI in 52 countries (15,152 cases, 14,820 controls) [[Yusuf2004INTERHEART]].
- Odds ratios with population attributable risk (PAR), each vs its reference group [[Yusuf2004INTERHEART]]:
  - raised ApoB/ApoA1 ratio (top vs lowest quintile): OR 3.25, PAR 49.2% for the top four quintiles
  - current smoking (vs never): OR 2.87, PAR 35.7% for current plus former smoking
  - psychosocial factors: OR 2.67, PAR 32.5%
  - diabetes: OR 2.37, PAR 9.9%
  - history of hypertension: OR 1.91, PAR 17.9%
  - abdominal obesity: OR 1.62 middle tertile and 1.12 top tertile vs lowest, PAR 20.1%
- Protective factors in INTERHEART [[Yusuf2004INTERHEART]]:
  - daily fruit and vegetables: OR 0.70 (PAR 13.7% for not eating them daily)
  - regular physical activity: OR 0.86 (PAR 12.2%)
  - regular alcohol use: OR 0.91 (PAR 6.7%)
- Together the nine risk factors accounted for 90% of the PAR in men and 94% in women, with similar associations across regions, ages and both sexes [[Yusuf2004INTERHEART]].
- Median age at first MI was 65 in women vs 56 in men. Women have their first MI about 9 years later than men on average, and higher risk-factor levels at younger ages in men largely explain the gap [[Anand2008Women]].
- Hypertension (OR 2.95 vs 2.32) and diabetes (OR 4.26 vs 2.67) were more strongly associated with MI in women than in men. Physical activity and moderate alcohol use were associated with lower odds of MI more strongly in women than in men [[Anand2008Women]].
- In women and men combined, the nine risk factors had a PAR above 94% (96% in women, 93% in men) [[Anand2008Women]].

### risk · Tobacco
- Current smoking: OR 2.95 (95% CI 2.77–3.14) for non-fatal MI vs never smoking. Risk rose by 5.6% for each additional cigarette smoked [[Teo2006Tobacco]].
- After quitting, the OR fell to 1.87 within 3 years. A residual excess (OR 1.22) remained 20 or more years after quitting [[Teo2006Tobacco]].
- Chewing tobacco alone: OR 2.23. Smoking plus chewing gave the highest risk (OR 4.09). Beedies (a South Asian hand-rolled cigarette) carried a risk similar to cigarettes (OR 2.89) [[Teo2006Tobacco]].
- Second-hand smoke showed a graded risk: OR 1.24 at 1–7 hours per week and 1.62 at more than 21 hours per week [[Teo2006Tobacco]].
- PAR of current smoking was highest in young men (58.3%) and lowest in older women (6.2%) [[Teo2006Tobacco]].

### risk · LDL cholesterol is causal
- The European Atherosclerosis Society consensus concluded that evidence from genetic, epidemiologic, Mendelian randomisation and randomised trials "unequivocally establishes that LDL causes ASCVD" (Established) [[Ference2017EAS]].
- Meta-analyses of more than 200 studies (over 2 million participants, more than 150,000 cardiovascular events) show a dose-dependent, log-linear link between LDL-C exposure and risk. The effect appears to grow with longer exposure [[Ference2017EAS]].
- Mendelian randomisation across 9 variants in 6 genes (312,321 participants): lifelong, naturally lower LDL-C was associated with a 54.5% lower risk of coronary heart disease per 1 mmol/L (38.7 mg/dL). That is about 3 times the reduction per unit seen with statins started later in life [[Ference2012MR]].
- PCSK9 nonsense mutations in Black participants (2.6% of 3363): 28% lower LDL-C and 88% lower coronary heart disease risk over 15 years (HR 0.11) [[Cohen2006PCSK9]].
- A PCSK9 variant in White participants (3.2% of 9524): 15% lower LDL-C and 47% lower coronary heart disease risk (HR 0.50) [[Cohen2006PCSK9]].
- Randomised statin trials (26 trials, about 170,000 participants): each 1.0 mmol/L reduction in LDL-C was associated with about 22% fewer major vascular events (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.76–0.80) [[CTT2010]].
- Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH): heterozygous FH has a prevalence of about 1 in 500 to 1 in 200, is diagnosed in under 1% in most countries, and carries up to a 13-fold increased risk of coronary heart disease [[Nordestgaard2013FH]].

### risk · Lipoprotein(a)
- Lp(a) concentration is more than 90% determined by genetic variation at the LPA locus, more than any other lipoprotein [[Kronenberg2022]].
- High Lp(a) affects about 1.4 billion people worldwide. Epidemiologic and genetic studies support a causal, continuous link with cardiovascular outcomes, even when LDL-C is very low [[Kronenberg2022]].
- Very high Lp(a) (>180 mg/dL or >430 nmol/L) carries a lifetime ASCVD risk equivalent to untreated heterozygous FH [[Kronenberg2022]].
- The EAS panel recommends measuring Lp(a) at least once in adults. As of 2022, no specific Lp(a)-lowering therapy was proven to reduce events [[Kronenberg2022]].
- Two LPA variants were associated with coronary disease: rs10455872 (OR 1.70) and rs3798220 (OR 1.92). With two or more variants the OR was 2.57, and the association disappeared after adjusting for Lp(a) level, which supports causality [[Clarke2009]].
- The 2019 ACC/AHA guideline counts Lp(a) ≥50 mg/dL or ≥125 nmol/L as a risk-enhancing factor [[Arnett2019ACC]].

### risk · Blood pressure, diabetes, kidney disease, obesity
- Blood pressure: in one million adults in 61 cohorts, each 20 mm Hg higher usual systolic pressure (or 10 mm Hg diastolic) at ages 40–69 was associated with a twofold higher death rate from ischaemic heart disease. There was no threshold down to at least 115/75 mm Hg [[Lewington2002BP]].
- Diabetes: in 698,782 people from 102 cohorts, diabetes was associated with an adjusted HR of 2.00 (95% CI 1.83–2.19) for coronary heart disease. The HR was higher in women and at ages 40–59 [[ERFC2010]].
- Chronic kidney disease: in 1,120,295 adults, adjusted HRs for cardiovascular events rose as estimated GFR fell: 1.4 (45–59), 2.0 (30–44), 2.8 (15–29) and 3.4 (<15 mL/min/1.73 m²) [[Go2004CKD]].
- Impaired kidney function and raised urinary albumin increase cardiovascular risk two- to four-fold after adjustment for traditional risk factors [[Gansevoort2013]].
- Waist-to-hip ratio showed a graded association with MI. ORs by quintile were 1.15, 1.39, 1.90 and 2.52 (2nd to 5th vs 1st, adjusted for age, sex, region and smoking) [[Yusuf2005Obesity]].
- PAR for a high waist-to-hip ratio (top two quintiles) was 24.3%, compared with 7.7% for high body-mass index. After adjustment for other risk factors, BMI was no longer associated with MI [[Yusuf2005Obesity]].

### risk · Family history, psychosocial stress and triggers
- Framingham Offspring (2302 adults): having at least one parent with premature cardiovascular disease (father <55, mother <65) gave a multivariable-adjusted OR of 2.0 (95% CI 1.2–3.1) in men and 1.7 (95% CI 0.9–3.1) in women for an 8-year event [[LloydJones2004]].
- INTERHEART psychosocial stress: permanent work stress OR 2.14, permanent stress at home 2.12, severe financial stress 1.33, a stressful life event in the past year 1.48, and depression 1.55 [[Rosengren2004Psychosocial]].
- Triggers in the hour before symptoms (case-crossover): heavy physical exertion OR 2.31, anger or emotional upset OR 2.44, both together OR 3.05 [[Smyth2016Triggers]].

### risk · Chronic inflammatory and infectious conditions
- Rheumatoid arthritis: pooled RR for MI 1.68 (95% CI 1.40–2.03) and for any incident cardiovascular disease 1.48 [[AvinaZubieta2012RA]].
- Psoriasis (UK cohort): for a 30-year-old, adjusted RR for MI was 1.29 with mild and 3.10 with severe psoriasis. For a 60-year-old the RRs were 1.08 and 1.36 [[Gelfand2006Psoriasis]].
- HIV: AMI rate 11.13 vs 6.98 per 1000 person-years. Adjusted RR was 1.75 overall, 2.98 in women and 1.40 in men. Smoking data were incomplete [[Triant2007HIV]].
- Systemic lupus erythematosus: women aged 35–44 with lupus were over 50 times more likely to have an MI than similar-age Framingham women (rate ratio 52.43, 95% CI 21.6–98.5) [[Manzi1997SLE]].
- The 2019 ACC/AHA guideline lists chronic inflammatory conditions (psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, HIV/AIDS) as risk-enhancing factors [[Arnett2019ACC]].

### risk · Women-specific factors
- Pre-eclampsia: in a meta-analysis of 3.49 million women, pre-eclampsia was associated with RR 2.16 (95% CI 1.86–2.52) for later ischaemic heart disease (mean 11.7 years) and RR 3.70 for hypertension [[Bellamy2007]].
- A 2017 meta-analysis (22 studies, more than 6.4 million women) reported adjusted RRs after pre-eclampsia of 2.50 for coronary heart disease, 4.19 for heart failure and 2.21 for cardiovascular death [[Wu2017Preeclampsia]].
- The AHA lists adverse pregnancy outcomes that raise later cardiovascular risk: hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, preterm delivery, gestational diabetes, small-for-gestational-age delivery, placental abruption and pregnancy loss [[Parikh2021APO]].
- Premature menopause (before 40), UK Biobank: adjusted HR 1.36 for natural and 1.87 for surgical premature menopause, for a composite of cardiovascular diseases [[Honigberg2019]].
- The 2019 ACC/AHA guideline lists premature menopause (before 40) and pre-eclampsia as risk-enhancing factors [[Arnett2019ACC]].

### risk · Ancestry and changing risk profiles
- South Asians have higher proportional mortality from ASCVD than other Asian groups and non-Hispanic White people in the US [[Volgman2018SouthAsian]].
- The biology of ASCVD in South Asians is "no different" from other groups. Most of the excess risk is explained by known risk factors, especially insulin resistance, and no unique risk factors have been found [[Volgman2018SouthAsian]].
- The 2019 ACC/AHA guideline lists South Asian ancestry as a "high-risk race/ethnicity" risk-enhancing factor [[Arnett2019ACC]].
- Atherosclerosis now affects younger people, more women, and more ethnic groups than before. Research attention has widened to triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and non-traditional drivers such as disturbed sleep, inactivity, air pollution and environmental stress [[Libby2021Nature]].

### risk · Risk estimation (teaching tools only)
- The 2019 ACC/AHA guideline recommends that adults aged 40–75 being evaluated for prevention have their 10-year ASCVD risk estimated with the Pooled Cohort Equations, followed by a clinician–patient risk discussion before starting medicines [[Arnett2019ACC]].
- Guideline risk categories (10-year ASCVD risk): borderline 5% to <7.5%, intermediate ≥7.5% to <20%, high ≥20% [[Arnett2019ACC]].
- Other risk-enhancing factors listed in the guideline [[Arnett2019ACC]]:
  - family history of premature ASCVD (men <55, women <65)
  - LDL-C 160–189 mg/dL
  - metabolic syndrome
  - CKD (eGFR 15–59)
  - persistently high triglycerides (≥175 mg/dL)
- The guideline advises at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity (or 75 minutes of vigorous) physical activity. It also advises asking about tobacco use at every visit (quoted as published, not as instructions) [[Arnett2019ACC]].
- The AHA PREVENT equations were derived from 3,281,919 people and validated in 3,330,085 more, US adults aged 30–79 without cardiovascular disease. They are sex-specific and race-free, add eGFR, and predict total cardiovascular disease including heart failure [[Khan2024PREVENT]].
- Median C-statistics for PREVENT in external validation were 0.794 in women and 0.757 in men. Adding urine albumin, HbA1c and a social deprivation index improved discrimination only slightly [[Khan2024PREVENT]].

### Exam hooks
- The initiating event in atherosclerosis is subendothelial retention of apoB-containing lipoproteins [[Tabas2007]].
- The fatty streak is an AHA type II lesion, the first lesion visible to the naked eye. Types I–II are the usual lesions in children [[Stary1994]].
- A thin-cap fibroatheroma has a fibrous cap <65 µm over a necrotic core [[Virmani2006]].
- Ruptured plaques have caps <55 µm in autopsy series [[Narula2013]].
- The most common cause of coronary thrombosis is plaque rupture, then erosion. The calcified nodule is uncommon [[Virmani2006,Falk2013]].
- Erosion is relatively more common in younger patients, women and NSTE-ACS, and has thicker caps and less stenosis [[Farb1996,Jia2013OCT]].
- About two-thirds of later MI culprit arteries had less than 50% stenosis on an earlier angiogram [[Little1988]].
- In PROSPECT, the high-risk non-culprit lesion features were plaque burden ≥70%, minimal lumen area ≤4.0 mm² and TCFA [[Stone2011PROSPECT]].
- Myocardial necrosis progresses from subendocardium to subepicardium (the wavefront). In dogs, transmural necrosis was 38% at 40 minutes, 57% at 3 hours and 71% at 6 hours [[Reimer1977]].
- Occlusion up to about 15 minutes causes reversible injury, and can leave stunning behind [[Kloner2001Stunning]].
- Stunning is dysfunction after reperfusion. Hibernation is dysfunction during ongoing low flow that reverses with revascularisation [[Braunwald1982,Rahimtoola1989]].
- Ischaemic preconditioning (four 5-minute occlusions) cut infarct size to 25% of control after a 40-minute occlusion in dogs [[Murry1986]].
- Fibrinolysis saved 65 lives per 1000 in the first hour vs 29 per 1000 at 3–6 hours [[Boersma1996]].
- In primary PCI, each 30 minutes of delay was associated with RR 1.075 for 1-year death [[DeLuca2004]].
- Each 5% larger infarct was associated with HR 1.19 for 1-year death after primary PCI [[Stone2016InfarctSize]].
- In INTERHEART, nine modifiable factors explained 90% of PAR in men and 94% in women. ApoB/ApoA1 had the largest PAR (49.2%) [[Yusuf2004INTERHEART]].
- Waist-to-hip ratio predicts MI better than BMI (PAR 24.3% vs 7.7%) [[Yusuf2005Obesity]].
- An Lp(a) ≥50 mg/dL (≥125 nmol/L) is a risk-enhancing factor, and Lp(a) is more than 90% genetically determined [[Arnett2019ACC,Kronenberg2022]].
- CANTOS showed that blocking IL-1β lowers cardiovascular events without lowering lipids (HR 0.85 at 150 mg) [[Ridker2017CANTOS]].
- Pre-eclampsia roughly doubles later ischaemic heart disease risk (RR 2.16) [[Bellamy2007]].

### In plain words
- If you think you or someone else is having a heart attack, call your local emergency number now (911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 112 in the EU).
- A heart attack (myocardial infarction, MI) usually happens when a blood clot suddenly blocks an artery that feeds the heart muscle [[Badimon2014]].
- The clot usually forms on a fatty bump in the artery wall called a plaque. A plaque builds up slowly over many years, often starting in the teens or twenties [[Strong1999PDAY,Tuzcu2001]].
- Cholesterol carried by LDL particles gets trapped in the artery wall and starts the process. Lower LDL over a lifetime means less risk [[Tabas2007,Ference2017EAS]].
- A plaque can crack open (rupture) or its surface can wear away (erosion). Either one can set off a clot [[Falk2013]].
- Many heart attacks start at plaques that were not blocking much of the artery beforehand. That is why a heart attack can come "out of the blue" [[Little1988]].
- Heart muscle starts to die within minutes to hours without blood. The damage spreads from the inner layer of the heart wall outward [[Reimer1977]].
- The sooner blood flow is restored, the more heart muscle can be saved. Doctors call this "time is muscle" [[Gersh2005JAMA,Boersma1996]].
- A smaller heart attack (less muscle lost) is linked to a lower chance of heart failure and death later. These are averages across many people [[Stone2016InfarctSize]].
- Some heart muscle that stops working after a heart attack is only "stunned" and can recover over days to weeks [[Braunwald1982]].
- Smoking, high blood pressure, diabetes, unhealthy cholesterol, extra weight around the waist and long-term stress all raise risk. Many of these can be changed, and support helps [[Yusuf2004INTERHEART]].
- Quitting smoking lowers heart attack risk within a few years, at any age [[Teo2006Tobacco]].
- Some risks you cannot change, like your genes, family history, or a high level of Lp(a), a type of cholesterol particle you inherit. Knowing about them helps you and your clinician plan [[Kronenberg2022,LloydJones2004]].
- For women, problems in pregnancy such as pre-eclampsia, or early menopause, are worth telling your clinician about. They can raise heart risk later in life [[Bellamy2007,Honigberg2019]].
- Risk calculators give an estimate for groups of people. They are for planning prevention with a clinician, never for deciding whether symptoms need emergency care [[Arnett2019ACC]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Should ACS caused by plaque erosion be treated without a stent? The uncontrolled EROSION study suggests antithrombotic therapy alone may be an option [[Jia2017EROSION]], but erosion is hard to diagnose without intracoronary OCT and its mechanisms are "largely unknown" [[Bentzon2014]]. Status: Early evidence. No randomised trial was found in this pass.
- Is the "vulnerable plaque" the right target? Autopsy and imaging tie events to thin-cap fibroatheromas [[Virmani2006,Narula2013]]. PROSPECT found that plaque burden and lumen area predicted events as strongly as TCFA morphology, and that half of events came from treated culprit sites [[Stone2011PROSPECT]]. Libby argues thinking has moved beyond the vulnerable-plaque concept [[Libby2021Nature]]. Status: debated.
- Is the share of erosion rising? Autopsy series from the 1990s put erosion at 31–44% of thrombotic deaths [[Farb1996,Burke1997]]. OCT series report 25–31% in ACS [[Jia2013OCT,Jia2017EROSION]]. Differences in method (autopsy vs imaging, sudden death vs hospital ACS) make direct trend comparison uncertain.
- Treating inflammation: CANTOS proved the concept [[Ridker2017CANTOS]], and colchicine trials lowered ischaemic events [[Tardif2019COLCOT,Nidorf2020LoDoCo2]]. But CANTOS raised fatal infections, and colchicine trials showed more non-cardiovascular deaths with no all-cause mortality benefit [[Fiolet2021Colchicine]]. Status: benefit on events Established, net benefit debated. Pass G covers current therapy.
- Reperfusion injury: reperfusion can itself kill heart cells [[Hausenloy2013JCI]], but many cardioprotective strategies that work in animals have failed in patients [[Heusch2020]]. Status: no proven therapy.
- What shape is the time–benefit curve? The Gersh curve is hypothetical and steepest in the first 2–3 hours [[Gersh2005JAMA]]. Fibrinolysis data fit a non-linear curve [[Boersma1996]], and primary PCI data suggest risk rises with every 30 minutes [[DeLuca2004]]. Status: the direction is consistent, the exact shape is uncertain.
- Lp(a): causal evidence is strong [[Kronenberg2022,Clarke2009]], but as of the 2022 consensus no specific Lp(a)-lowering drug had been shown to reduce events [[Kronenberg2022]]. Status: outcome trials pending in the sources reviewed.
- Which calculator should be used? PREVENT is race-free, adds kidney function and predicts heart failure [[Khan2024PREVENT]], while the 2019 guideline was built around the Pooled Cohort Equations [[Arnett2019ACC]]. How their estimates compare across groups was not quantified in the abstracts read. Status: unresolved here.
- Animal-to-human translation of the wavefront: dog timings (38% transmural at 40 minutes, 71% at 6 hours) depend on collateral flow and bed size [[Reimer1977,Reimer1979]]. Human salvage falls sharply after about 90 minutes in one CMR study [[Francone2009]]. Status: the principle is Established, human timing is variable.
