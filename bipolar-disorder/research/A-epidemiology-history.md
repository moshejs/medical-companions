## Findings

### what · How common is bipolar disorder (population surveys)
- In the WHO World Mental Health (WMH) surveys (61,392 community adults in 11 countries, DSM-IV, CIDI 3.0), aggregate lifetime prevalence was 0.6% for bipolar I disorder (BP-I), 0.4% for bipolar II (BP-II), 1.4% for subthreshold bipolar disorder and 2.4% for the whole bipolar spectrum (BPS) [[Merikangas2011]].
- Twelve-month prevalence in the same WMH surveys was 0.4% for BP-I, 0.3% for BP-II, 0.8% for subthreshold bipolar and 1.5% for the whole spectrum [[Merikangas2011]].
- Prevalence varied by country in the WMH surveys. The United States had the highest lifetime and 12-month spectrum prevalence (4.4%, 2.8%) and India the lowest (0.1%, 0.1%). Japan, a high-income country, was very low (0.7%, 0.2%), and Colombia, a low-income country, was high (2.6% lifetime) [[Merikangas2011]].
- In the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication (NCS-R; 9,282 adults), lifetime prevalence (12-month in brackets) was 1.0% (0.6%) for BP-I, 1.1% (0.8%) for BP-II and 2.4% (1.4%) for subthreshold bipolar disorder [[Merikangas2007]].
- The NCS-R authors defined subthreshold bipolar disorder as recurrent hypomania without a major depressive episode, or with fewer symptoms than threshold hypomania. They found it "common, clinically significant, and underdetected in treatment settings" [[Merikangas2007]].
- In the NCS-R, most people with bipolar disorder had received lifetime professional treatment for emotional problems, but use of antimanic medication was uncommon, especially in general medical settings [[Merikangas2007]].
- Broader spectrum definitions give higher figures. One review reported lifetime bipolar-spectrum rates of 3–6.5%. In the Zurich cohort, 5.5% had DSM-IV hypomania or mania by age 35, and a further 2.8% had recurrent brief hypomania lasting 1–3 days (Early evidence) [[Angst1998]].
- A 2023 JAMA review estimates that bipolar disorder affects about 8 million adults in the US and about 40 million people worldwide [[Nierenberg2023]].
- Two major reviews describe bipolar disorders as affecting more than 1% of the world's population, "irrespective of nationality, ethnic origin, or socioeconomic status" [[Grande2016,Vieta2018]].
- A meta-analysis of 19 pediatric epidemiological studies (N = 56,103) found a weighted prevalence of bipolar spectrum disorders in young people of 3.9% (95% CI 2.6–5.8%), and 0.6% (0.3–1.2%) for BP-I. Rates were not higher in the US than in other Western countries, and were not rising over time [[VanMeter2019]].
- In the WMH surveys, three-quarters of people with bipolar spectrum disorder met criteria for at least one other disorder. Anxiety disorders, especially panic attacks, were the most common [[Merikangas2011]].

### what · Global Burden of Disease estimates
- GBD 2013 estimated 32.7 million prevalent cases of bipolar disorder worldwide in 1990 and 48.8 million in 2013. This 49.1% rise was entirely explained by population growth and ageing [[Ferrari2016]].
- In GBD 2013, bipolar disorder caused 9.9 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs). That was 0.4% of all DALYs and 1.3% of all years lived with disability (YLDs) [[Ferrari2016]].
- In GBD 2013, female individuals accounted for 5.5 million and male individuals for 4.4 million bipolar DALYs. DALYs appeared from age 10, peaked in the 20s and fell after that [[Ferrari2016]].
- GBD attributes no years of life lost (YLLs) to bipolar disorder, so its DALYs equal its YLDs. This means GBD burden figures leave out the excess deaths described in the mortality section below [[Ferrari2016,GBD2019Mental]].
- GBD 2017 estimated new (incident) cases at 3.06 million in 1990 and 4.53 million in 2017 (+47.74%). Bipolar DALYs rose from 6.02 million to 9.29 million (+54.4%), while age-standardised rates barely changed (EAPC 0.14 for incidence and 0.05 for DALYs) [[He2020GBD]].
- In GBD 2017, ages 10–19 contributed the most new cases and ages 20–44 the most DALYs. Age-standardised incidence was highest in tropical Latin America and lowest in East Asia [[He2020GBD]].
- In GBD 2017, the lowest Socio-demographic Index (SDI) quintile had the highest age-standardised incidence (about 64 per 100,000), and the highest SDI quintile had the highest age-standardised DALY rate (about 140 per 100,000) [[He2020GBD]].
- An analysis of GBD 2021 reports a global count that rose from 30,239,332 in 1990 to 53,917,371 in 2021, with higher age-standardised rates in high-SDI countries. The abstract labels these counts as "incidence"; see Open questions [[Jiang2025GBD]].
- A second GBD 2021 analysis found that incidence peaks at ages 15–19 and is higher in females than in males. Australasia had the highest age-standardised prevalence and incidence, and incidence, prevalence and DALYs are projected to keep rising through 2035 [[Liu2026GBD]].
- In GBD 2019, bipolar disorder was one of 12 mental disorders modelled. Mental disorders as a group rose from 80.8 million DALYs in 1990 to 125.3 million in 2019, and from 3.1% to 4.9% of all global DALYs [[GBD2019Mental]].
- The GBD 2019 mental disorders analysis found no marked difference in burden by sex for bipolar disorder, and less variation in prevalence across regions for bipolar disorder than for most other disorders [[GBD2019Mental]].
- In GBD 2023, which also includes bipolar disorder, mental disorders together were the fifth leading cause of global DALYs (up from 12th in 1990) and the leading cause of YLDs, at 17.3% of all YLDs [[GBD2023Mental]].
- Major reviews call bipolar disorders one of the leading causes of disability in young people, through cognitive and functional impairment and raised mortality [[Grande2016,Vieta2018]].

### what · Age at onset
- In the WMH surveys, mean (SE) age of onset was 18.4 (0.7) years for BP-I, 20.0 (0.6) years for BP-II and 21.9 (0.4) years for subthreshold bipolar disorder. About half of people with BP-I or subthreshold bipolar disorder reported onset before age 25 [[Merikangas2011]].
- A JAMA review states that onset is usually between ages 15 and 25, and that depression is the most frequent first presentation [[Nierenberg2023]].
- A meta-analysis of delays in bipolar care puts the peak age at onset at 15–25 years [[Scott2022Delay]].
- An admixture analysis of 368 people with BP-I found three onset subgroups, with mean onset ages of 17.4, 25.1 and 40.4 years. Affected siblings tended to fall in the same subgroup (Early evidence) [[Bellivier2003]].
- A meta-analysis of 192 general-population studies (n = 708,561) found a peak onset age of 20.5 years for mood disorders as a block (median 31). It placed bipolar disorder among the later-onset disorders, with a median onset of 30–35 years. Population surveys date onset later than clinical samples do; see Open questions [[Solmi2022]].
- In the NCS-R, median age of onset for mood disorders as a group was 30 years. Half of all lifetime mental disorders had started by age 14 and three-quarters by age 24 [[Kessler2005Onset]].
- Across the WMH surveys of 29 countries (156,331 respondents), first onset of any mental disorder peaked at about age 15 [[McGrath2023]].
- GBD analyses put the largest share of new bipolar cases at ages 10–19, with incidence peaking at 15–19 years [[He2020GBD,Liu2026GBD]].

### what · Sex and gender
- Most studies report a near-equal sex ratio for bipolar disorder overall. Most also find that women have higher rates of bipolar II/hypomania, rapid cycling and mixed episodes [[Diflorio2010]].
- In the WMH surveys, lifetime rates of BP-I and subthreshold bipolar disorder were higher in males, and BP-II was higher in females [[Merikangas2011]].
- A review found no consistent sex differences in rates of depressive episodes, age or polarity of onset, severity, treatment response or suicidal behaviour. The biggest difference was the effect of childbirth and other reproductive events on women [[Diflorio2010]].
- Lifetime rapid cycling is more common in women than men (meta-regression p = 0.003) [[Miola2023]].

### what · Delay to diagnosis and misdiagnosis
- A meta-analysis (27 studies, 51 samples, 9,415 patients) found a pooled interval of 5.8 years between bipolar onset and its first management. The interval was longer when onset was dated from the first episode and management meant diagnosis [[Dagani2017]].
- A 2022 meta-analysis (59 studies, more than 40,000 people) found median delays of 3.5 years to seeking help, 6.7 years to diagnosis and 5.9 years of untreated illness. Diagnosis and guideline treatment are likely delayed until ages 25–35 [[Scott2022Delay]].
- Diagnosis came sooner for older people and for those living outside North America. Untreated illness was shorter after a psychotic or manic onset and where early-intervention services existed [[Scott2022Delay]].
- A 2025 meta-analysis found a pooled mean duration of undiagnosed or untreated bipolar disorder of 9.10 years. The delay was longer with early onset, a depressive first episode, lifetime suicide attempts, comorbid anxiety or alcohol use, and a family history of bipolar disorder. It was shorter with BP-I and with psychotic symptoms [[Keramatian2025]].
- A JAMA review states that diagnosis and optimal treatment are often delayed by a mean of about 9 years after the first depressive episode [[Nierenberg2023]].
- In a 2000 US survey of 600 people with bipolar disorder in support groups, 69% had been misdiagnosed, most often with unipolar depression. Those misdiagnosed had seen a mean of 4 physicians before the correct diagnosis [[Hirschfeld2003]].
- In the same survey, more than a third waited 10 years or more for an accurate diagnosis, although more than a third had sought help within a year of symptoms starting [[Hirschfeld2003]].
- Diagnosis is difficult because the illness usually begins with a depressive episode that looks like unipolar depression, and no valid biomarkers exist. Spotting past hypomania and following the person over time are key [[Grande2016,Vieta2018]].

### episodes · What mania, hypomania, depression and mixed states look like
- Bipolar I disorder is defined by a syndromal manic episode. Bipolar II disorder is defined by a syndromal hypomanic episode plus a major depressive episode [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].
- Both ICD-11 and DSM-5 require, as the core of a manic or hypomanic episode, elated, euphoric, expansive or irritable mood plus increased activity or energy. ICD-10 and DSM-IV-TR required only the mood change [[Angst2020ICD11]].
- The accompanying symptoms in both systems are increased talkativeness or pressured speech, flight of ideas, increased self-esteem or grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, distractibility, impulsive or reckless behaviour, and increased sexual drive, sociability or goal-directed activity [[Angst2020ICD11]].
- DSM-5 sets the minimum length of a hypomanic episode at 4 or more days, while ICD-11 says "several days". When mood is only irritable, DSM-5 requires 4 rather than 3 of the 7 symptoms; ICD-11 does not treat irritability differently [[Angst2020ICD11]].
- Among 316 inpatients with mania, the most frequent signs were motor activation, accelerated thinking, pressured speech and decreased sleep. Irritability, dysphoric mood and mood lability were also prominent, so mania is "not a purely euphoric state" [[Cassidy1998]].
- In the same cohort, grandiosity, euphoric mood and pressured speech were more common in pure mania, and dysphoria, lability, anxiety, guilt and irritability in mixed mania. Suicidality was present in 7% of the whole cohort [[Cassidy1998]].
- Bipolar depressive episodes resemble major depressive episodes. Manic and hypomanic episodes are a distinct change in mood and behaviour over discrete periods [[Nierenberg2023]].
- A meta-analysis of DSM-5 "mixed features" found pooled prevalences of 11.6% (95% CI 7.9–16.7%) in major depressive episodes and 26.8% (17.0–39.5%) in manic or hypomanic episodes [[Na2021]].
- DSM-5 (2013) dropped the separate DSM-IV "mixed episode" and replaced it with a "with mixed features" specifier [[Mason2016]].
- Rapid cycling (4 or more episodes a year) has a pooled lifetime prevalence of 35.5% (95% CI 27.6–44.3%) and a 1-year prevalence of 22.3% (14.4–32.9%) among people with bipolar disorder. It is linked to suicide attempts and poorer response to mood stabilisers (strong evidence) [[Miola2023]].
- Predominant polarity: about 30.0% (95% CI 23.1–37.4%) of people with bipolar disorder have mainly manic/hypomanic episodes and 28.5% (23.7–33.7%) mainly depressive episodes. A depressive onset was strongly associated with depressive predominance (OR 12.09) [[Bartoli2024]].
- In 206 people with BP-II, 80% showed a "biphasic" course, with an episode followed straight away by one of the opposite polarity. Baillarger described this pattern in 1854 [[Benazzi2006]].

### episodes · Psychotic features
- In a meta-analysis (54 studies, N = 23,461), pooled lifetime prevalence of psychotic symptoms (delusions or hallucinations) was 63% (95% CI 57.5–68%) in BP-I and 22% (14–33%) in BP-II, and 71% (61–79%) in BP-I inpatients [[Aminoff2022]].
- Point prevalence of psychotic symptoms in BP-I was 54% overall, 57% (47–66%) in manic episodes and 13% (7–23.5%) in depressive episodes [[Aminoff2022]].
- A review found that more than half of people with bipolar disorder have psychotic symptoms during their lifetime, with grandiose delusions the most common. Psychosis can mask the mood symptoms and make bipolar disorder hard to tell apart from other psychotic disorders [[Dunayevich2000]].
- Kraepelin's own case histories counted classical Schneiderian psychotic symptoms and full catatonia as consistent with manic-depressive insanity [[Kendler2021]].

### episodes · How much time is spent ill
- Over a mean of 12.8 years of weekly follow-up of 146 people with BP-I (NIMH Collaborative Depression Study), participants had symptoms in 47.3% of weeks. Depressive symptoms took up 31.9% of weeks, manic/hypomanic symptoms 8.9% and cycling/mixed symptoms 5.9% [[Judd2002]].
- In the same BP-I cohort, subsyndromal, minor depressive and hypomanic symptoms (29.9% of weeks) were nearly three times as frequent as syndromal major depressive or manic symptoms (11.2%) [[Judd2002]].
- People with BP-I changed symptom status on average 6 times a year and changed polarity more than 3 times a year [[Judd2002]].
- Over a mean of 13.4 years of follow-up of 86 people with BP-II, participants had symptoms in 53.9% of weeks. Depressive symptoms took up 50.3% of weeks, hypomanic symptoms 1.3% and cycling/mixed symptoms 2.3% [[Judd2003]].
- In BP-II, people with brief hypomanias (2–6 days) did not differ on any measure from those with hypomanias of 7 days or more [[Judd2003]].
- About 75% of symptomatic time in bipolar disorder is depressive episodes or symptoms [[Nierenberg2023]].
- Although mania defines bipolar I, depressive episodes and symptoms dominate the long-term course and account for a disproportionate share of illness and deaths [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].

### episodes · Course after a first episode
- In the McLean-Harvard First-Episode Mania Study (N = 166, followed 2–4 years after a first hospitalisation for mania), 98% reached syndromal recovery by 2 years, half of them by 5.4 weeks, and 72% reached symptomatic recovery [[Tohen2003]].
- Only 43% of the same first-episode group regained their previous work and living status (functional recovery) within 2 years [[Tohen2003]].
- Within 2 years of recovery, 40% had a new episode (20% mania, 20% depression), and 19% switched polarity without recovering first [[Tohen2003]].
- In 82 outpatients followed for a mean of 4.3 years on continuous maintenance treatment, the 5-year risk of a manic or depressive relapse was 73%. Two-thirds of those who relapsed had more than one relapse [[Gitlin1995]].
- Earlier diagnosis and treatment are associated with a better prognosis [[Nierenberg2023]].

### what · Burden: function, work and cost
- In the WMH surveys, severe role impairment was reported by about 74.0% of respondents during depression and 50.9% during mania. Role impairment was similar across bipolar subtypes [[Merikangas2011]].
- In the NCS-R, role impairment was greater during BP-II depressive episodes than BP-I depressive episodes. Subthreshold cases still had moderate to severe impairment [[Merikangas2007]].
- A systematic review of 25 studies (4,892 people, mean follow-up 4.9 years) found that about 40–60% of people with established bipolar disorder are employed. Some 40–50% may slip in occupational status over time, and employment was higher early in the illness [[Marwaha2013]].
- A meta-analysis (22 studies, n = 6,301) linked better employment outcomes to better verbal memory (Rw = 0.33), executive function (0.26) and more years of education (0.23). Worse outcomes went with more lifetime hospitalisations (−0.35), depressive symptoms (−0.25) and personality disorder (−0.49) [[Tse2014]].
- A 2000 survey reported a greater negative effect of bipolar disorder on families, relationships and employment than a similar 1992 survey [[Hirschfeld2003]].
- The total US cost of BP-I in 2015 was estimated at $202.1 billion, or $81,559 per person. Excess costs were $119.8 billion ($48,333 per person), driven mainly by caregiving (36%), direct healthcare (21%) and unemployment (20%) [[Cloutier2018]].
- Less than half of WMH respondents with lifetime bipolar spectrum disorder had received mental health treatment. Lifetime treatment was 50.2% in high-income, 33.9% in middle-income and 25.2% in low-income countries [[Merikangas2011]].
- More than 50% of people with bipolar disorder do not take their treatment as prescribed [[Nierenberg2023]].
- Effective medicines for bipolar disorder are not available everywhere, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].

### what · Mortality gap and life expectancy
- A meta-analysis (11 studies, n = 96,601) found a pooled life expectancy of 66.88 years (95% CI 64.47–69.28) in people with bipolar disorder: 70.51 years in women and 64.59 years in men. The lowest figures were in Africa [[Chan2022]].
- The weighted average years of potential life lost was 12.89 (95% CI 12.72–13.07), of which 5.94 years were from natural causes and 5.69 years from unnatural causes [[Chan2022]].
- In Danish national registers (22,635 people with bipolar disorder), remaining life expectancy at age 15 (counted to age 90) was 12.7 years shorter for men and 8.9 years shorter for women than in the general population [[Kessing2015]].
- In the same Danish data, natural causes (physical illness) accounted for 58% of lost life-years in 15-year-old boys and 67% in 15-year-old girls. This rose to 74% and 80% at age 45 [[Kessing2015]].
- A JAMA review states that life expectancy is reduced by about 12–14 years. Cardiovascular mortality is 1.6 to 2 times higher and occurs a mean of 17 years earlier than in the general population [[Nierenberg2023]].
- The Lancet seminar puts the loss at about 10–20 potential years of life, mainly from excess cardiovascular deaths and suicide [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].
- A meta-analysis of 31 studies found an all-cause standardised mortality ratio (SMR) of 2.05 (95% CI 1.89–2.23). The SMR was 7.42 for unnatural deaths and 1.64 for natural deaths [[Hayes2015]].
- Cause-specific SMRs in that meta-analysis were: suicide 14.44 (12.43–16.78), other violent death 3.68, respiratory disease 2.92, infection 2.25, circulatory disease 1.73 and neoplasm 1.14 [[Hayes2015]].
- A larger 2023 meta-analysis (57 studies, n = 678,353) found an all-cause mortality risk ratio of 2.02 (95% CI 1.89–2.16), with suicide 11.69 (9.22–14.81), unnatural causes 7.29 and natural causes 1.90 [[Biazus2023]].
- Natural-cause risk ratios in that analysis were respiratory 3.18 (2.55–3.96), cardiovascular 1.76 (1.53–2.01) and cerebrovascular 1.57 (1.34–1.84). Cancer mortality did not differ (0.99, 0.88–1.11) [[Biazus2023]].
- In UK primary-care records from 2000 to 2014, people with bipolar disorder had higher all-cause mortality (adjusted HR 1.79, 95% CI 1.67–1.88). The HR rose by 0.14 a year from 2006 to 2014, so the mortality gap was widening [[Hayes2017]].
- Metabolic syndrome (37%), obesity (21%), cigarette smoking (45%) and type 2 diabetes (14%) are more common in people with bipolar disorder and contribute to early death [[Nierenberg2023]].
- A meta-review of 20 reviews (over 1.7 million patients) found raised all-cause mortality in every mental disorder studied, equal to about 10–20 years of lost life expectancy. Bipolar disorder was among the disorders with the highest suicide risk [[Chesney2014]].

### what · Suicide risk (safe-messaging framing)
- People with bipolar disorder have a much higher suicide risk than the general population. Estimates range from about 10–30 times [[Dome2019]] to roughly 20–30 times higher [[Plans2019]]; meta-analytic suicide SMRs are 14.44 [[Hayes2015]], with a risk ratio of 11.69 [[Biazus2023]].
- The ISBD Task Force pooled a suicide rate of 164 per 100,000 person-years (95% CI 5–324). This is lower than earlier published estimates [[Schaffer2015Epi]].
- The ISBD Task Force found that suicide rates were about 1.7 times higher in men than in women with bipolar disorder, and that people with bipolar disorder account for 3.4–14% of all suicide deaths [[Schaffer2015Epi]].
- A JAMA review gives an annual suicide rate of about 0.9% in people with bipolar disorder, against 0.014% in the general population [[Nierenberg2023]].
- Epidemiological studies report that 23–26% of people with bipolar disorder attempt suicide, with higher rates in clinical samples [[Schaffer2015Epi]].
- In the WMH surveys, about 1 in 4 people with BP-I, 1 in 5 with BP-II and 1 in 10 with subthreshold bipolar disorder reported a past suicide attempt [[Merikangas2011]].
- Retrospective reports of lifetime suicide attempts did not differ significantly between BP-II (32.4%) and BP-I (36.3%) (OR 1.21, 95% CI 0.98–1.48) [[Novick2010]].
- Factors linked to suicide attempts were female sex, younger onset, a depressive first or most recent episode, comorbid anxiety, substance use or cluster B/borderline personality disorder, and a family history of suicide. Suicide deaths were linked to male sex and a family history of suicide [[Schaffer2015Meta]].
- Lithium is the treatment with the clearest anti-suicide evidence. In randomised trials it reduced suicides compared with placebo (OR 0.13, 95% CI 0.03–0.66) and deaths from any cause (OR 0.38, 0.15–0.95) [[Cipriani2013,Plans2019]].
- Help works: suicide risk in bipolar disorder can be reduced by treatment, which is why early recognition, ongoing risk assessment and access to care matter [[Cipriani2013,McIntyre2020Lancet]]. Anyone in crisis or thinking about suicide should contact local emergency services or a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).

### home · Hope and recovery (cited)
- With treatment, most people recover from an acute episode: 98% of people hospitalised for a first manic episode reached syndromal recovery within 2 years [[Tohen2003]].
- Many people with bipolar disorder work. Up to 60% of people with established illness are employed, and employment is higher earlier in the illness [[Marwaha2013]].
- Effective treatments exist for every phase. CANMAT/ISBD 2018 recommends lithium, quetiapine, divalproex and several other antipsychotics as first-line options for acute mania, and lists first-line options for bipolar depression and maintenance [[Yatham2018]].
- Lithium is described as the gold-standard mood stabiliser, with antimanic, antidepressant and anti-suicide effects [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].
- Treating medical and psychiatric comorbidity, adding psychosocial treatments and involving caregivers have been shown to improve outcomes [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].

### history · From antiquity to Kraepelin
- Aretaeus of Cappadocia, a Greek physician of the 1st century AD, was perhaps the first to describe mania and melancholia as two states of one disease [[Angst2001]].
- On 31 January 1854, Jules Baillarger lectured to the French Imperial Academy of Medicine on "la folie à double forme", in which manic and depressive episodes occur in the same patient [[Pichot2004]].
- Jean-Pierre Falret answered that he had already described the same illness as "folie circulaire", and lectured on it to the Academy on 14 February 1854 [[Pichot2004]].
- Baillarger accused Falret of plagiarism. A later study of the printed record supports Falret's priority and finds the plagiarism claim unfounded, and judges Falret's description the closer of the two to modern ideas [[Pichot2004]].
- Falret's 1854 paper in the Bulletin de l'Académie de Médecine described the "regular alternation of mania and melancholia". He also hinted that brief hypomanias at the end of depressions might be mild forms of circular insanity, an early idea of the soft bipolar spectrum [[Haustgen2006]].
- Magnan's "intermittent madness" (1893) was a forerunner of Kraepelin's manic-depressive psychosis [[Haustgen2006]].
- In 1899 Emil Kraepelin brought all types of affective disorder together as "manic-depressive insanity", and this unitary concept was adopted worldwide [[Angst2001]].
- Kraepelin divided most psychoses into two diseases, dementia praecox (later schizophrenia) and manic-depressive psychosis, which he separated mainly by the deterioration seen in the former. Falret's folie circulaire became one of manic-depressive psychosis's forms [[Pichot2004]].
- Kraepelin's descriptions of mood-disorder symptoms "have arguably never been surpassed" and remain a touchstone for modern classifications [[Mondimore2005]].
- Kraepelin's final views stressed mixed features and an emotional disposition seen between episodes and in unaffected relatives [[Kendler2021]].

### history · The bipolar–unipolar split (1957–1966)
- Karl Leonhard's 1957 classification of the endogenous psychoses distinguished bipolar from unipolar (monopolar) forms [[Haustgen2006,Perris1992]].
- In 1966 Jules Angst and Carlo Perris independently published family, clinical and course data separating bipolar from unipolar depressive illness [[Angst1966,Perris1966,Angst2001]].
- George Winokur's work in the 1960s independently supported the same unipolar–bipolar distinction [[Angst2001]].
- According to Pichot, Falret's illness "regained its autonomy only in 1966", when Angst and Perris showed its distinct heredity and named it bipolar disorder [[Pichot2004]].
- Later landmarks include the revival of Kraepelin's mixed states, of Kahlbaum's and Hecker's cyclothymia, and Akiskal's "soft bipolar spectrum" [[Angst2001]].

### history · Lithium and other medicines
- Lithium was used in psychiatry in the mid-19th century, but that early work was forgotten [[Shorter2009]].
- In 1949 John Cade published "Lithium salts in the treatment of psychotic excitement" in the Medical Journal of Australia, reintroducing lithium for mania [[Cade1949,Shorter2009]].
- In 1954 Mogens Schou and colleagues reported a randomly controlled trial of lithium salts in manic psychoses [[Schou1954,Shorter2009]].
- Baastrup and Schou reported lithium as a preventive treatment against recurrent depression and manic-depressive psychosis in 1967 [[Baastrup1967]].
- In 1970 a double-blind discontinuation trial in the Lancet tested preventive lithium in manic-depressive and recurrent depressive disorders [[Baastrup1970]].
- In 1970 the United States became the 50th country to allow lithium onto the market [[Shorter2009,Gajwani2006]].
- Chlorpromazine, a typical antipsychotic, was approved for bipolar disorder in 1973 [[Gajwani2006]].
- In a 1994 randomised trial in acute mania (179 inpatients), at least 50% improvement was seen in 48% on divalproex and 49% on lithium versus 25% on placebo [[Bowden1994]].
- Gajwani 2006 dates FDA approval of divalproex for acute mania to 1994 (often cited as 1995; see Open questions) [[Gajwani2006]].
- Olanzapine was approved for acute mania in 2000. Risperidone (2003), quetiapine (2004), ziprasidone (2004) and aripiprazole (2004) followed [[Gajwani2006]].
- In the pivotal 3-week olanzapine trial, 48.6% of patients on olanzapine responded versus 24.2% on placebo [[Tohen1999]].

### history · How the diagnosis was written down (DSM and ICD)
- DSM-I (1952) classified "manic depressive reaction" as a psychotic disorder [[Mason2016]].
- DSM-II (1968) renamed it "manic-depressive illness" and placed it under the affective disorders [[Mason2016]].
- DSM-III (1980) introduced specific diagnostic criteria and the term "bipolar disorder". A manic episode alone became enough for the diagnosis [[Mason2016]].
- DSM-III used the term "Bipolar II" only as an example within atypical bipolar disorder, not as a diagnosis of its own [[Mason2016]].
- DSM-III-R (1987) added a "hypomanic syndrome" for cases with the symptoms but without marked impairment [[Mason2016]].
- DSM-IV (1994) introduced a stand-alone hypomanic episode (at least 4 days, no marked impairment, no psychosis), the building block of bipolar II disorder. It also added a "mixed episode": full manic and major depressive criteria nearly every day for at least 1 week [[Mason2016]].
- DSM-5 (2013) made increased activity or energy a required core symptom of mania and hypomania, alongside the mood change [[Mason2016,MachadoVieira2017]].
- DSM-5 also allowed a manic episode that starts during antidepressant treatment, but persists at a full level well beyond the drug's effect, to count towards bipolar I [[Mason2016]].
- Applying the DSM-5 energy/activity criterion to 907 Bipolar Collaborative Network patients cut the number with a hypomanic or manic visit by 34% compared with DSM-IV [[Fredskild2019]].
- In STEP-BD (4,360 participants), adding the criterion lowered the prevalence of mania and hypomania but did not change longitudinal outcomes [[MachadoVieira2017]].
- A review concludes that the DSM-5 change cuts the prevalence of manic or hypomanic episodes by at least a third [[Grunze2021]].
- In the Bridge study, 810 of 3,618 patients with major depression (22.5%) who met DSM-IV bipolar criteria through a single mood criterion were reclassified as having major depressive disorder under DSM-5 [[Angst2020ICD11]].
- The APA published DSM-5-TR (text revision) in March 2022 [[APA2022]].
- The World Health Assembly approved ICD-11 in May 2019, and WHO member states began reporting health statistics with it on 1 January 2022 [[Reed2019]].
- ICD-11 keeps the bipolar type I and type II split based on mania versus hypomania, in line with DSM-5. Neither manual codes pure mania as a separate disorder [[Angst2020ICD11]].
- In the Zurich cohort, ICD-11 rules identified hypomania in 9.9% of participants (weighted), against 2.2% under DSM-5. Mania rates were similar (5.4% vs 4.7%) [[Angst2020ICD11]].
- A comparison of 103 diagnoses that appear in both systems found ICD-11 and DSM-5 "closer than at any time since the ICD-8 and DSM-II". Twenty diagnoses (19.4%) showed major differences and 31 (30.1%) were essentially identical [[First2021]].

### Exam hooks
- WMH lifetime prevalence: BP-I 0.6%, BP-II 0.4%, subthreshold 1.4%, total spectrum 2.4% [[Merikangas2011]].
- NCS-R (US) lifetime prevalence: BP-I 1.0%, BP-II 1.1%, subthreshold 2.4% [[Merikangas2007]].
- Sex ratio is about equal overall; BP-II, rapid cycling and mixed episodes are more common in women [[Diflorio2010]].
- Mean onset in the WMH surveys: 18.4 years (BP-I), 20.0 (BP-II), 21.9 (subthreshold) [[Merikangas2011]].
- Depression dominates the course: in BP-I, 31.9% of weeks depressive vs 8.9% manic/hypomanic; in BP-II, 50.3% vs 1.3% [[Judd2002,Judd2003]].
- The most common first presentation is a depressive episode, which is why bipolar disorder is often misdiagnosed as unipolar depression [[Grande2016,Nierenberg2023]].
- 69% of surveyed patients had been misdiagnosed, most often with unipolar depression [[Hirschfeld2003]].
- Median delay to diagnosis is 6.7 years [[Scott2022Delay]]; the pooled onset-to-management interval is 5.8 years [[Dagani2017]].
- Lifetime psychotic symptoms: about 63% in BP-I vs 22% in BP-II [[Aminoff2022]].
- All-cause SMR is about 2 (2.05) and the suicide SMR about 14 [[Hayes2015]].
- Years of potential life lost: about 13 (12.89) [[Chan2022]].
- Most lost life-years come from natural causes such as cardiovascular disease, not only from suicide [[Kessing2015,Biazus2023]].
- GBD attributes no YLLs to bipolar disorder, so its DALYs equal its YLDs [[Ferrari2016]].
- Falret's "folie circulaire" and Baillarger's "folie à double forme" both date to 1854 [[Pichot2004]].
- Kraepelin named "manic-depressive insanity" in 1899 [[Angst2001]].
- Angst and Perris separated bipolar from unipolar illness in 1966 [[Pichot2004]].
- Cade reported lithium for mania in 1949; lithium reached the US market in 1970 [[Cade1949,Shorter2009]].
- DSM-III (1980) introduced the term "bipolar disorder"; DSM-5 (2013) added increased energy/activity as a core criterion [[Mason2016]].
- DSM-5 replaced the "mixed episode" with a "with mixed features" specifier [[Mason2016]].

### In plain words
- Bipolar disorder is a long-term illness in which mood and energy swing between lows (depression) and highs (mania or hypomania) [[Grande2016]].
- About 1 to 2 people in every 100 have bipolar I or II at some point in life, and more have milder forms [[Merikangas2011,Merikangas2007]].
- It usually starts in the teens or twenties [[Merikangas2011,Nierenberg2023]].
- Men and women get bipolar disorder about equally often [[Diflorio2010]].
- The low periods usually last longer than the high ones. Many people spend more time feeling depressed than high [[Judd2002,Judd2003]].
- Mania is not always "happy". People can feel irritable, restless, sleepless and full of racing thoughts [[Cassidy1998]].
- Hypomania is a milder high. It can feel good and go unnoticed, which is one reason diagnosis often takes years [[Grande2016,Scott2022Delay]].
- Many people are first told they have ordinary depression [[Hirschfeld2003]].
- On average, people with bipolar disorder die younger, mostly from heart and other physical illnesses as well as suicide. Looking after physical health matters [[Kessing2015,Biazus2023]].
- Most people recover from an episode with treatment, and many people living with bipolar disorder work and live full lives [[Tohen2003,Marwaha2013]].
- Lithium, first used for mania in 1949, is still one of the best treatments and lowers the risk of suicide [[Shorter2009,Cipriani2013]].
- If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact local emergency services or a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).

## Open questions and controversies
- How many people die by suicide? Older and review figures put lifetime suicide at 15–20% of people with bipolar disorder [[Nierenberg2023]], or up to 20% in mostly untreated samples [[Dome2019]]. The ISBD Task Force pooled a lower rate of 164 per 100,000 person-years and says this is below earlier published estimates [[Schaffer2015Epi]]. Status: unresolved. The higher figures come mostly from older, more severe and less-treated hospital cohorts.
- Is suicide risk higher in BP-II than BP-I? Plans 2019 reports the highest risk of suicide death in BD-II [[Plans2019]]. Novick 2010 found no significant difference in attempt rates (32.4% vs 36.3%) [[Novick2010]]. Status: attempts look similar; deaths remain uncertain.
- When does bipolar disorder start? Community surveys and clinical sources put mean or peak onset at 15–25 years [[Merikangas2011,Scott2022Delay,Nierenberg2023]]. A large general-population meta-analysis puts the bipolar median onset at 30–35 years [[Solmi2022]]. Status: estimates differ by definition (first symptoms vs first full episode) and sample; both are cited, with the definition stated.
- How long is the diagnostic delay? Estimates range from 5.8 years [[Dagani2017]] and a median of 6.7 years [[Scott2022Delay]] to a mean of 9.10 years [[Keramatian2025]]. Status: depends on whether onset means first symptoms or first episode, and whether the endpoint is diagnosis or treatment.
- Life expectancy gap: the Lancet seminar says 10–20 years [[McIntyre2020Lancet]], JAMA 12–14 years [[Nierenberg2023]], the meta-analysis 12.89 YPLL [[Chan2022]], and Danish registers 8.9 (women) to 12.7 (men) years at age 15 [[Kessing2015]]. Status: figures agree in direction; the size varies by method, sex and region.
- Is the mortality gap closing? UK data show it widened from 2006 to 2014 [[Hayes2017]]. Status: no evidence of narrowing in the sources reviewed.
- Mislabelled GBD 2021 counts: Jiang 2025 calls 30.2 million (1990) and 53.9 million (2021) bipolar cases "incidence" [[Jiang2025GBD]]. GBD 2017 incidence was 4.53 million [[He2020GBD]] and GBD 2013 prevalence was 48.8 million [[Ferrari2016]], so the Jiang counts are probably prevalent cases. Status: builders should not show Jiang's counts as new cases.
- The DSM-5 energy/activity criterion: it cuts manic/hypomanic diagnoses by about a third [[Fredskild2019,Grunze2021]] without changing outcomes [[MachadoVieira2017]]. Critics argue it may under-diagnose hypomania, and ICD-11 rules find about 4 times more hypomania than DSM-5 in the Zurich cohort [[Angst2020ICD11]]. Status: debated.
- Underdiagnosis versus overdiagnosis: subthreshold bipolar disorder is common and underdetected [[Merikangas2007]] and misdiagnosis as depression is frequent [[Hirschfeld2003]]. On the other side, the DSM-5 criterion was partly meant to curb overdiagnosis [[Fredskild2019]]. Status: both problems coexist.
- Divalproex approval year: Gajwani 2006 gives 1994 [[Gajwani2006]]. Many sources give 1995, but that could not be checked against Drugs@FDA (endpoint unavailable). Status: unverified; the year is shown as given in the source.
- Pediatric bipolar disorder: youth spectrum prevalence is 3.9% and not higher in the US [[VanMeter2019]], but prevalence depends heavily on whether narrow or broad criteria are used. Status: definitions still differ.

## Provenance
- Validation: all 73 journal sources were checked in one batch against PubMed E-utilities esummary. The authors, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, PMID, PMCID and DOI in each line were generated from that record. APA2022 (DSM-5-TR) was checked against Crossref (DOI 10.1176/appi.books.9780890425787, issued 2022-03-18, American Psychiatric Association Publishing).
- Facts: every number was read from the PubMed abstract (efetch) of the cited paper. Some facts came from open PMC full text:
  - Merikangas2011 (PMC3486639): mean onset ages, country range, sex pattern, suicide-attempt fractions, treatment by income group
  - GBD2019Mental (PMC8776563): no marked sex difference for bipolar burden; less regional variation
  - Mason2016 (PMC5039514): DSM-I to DSM-5 history
  - Angst2020ICD11 (PMC6970089): ICD-11 vs DSM-5 criteria, Zurich and Bridge figures
- Abstract-only papers: Merikangas2007, Reed2019 and First2021 were read as abstracts only, because their full texts were not retrievable.
- Title-only sources: Cade1949, Schou1954, Baastrup1967, Baastrup1970, Angst1966 and Perris1966 have no PubMed abstract. They are cited only for the fact of publication (year, title, journal), with the substance taken from Shorter2009, Pichot2004 and Angst2001.
