# C · Mechanisms: circadian rhythm, brain networks and how lithium works

Pass date: 2026-11-03. Modules served: brain, lithium, home.

This pass covers what is known, and what is only proposed, about the biology of bipolar disorder: sleep and the body clock, social rhythms, light and season, brain structure and brain networks from the large ENIGMA imaging studies, neurochemistry (dopamine, glutamate, energy metabolism, inflammation, stress hormones, BDNF), the kindling and neuroprogression ideas, and the candidate mechanisms of lithium. Every number below was read from the abstract (or, where stated, the full text) of the cited paper. Doses and serum levels are quoted from the US Lithobid label as published, for teaching only, never as instructions.

Access notes:

- Evidence labels: (Established) = replicated in large studies or meta-analyses; (Early evidence) = consistent but small, cross-sectional, or animal/cell-based; (Hypothesis) = a proposed model not yet confirmed.

## Findings

### brain · Big picture: what causes bipolar disorder at the biological level
- The cause of bipolar disorder is not known. Processes implicated include disturbances in neuronal-glial plasticity, monoaminergic signalling, inflammatory homoeostasis, cellular metabolic pathways and mitochondrial function [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].
- Bipolar disorder has a heritability of approximately 70% [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].
- The largest genome-wide study (41,917 cases, 371,549 controls of European ancestry) found 64 risk loci; risk alleles were enriched in synaptic signalling genes and in genes expressed in neurons of the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus (Established) [[Mullins2021]].
- Risk-gene signal was enriched in genes that encode targets of antipsychotics, calcium channel blockers, antiepileptics and anaesthetics, pointing to drug targets already in use (Established) [[Mullins2021]].
- Voltage-gated calcium channels are a leading biological lead from genetics, both as part of the disease process and as a possible drug target (Early evidence) [[Harrison2018TINS]].
- Persistent mood instability between episodes is increasingly recognised as a feature of the illness in its own right, not just recurring manic and depressive episodes (Early evidence) [[Harrison2018TINS]].
- No single mechanism explains the illness; the models below are best seen as interacting layers (genes, clock, networks, chemistry, stress) rather than rival single causes (Hypothesis) [[McIntyre2020Lancet,Berk2011]].

### brain · Sleep and mania: the core link
- Decreased need for sleep is one of the DSM-5-TR criterion symptoms of a manic or hypomanic episode [[APA2022]].
- The US lithium label lists "reduced need for sleep" among the typical symptoms of mania, alongside pressure of speech, motor hyperactivity, flight of ideas and grandiosity [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Wehr, Sack and Rosenthal (1987) proposed that sleep reduction is a "final common pathway" to mania: many triggers (psychological, interpersonal, environmental, pharmacological) may act by causing sleep loss, and because mania itself causes insomnia, the process can become self-reinforcing (Hypothesis, with experimental support) [[Wehr1987]].
- In longitudinal monitoring of 15 rapid-cycling and 52 non-rapid-cycling patients, most experienced one or more 48-hour sleep-wake cycles (alternate nights without sleep) when they switched from depression into mania or hypomania [[Wehr1982]].
- When 9 rapid-cycling patients in a depressive phase stayed awake for 40 hours (one night of total sleep deprivation), 8 switched out of depression and 7 were rated manic or hypomanic [[Wehr1982]].
- In 206 people with bipolar depression treated with three cycles of therapeutic sleep deprivation, 4.85% switched into mania and 5.83% into hypomania, rates the authors judged comparable to antidepressant drugs [[Colombo1999]].
- In 3,140 people with bipolar disorder interviewed about triggers, sleep loss triggering high mood was associated with bipolar I subtype (OR 2.81, 95% CI 2.26–3.50) and female gender (OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.17–1.75); sleep loss triggering low mood was not significant after adjustment [[Lewis2017Sleep]].
- In daily self-ratings from 59 outpatients (mean 169 days each), changes in sleep plus bedrest were inversely correlated with the next mood change, most often with a one-day lag; 24 of 59 (41%) showed a significant correlation for the night before or the night of a mood change [[Bauer2006Sleep]].
- In the same study, the patients with a significant sleep-mood correlation reported about two-thirds of all large sleep changes of more than 3 hours and three-quarters of all large mood changes; sleep changes of more than 3 hours from a person's usual pattern may signal that a large mood change is imminent [[Bauer2006Sleep]].
- In 11 people with rapid-cycling bipolar disorder followed with daily logs for 18 months, decreased sleep duration was the best predictor of mania or hypomania the next day; 4 of the 11 showed no sleep-mood relationship at all [[Leibenluft1996]].
- A systematic review of 17 studies (1,191 people) found that at least 80% of people with a mood disorder can identify one or more early warning symptoms; sleep disturbance was the most robust early symptom of mania (median prevalence 77%) [[Jackson2003]].
- Manic prodromes lasted on average more than 20 days (range 1–120 days), while depressive prodromes were under 19 days on average but far more variable (2–365 days) [[Jackson2003]].
- Reviews conclude that impaired sleep can both induce and predict manic episodes, and that residual insomnia between episodes may be a vulnerability to relapse (Established for prediction; causal role Early evidence) [[Plante2008]].
- In a 10-year community cohort of 1,943 adolescents and young adults free of major mental disorder at baseline, poor sleep quality increased the risk of later bipolar disorder (OR 1.75); trouble falling asleep and early-morning waking were the predictive items (Early evidence) [[Ritter2015]].

### brain · Sleep and activity between episodes (actigraphy)
- 70% of euthymic people with bipolar disorder in one controlled study (n=20 per group) had a clinically significant sleep disturbance, with lower sleep efficiency, more anxiety about sleep and lower daytime activity than good sleepers [[Harvey2005Sleep]].
- A meta-analysis of 21 studies (531 people with interepisode bipolar disorder) found sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset and night-to-night variability were the most consistently impaired measures; on some measures people with bipolar disorder resembled people with primary insomnia (Established) [[Ng2015Sleep]].
- In the same meta-analysis, 157 people at high risk for bipolar disorder showed more variable sleep efficiency and lower relative amplitude of the rest-activity rhythm than controls (Early evidence) [[Ng2015Sleep]].
- A meta-analysis of 13 actigraphy studies (821 participants) found lower mean activity and an altered sleep pattern in people with bipolar disorder compared with controls, apparently across manic, depressed and euthymic phases [[DeCrescenzo2017]].
- A systematic review of 56 studies found the most robust activity finding was lower mean activity during euthymia and depression (11 studies); mania may be better described by changes in the variability, predictability or complexity of activity than by its mean level [[Scott2017Activation]].
- Factor-analytic studies suggest that mood and activation (energy and motor activity) are distinct dimensions of bipolar disorder, supporting activation as a core criterion A symptom in DSM-5 [[Scott2017Activation]].
- A systematic review of 42 studies (3,432 patients) found circadian rhythm disruption even in drug-naive patients and independent of mood state; melatonin and cortisol profiles pointed to a delayed phase, and evening chronotype was more common in adults with bipolar disorder (Established for eveningness; Early evidence for causation) [[Melo2017]].

### brain · Social rhythms and the social zeitgeber theory
- Ehlers, Frank and Kupfer (1988) proposed that "social zeitgebers" (people, social demands or tasks that set the biological clock) link life events to biology: losing them disrupts social rhythms, which may destabilise biological rhythms and trigger mood episodes in vulnerable people (Hypothesis) [[Ehlers1988]].
- In 39 people with bipolar disorder, life events that disrupted social routines (social rhythm disruption) were more common in the 8 weeks before a manic onset than in a control period, but not before depressive onsets (Early evidence) [[MalkoffSchwartz1998]].
- In 414 students followed for an average of 33 months, people with cyclothymia or bipolar II reported fewer regular daily activities than controls, and lower social rhythm regularity predicted a shorter time to the next depressive, hypomanic or manic episode (Early evidence) [[Shen2008]].
- In a randomised trial of 175 acutely ill adults with bipolar I disorder, those assigned to interpersonal and social rhythm therapy (IPSRT) in the acute phase survived longer without a new episode over 2 years of maintenance (P = .01), regardless of maintenance assignment [[Frank2005IPSRT]].
- In that trial, IPSRT increased social rhythm regularity by the end of acute treatment (P < .001), and people who increased regularity more were less likely to have a recurrence (P = .05), consistent with the social zeitgeber model [[Frank2005IPSRT]].
- A review of life stress in bipolar disorder notes that the circadian and social rhythm theory is one of two leading biopsychosocial models, alongside the behavioural approach system (reward sensitivity) model [[Bender2011]].

### brain · Clock genes and cellular clocks
- Mice carrying a mutation in the Clock gene show a mania-like profile: hyperactivity, less sleep, less depression-like and anxiety-like behaviour and increased reward value of cocaine, sucrose and brain stimulation (Early evidence; animal model) [[Roybal2007]].
- In these Clock mutant mice, chronic lithium returned many behaviours to wild-type levels, and restoring functional CLOCK protein only in the ventral tegmental area (a dopamine centre) rescued the behaviour, linking the clock to dopamine signalling (Early evidence) [[Roybal2007]].
- The idea that circadian clock dysfunction is causal in mood disorders has endured for decades but remains controversial and hard to test; clocks exist in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (the master pacemaker) and also in mood-related regions such as the lateral habenula, ventral tegmentum and hippocampus (Hypothesis) [[McCarthy2012]].
- In skin fibroblasts carrying a PER2 bioluminescent clock reporter, baseline circadian period was longer in cells from people with bipolar disorder (n=19) than from controls (n=19) (Early evidence) [[McCarthy2013]].
- The largest bipolar GWAS did not report core clock genes among its headline findings; its strongest enrichment was in synaptic signalling, so the genetic evidence for clock genes as risk genes remains limited (Early evidence) [[Mullins2021,McCarthy2012]].

### brain · Light and season
- A systematic review of 51 papers found seasonal peaks for mood episodes worldwide: mania peaks in spring/summer (and to a lesser extent autumn), depression in early winter (and to a lesser extent summer), and mixed episodes in early spring or mid/late summer (Established) [[Geoffroy2014]].
- In that review, a seasonal pattern was frequent for manic episodes (15%) and depressive episodes (25%); seasonal depressive patterns were linked to bipolar II, eating disorders, more relapses and rapid cycling [[Geoffroy2014]].
- In 5,536 people with bipolar I disorder from 50 sites in 32 countries, a larger maximum monthly increase in springtime solar insolation at the place of onset was associated with a younger age of onset, especially with a family history of mood disorders (Early evidence) [[Bauer2017Solar]].
- In a 6-week double-blind trial in bipolar depression (n=23 per arm, all on antimanic medication), 7,000-lux bright white light at midday gave a remission rate of 68.2% versus 22.2% with 50-lux dim red placebo light (adjusted OR 12.6), with no mood polarity switches [[Sit2018]].
- A 2020 meta-analysis of 6 RCTs found no significant overall difference between bright light and placebo for remission in bipolar depression (RR 1.81, 95% CI 0.43–7.64), but a sensitivity analysis of studies with low indirectness did (RR 3.09, 95% CI 1.62–5.90) [[Takeshima2020]].
- The 2025 ISBD Chronobiology Task Force concluded that adjunctive bright light is likely an efficacious acute treatment for bipolar depression; it recommends anti-manic prophylaxis (especially for bipolar I) and clinical monitoring, a target of 30 min/day, preferably in the morning or at midday, with improvement typically within 1–2 weeks [[Geoffroy2025]].
- In a randomised trial of 23 people hospitalised with mania, orange blue-blocking glasses worn from 6 p.m. to 8 a.m. for 7 days (added to usual treatment) reduced the Young Mania Rating Scale by 14.1 points (95% CI 9.7–18.5) versus 1.7 (95% CI −4.0 to 7.4) with clear glasses (Cohen's d 1.86) (Early evidence) [[Henriksen2016]].
- The rationale for blue-blocking glasses is that a blue-light-sensitive retinal photoreceptor signals daytime to the brain's clock, so blocking blue light in the evening creates a "virtual darkness" for the circadian system [[Henriksen2016]].

### brain · Brain structure: ENIGMA grey matter
- The ENIGMA Bipolar Disorder Working Group pools data from over 150 researchers in 20 countries and 55 institutions; its studies show widespread lower cortical thickness, lower subcortical volume and disrupted white matter integrity in bipolar disorder (Established) [[Ching2022]].
- In 1,837 adults with bipolar disorder versus 2,582 controls, cortical grey matter was thinner in frontal, temporal and parietal regions of both hemispheres (Established) [[Hibar2018]].
- The largest cortical thickness effects were in the left pars opercularis (Cohen's d = −0.293), left fusiform gyrus (d = −0.288) and left rostral middle frontal cortex (d = −0.276) [[Hibar2018]].
- Longer illness duration (after adjusting for age) was associated with thinner cortex in frontal, medial parietal and occipital regions; a history of psychosis was associated with smaller cortical surface area, but mood state at scanning was not [[Hibar2018]].
- People taking lithium (n=700) had thicker cortex than those not taking lithium (n=892), largest in the left paracentral gyrus (d = 0.211) and superior parietal gyri (d = 0.202 left, 0.188 right); antipsychotics showed a negative relationship (full text) [[Hibar2018]].
- In 1,710 people with bipolar disorder versus 2,594 controls, the hippocampus (d = −0.232) and thalamus (d = −0.148) were smaller and the lateral ventricles larger (reported d = −0.260) (Established) [[Hibar2016]].
- In that subcortical study, bipolar I but not bipolar II differed from controls, bipolar I and II did not differ directly, and people taking lithium had larger thalamic volumes than those not taking it (exploratory) [[Hibar2016]].
- An ENIGMA mega-analysis of hippocampal subfields (1,472 with bipolar disorder, 3,226 controls) found smaller whole hippocampus (d = −0.20) and smaller volumes in 9 of 12 subfields; lithium users did not differ from controls, while non-users did [[Haukvik2022]].
- An earlier meta-analysis of 98 structural imaging studies found lateral ventricle enlargement (effect size 0.39, 95% CI 0.24–0.55) and more deep white matter hyperintensities (OR 2.49, 95% CI 1.64–3.79); grey matter volume was larger in studies with more lithium-treated patients [[Kempton2008]].
- These brain differences are small on average (most Cohen's d between −0.1 and −0.3), overlap heavily with healthy people and cannot be used to diagnose an individual [[Hibar2018,Hibar2016,Kempton2008]].
- A 2026 ENIGMA mega-analysis (2,664 patients, 4,065 controls) found that medication-free patients (n=410) had only mild ventricular enlargement (d = 0.07), whereas antiepileptic and antipsychotic use was associated with smaller hippocampal and thalamic volumes (d = −0.07 to −0.14), showing how medication confounds imaging findings [[King2026ENIGMA]].
- In the same analysis, concurrent lithium weakened the negative association between antiepileptic use and hippocampal volume (β = 0.19, q = 0.038) [[King2026ENIGMA]].

### brain · Brain structure: white matter and change over time
- In the largest diffusion imaging dataset (1,482 with bipolar disorder, 1,551 controls from 26 cohorts), fractional anisotropy was lower in 29 of 43 regions, with the largest effects in the corpus callosum and cingulum (R² about 0.04) (Established) [[Favre2019]].
- Lithium use, later onset and shorter illness duration were associated with higher fractional anisotropy (more intact-looking white matter) in multiple regions [[Favre2019]].
- In a 6-year longitudinal MRI study of bipolar I disorder, people who had at least one manic episode (n=13) showed decreased dorsolateral prefrontal and inferior frontal cortical volume, while those with no mania (n=18) showed no change (Early evidence) [[Abe2015]].
- An international mega-analysis of 321 adults with bipolar I and 442 controls found that lithium users had larger hippocampal and amygdala volumes than non-users and controls, and that reduced cerebral volume was associated with illness duration [[Hallahan2011]].

### brain · Brain networks: emotion regulation and reward
- The leading circuit model describes parallel dysfunction in prefrontal (especially ventrolateral prefrontal)-hippocampal-amygdala emotion-processing and emotion-regulation circuits, together with an overactive left-sided ventral striatal-ventrolateral and orbitofrontal reward circuit (Established as a model) [[Phillips2014]].
- These circuit changes are proposed to explain three behavioural features: emotional lability, emotional dysregulation and heightened reward sensitivity [[Phillips2014]].
- A meta-analysis of 65 fMRI studies (1,040 with bipolar disorder, 1,074 controls) found under-activation of the inferior frontal gyrus/ventrolateral prefrontal cortex and over-activation of limbic areas including the amygdala, hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus (Established) [[Chen2011fMRI]].
- In that meta-analysis, the inferior frontal under-activation was seen during both emotional and cognitive tasks and was particularly linked to mania; increased limbic activation was mainly seen with emotional tasks and was not clearly tied to mood state [[Chen2011fMRI]].
- A consensus model from leading imaging groups proposes that disrupted early development of ventral prefrontal-limbic connections (white matter, prefrontal pruning) reduces top-down control of the amygdala, underlying the onset of mania, with progressive change over episodes (Hypothesis) [[Strakowski2012]].

### brain · Neurochemistry: dopamine and glutamate
- The dopamine hypothesis holds that too much dopamine signalling underlies mania and too little underlies depression; it has been a key theory for over four decades (Established as a model; mechanism Early evidence) [[Ashok2017]].
- Converging pharmacological and imaging evidence supports hyperdopaminergia in mania, specifically elevated striatal D2/3 receptor availability and a hyperactive reward-processing network [[Ashok2017]].
- In bipolar depression, imaging shows increased dopamine transporter (DAT) levels, which would lower dopamine signalling; other dopamine findings in depression are inconsistent [[Ashok2017]].
- Berk and colleagues proposed a cycle in which high dopamine transmission in mania causes a secondary down-regulation of dopamine receptor sensitivity, leading to a low-dopamine depressive phase (Hypothesis) [[Berk2007]].
- In Parkinson's disease, high-dose dopamine precursors can produce a mania-like picture that switches into a depression-like state on withdrawal, used as a model for this cycle [[Berk2007]].
- A meta-analysis of proton MRS studies found higher brain glutamate plus glutamine (Glx) in people with bipolar disorder than in controls, in medicated and unmedicated patients and in adult frontal regions (Early evidence) [[Gigante2012]].

### brain · Energy metabolism and oxidative stress
- The mitochondrial dysfunction hypothesis proposes that impaired mitochondrial energy production, possibly via deletions in mitochondrial DNA and disturbed calcium handling, contributes to bipolar disorder in at least some people (Hypothesis) [[Kato2017]].
- Mice that accumulate mitochondrial DNA deletions in the brain show recurrent spontaneous depression-like episodes that are worsened by lithium withdrawal (Early evidence; animal model) [[Kato2017]].
- Five of six MRS studies found raised brain lactate in bipolar disorder, especially in the cingulate cortex, and 4 of 13 found reduced frontal pH, consistent with a shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis (Early evidence) [[Dogan2018]].
- A meta-analysis of 27 studies (971 patients, 886 controls) found increased lipid peroxidation, DNA/RNA damage and nitric oxide in bipolar disorder; the lipid peroxidation effect size was very high (Early evidence; high heterogeneity) [[Brown2014Oxidative]].

### brain · Inflammation
- A meta-analysis of 30 studies (1,351 patients, 1,248 controls) found raised IL-4, IL-10, soluble IL-2 receptor, soluble IL-6 receptor, TNF-α, soluble TNF receptor-1 and IL-1 receptor antagonist in bipolar disorder (Established for association) [[Modabbernia2013]].
- An 18-study meta-analysis (761 patients, 919 controls) found raised sIL-2R, TNF-α and sTNFR1 (p < 0.001 each), sIL-6R and IL-4, with no difference for IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10 or interferon-γ [[Munkholm2013JPR]].
- Analysed by mood state (13 studies), TNF-α, sTNFR1 and sIL-2R were raised in mania compared with controls, and sTNFR1 was also raised in euthymia [[Munkholm2013JAD]].
- A cross-disorder meta-analysis found IL-6, TNF-α, sIL-2R and IL-1RA raised in acute mania as in acute schizophrenia and depression, suggesting shared, non-specific immune pathways; IL-1RA fell after treatment of mania [[Goldsmith2016]].
- C-reactive protein (27 studies, 2,161 patients) was moderately raised in bipolar depression (g 0.67) and euthymia (g 0.65) and more raised in mania (g 0.87); it fell after a manic episode resolved (g −0.36) [[Fernandes2016]].
- A 2021 meta-analysis (49 studies) found CRP (g 0.70), IL-6 (g 0.81) and TNF-α (g 0.49) raised in bipolar disorder overall; CRP and TNF-α behaved like state markers (raised only during episodes), while IL-6 stayed raised regardless of mood state (trait-like) [[Solmi2021Inflammation]].
- Adjunctive anti-inflammatory agents (NSAIDs, omega-3, N-acetylcysteine, pioglitazone; 8 RCTs, n=312) showed a moderate antidepressant effect in bipolar depression (SMD −0.40, 95% CI −0.14 to −0.65) with no manic switches reported, but studies were few and small (Early evidence) [[Rosenblat2016]].
- Whether inflammation causes episodes or follows from them, from medication, weight or lifestyle, is not settled; current evidence is associational (Hypothesis for causation) [[Munkholm2013JPR,Solmi2021Inflammation]].

### brain · Stress hormones (HPA axis)
- A meta-analysis of 41 studies found raised cortisol (basal and after dexamethasone) and ACTH in bipolar disorder, but not CRH (Established for association) [[BelvederiMurri2016]].
- Cortisol differences were larger in the manic phase (p = 0.005) and smaller with antipsychotic use (p = 0.001) [[BelvederiMurri2016]].
- Studies of unaffected relatives suggest HPA axis dysregulation is not an inherited marker of bipolar disorder but relates to environmental risk such as childhood trauma; progressive HPA dysfunction may contribute to clinical and cognitive decline (Hypothesis) [[BelvederiMurri2016]].

### brain · Neurotrophins (BDNF)
- A meta-analysis of 52 studies (6,481 participants) found peripheral BDNF reduced to a similar extent in mania (Hedges' g −0.57) and depression (g −0.93) but not in euthymia, making BDNF a possible marker of illness activity rather than of illness stage (Early evidence) [[Fernandes2015]].
- BDNF levels correlated negatively with the severity of both manic and depressive symptoms, and plasma (not serum) BDNF rose after successful treatment of mania [[Fernandes2015]].
- An earlier meta-analysis (13 studies, 1,113 people) reported a similar pattern (mania ES −0.81; depression ES −0.97; euthymia not different, ES −0.20), with BDNF in euthymia falling with age and illness length [[Fernandes2011]].

### brain · Kindling, neuroprogression and allostatic load
- Post (1992) proposed that both stressors and episodes leave lasting traces in gene expression (sensitisation), so later episodes need less stress to start and may eventually occur spontaneously, with possible progression to rapid cycling and treatment resistance (Hypothesis) [[Post1992]].
- Post's model emphasised early intervention to prevent this progression [[Post1992]].
- In a Danish register of 20,350 first-admission patients (1971–1993), the rate of recurrence increased with the number of previous episodes in both unipolar and bipolar disorder (Established for association) [[Kessing1998Recurrence]].
- A later Danish register study (1994–1999; 1,106 with mania or bipolar disorder) again found relapse leading to hospitalisation rose with each previous episode, and relapse rates had not declined despite newer treatments [[Kessing2004Course]].
- A 2017 systematic review concluded that more episodes are associated with higher recurrence risk, longer and more severe episodes, a lower threshold for new episodes and a higher risk of dementia, while noting the course is heterogeneous (Early evidence for causal progression) [[Kessing2017Progression]].
- A review of life stress in bipolar disorder found that the methodologically strongest studies are inconsistent with the kindling hypothesis as applied to bipolar disorder (Hypothesis remains contested) [[Bender2011]].
- The allostatic load model proposes that repeated episodes and stress cause cumulative "wear and tear" on brain and body, which could link episodes to cognitive impairment, physical illness and mortality; mood stabilisers may oppose these effects (Hypothesis) [[Kapczinski2008]].
- Berk and colleagues propose that inflammation, oxidative stress, neurotrophin loss, mitochondrial dysfunction and corticosteroids together drive neuroprogression, explaining shorter intervals between episodes and lower treatment response later in illness (Hypothesis) [[Berk2011]].
- Association between more episodes and worse course does not prove that episodes cause progression; people with a more severe illness from the start would also have more episodes (open question) [[Kessing2017Progression,Bender2011]].

### lithium · What the label says about mechanism
- The US Lithobid label states that lithium alters sodium transport in nerve and muscle cells and shifts catecholamine metabolism inside neurons, "but the specific biochemical mechanism of lithium action in mania is unknown" [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- The label states that lithium given during a manic episode "may produce a normalization of symptomatology within 1 to 3 weeks" [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- As published in the label, desirable serum lithium concentrations for long-term control are 0.6 to 1.2 mEq/L, and doses for acute mania normally produce 1.0 to 1.5 mEq/L (quoted for context, not as instructions; lithium is monovalent, so 1 mEq/L equals 1 mmol/L) [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- The boxed warning states that lithium toxicity is closely related to serum levels and can occur at doses close to therapeutic levels; suspected toxicity is an emergency [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- No single mechanism has been conclusively supported or rejected; the common theme is that lithium acts on many steps in cell signalling, usually enhancing basal and dampening stimulated activity [[Alda2015]].
- Key nodes in lithium's signalling networks include GSK3, CREB and the Na+/K+ ATPase [[Alda2015]].

### lithium · Inositol depletion hypothesis
- Lithium inhibits inositol monophosphatase (myo-inositol-1-phosphatase), the enzyme that recycles inositol; in bovine brain enzyme, lithium chloride inhibited it by 50% at 0.8 mM (Established biochemistry) [[Hallcher1980]].
- Lithium is an uncompetitive inhibitor with respect to the inositol phosphate substrate, and in rat brain lithium raises inositol 1-phosphate and lowers myo-inositol [[Hallcher1980]].
- Berridge, Downes and Hanley (1989) unified these findings into the inositol depletion hypothesis: by limiting inositol recycling, lithium dampens phosphoinositide signalling, with the greatest effect in the most active cells (Hypothesis) [[Berridge1989,Harwood2005]].
- Over 35 years, cell experiments have shown lithium lowers myo-inositol and attenuates signalling, but the hypothesis "still remains to be either proven or refuted" clinically [[Harwood2005]].
- Lithium, carbamazepine and valproate all prevent sensory neuron growth cone collapse and increase growth cone area; inositol reverses these effects, while GSK3 or HDAC inhibition does not explain them, supporting inositol depletion as a shared mood-stabiliser mechanism (Early evidence; cell model) [[Williams2002Nature]].
- In 12 depressed patients studied with proton MRS, lithium lowered right frontal myo-inositol by about 30% within 5–7 days, and the fall persisted at 3–4 weeks [[Moore1999Inositol]].
- That early inositol fall happened while patients' clinical state was still unchanged, so short-term inositol reduction alone does not explain response; the authors propose it starts slower downstream signalling and gene-expression changes (Hypothesis) [[Moore1999Inositol]].

### lithium · GSK-3 inhibition
- Klein and Melton (1996) showed that lithium directly inhibits glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) with a Ki of about 2 mM, without being a general kinase inhibitor, and that complete IMPase inhibition did not reproduce lithium's effects on frog embryo development (Established biochemistry) [[Klein1996]].
- Stambolic and colleagues independently showed that lithium at physiological doses inhibits human GSK-3β, reversibly and specifically, reduces GSK-3-dependent tau phosphorylation and causes β-catenin to accumulate, mimicking Wnt (Wingless) signalling [[Stambolic1996]].
- Lithium inhibits GSK-3 by competing with magnesium, a similarly sized cation [[Gould2005]].
- Lithium also blocks GSK-3 indirectly: in mouse striatum, high dopamine (from amphetamine or loss of the dopamine transporter) inactivates Akt and activates GSK-3; lithium, D2 blockade or dopamine synthesis inhibition reverse this, and GSK-3 inhibition reduces dopamine-driven hyperactivity (Early evidence; animal model) [[Beaulieu2004]].
- In human blood cells, inhibitory Ser9-phosphorylated GSK-3β was eightfold higher in 9 lithium-treated people with bipolar disorder than in 23 healthy controls, showing that therapeutic lithium engages this target in people (Early evidence) [[Li2007GSK3]].
- Structurally different GSK-3 inhibitors and removing one copy of the Gsk3b gene reproduce several behavioural effects of chronic lithium in mice, supporting GSK-3 as a central target; but lithium also inhibits IMPase, related phosphomonoesterases, phosphoglucomutase and β-arrestin-2 scaffolding (Early evidence) [[OBrien2009]].
- Final proof that GSK-3 is the therapeutic target would need clinical trials of selective GSK-3 inhibitors (Hypothesis) [[Gould2005]].

### lithium · Neuroprotection, BDNF and brain structure
- Lithium raises protective proteins such as BDNF and Bcl-2, reduces apoptosis via GSK-3 inhibition, may reduce oxidative stress, and at the neurotransmitter level reduces excitatory (dopamine, glutamate) and increases inhibitory (GABA) transmission (Early evidence) [[Malhi2013CNSDrugs]].
- Lithium appears to preserve or increase the volume of emotion-regulation structures such as the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus and amygdala [[Malhi2013CNSDrugs,Hallahan2011]].
- In 10 people with bipolar I mania given lithium alone, plasma BDNF rose from 406.3 ± 69.5 pg/mL to 510.9 ± 127.1 pg/mL after 28 days (p = 0.03); 87% of responders showed a rise, though BDNF change did not correlate significantly with symptom improvement (Early evidence; very small study) [[deSousa2011]].
- Grey matter volume increased after 4 weeks of lithium in people with bipolar disorder, interpreted as a neurotrophic effect (Early evidence) [[Moore2000Lancet]].
- People with at least 2 years of lithium had hippocampal volumes like healthy controls, while people with similar illness burden but little lithium exposure had smaller hippocampi (Early evidence) [[Hajek2012]].
- This lithium-hippocampus association held even for people who still had episodes while on lithium, suggesting a direct effect rather than simply fewer episodes (Early evidence; n = 37 lithium, 19 non-lithium, 50 controls) [[Hajek2014]].
- Across ENIGMA, lithium is associated with thicker cortex, larger thalamus, preserved hippocampal subfields and higher white matter integrity, but these are cross-sectional associations and could partly reflect who is prescribed lithium (Early evidence) [[Hibar2018,Hibar2016,Haukvik2022,Favre2019]].

### lithium · Lithium and the body clock
- A systematic review of 95 studies found lithium acts directly on molecular clocks, delays the phase of sleep-wake and body-temperature rhythms, lengthens free-running rhythms and stabilises day-to-day activity rhythms, with effects that appear dose-related (Established in animals; Early evidence in people) [[Moreira2016]].
- Lithium response has been associated with variants in circadian genes including NR1D1, GSK3β, CRY1, ARNTL, TIM and PER2, and lithium affects the retina-hypothalamus-pineal pathway, light sensitivity and melatonin secretion (Early evidence) [[Moreira2016]].
- In fibroblasts, lithium 1 mM increased clock rhythm amplitude by 36% in cells from controls but failed to do so in cells from people with bipolar disorder; lithium 10 mM lengthened period in both [[McCarthy2013]].
- Cells from people with a past suicide attempt were more likely to show period lengthening with lithium 1 mM, and lithium helped weakened rhythms resynchronise (Early evidence) [[McCarthy2013]].
- Among 193 patients from a prospective lithium monotherapy trial, lithium responders were more morning-type than non-responders, and evening chronotype was associated with more depression, mania and insomnia at baseline (Early evidence) [[McCarthy2019]].
- Neurons grown from stem cells of lithium non-responders had low-amplitude, profoundly weakened rhythms; lithium lengthened period in responder and control neurons but not in non-responder neurons (Early evidence; preliminary) [[Mishra2021]].
- In immortalised blood cells, lithium 1 mM changed expression of core clock genes differently in excellent responders (n=16) and non-responders (n=20), for example NR1D1 at day 2 and CRY1 at day 4 (Early evidence) [[Geoffroy2018]].

### lithium · Who responds: lithium response genetics
- Lithium non-response is estimated at up to 30% [[Amare2018]]; a stem-cell study states that 30–40% of patients fail to respond adequately [[Mishra2021]].
- Response to lithium runs in families: 67% of bipolar relatives of lithium responders responded unequivocally, versus 35% of a clinic comparison group (p = .014) [[Grof2002]].
- Lithium response in research is rated with the Alda scale ("Retrospective Criteria of Long-Term Treatment Response"); mixture modelling of 1,308 patients suggested three groups: full, partial and non-responders [[Manchia2013]].
- The ConLiGen genome-wide study of 2,563 patients found one genome-wide significant locus on chromosome 21 (four linked SNPs, e.g. rs74795342, p = 3.31 × 10⁻⁹), containing two long non-coding RNA genes [[Hou2016]].
- In an independent prospective sample of 73 people on lithium monotherapy for up to 2 years, carriers of the response-associated alleles had a significantly lower relapse rate than carriers of the alternate alleles (p = 0.033; hazard ratio 3.8, 95% CI 1.1–13.0) [[Hou2016]].
- In 2,586 ConLiGen patients, a low polygenic score for schizophrenia predicted better lithium response: OR 3.46 (95% CI 1.42–8.41) for the lowest decile versus the highest; cross-trait loci pointed to the HLA complex and inflammatory cytokines [[Amare2018]].
- Full lithium responders may be a distinct clinical group, so studies of responders may reveal lithium's specific effects (Hypothesis) [[Alda2015]].
- No genetic test for lithium response is ready for clinical use; findings need replication and evidence of clinical utility (Early evidence) [[Hou2016,Amare2018]].

### Exam hooks
- Reduced need for sleep is a core manic symptom, and sleep disturbance is the most common early warning sign of mania (median 77% of prodromes) [[APA2022,Jackson2003]].
- Wehr 1987: sleep reduction is a "final common pathway" to mania, and mania worsens insomnia, a self-reinforcing loop [[Wehr1987]].
- One night of total sleep deprivation switched 7 of 9 rapid-cycling depressed patients into mania or hypomania [[Wehr1982]].
- Social zeitgeber theory (Ehlers, Frank, Kupfer 1988) underlies interpersonal and social rhythm therapy (IPSRT) [[Ehlers1988,Frank2005IPSRT]].
- Bipolar disorder shows evening chronotype and delayed melatonin/cortisol phase more often than controls [[Melo2017]].
- Seasonal peaks: mania in spring/summer, depression in early winter [[Geoffroy2014]].
- ENIGMA cortex: thinner frontal, temporal and parietal cortex; largest effect in left pars opercularis (d = −0.29) [[Hibar2018]].
- ENIGMA subcortical: smaller hippocampus and thalamus, larger lateral ventricles [[Hibar2016]].
- ENIGMA white matter: lower fractional anisotropy, most in the corpus callosum and cingulum [[Favre2019]].
- fMRI pattern: under-active ventrolateral prefrontal/inferior frontal cortex plus over-active amygdala and limbic areas [[Chen2011fMRI,Phillips2014]].
- Dopamine model: raised striatal D2/3 receptor availability in mania; raised dopamine transporter in bipolar depression [[Ashok2017]].
- BDNF falls during mania and depression and normalises in euthymia, a state marker [[Fernandes2015]].
- Lithium inhibits inositol monophosphatase (50% at 0.8 mM) and GSK-3β (Ki about 2 mM) [[Hallcher1980,Klein1996]].
- Lithium lowers frontal myo-inositol by about 30% within a week, before clinical improvement, which argues against a simple inositol-depletion explanation [[Moore1999Inositol]].
- The ConLiGen GWAS found a chromosome 21 locus (lncRNA genes) for lithium response [[Hou2016]].
- High polygenic risk for schizophrenia predicts poorer lithium response [[Amare2018]].
- Lithium use is associated with thicker cortex, larger hippocampus and higher white matter integrity in imaging studies [[Hibar2018,Hallahan2011,Favre2019]].
- Kindling (Post 1992): episodes and stressors sensitise the brain so later episodes need less trigger; register data show recurrence risk rises with episode number, but the strongest life-stress studies do not support kindling in bipolar disorder [[Post1992,Kessing1998Recurrence,Bender2011]].

### In plain words
- Scientists do not yet know exactly what causes bipolar disorder. Genes, the body clock, brain networks and stress all seem to play a part [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].
- Needing much less sleep without feeling tired is one of the clearest signs that mania may be starting [[APA2022,Jackson2003]].
- Losing sleep can push some people with bipolar disorder toward a high mood, and a high mood then makes sleep even harder, so the two can feed each other [[Wehr1987]].
- A big change in sleep, more than about 3 hours from your usual pattern, can come a day before a big mood change, so it is worth noticing and telling your care team [[Bauer2006Sleep]].
- Regular daily routines (waking, eating, working and sleeping at similar times) help keep the body clock steady, and a therapy built on this idea helped people stay well longer in a trial [[Frank2005IPSRT]].
- Many people with bipolar disorder notice their mood follows the seasons, with highs more common in spring and summer and lows in winter [[Geoffroy2014]].
- Brain scans of large groups show small average differences in some brain areas, but a scan cannot diagnose bipolar disorder in one person [[Hibar2018,Ching2022]].
- People taking lithium tend to have brain measures closer to people without bipolar disorder, which may mean lithium protects brain cells, but this is not yet proven [[Hibar2018,Hajek2014]].
- Lithium works on several switches inside brain cells at once, including enzymes called IMPase and GSK-3 and the cell's own clock [[Alda2015,Moreira2016]].
- Lithium helps many people a lot, some a little and some not at all, and this partly runs in families [[Grof2002,Manchia2013]].
- The amount of lithium in the blood matters a great deal. Your care team checks it with blood tests. Signs of too much lithium are an emergency [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Signs of inflammation and stress hormones are often a little higher in bipolar disorder, especially during episodes. Scientists are still working out whether this is a cause or a result [[Solmi2021Inflammation,BelvederiMurri2016]].
- With treatment, many people with bipolar disorder stay well for long periods; spotting early warning signs like sleep changes helps catch episodes early [[Jackson2003,Frank2005IPSRT]].
- If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact local emergency services or a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).

## Open questions and controversies
- Is lithium's key target IMPase or GSK-3? GSK-3 inhibition explains many effects and is engaged in human blood cells [[Klein1996,Li2007GSK3,OBrien2009]]; inositol depletion explains a shared action of three mood stabilisers in neurons [[Williams2002Nature]]; but GSK-3's Ki (about 2 mM) is above the label's therapeutic serum range [[Klein1996,FDA-Lithobid]], and brain inositol falls before clinical response [[Moore1999Inositol]]. Status: unresolved; both may contribute, along with other targets [[Alda2015,Harwood2005]].
- Are circadian changes a cause or a consequence of bipolar disorder? Clock-mutant mice show mania-like behaviour [[Roybal2007]] and rhythm disruption is seen even in drug-naive and euthymic patients [[Melo2017]]; but causal evidence in people is limited and controversial [[McCarthy2012]]. Status: Early evidence.
- Does bipolar disorder progress with each episode (kindling/neuroprogression)? Register data show recurrence risk rising with episode count [[Kessing1998Recurrence,Kessing2017Progression]] and mania is linked to frontal volume loss over 6 years [[Abe2015]]; but life-stress studies with the best methods do not support kindling [[Bender2011]], and more severe illness from the start could explain the association. Status: contested.
- Are imaging differences caused by the illness or by medication? Lithium associates with larger or thicker structures, and antipsychotics and antiepileptics with smaller ones [[Hibar2018,King2026ENIGMA]]; medication-free patients show only small differences [[King2026ENIGMA]]. Status: confounding remains a major limit of cross-sectional data.
- Is lithium neuroprotective in people? Supported by cross-sectional imaging and small longitudinal studies [[Moore2000Lancet,Hajek2014,Hallahan2011]], but no randomised imaging trial establishes it. Status: Early evidence.
- Is inflammation a cause, a state marker or a by-product? CRP and TNF-α look like state markers and IL-6 like a trait marker [[Solmi2021Inflammation]]; cytokine changes are shared with schizophrenia and depression [[Goldsmith2016]]; anti-inflammatory add-ons show a moderate signal in small trials [[Rosenblat2016]]. Status: Early evidence.
- Does bright light help bipolar depression? One strong RCT says yes [[Sit2018]], a meta-analysis is inconclusive overall [[Takeshima2020]], and the ISBD task force judges it likely efficacious with anti-manic cover [[Geoffroy2025]]. Status: Early evidence, moving toward guideline use.
- Can genetics predict lithium response? A chromosome 21 locus and schizophrenia polygenic score are associated [[Hou2016,Amare2018]], but effect sizes are modest and replication and clinical utility are not established. Status: Early evidence.

## Provenance
- 90 journal records were validated against PubMed E-utilities: citation fields (authors, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, PMID, PMCID, DOI) were generated directly from esummary, and every abstract was read with efetch before numbers were taken from it.
- Hibar2018: the lithium effect on cortical thickness (direction, n and d values) was read from the open full text via the Europe PMC REST API (PMC5668195), since the abstract only says the association was significant.
- Berridge1989 has no abstract in PubMed; it is cited only as the origin of the inositol-depletion hypothesis, with the mechanism detail sourced from Hallcher1980, Harwood2005, Williams2002Nature and Moore1999Inositol.
- FDA-Lithobid: read from the openFDA drug label endpoint (Lithobid, ANI Pharmaceuticals, effective 2026-08-24). The label has no separate mechanism section; mechanism wording comes from its "Actions" paragraph.
- APA2022: validated as a record through Crossref (DOI 10.1176/appi.books.9780890425787, APA Publishing, 2022); the manual text itself was not read, so only the inclusion of decreased need for sleep as a manic-episode criterion is cited to it.
- Moore2000Lancet abstract gives no percentage for the grey matter increase, so none is quoted. Kessing1998Recurrence and Kessing2004Course abstracts give no recurrence percentages, so only directions are quoted.
- Considered but not used: Hallcher-era rat inositol data beyond the abstract, CLOCK polymorphism association studies, and mitochondrial DNA meta-analyses (not needed beyond Kato2017 and Dogan2018). No source is marked unverified.
- PubMed rate limiting (shared IP) caused some failed fetches; all were re-run and confirmed before use.
