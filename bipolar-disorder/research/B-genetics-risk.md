# B · Causes: genetics, family risk and environment

Pass date: 2026-11-03. Modules served: genes, frontier.

This pass covers why bipolar disorder runs in families and what else shapes risk: twin, family and adoption studies; recurrence risks for relatives and for children of parents with bipolar disorder (including high-risk offspring cohorts); genetic overlap with schizophrenia and depression; the genome-wide association studies (GWAS) from 2008 to 2025; SNP-heritability, polygenic risk scores (PRS) and their predictive limits; bipolar I versus bipolar II genetics; rare copy-number and coding variants; and environmental and developmental factors (childhood adversity, cannabis, life events, sleep loss, the postpartum period, antidepressant-associated switch, season and light, paternal age, preterm birth, prenatal exposures, infection and head injury). It ends with the "risk, not destiny" framing for patients and families.

## Findings

### genes · How heritable is bipolar disorder? (twin and adoption studies)
- In a UK twin series (67 pairs whose proband had bipolar disorder: 30 monozygotic (MZ) and 37 dizygotic (DZ); DSM-IV), heritability was 85% (95% CI 73–93%) with narrow concordance and 89% (95% CI 61–100%) with broad concordance. No shared-environment effect was detected [[McGuffin2003]].
- The same study found a genetic correlation of 0.65 (95% CI 0.58–0.75) between mania and depression. About 71% of the genetic variance for mania was not shared with depression, so most of the genetic liability to mania is specific to the manic syndrome [[McGuffin2003]].
- In a nationwide Finnish twin sample (19,124 same-sex twins born 1940–1957; 38 index pairs), bipolar I disorder had a probandwise concordance of 0.43 (95% CI 0.10–0.82) in MZ twins and 0.06 (95% CI 0.00–0.27) in DZ twins. Heritability was 0.93 (95% CI 0.69–1.00) [[Kieseppa2004]].
- In the population-based Danish twin study of manic-depressive disorders (11,288 same-sex pairs born 1870–1920), strict probandwise concordance was 0.67 in MZ twins and 0.20 in DZ twins. Pairwise concordance was 0.58 and 0.17. This older study grouped unipolar and bipolar forms together [[Bertelsen1977]].
- In a Norwegian twin study (N = 303, interviewed with DSM-III-R criteria), heritability was 0.73 for bipolar I, 0.77 for bipolar I + II, and 0.71 for bipolar I + II + cyclothymia. No shared family-environment effect was found, which suggests the bipolar spectrum shares one underlying genetic liability [[Edvardsen2008]].
- A Swedish twin-register study (804 twins with bipolar disorder, 91,604 unaffected) that corrected for censoring and truncation estimated heritability at 60.4% (95% CI 50.3–70.5%). It found no sex difference in heritability and no sex-specific genetic effects [[Johansson2019]].
- Two large Swedish studies built on whole-population family registers estimate heritability at about 58–59%, lower than the clinical twin series (85–93%): 59% in nine million people [[Lichtenstein2009]] and 58% in 8.1 million offspring [[Song2015Family]].
- In Swedish twins, the twin heritability of "psychosis" (schizophrenia and bipolar disorder combined) was 0.73 (95% CI 0.30–1.00) in a clinical sample and 0.69 (95% CI 0.43–0.85) across the full Swedish Twin Registry [[Song2024Twins]].
- In that study, a 1-SD higher psychosis polygenic score went with a higher chance of an affected co-twin: OR 2.12 (95% CI 1.23–3.87) in MZ twins and 2.74 (1.56–5.30) in DZ twins [[Song2024Twins]].
- A 1977 adoption study published in Nature supported genetic transmission of manic-depressive illness [[Mendlewicz1977]].
- Reviews agree that family studies show excess risk in first-degree relatives, that twin and adoption studies attribute most of this to genes, and that bipolar disorder is genetically and phenotypically complex (Established) [[Smoller2003,Craddock2013]].
- The large GWAS papers summarise twin heritability as 60–85% [[Mullins2021]] or 60–80% [[OConnell2025]].

### genes · Family recurrence risk (relatives of a person with bipolar disorder)
- **Population baseline.** In the WHO World Mental Health surveys (61,392 adults, 11 countries), lifetime prevalence was 0.6% for bipolar I, 0.4% for bipolar II, 1.4% for subthreshold bipolar disorder and 2.4% for the whole bipolar spectrum [[Merikangas2011]].
- **Danish registers.** People with a first-degree relative with bipolar disorder had a 13.63-fold higher risk (95% CI 11.81–15.71) in a nationwide cohort of 2.1 million (2,299 incident cases) [[Mortensen2003]].
- **Swedish family register (54,723 people with bipolar disorder among 8.1 million offspring).** Relative risk for first-degree relatives was 5.8–7.9, and risk fell with genetic distance [[Song2015Family]].
- **Swedish multi-generation register (40,487 probands).**
  - Full siblings: relative risk 7.9 (95% CI 7.1–8.8).
  - Maternal half-siblings: 4.5 (2.7–7.4).
  - Paternal half-siblings: 2.4 (1.4–4.1).
  - Shared environment explained only 3.4% (2.3–6.2%) of liability [[Lichtenstein2009]].
- Relatives of people with bipolar disorder also have more schizophrenia, including adopted-away children of biological parents with bipolar disorder [[Lichtenstein2009]].
- People with bipolar disorder had 9.7–22.9 times the risk of other psychiatric disorders, and their full siblings had 1.7–2.8 times the risk [[Song2015Family]].
- The Danish study found that early loss of a parent, especially the mother, was linked to bipolar disorder: maternal loss before age 5 gave a relative risk of 4.05 (95% CI 1.68–9.77). Urban birth, season of birth, birth order and influenza epidemics during pregnancy showed no consistent association [[Mortensen2003]].

### genes · Children of parents with bipolar disorder (registry studies)
- **Danish register, 2.7 million people, cumulative incidence of bipolar disorder by age 52:**
  - neither parent admitted with bipolar disorder: 0.48%
  - one parent: 4.4% (23,152 offspring)
  - both parents: 24.9% (146 offspring of 83 couples), rising to 36.0% if unipolar depression is included [[Gottesman2010]].
- In the same cohort, when one parent had schizophrenia and the other bipolar disorder, offspring risk was 11.7% for bipolar disorder and 15.6% for schizophrenia. When both parents had bipolar disorder, the risk of any psychiatric disorder in the offspring was 44.2% [[Gottesman2010]].
- **Swedish register, 289,637 offspring born 1970–1990, hazard ratios for offspring bipolar I disorder by parental pairing:**
  - unaffected × major depression: 1.70
  - unaffected × bipolar disorder: 5.59
  - major depression × bipolar disorder: 6.26
  - bipolar disorder × bipolar disorder: 13.61 [[Kendler2026Mating]].
- In that study, parental bipolar disorder (but not parental depression) raised the rate at which offspring depression later converted to bipolar disorder. Offspring bipolar disorder was predicted equally by mothers' and fathers' illness [[Kendler2026Mating]].
- A meta-analysis of 211 family high-risk and registry studies (3.17 million offspring of affected parents) reported for offspring of parents with bipolar disorder:
  - bipolar disorder itself: relative risk 5.1, lifetime risk 5%
  - any mental disorder: relative risk 2.1, lifetime risk 55%
  - 96% of the included studies were at high risk of bias [[Uher2023]].
- The same meta-analysis's Kaplan-Meier curves show that risk of bipolar disorder in these offspring begins at about age 5. Cumulative risk is reported as 19% by age 18 and 36% by age 28 [[Uher2023]].
- An earlier meta-analysis (33 family high-risk studies; 3,863 offspring of parents with severe mental illness) found:
  - 32% (95% CI 24–42%) of offspring developed a severe mental illness by adulthood (age over 20), with RR 2.52 against control offspring.
  - Risk of the parent's own disorder: RR 3.59.
  - Schizophrenia in offspring of parents with bipolar disorder: RR 6.42 (2.20–18.78) [[Rasic2014]].

### genes · High-risk offspring cohorts (prospective)
- **Dutch Bipolar Offspring Study, 12-year follow-up** (140 offspring aged 12–21 at entry; 108 followed for 12 years):
  - 72% developed any DSM-IV axis I disorder, 54% a mood disorder, 13% a bipolar spectrum disorder, and only 3% bipolar I disorder.
  - In 88% of those with a bipolar spectrum disorder, the illness began with a depressive episode [[Mesman2013]].
- In the same cohort at 12 years, 24% of offspring with a unipolar mood disorder developed a bipolar spectrum disorder over time. Mood disorders were often recurrent (31%) and comorbid (67%), and began before age 25 [[Mesman2013]].
- **Dutch study, 22-year follow-up** (n = 100, mean age 38.3):
  - No new cases of bipolar disorder appeared after the 12-year visit. Lifetime bipolar disorder was 11–13% (bipolar I 4%, bipolar II 7%).
  - Lifetime prevalence was 65% for any mood disorder and 36% for major depression; 37% had recurrent mood episodes [[Helmink2025]].
- In that cohort, point prevalence of any psychopathology peaked at ages 20–25 (38–46%) and fell to 29–35% a year after age 30. In the decade before the 22-year visit, 71% had contacted mental health services. The authors conclude that the risk of the parent's own diagnosis (bipolar disorder) is highest in adolescence, while the risk of mood disorders in general continues through adult life [[Helmink2025]].
- **Canadian high-risk offspring cohort** (279 offspring of parents with bipolar disorder, 87 controls, about two decades of follow-up):
  - Cumulative incidence of bipolar disorder was 24.5%, with a median age at onset of 20.7 years (range 12.4–30.3) [[Duffy2019AJP]].
- In the Canadian cohort, these childhood problems raised the later risk of mood disorder:
  - sleep disorders: 1.6-fold
  - anxiety disorders: 1.8-fold
  - depressive symptoms: 2.7-fold
  - manic symptoms: 2.3-fold.
  The best-fitting model ran from nonspecific childhood problems, to adolescent depression, to a first manic or hypomanic episode [[Duffy2019AJP]].
- **Pittsburgh Bipolar Offspring Study, entry assessment** (388 offspring of 233 parents with bipolar disorder vs 251 community offspring), odds ratios:
  - bipolar spectrum disorders: 13.4 (95% CI 2.9–61.6)
  - any mood disorder: 5.2
  - anxiety disorder: 2.3 [[Birmaher2009]].
- In the same study, families in which both parents had bipolar disorder had more affected offspring than families with one affected parent (OR 3.6, 95% CI 1.1–12.2). Over 75% of offspring who developed bipolar disorder had their first mood episode before age 12 [[Birmaher2009]].
- **Pittsburgh longitudinal analysis** (391 high-risk vs 248 community offspring; mean follow-up 6.8 years), high-risk vs community:
  - subthreshold mania or hypomania: 13.3% vs 1.2%
  - manic, mixed or hypomanic episodes: 9.2% vs 0.8%
  - major depressive episodes: 32.0% vs 14.9%
  - ADHD: 30.7% vs 18.1%
  - anxiety disorders: 39.9% vs 21.8%
  - substance use disorders: 19.9% vs 10.1% [[Axelson2015]].
- In prospective analyses, subthreshold manic or hypomanic episodes were the main precursor of a later full manic, mixed or hypomanic episode (hazard ratio 7.57) [[Axelson2015]].

### genes · Overlap with schizophrenia, depression and other disorders
- In Sweden, 63% of the comorbidity between schizophrenia and bipolar disorder came from additive genetic effects shared by both disorders [[Lichtenstein2009]].
- The Swedish family study estimated these genetic correlations between bipolar disorder and other disorders (range 0.37–0.62, mostly genetic):
  - depression: 0.62
  - schizophrenia: 0.44
  The authors place bipolar disorder in both the mood and psychotic spectra, closer to mood disorders [[Song2015Family]].
- Molecular evidence first came in 2009: a polygenic component of schizophrenia risk, made of thousands of common alleles of very small effect, also contributed to bipolar disorder risk but not to non-psychiatric diseases [[Purcell2009]].
- From genome-wide SNPs, the Cross-Disorder Group estimated these genetic correlations [[PGCCDG2013NatGenet]]:
  - schizophrenia–bipolar disorder: 0.68 ± 0.04
  - bipolar disorder–major depression: 0.47 ± 0.06
  - SNPs explained 17–29% of liability across the five disorders studied.
- A five-disorder GWAS (33,332 cases, 27,888 controls) found four loci with cross-disorder effects, including two L-type voltage-gated calcium channel subunit genes, CACNA1C and CACNB2. Calcium-channel signalling genes were implicated in all five disorders [[PGCCDG2013Lancet]].
- A joint bipolar–schizophrenia analysis (20,129 bipolar disorder cases, 33,426 schizophrenia cases) found:
  - 114 genome-wide significant loci shared between the disorders, pointing to synaptic and neuronal pathways
  - 4 genomic regions that distinguish the two disorders
  - higher schizophrenia polygenic scores in people with bipolar disorder who have psychotic features or earlier onset [[PGCBDSCZ2018]].
- Across eight psychiatric disorders (232,964 cases):
  - Schizophrenia and bipolar disorder had the highest genetic correlation (rg 0.70 ± 0.02).
  - Bipolar disorder, major depression and schizophrenia formed one shared "mood and psychotic" genetic factor.
  - 72% of the 109 pleiotropic loci involved bipolar disorder [[PGCCDG2019]].
- The Brainstorm analysis of 25 brain disorders found that psychiatric disorders share common-variant risk (notably ADHD, bipolar disorder, major depression and schizophrenia), whereas neurological disorders are more distinct. Bipolar disorder was positively genetically correlated with years of education and college attainment [[Brainstorm2018]].
- In Mullins 2021, bipolar disorder had positive genetic correlations with schizophrenia (rg 0.68), major depression (0.44), and, more weakly, anorexia, ADHD and autism (rg about 0.2) [[Mullins2021]].
- The same study's Mendelian randomisation suggested that starting to smoke raises bipolar disorder risk (OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.38–1.61) (Early evidence) [[Mullins2021]].

### genes · Genome-wide association studies: from 2 loci to 298
- **2008.** A collaborative GWAS (4,387 cases, 6,209 controls) found genome-wide association in ANK3 (ankyrin G; rs10994336, P = 9.1 × 10⁻⁹). It also supported CACNA1C (L-type calcium channel α1C subunit; rs1006737, combined P = 7.0 × 10⁻⁸), suggesting a role for ion-channel dysfunction [[Ferreira2008]].
- **2011.** The first Psychiatric GWAS Consortium bipolar analysis (11,974 cases, 51,792 controls) confirmed CACNA1C and found a new intronic variant in ODZ4 (now called TENM4). A pathway made of calcium-channel subunits was enriched [[PGCBD2011]].
- **2013 review.** Robust common-variant hits included CACNA1C, ODZ4 and NCAN. The evidence for a polygenic contribution was strong. Structural (copy-number) variation seemed to matter less than in schizophrenia [[Craddock2013]].
- **2019 (PGC2).** Stahl et al. studied 20,352 cases and 31,358 controls, followed up in 9,412 cases and 137,760 controls. They found 30 genome-wide significant loci, 20 of them new [[Stahl2019]].
  - Implicated genes encode ion channels, neurotransmitter transporters and synaptic components.
  - Enriched pathways included regulation of insulin secretion and endocannabinoid signalling.
- Stahl 2019 named genes in the new loci: other ion channels and transporters (SCN2A, SLC4A1) and neurotransmitter receptors such as GRIN2A. The authors note that CACNA1C and other calcium-channel genes renewed interest in calcium-channel blockers as possible treatments (Hypothesis) [[Stahl2019]].
- **2021 (PGC3).** Mullins et al. studied 41,917 cases and 371,549 controls of European ancestry and found 64 loci. Risk alleles were enriched in [[Mullins2021]]:
  - synaptic-signalling genes
  - genes expressed in neurons of the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus.
- GWAS signal in Mullins 2021 was enriched in genes that encode targets of these drug classes [[Mullins2021]]:
  - antipsychotics
  - calcium-channel blockers
  - antiepileptics
  - anaesthetics.
  Expression data linked 15 genes to bipolar disorder, including druggable targets such as HTR6, MCHR1, DCLK3 and FURIN.
- The 64 loci in Mullins 2021 include several that are named in teaching, for example CACNA1C, ANK3, ODZ4, SCN2A and SHANK2 [[Mullins2021]].
- **2025 (multi-ancestry).** O'Connell et al. studied 158,036 cases and about 2.8 million controls of European, East Asian, African American and Latino ancestry, from 79 cohorts [[OConnell2025]].
  - They found 298 genome-wide significant loci, a fourfold increase.
  - One association was specific to East Asian ancestry.
  - Fine-mapping and gene-mapping methods identified 36 credible genes.
- In O'Connell 2025, SNPs within the 298 loci accounted for 18.5% of the SNP-heritability, against 8.3% for the 64 earlier loci. Most of the common-variant heritability therefore still lies outside known loci [[OConnell2025]].
- O'Connell 2025 implicated specific cell types, including GABAergic interneurons and medium spiny neurons. Eight of the 36 credible genes have synaptic annotations, for example CACNA1B, SHANK2, ERBB4 and SORCS3 [[OConnell2025]].
- Each common risk variant raises risk only slightly, but together common variants account for about 25% of the heritability of bipolar disorder (Established) [[Gordovez2020]].

### genes · SNP-heritability (how much common variants explain)
- **Stahl 2019.** SNP-heritability was 0.17–0.23 on the liability scale, assuming a population prevalence of 0.5–2% [[Stahl2019]].
- **Mullins 2021.** SNP-heritability was 18.6% (s.e. 0.008) assuming 2% prevalence, or 15.6% assuming 1% prevalence [[Mullins2021]].
- **O'Connell 2025.** SNP-heritability depended on how cases were found, assuming 2% prevalence [[OConnell2025]]:
  - clinical samples: 0.22
  - community samples: 0.05
  - self-report: 0.08.
- In O'Connell 2025, clinical and community cases were genetically almost identical (rg 0.95), but self-reported bipolar disorder correlated only 0.47 with clinical cases. Minimal phenotyping (asking people whether they have the diagnosis) dilutes the signal [[OConnell2025]].

### genes · Bipolar I vs bipolar II genetics
- **SNP-heritability is higher for bipolar I.** Every GWAS so far finds bipolar I about twice as heritable from common SNPs as bipolar II:
  - Stahl 2019: bipolar I 0.25, bipolar II 0.11, schizoaffective (bipolar type) 0.25 [[Stahl2019]]
  - Mullins 2021: bipolar I 20.9%, bipolar II 11.6% (1% prevalence each; significantly different) [[Mullins2021]]
  - O'Connell 2025: bipolar I 0.21, bipolar II 0.11 [[OConnell2025]].
- **Shared but not identical genetics.** Bipolar I and bipolar II overlap strongly but not fully: rg 0.85 (s.e. 0.05; significantly below 1) [[Mullins2021]] and rg 0.88 [[OConnell2025]].
- **Schizophrenia versus depression links.** Bipolar I's genetic correlation with schizophrenia is driven by psychosis, while bipolar II correlates more with major depression [[Stahl2019]]. Correlation with schizophrenia was rg 0.66 for bipolar I and 0.54 for bipolar II [[Mullins2021]].
- **Subtype-specific GWAS.** A bipolar I GWAS (25,060 cases) found 44 loci. A bipolar II GWAS (6,781 cases) found 1 [[Mullins2021]].
- **Family studies.** The bipolar spectrum (I, II and cyclothymia) shows high heritability with cross-concordance between subtypes in twins [[Edvardsen2008]].

### genes · Rare variants: copy-number variants and exomes
- In the Wellcome Trust sample (1,697 cases, 2,806 controls), the overall burden of large, rare CNVs in bipolar disorder was not higher than in controls and was significantly lower than in schizophrenia [[Grozeva2010]].
- A UK sample (2,591 cases) combined with earlier data found three schizophrenia-associated CNVs that also contribute to bipolar disorder: 1q21.1 duplications, 3q29 deletions and 16p11.2 duplications. Only 16p11.2 (P = 2.3 × 10⁻⁴) survived multiple-testing correction. Very large CNVs (>500 kb) contribute less to bipolar disorder than to schizophrenia [[Green2016CNV]].
- In 6,353 cases, CNV burden did not differ from controls when bipolar disorder was analysed as one diagnosis. Burden was raised only in schizoaffective disorder, bipolar type [[Charney2019]].
- **Exomes.** The Bipolar Exome study (13,933 cases, 14,422 controls) found an excess of ultra-rare protein-truncating variants in evolutionarily constrained genes, in both bipolar I and bipolar II [[Palmer2022Exome]].
- Combined with schizophrenia exome data, AKAP11 emerged as a definitive risk gene (OR 7.06, P = 2.83 × 10⁻⁹). Its protein interacts with GSK3B, a hypothesised lithium target (Early evidence) [[Palmer2022Exome]].
- In the 2025 GWAS, genes prioritised by fine-mapping were enriched for ultra-rare damaging variants in cases, so common and rare variant signals converge (Early evidence) [[OConnell2025]].

### genes · Polygenic risk scores: what they can and cannot do
- **Mullins 2021, how much a PRS explains.** A bipolar PRS explained about 4.57% of liability-scale variance (2% prevalence), equal to an area under the curve (AUC) of 65% [[Mullins2021]].
- **Mullins 2021, extreme deciles.** People in the top PRS decile had OR 3.5 (95% CI 1.7–7.3) against the middle decile and OR 9.3 (95% CI 1.7–49.3) against the lowest decile [[Mullins2021]].
- **O'Connell 2025.** Using the multi-ancestry score without self-reported cases [[OConnell2025]]:
  - top PRS quintile vs middle quintile: OR 7.06 (95% CI 3.9–10.4)
  - median AUC: 0.70 (95% CI 0.67–0.73)
  - the authors conclude that "the BD liability explained remains insufficient for diagnostic prediction in the general population."
- **Earlier result.** Stahl 2019 reported a PRS liability-scale R² of 0.04 (observed Nagelkerke R² 0.08) [[Stahl2019]].
- PRS alone "will never be able" to establish or definitively predict a diagnosis of a common complex condition such as a mental disorder. Genes contribute only part of the risk, and a PRS captures only part of the genetic part. Combining PRS with clinical risk factors may help (Established) [[Murray2021PRS]].
- PRS currently have low discriminative ability in the general population. They may be more useful where the prior probability is higher, such as early illness. They have a weaker evidence base in non-European ancestries [[LewisVassos2020]].
- **Pittsburgh offspring with PRS** (156 parents with bipolar disorder, 251 of their offspring, 13 years of follow-up):
  - Bipolar disorder developed in 23.1% of offspring of parents with bipolar disorder vs 5.1% of offspring of other parents; depression in 50.2% vs 32.9% [[Birmaher2022]].
  - Offspring bipolar PRS gave a hazard ratio of 1.34 (95% CI 1.03–1.7) and explained 5.0% of variance.
  - Parental bipolar disorder and offspring PRS were independent predictors. The authors say PRS cannot be used alone to determine risk.
- **Adding PRS to a risk calculator.** In 227 high-risk offspring (38 developed bipolar disorder), adding PRS to a published clinical risk calculator raised its AUC to 0.81. Removing the PRS terms lowered AUC by 0.07 (p = 0.039) (Early evidence) [[Hafeman2025]].
- **Family genetic risk scores.** In Sweden (15,029 bipolar disorder cases), people in the top quintile of a multi-generation family genetic risk score had an incidence rate ratio of 6.30 (95% CI 5.80–6.84) for bipolar disorder. These scores worked largely independently of environmental exposures [[Robinson2026FGRS]].

### frontier · Pharmacogenetics and lithium response (link to E)
- **Lithium-response GWAS.** In 2,563 patients (ConLiGen consortium), one chromosome 21 locus containing two long non-coding RNA genes reached genome-wide significance for lithium response [[Hou2016Lithium]].
- In an independent group of 73 patients on lithium monotherapy, carriers of the alternative (non-response) alleles relapsed more often (HR 3.8, 95% CI 1.1–13.0) (Early evidence) [[Hou2016Lithium]].
- **Schizophrenia polygenic load predicts poorer lithium response.** Up to 30% of patients do not respond to lithium. In 2,586 lithium-treated patients, those with a low schizophrenia polygenic score responded better: OR for response 3.46 (95% CI 1.42–8.41) in the lowest decile vs the highest. Pathway analyses pointed to the HLA complex and inflammatory cytokines (Early evidence) [[Amare2018]].
- Mullins 2021 found GWAS signal enriched in targets of existing mood-stabilising and antipsychotic drugs. Its 15 expression-linked genes are offered as "novel therapeutic leads" (Hypothesis) [[Mullins2021]].

### frontier · Genetic testing and genetic counselling
- There are no clinical practice guidelines for using genetic testing in bipolar disorder (unlike childhood neurodevelopmental conditions). Genetic counselling is not the same thing as genetic testing, and it can improve outcomes such as empowerment [[Austin2020GC]].
- **Pilot randomised trial of genetic counselling** (n = 40 per arm; schizophrenia, schizoaffective or bipolar disorder):
  - Genetic counselling and an educational booklet both improved knowledge.
  - Only genetic counselling made people's risk estimates more accurate.
  - No difference in internalised stigma was shown [[Hippman2016]].

### genes · Environment: childhood adversity
- In 19 studies, childhood adversity was 2.63 times (95% CI 2.00–3.47) more likely in people with bipolar disorder than in non-clinical controls, with emotional abuse strongest (OR 4.04, 95% CI 3.12–5.22). Rates were similar to those in other psychiatric patients, so the link is not specific to bipolar disorder [[PalmierClaus2016]].
- Among people with bipolar disorder (30 studies), childhood maltreatment was linked to an earlier age at onset (14 studies, 5,733 participants; OR 1.85, 95% CI 1.43–2.40) and to rapid cycling (OR 1.89, 1.45–2.48) [[AgnewBlais2016Lancet]].
- The same meta-analysis linked maltreatment to [[AgnewBlais2016Lancet]]:
  - more severe mania (OR 2.02) and depression (OR 1.57)
  - comorbid PTSD (OR 3.60) and substance misuse (OR 1.84)
  - suicide attempts (OR 2.25).
  These associations are why trauma-informed care and safety planning matter.
- In Sweden, adverse childhood experiences had the largest population attributable fraction for bipolar disorder among the environmental factors studied: 19.8% (95% CI 18.4–21.2). The fraction for substance use was 13.2% [[Robinson2026FGRS]].
- A systematic review of adolescents (14 studies) found that negative life events, especially sexual and physical abuse and emotional mistreatment, go with more severe illness in adolescents with bipolar disorder and with higher risk of mood disorders in offspring of parents with bipolar disorder [[Menculini2020]].

### genes · Environment: cannabis and other substances
- A meta-analysis of prospective studies (6 studies; 2,391 people with manic symptoms; mean follow-up 3.9 years) found [[Gibbs2015]]:
  - cannabis use was associated with about 3-fold higher risk of new-onset manic symptoms (OR 2.97, 95% CI 1.80–4.90; 2 studies)
  - cannabis use worsened manic symptoms in people already diagnosed with bipolar disorder
  - the authors call the evidence preliminary.
- In a Danish cohort of 6.65 million people, cannabis use disorder was associated with later bipolar disorder [[Jefsen2023]]:
  - men: HR 2.96 (2.73–3.21)
  - women: HR 2.54 (2.31–2.80)
  - psychotic bipolar disorder: HR 4.05 (3.52–4.65).
- A bidirectional Mendelian randomisation study found that genetic liability to bipolar disorder raised the chance of ever using cannabis. It found no evidence for the reverse, so the causal direction is unresolved (Early evidence) [[Jefsen2021]].

### genes · Environment: life events, sleep loss and triggers
- In 125 people with bipolar I disorder interviewed monthly for about 27 months [[Johnson2008Mania]]:
  - goal-attainment life events predicted rises in manic symptoms
  - negative events predicted depressive symptoms in regression models.
- In an earlier group of 43 people with bipolar I disorder, manic symptoms rose over the 2 months after goal-attainment events [[Johnson2000Goal]].
- A review of longitudinal studies found that the recent exposures most predictive of mania are activating, reward-related or sleep-disrupting. Conflict and hostility, but not general social support, predicted the course [[Johnson2022Mania]].
- In the Dutch offspring cohort (n = 108), life events raised the risk of first mood episodes and, less strongly, of recurrences. This fits a "kindling" pattern [[Kemner2015PsycholMed]].
- A separate study of 51 people with bipolar disorder found that the effect of life events on hospital admissions faded by about 25% a year [[Kemner2015IJBD]].
- Sleep loss has been proposed as a "final common pathway" by which many psychological, social, environmental and drug triggers set off mania. Because mania itself reduces sleep, the process can become self-reinforcing (Hypothesis, supported by sleep-deprivation experiments) [[Wehr1987]].
- In 3,140 people with bipolar disorder, sleep loss triggering a high-mood episode was more often reported by women (OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.17–1.75) and by people with bipolar I (OR 2.81, 2.26–3.50). The authors flag shift work and international travel as situations to plan for [[Lewis2017Sleep]].
- **Antidepressant-associated switch (detail in passes D/F).** In 3,240 Swedish patients:
  - antidepressant monotherapy raised the risk of mania in the first 3 months (HR 2.83, 95% CI 1.12–7.19)
  - no acute rise was seen with a concurrent mood stabiliser (HR 0.79, 0.54–1.15) [[Viktorin2014]].

### genes · Environment: the postpartum period (detail and planning in pass H)
- In Danish registers, first-time mothers' risk of psychiatric admission peaked 10–19 days after birth (RR 7.31, 95% CI 5.44–9.81, compared with mothers 11–12 months after giving birth). Fathers had no increase [[MunkOlsen2006]].
- In 1,212 parous women with bipolar disorder, about 50% of pregnancies in women with bipolar I were followed by a perinatal major mood episode, against about 40% in bipolar II. Most episodes began in the first month after birth [[DiFlorio2013]].
- In a meta-analysis of 5,700 deliveries, the overall postpartum relapse risk was 35%. Women with bipolar disorder who took no medication during pregnancy relapsed at 66%, against 23% for those on preventive medication. These are population findings for a care team to plan with, not advice to start or stop any medicine [[Wesseloo2016]].

### genes · Environment: season and light
- A systematic review of 51 papers found that [[Geoffroy2014]]:
  - manic episodes peak in spring and summer, and depressive episodes in early winter
  - a seasonal pattern was frequent for manic (15%) and depressive (25%) episodes.
- In 5,536 patients at 50 sites in 32 countries, a larger springtime rise in solar insolation at the place of onset was associated with a younger age at onset. The effect was weaker in people without a family history of mood disorders (Early evidence) [[Bauer2017Solar]].
- Across more than 250 studies, people who develop schizophrenia or bipolar disorder show a 5–8% winter-spring excess of births [[Torrey1997]].

### genes · Environment: parental age, birth and prenatal factors
- In Sweden (13,428 cases), children of fathers aged 55 or older were 1.37 times (95% CI 1.02–1.84) more likely to be diagnosed with bipolar disorder than children of fathers aged 20–24. For early-onset cases (before age 20), the odds ratio was 2.63 (1.19–5.81) [[Frans2008]].
- Comparing siblings in a cohort of 2.6 million Swedes, offspring of fathers aged 45 or older had a hazard ratio for bipolar disorder of 24.70 (95% CI 12.12–50.31) against offspring of fathers aged 20–24. The authors link this to new mutations during sperm production (Early evidence; very wide CI) [[DOnofrio2014]].
- In 1.3 million Swedish births, hazard ratios for later hospitalisation with bipolar affective disorder, compared with term birth, were [[Nosarti2012]]:
  - born at 32–36 weeks: 2.7 (95% CI 1.6–4.5)
  - born before 32 weeks: 7.4 (95% CI 2.7–20.6)
- In a California birth cohort, maternal influenza during pregnancy was associated with bipolar disorder in the adult child (OR 3.82, 95% CI 1.58–9.24; 92 cases). The authors say it needs replication (Early evidence) [[Parboosing2013]].
- In the same cohort, exposure to maternal smoking in the womb carried twice the risk of bipolar disorder (OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.48–2.53). Unmeasured family factors are not excluded (Early evidence) [[Talati2013]].

### genes · Environment: infection, autoimmunity and head injury
- In 3.56 million Danes, a prior hospital contact for [[Benros2013]]:
  - autoimmune disease raised the risk of a later mood disorder (bipolar disorder and depression combined) by 45% (IRR 1.45)
  - infection raised it by 62% (IRR 1.62)
  - both together gave IRR 2.35.
  This fits an immunological hypothesis for some patients (Hypothesis).
- In 113,906 people with a head injury, the risk of later bipolar disorder rose modestly (IRR 1.28, 95% CI 1.10–1.48). Injury at ages 11–15 was the strongest predictor [[Orlovska2014]].

### genes · Putting it together: gene–environment framing
- Family genetic risk and most environmental exposures (adverse childhood experiences, substance use, perinatal factors, infections, urban birth) were each independently associated with bipolar disorder risk in Swedish registers. Their effects added together rather than one explaining the other [[Robinson2026FGRS]].
- Even among identical twins, the Finnish co-twin of a person with bipolar I disorder developed it in fewer than half of cases (probandwise concordance 0.43). Genes raise risk but do not decide it [[Kieseppa2004]].
- Most children of a parent with bipolar disorder do not develop bipolar disorder. By the 22-year Dutch follow-up, 87–89% had not [[Helmink2025]]. In the Danish register, 95.6% of children with one affected parent had not been diagnosed by age 52 [[Gottesman2010]].
- Bipolar disorder is polygenic: many risk alleles of small effect, overlapping with schizophrenia and depression. A smaller role is played by rare variants (Established) [[Craddock2013,Gordovez2020,OConnell2025]].

### Exam hooks
- Twin heritability of bipolar disorder is about 60–85% (UK twins 85% [[McGuffin2003]]; Finnish twins 93% [[Kieseppa2004]]; Swedish twins 60% [[Johansson2019]]).
- Family-register heritability is about 58–59% [[Lichtenstein2009,Song2015Family]].
- MZ co-twin concordance for bipolar I is about 0.43, against 0.06 for DZ twins (Finland) [[Kieseppa2004]]. Older Danish data give 0.67 vs 0.20 [[Bertelsen1977]].
- First-degree relatives have a relative risk of about 6–14 (Sweden 5.8–7.9 [[Song2015Family]]; Denmark 13.63 [[Mortensen2003]]).
- Offspring risk of bipolar disorder by age 52 (Denmark): 0.48% with no affected parent, 4.4% with one, 24.9% with two [[Gottesman2010]].
- Shared family environment explains only about 3–4% of bipolar disorder liability [[Lichtenstein2009]].
- Genetic correlation between bipolar disorder and schizophrenia from SNPs is about 0.68–0.70 [[PGCCDG2013NatGenet,PGCCDG2019]].
- Bipolar I correlates more with schizophrenia (driven by psychosis). Bipolar II correlates more with major depression [[Stahl2019]].
- GWAS milestones: 30 loci in 2019 [[Stahl2019]], 64 loci from 41,917 cases in 2021 [[Mullins2021]], 298 loci from 158,036 multi-ancestry cases in 2025 [[OConnell2025]].
- Classic named genes: CACNA1C (L-type calcium channel) and ANK3 (ankyrin G) [[Ferreira2008]]; ODZ4/TENM4 [[PGCBD2011]].
- SNP-heritability is about 16–23% on the liability scale [[Stahl2019,Mullins2021]].
- A PRS explains about 4.6% of liability (AUC about 0.65–0.70) and is not a diagnostic test [[Mullins2021,OConnell2025]].
- Rare CNV burden in bipolar disorder is lower than in schizophrenia; 16p11.2 duplication is the best-supported CNV [[Grozeva2010,Green2016CNV]].
- AKAP11 is the first exome-wide significant bipolar risk gene shared with schizophrenia (OR 7.06) [[Palmer2022Exome]].
- In the Dutch offspring cohort, bipolar disorder in offspring usually starts with a depressive episode (88%) [[Mesman2013]].
- Subthreshold hypomania is the strongest prospective precursor of mania in high-risk offspring (HR 7.57) [[Axelson2015]].
- Childhood maltreatment goes with earlier onset (OR 1.85) and rapid cycling (OR 1.89) [[AgnewBlais2016Lancet]].
- Cannabis is linked to about 3-fold odds of new-onset manic symptoms [[Gibbs2015]].
- Postpartum psychiatric admission risk peaks 10–19 days after the first birth (RR 7.31) [[MunkOlsen2006]].
- Antidepressant monotherapy is linked to a higher risk of mania in the first 3 months (HR 2.83). Combined with a mood stabiliser, it is not (HR 0.79) [[Viktorin2014]].

### In plain words
- Bipolar disorder often runs in families, but no single gene causes it. Hundreds of common gene differences each nudge risk up a tiny amount [[OConnell2025,Craddock2013]].
- Having a parent with bipolar disorder raises a child's risk, but most children of a parent with bipolar disorder never develop it [[Gottesman2010,Helmink2025]].
- Even an identical twin of someone with bipolar disorder often stays well, so genes are not destiny [[Kieseppa2004]].
- There is no blood or DNA test that can diagnose bipolar disorder today. Diagnosis comes from a careful talk with a clinician about your mood history [[OConnell2025,Murray2021PRS]].
- Some of the same genes are involved in bipolar disorder, depression and schizophrenia. That is why these conditions can show up in the same family [[Lichtenstein2009,PGCCDG2019]].
- Hard experiences in childhood, such as abuse or neglect, are linked to bipolar disorder starting earlier. Support and trauma-informed care can help [[AgnewBlais2016Lancet,PalmierClaus2016]].
- Losing sleep can tip some people toward a high mood. Keeping a steady sleep routine is a practical way to protect yourself [[Wehr1987,Lewis2017Sleep]].
- Cannabis use is linked to manic symptoms. If you have bipolar disorder in the family, that is worth knowing [[Gibbs2015,Jefsen2023]].
- The weeks after having a baby are a higher-risk time. If you have bipolar disorder and are planning a pregnancy, make a plan with your care team [[DiFlorio2013,Wesseloo2016]].
- Big exciting events, like a new job or a big win, can also push mood up in some people, not only stressful ones [[Johnson2000Goal,Johnson2008Mania]].
- In children at family risk, the first sign is usually low mood or anxiety, not mania. Getting help early makes sense [[Mesman2013,Duffy2019AJP]].
- A genetic counsellor can help families understand their real risk, which is often lower than people fear [[Hippman2016,Austin2020GC]].

## Open questions and controversies
- **Twin vs register heritability.** Clinical twin series give 85–93% [[McGuffin2003,Kieseppa2004]], while whole-population Swedish twin and family registers give 58–60% [[Lichtenstein2009,Song2015Family,Johansson2019]]. Likely reasons include ascertainment of severe cases, diagnostic method and small twin samples with wide CIs. Status: both are cited, and "about 60–85%" is the usual summary [[Mullins2021]].
- **Missing heritability.** Twin heritability of 60–85% vs SNP-heritability of about 16–23% [[Stahl2019,Mullins2021]]. One account says the gap is filled by rare variants, by common variants not yet found, and by non-additive effects. The 298 loci explain only 18.5% of SNP-heritability [[OConnell2025]], and rare-variant evidence is growing but explains little so far [[Palmer2022Exome]]. Status: open.
- **What is a "case"?** Adding self-reported and biobank cases boosts locus discovery [[OConnell2025]], but self-reported bipolar disorder correlates only 0.47 with clinical cases and shifts correlations toward depression. Clinically ascertained samples are more heritable (0.22 vs 0.05–0.08) [[OConnell2025]]. Status: debated. Minimal phenotyping trades specificity for size.
- **Is bipolar I separate from bipolar II?** One view is one spectrum with high heritability and cross-concordance [[Edvardsen2008]]. The other is partly distinct genetics: rg 0.85–0.88, about half the SNP-heritability in bipolar II, and a different correlation profile (schizophrenia vs depression) [[Mullins2021,Stahl2019,OConnell2025]]. Status: high but imperfect overlap.
- **Bipolar disorder and schizophrenia: one continuum or two disorders?** Arguments for overlap are a shared genetic cause and rg about 0.7 [[Lichtenstein2009,PGCCDG2019]]. Arguments for difference are 4 distinguishing loci and lower CNV burden in bipolar disorder outside schizoaffective cases [[PGCBDSCZ2018,Charney2019,Grozeva2010]]. Status: partly shared and partly distinct.
- **Clinical use of PRS.** Against: AUC about 0.65–0.70 is "insufficient for diagnostic prediction in the general population" [[OConnell2025,Mullins2021]]; PRS alone will never establish a diagnosis [[Murray2021PRS]]. For: in high-risk offspring, adding PRS to a clinical calculator improved AUC to 0.81 [[Hafeman2025]]. Status: research tool only. Evidence is weaker in non-European ancestries [[LewisVassos2020]].
- **Cannabis: cause or consequence?** Observational studies show 2.5–3-fold associations [[Gibbs2015,Jefsen2023]], but Mendelian randomisation supports bipolar liability leading to cannabis use, not the reverse [[Jefsen2021]]. Status: unresolved. Avoiding cannabis is still advised clinically [[Gibbs2015]].
- **Paternal age effect size.** The population OR is 1.37 for fathers 55+ [[Frans2008]], but the within-sibling HR is 24.70 for fathers 45+ [[DOnofrio2014]]. The very wide CI and the design difference mean the magnitude is uncertain.
- **Offspring lifetime risk figures.** Uher 2023 reports a pooled 5% lifetime risk of bipolar disorder in offspring, but meta-analytic Kaplan-Meier curves reaching 36% by age 28 [[Uher2023]]. Individual cohorts give 11–13% [[Helmink2025]], 23.1% [[Birmaher2022]] and 24.5% [[Duffy2019AJP]]. Differences reflect ascertainment, follow-up length, whether bipolar II and "not otherwise specified" are counted, and the high risk of bias. Builders should label which figure they show.
- **Childhood adversity specificity.** Adversity is 2.63 times more common than in healthy controls but similar to other psychiatric patients [[PalmierClaus2016]]. It may be a general rather than bipolar-specific risk factor and shapes course more clearly than onset [[AgnewBlais2016Lancet]].

## Provenance
- **Validation.** All 74 sources were validated against PubMed E-utilities `esummary`. Authors, title, journal, year, volume, pages, PMID, PMCID and DOI were taken from the record. No source is unverified.
- **Abstracts.** Abstracts were read through Europe PMC (`resultType=core`) or PubMed `efetch`. When Europe PMC cut off results text at a "<" sign, the abstract was re-read in PubMed (Rasic 2014, Agnew-Blais 2016, Lewis 2017, Frans 2008, Green 2016, the Cross-Disorder Lancet 2013 paper, Amare 2018, Birmaher 2022 and Hafeman 2025).
- **Full-text numbers.** These come from PubMed Central full text fetched through E-utilities (`db=pmc`) or Europe PMC `fullTextXML`:
  - SNP-heritability, PRS AUC and odds ratios, subtype heritabilities and genetic correlations, named genes in loci, and the smoking Mendelian randomisation: Stahl 2019, Mullins 2021, O'Connell 2025
  - the three-factor structure and rg 0.70: PGCCDG2019
  - the education correlation: Brainstorm2018.
- **Abstract-only or title-only sources.** Mendlewicz 1977 has no PubMed abstract and is cited only for its title claim. Smoller 2003, Craddock 2013 and Gordovez 2020 are cited only for statements in their abstracts.
- **Collective-author records.** Three PubMed records list a collective author only, so the consortium name is used as the author in the Sources line: PGCBD2011, PGCCDG2013Lancet and PGCBDSCZ2018. PGCCDG2019 and Brainstorm2018 keep the text used in the ADHD companion.
- **Key choices.** AgnewBlais2016 is taken in the ADHD companion by a different paper, so this pass uses AgnewBlais2016Lancet. Common surnames carry a work suffix (Song2015Family, Song2024Twins, Green2016CNV, Lewis2017Sleep, Johnson2000Goal, Johnson2008Mania, Johnson2022Mania, Murray2021PRS, Hou2016Lithium, Bauer2017Solar, Kendler2026Mating, Robinson2026FGRS, Austin2020GC, Palmer2022Exome, Duffy2019AJP).
- **Considered and not used.**
  - Marconi 2016 (cannabis and psychosis): it is about psychosis, not bipolar disorder.
  - A specific International Society of Psychiatric Genetics statement on genetic testing: no PubMed record was found.
  - Benros 2013 reports mood disorders as a whole, not bipolar disorder alone, and is labelled that way.
