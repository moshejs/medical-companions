# H · Course, comorbidity, pregnancy and special groups, and research toward better treatment

Pass date: 2026-11-03. Modules served: course, pregnancy, frontier.

This pass covers the long-term course of bipolar disorder (symptom burden, recurrence, recovery, rapid cycling, later life), the conditions that often occur alongside it (anxiety, substance use, ADHD, cardiometabolic disease, migraine, premature mortality), pregnancy, the postpartum period and breastfeeding, children and adolescents, and the research frontier (genomics, lithium response prediction, digital monitoring, ketamine, anti-inflammatory adjuncts, chronotherapy, newer antipsychotics and registered trials). Every number was read from the PubMed or Europe PMC abstract, the openFDA label, the ClinicalTrials.gov record or the NICE recommendations page named in the citation.

## Findings

### course · Long-term symptom burden (NIMH Collaborative Depression Study)
- In 146 people with bipolar I disorder followed prospectively for a mean of 12.8 years, symptoms of some kind were present in 47.3% of all follow-up weeks [[Judd2002]].
- In that bipolar I cohort, depressive symptoms took up 31.9% of weeks, manic or hypomanic symptoms 8.9% and cycling or mixed symptoms 5.9% [[Judd2002]].
- Subsyndromal, minor depressive and hypomanic symptoms together were nearly 3 times more frequent than full syndromal major depression or mania (29.9% vs 11.2% of weeks) [[Judd2002]].
- People with bipolar I disorder changed symptom status about 6 times a year and changed polarity more than 3 times a year; comorbid drug-use disorder predicted greater chronicity [[Judd2002]].
- In 86 people with bipolar II disorder followed for a mean of 13.4 years, symptoms were present in 53.9% of weeks: depressive 50.3%, hypomanic 1.3% and cycling or mixed 2.3% [[Judd2003]].
- In bipolar II disorder, people with brief (2–6 day) and longer (7 days or more) hypomanias did not differ on any course measure [[Judd2003]].
- Across 219 people with bipolar I disorder followed for up to 25 years, the median length of a mood episode was 13 weeks, and more than 75% recovered from their episodes within 1 year of onset [[Solomon2010]].
- Recovery was slower for episodes with a severe onset, such as psychosis or severe impairment in the first week (HR 0.746, 95% CI 0.578–0.963), and for people with more cumulative years ill [[Solomon2010]].
- In a Danish case register of 20,350 first-admission patients with affective disorder (1971–1993), the rate of recurrence rose with each previous episode in both unipolar and bipolar disorder (Established) [[Kessing1998]].

### course · Recurrence after recovery
- In STEP-BD, 858 of 1,469 participants (58.4%) who were symptomatic at entry recovered. Of these, 416 (48.5%) had a recurrence within up to 2 years [[Perlis2006STEPBD]].
- More than twice as many STEP-BD recurrences were depressive (298; 34.7%) as manic, hypomanic or mixed (118; 13.8%) [[Perlis2006STEPBD]].
- In STEP-BD, it took 21.4 weeks for 25% of recovered people to have a depressive episode and 85.0 weeks for 25% to have a manic, hypomanic or mixed episode [[Perlis2006STEPBD]].
- Residual mood symptoms at recovery predicted earlier recurrence in STEP-BD. The authors suggest that treating residual symptoms is a target for maintenance care [[Perlis2006STEPBD]].
- A meta-analysis of 8 prospective first-episode mania cohorts (734 patients) found recurrence rates of 25.7% by 6 months, 41.0% by 1 year and 59.7% by 4 years [[Gignac2015]].
- In the same meta-analysis, syndromal recovery was 77.4% at 6 months and 84.2% at 1 year, but only 62.1% reached symptomatic recovery within 1 year. Younger age at first episode was linked to recurrence after 1 year [[Gignac2015]].
- The authors read the lower recurrence after a first episode than in mixed-stage cohorts as a possible "window of opportunity" for early treatment (Early evidence) [[Gignac2015]].

### course · Syndromal versus functional recovery (McLean-Harvard First-Episode Mania Study)
- Of 166 people followed for 2–4 years after a first hospitalization for mania or a mixed episode, 98% reached syndromal recovery by 2 years. Half had recovered by 5.4 weeks [[Tohen2003McLean]].
- Only 72% reached symptomatic recovery, and only 43% reached functional recovery (regaining their previous work and living status) [[Tohen2003McLean]].
- Within 2 years of syndromal recovery, 40% had a new episode (20% mania, 20% depression), and 19% switched to the opposite pole without recovering [[Tohen2003McLean]].
- Mood-congruent psychosis at first episode, lower premorbid occupational status and a manic first presentation predicted a manic recurrence. A mixed first presentation and any comorbidity predicted depression [[Tohen2003McLean]].

### course · Rapid cycling
- Rapid cycling is defined as 4 or more mood episodes per year [[Kupka2003]].
- In 8 studies of consecutively admitted patients (2,054 people), 16.3% had rapid cycling [[Kupka2003]].
- Rapid cycling was slightly more common in women and in bipolar II disorder, each a small but statistically significant effect [[Kupka2003]].
- With lithium prophylaxis, 34% of people with rapid cycling had no further recurrences, compared with 47% of people without rapid cycling. Partial response was seen in 59% and 65% [[Kupka2003]].
- In 46% of cases, rapid cycling was preceded by antidepressant treatment, but the meta-analysis found no systematic data showing that antidepressants cause it (Hypothesis) [[Kupka2003]].
- Hypothyroidism was associated with rapid cycling in studies using current (not lifetime) definitions [[Kupka2003]].

### course · Later life, cognition and dementia
- The ISBD Task Force on Older-Age Bipolar Disorder says older adults with bipolar disorder will grow in number and as a share of the population. It names late onset, medical comorbidity and cognitive decline as the main open issues [[Sajatovic2015]].
- An individual-patient-data meta-analysis (31 data sets, N = 2,876) found cognitive impairment on all 11 test measures in euthymic bipolar disorder, with effect sizes of 0.26–0.63 after controlling for age, IQ and sex [[Bourne2013]].
- That analysis found that residual mood symptoms did not explain the deficits, which seemed unrelated to drug treatment. It also concluded that deficits may be smaller than earlier meta-analyses reported [[Bourne2013]].
- In euthymic late-life bipolar disorder, global screening tests (MMSE, clock drawing) did not differ from controls. Deficits in attention, memory, cognitive flexibility and verbal fluency had Hedges' g of 0.61–0.88, similar to those in remitted younger adults [[Samame2013]].
- A meta-analysis of 6 studies (3,026 people with bipolar disorder; 191,029 without) found a pooled odds ratio for dementia of 2.36 (95% CI 1.36–4.09), with heterogeneity and publication bias [[Diniz2017]].
- A later meta-analysis of 10 studies (6,859 people with bipolar disorder; 487,966 controls) found an odds ratio for dementia of 2.96 (95% CI 2.09–4.18). The number of mood episodes predicted dementia [[Velosa2020]].
- In 5 studies, lithium treatment in bipolar disorder was associated with a lower risk of dementia (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.36–0.72). These were observational studies (Early evidence) [[Velosa2020]].
- In Danish registers, people who bought lithium at least once had a higher rate of dementia (RR 1.47, 95% CI 1.22–1.76). In those who continued lithium, the rate fell to that of the general population. The authors note that methodological explanations cannot be excluded (Early evidence) [[Kessing2008Dementia]].
- In GERI-BD, 224 people aged 60 or older with bipolar I mania, hypomania or a mixed episode were randomized to lithium (target 0.80–0.99 mEq/L) or divalproex (target 80–99 μg/mL) for 9 weeks [[Young2017GERIBD]].
- In GERI-BD, 9-week response rates were 79% with lithium and 73% with divalproex (not significantly different). Lithium gave a greater fall in mania scores (YMRS difference 3.90; 97.5% CI 1.71–6.09) and tended to cause more tremor [[Young2017GERIBD]].

### course · Anxiety disorders
- In the WHO World Mental Health surveys (61,392 adults, 11 countries), three-quarters of people with bipolar spectrum disorder met criteria for at least one other disorder. Anxiety disorders, especially panic attacks, were the most common [[Merikangas2011]].
- In those surveys, fewer than half of people with lifetime bipolar spectrum disorder had received mental health treatment. In low-income countries, only 25.2% had any contact with mental health services [[Merikangas2011]].
- Across 40 studies (14,914 people), the lifetime prevalence of any anxiety disorder in bipolar disorder was 45% (95% CI 40–51) [[Pavlova2015]].
- Compared with population controls, people with bipolar disorder had about three times the prevalence of anxiety disorders (RR 3.22, 95% CI 2.41–4.29). Bipolar I and II did not differ (RR 1.07) [[Pavlova2015]].
- Even during euthymia, 34.7% (95% CI 23.9–45.5) of people with bipolar disorder met criteria for a current anxiety disorder, a 4.6-fold increase over population controls (RR 4.60, 95% CI 2.37–8.92) [[Pavlova2017]].

### course · Substance use disorders
- In clinical samples (1990–2015), the most common substance use disorders in bipolar disorder were alcohol use disorder (42%), cannabis use disorder (20%) and other illicit drug use disorder (17%) [[Hunt2016Clinical]].
- In clinical samples, men had a higher lifetime risk of substance use disorders than women. Comorbid substance use was linked to earlier onset and slightly more hospitalizations [[Hunt2016Clinical]].
- In 9 national household surveys (218,397 respondents), people with bipolar disorder had a mean prevalence of 33% for any substance use disorder, 24% for alcohol use disorder and 17% for illicit drug use disorder [[Hunt2016Surveys]].
- In those surveys, the odds of bipolar disorder were raised for alcohol use disorder (OR 4.09, 95% CI 3.37–4.96) and for illicit drug use (OR 4.96, 95% CI 3.98–6.17) [[Hunt2016Surveys]].

### course · ADHD
- Across 92 studies (17,089 people), ADHD was present in 73% (95% CI 66–79) of children, 43% (35–50) of adolescents and 17% (14–20) of adults with bipolar disorder [[Sandstrom2021]].
- ADHD was about 3 times more common in people with mood disorders than in those without, and 1.7 times more common in bipolar disorder than in major depressive disorder [[Sandstrom2021]].
- In 2,307 Swedish adults with bipolar disorder who started methylphenidate, monotherapy was followed by a higher rate of mania within 3 months (HR 6.7, 95% CI 2.0–22.4). With a concurrent mood stabilizer, the rate was lower (HR 0.6, 95% CI 0.4–0.9) [[Viktorin2017]].

### course · Cardiometabolic disease, migraine and premature mortality
- Across 37 publications (N = 6,983), 37.3% (95% CI 36.1–39.0) of people with bipolar disorder had metabolic syndrome. The odds were about twice those of general population groups (OR 1.98, 95% CI 1.74–2.25) [[Vancampfort2013]].
- Metabolic syndrome was more common in people taking antipsychotics (45.3%) than in people not taking them (32.4%; OR 1.72, 95% CI 1.24–2.38) [[Vancampfort2013]].
- In a meta-analysis of 3,211,768 people with severe mental illness (schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depression), new cardiovascular disease was more frequent than in controls (adjusted HR 1.78, 95% CI 1.60–1.98; 31 studies) [[Correll2017CVD]].
- An American Heart Association scientific statement classes major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder in youth as "tier II moderate-risk conditions" for accelerated atherosclerosis and early cardiovascular disease [[Goldstein2015AHA]].
- Across 14 studies (3,976 people with bipolar disorder), the pooled prevalence of migraine was 34.8% (95% CI 25.54–44.69). It was higher in bipolar II (54.17%) than in bipolar I (32.7%) [[Fornaro2015]].
- A meta-analysis of 31 studies found an all-cause standardized mortality ratio of 2.05 (95% CI 1.89–2.23) for bipolar disorder. Natural-cause deaths had an SMR of 1.64 and unnatural deaths 7.42 [[Hayes2015SMR]].
- In that meta-analysis, deaths from circulatory disease had an SMR of 1.73 (95% CI 1.54–1.94), from respiratory disease 2.92 and from infection 2.25 [[Hayes2015SMR]].
- A larger meta-analysis (57 studies; 678,353 people with bipolar disorder) found all-cause mortality RR 2.02 (95% CI 1.89–2.16) and natural-cause mortality RR 1.90 (1.75–2.06) [[Biazus2023]].
- In that meta-analysis, cardiovascular mortality had RR 1.76, cerebrovascular 1.57 and respiratory 3.18. Cancer mortality was not raised (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.88–1.11) [[Biazus2023]].
- The same meta-analysis found that suicide carried the highest cause-specific risk (RR 11.69, 95% CI 9.22–14.81). The authors conclude that preventing suicide and promoting physical health are both needed [[Biazus2023]].
- Pooled life expectancy in bipolar disorder was 66.88 years (95% CI 64.47–69.28): 70.51 years in women and 64.59 in men [[Chan2022LifeExp]].
- The weighted average was 12.89 years of potential life lost. About 5.94 of those years were from natural causes and 5.69 from unnatural causes [[Chan2022LifeExp]].
- In Danish registers (19,955 patients), bipolar disorder was associated with 7.7 (95% CI 7.4–8.1) fewer years of life expectancy. Unaffected siblings had no shortening, which points to the illness itself rather than shared family factors [[Kessing2021LifeExp]].

### pregnancy · Relapse risk in pregnancy and after birth
- Among 89 pregnant women with bipolar disorder who were well at conception, 71% had at least one recurrence during pregnancy [[Viguera2007Pregnancy]].
- Compared with continuing mood stabilizer treatment, stopping it doubled the recurrence risk, cut the median time to recurrence by more than four times and made the proportion of weeks ill five times greater [[Viguera2007Pregnancy]].
- Median time to recurrence was 11 times shorter after abrupt or rapid stopping than after gradual stopping. 74% of recurrences were depressive or mixed, and 47% occurred in the first trimester [[Viguera2007Pregnancy]].
- After stopping lithium, recurrence over the next 40 weeks was similar in pregnant (52%) and non-pregnant women (58%). It was 21% in the year before lithium was stopped [[Viguera2000]].
- Among women who stayed well for 40 weeks after stopping lithium, postpartum recurrence was 2.9 times more frequent than recurrence in non-pregnant women over the same period (70% vs 24%) [[Viguera2000]].
- In a meta-analysis of 37 articles (5,700 deliveries; 4,023 patients), the overall postpartum relapse risk was 35% (95% CI 29–41) [[Wesseloo2016]].
- Severe postpartum episodes occurred in 17% (95% CI 13–21) of women with bipolar disorder and in 29% (95% CI 20–41) of women with a history of postpartum psychosis [[Wesseloo2016]].
- In women with bipolar disorder, postpartum relapse was 66% (95% CI 57–75) among those who were medication-free in pregnancy and 23% (95% CI 14–37) among those who used preventive medication [[Wesseloo2016]].
- In 1,785 parous women, those with bipolar I disorder reported about a 50% risk of a perinatal major mood episode per pregnancy. The risk was about 40% for bipolar II disorder and for recurrent major depression [[DiFlorio2013]].
- Most perinatal episodes began within the first postpartum month. Mania or psychosis started earlier than depression [[DiFlorio2013]].
- In a high-risk clinic, none of 20 women with a history of psychosis limited to the postpartum period who started preventive treatment right after delivery relapsed, compared with 44.4% of those who declined it [[Bergink2012]].
- In that clinic, 24.4% of 41 women with bipolar disorder relapsed during pregnancy. Postpartum relapse was highest (60.0%) in those who had mood episodes during pregnancy [[Bergink2012]].
- A Lancet series review notes that suicide is a leading cause of maternal death and recommends an individual risk-benefit analysis for each woman [[Jones2014Lancet]].

### pregnancy · Postpartum psychosis
- Five population studies reported postpartum psychosis incidence of 0.89 to 2.6 per 1,000 women. The data were too varied to pool [[VanderKruik2017]].
- In Denmark (1973–2005), severe mental disorders needing admission in the first 3 months after a first birth had a prevalence of 1.03 per 1,000 births for mothers and 0.37 per 1,000 for fathers [[MunkOlsen2006]].
- First-time mothers had their highest risk of a psychiatric admission 10–19 days after giving birth (RR 7.31, 95% CI 5.44–9.81, compared with mothers 11–12 months after birth). Fatherhood was not associated with a similar rise [[MunkOlsen2006]].
- A 1987 record-linkage study of 54,087 births found a very high relative risk of admission with psychosis in the first 30 days after childbirth. It concluded that most puerperal psychoses are manic-depressive (bipolar-spectrum) illnesses [[Kendell1987]].

### pregnancy · Lithium in pregnancy
- In 1,325,563 Medicaid pregnancies, cardiac malformations occurred in 2.41% of 663 infants exposed to lithium in the first trimester, 1.15% of unexposed infants and 1.39% of lamotrigine-exposed infants [[Patorno2017]].
- The adjusted risk ratio for cardiac malformations with first-trimester lithium was 1.65 (95% CI 1.02–2.68) [[Patorno2017]].
- The risk was dose-dependent: RR 1.11 (95% CI 0.46–2.64) for 600 mg/day or less, 1.60 (0.67–3.80) for 601–900 mg/day and 3.22 (1.47–7.02) for more than 900 mg/day [[Patorno2017]].
- Right ventricular outflow tract obstruction defects, the group that includes Ebstein's anomaly, occurred in 0.60% of lithium-exposed infants vs 0.18% of unexposed infants (adjusted RR 2.66, 95% CI 1.00–7.06). The authors call the effect smaller than previously thought [[Patorno2017]].
- A meta-analysis of 6 cohorts (22,124 pregnancies; 727 lithium-exposed) found first-trimester lithium associated with major malformations (7.4% vs 4.3%; pooled aOR 1.71, 95% CI 1.07–2.72) [[MunkOlsen2018]].
- In that meta-analysis, major cardiac malformations did not differ significantly (2.1% vs 1.6%; aOR 1.54, 95% CI 0.64–3.70) [[MunkOlsen2018]].
- Neonatal readmission within 28 days was more common after lithium exposure (27.5% vs 14.3%; aOR 1.62, 95% CI 1.12–2.33). Lithium was not associated with any of the predefined pregnancy or delivery complications [[MunkOlsen2018]].
- The Lithobid label states that lithium "may cause fetal harm" and that birth registry data suggest more cardiac and other anomalies, "especially Ebstein's anomaly" [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- NICE CG192 says not to offer lithium to women planning a pregnancy or pregnant "unless antipsychotic medication has not been effective". If it is offered, the woman should know that the size of the fetal heart risk is uncertain, and levels should be monitored more often [[NICE2014CG192]].
- If lithium is continued in pregnancy, NICE CG192 advises checking plasma lithium levels every 4 weeks, then weekly from the 36th week. These are guideline wording, not instructions [[NICE2014CG192]].
- NICE CG192 also lists options for a woman who becomes pregnant while taking lithium: stopping gradually over 4 weeks if she is well, switching gradually to an antipsychotic, or continuing lithium if she is at high risk of relapse. It warns that "there is a risk of relapse, particularly in the postnatal period" [[NICE2014CG192]].

### pregnancy · Valproate, carbamazepine and lamotrigine
- In the EURAP registry (7,355 monotherapy pregnancies), major congenital malformations occurred in 10.3% with valproate, 5.5% with carbamazepine, 2.9% with lamotrigine and 2.8% with levetiracetam [[Tomson2018]].
- In EURAP, malformation risk rose with dose at conception for valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine and phenobarbital [[Tomson2018]].
- In the NEAD study, mean IQ at age 6 was 97 (95% CI 94–101) after fetal valproate exposure, compared with 105 after carbamazepine, 108 after lamotrigine and 108 after phenytoin [[Meador2013NEAD]].
- In NEAD, higher valproate doses were associated with lower IQ (r = −0.56), and the effect spanned verbal, memory and executive domains [[Meador2013NEAD]].
- The Depakote boxed warning (Fetal Risk) says valproate "can cause major congenital malformations, particularly neural tube defects", decreased IQ and neurodevelopmental disorders. It "should not be used to treat women with epilepsy or bipolar disorder who are pregnant or who plan to become pregnant unless other medications have failed" [[FDA-Depakote]].
- NICE CG192 says not to offer valproate for a mental health problem to women or girls of childbearing potential "unless other options are ineffective or not tolerated and the pregnancy prevention programme is in place" [[NICE2014CG192]].
- NICE CG192 also says not to offer carbamazepine to women planning a pregnancy, pregnant or considering breastfeeding, and to check lamotrigine levels often around pregnancy "because they vary substantially at these times" [[NICE2014CG192]].
- The US lamotrigine label states that pregnancy registries and epidemiological studies "have not detected an increased frequency of major congenital malformations" with lamotrigine compared with the general population [[FDA-Lamictal]].

### pregnancy · Breastfeeding (label and guideline statements)
- The Lithobid label says lithium is excreted in human milk and that nursing "should not be undertaken during lithium therapy except in rare and unusual circumstances". It lists infant toxicity signs that have been reported (hypertonia, hypothermia, cyanosis, ECG changes) [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- In 10 mother–infant pairs, average trough lithium was 0.76 mEq/L in maternal serum, 0.35 in breast milk and 0.16 in infant serum, each about half the one before. No serious infant adverse events were seen [[Viguera2007Breastmilk]].
- NICE CG192 says to encourage breastfeeding unless the woman is taking carbamazepine, clozapine or lithium, and to support each woman's own feeding choice [[NICE2014CG192]].
- The Depakote label reports valproate in milk at 1%–10% of maternal serum levels and advises monitoring breastfed infants for signs of liver damage [[FDA-Depakote]].
- The lamotrigine label warns that infant levels can rise if the maternal dose raised in pregnancy is not reduced after delivery. It reports infant events (rash, apnea, drowsiness, poor sucking, poor weight gain) for which causality is unknown [[FDA-Lamictal]].
- The Vraylar (cariprazine) label states that no lactation studies have been conducted in humans [[FDA-Vraylar]].

### pregnancy · Children and adolescents
- A meta-analysis of 12 community studies (16,222 youths aged 7–21) found a bipolar disorder rate of 1.8% (95% CI 1.1–3.0). The highest estimates came from broad definitions, and rates did not rise over time [[VanMeter2011]].
- US office visits by youth with a bipolar disorder diagnosis rose from 25 to 1,003 per 100,000 population between 1994–1995 and 2002–2003. Adult visits rose from 905 to 1,679 [[Moreno2007Youth]].
- Most youth bipolar visits were by males (66.5%), whereas most adult visits were by females (67.6%). Youth were far more likely to have a co-diagnosis of ADHD (32.2% vs 3.0%) [[Moreno2007Youth]].
- Severe mood dysregulation was defined to describe children with severe, non-episodic irritability who lack the distinct mood episodes of bipolar disorder [[Leibenluft2011]].
- Long-term studies link non-episodic irritability in youth to later anxiety and unipolar depression, not to bipolar disorder. Youths with severe mood dysregulation also have lower family rates of bipolar disorder [[Leibenluft2011]].
- Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD) is a DSM-5 depressive disorder for children with severe, chronic irritability. It was added in part to address overdiagnosis of bipolar disorder in children [[APA2022,Copeland2013DMDD]].
- In 3 community samples aged 2–17, the 3-month prevalence of DMDD was 0.8%–3.3%, highest in preschoolers. It co-occurred with another disorder 62%–92% of the time [[Copeland2013DMDD]].
- In the COBY study (413 youths aged 7–17 with bipolar spectrum disorders), 81.5% had fully recovered about 2.5 years after onset of the index episode, but 62.5% had a syndromal recurrence 1.5 years later, mostly depression [[Birmaher2009COBY]].
- COBY participants were symptomatic for 60% of follow-up. 25% of youths with bipolar II and 38% with bipolar disorder not otherwise specified converted to bipolar I or II [[Birmaher2009COBY]].
- The Vraylar label now includes acute manic or mixed episodes of bipolar I disorder in patients aged 10 and older. Its safety and effectiveness for bipolar depression in pediatric patients have not been established [[FDA-Vraylar]].
- A phase 3 trial of lumateperone for bipolar I or II depression in pediatric patients is recruiting (estimated 384 participants; primary outcome CDRS-R at week 6; estimated primary completion 2031-10-31) [[NCT06372964]].

### frontier · Genomics and new treatment targets
- A GWAS of 41,917 bipolar disorder cases and 371,549 controls found 64 associated loci. Risk genes were enriched in synaptic signalling and in neurons of the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus (Established) [[Mullins2021]].
- That GWAS found signal enrichment in genes encoding targets of antipsychotics, calcium channel blockers, antiepileptics and anesthetics. It named druggable genes such as HTR6, MCHR1, DCLK3 and FURIN as possible new leads (Hypothesis) [[Mullins2021]].
- A 2025 multi-ancestry GWAS (158,036 cases; 2.8 million controls) found 298 genome-wide significant loci, a fourfold increase. It prioritized 36 credible genes and implicated GABAergic interneurons and medium spiny neurons (Established for loci; Hypothesis for cell-type mechanisms) [[OConnell2025]].
- The genetic architecture differed by how cases were recruited (clinical, community or self-report) and by subtype (bipolar I vs II) [[OConnell2025]].

### frontier · Predicting who will respond to lithium
- ConLiGen's GWAS of lithium response in 2,563 patients found one genome-wide significant locus on chromosome 21, containing two long non-coding RNA genes (Early evidence) [[Hou2016ConLiGen]].
- In an independent prospective sample of 73 patients on lithium alone, carriers of the alleles linked to response had a lower relapse rate (HR 3.8, 95% CI 1.1–13.0, for carriers of the other alleles) (Early evidence) [[Hou2016ConLiGen]].
- In 2,586 lithium-treated patients, a higher polygenic score for schizophrenia was associated with poorer lithium response. The authors cite a non-response rate of up to 30% (Early evidence) [[Amare2018]].
- iPSC-derived hippocampal dentate gyrus-like neurons from people with bipolar disorder showed mitochondrial abnormalities and hyperexcitability [[Mertens2015]].
- Lithium reversed that hyperexcitability only in neurons from patients who had responded to lithium clinically (Early evidence) [[Mertens2015]].

### frontier · Digital phenotyping and smartphone monitoring
- In the MONARCA I trial (78 patients, 6 months), daily smartphone self-monitoring with clinical feedback did not reduce depressive or manic symptoms compared with ordinary smartphone use [[FaurholtJepsen2015]].
- In 28 outpatients followed for 12 weeks, voice features from everyday phone calls classified manic or mixed states with an AUC of 0.89 and depressive states with an AUC of 0.78 (Early evidence) [[FaurholtJepsen2016]].
- A 2026 systematic review found 57 studies of passive digital markers. Most had 50 participants or fewer (60%) and lasted no more than a year (81%) [[Kutcher2026]].
- That review found depression linked to less movement and social contact, later or more variable sleep and less daytime light. Mania and hypomania were linked to more, and more variable, activity, shorter sleep and more communication (Early evidence) [[Kutcher2026]].
- A 2026 review of machine learning reported AUCs of 0.75–0.85 for smartphone-sensor models predicting manic or depressive episodes 1–2 weeks ahead. It called for larger prospective studies with external validation (Early evidence) [[Fang2026ML]].
- In the 12-month BipoSense study (28 patients; 26 depressive and 20 hypomanic or manic emerging episodes), passive sensing did not reliably detect emerging episodes. Self-rated mood did better, and false alarms were too many for clinical use [[Ludwig2025]].

### frontier · Ketamine and esketamine in bipolar depression
- In 18 people with treatment-resistant bipolar depression on lithium or valproate, a single ketamine infusion (0.5 mg/kg) improved depression within 40 minutes compared with placebo. The benefit lasted through day 3 [[Diazgranados2010]].
- In that trial, 71% responded to ketamine and 6% to placebo at some point. One participant on ketamine and one on placebo developed manic symptoms [[Diazgranados2010]].
- A replication in 15 people found 79% response with ketamine vs 0% with placebo. Suicidal ideation also improved within 40 minutes (d = 0.98) (Early evidence) [[Zarate2012Ketamine]].
- A network meta-analysis of 69 trials (8,007 participants) of add-on treatments for bipolar depression found the largest response ratio for IV racemic ketamine (RR 12.49, 95% CI 3.06–50.93). The interval is wide because the trials were few and small [[Bahji2021NMA]].
- A 2026 living umbrella review (2,510 graded meta-analyses) lists ketamine augmentation among interventions effective for adult bipolar depression [[DePrisco2026]].
- A phase 2 trial of inhaled esketamine in treatment-resistant bipolar depression (88 participants) was completed in 2021. No results have been posted (Hypothesis) [[NCT03965871]].

### frontier · Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant add-on treatments
- A 2016 meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (n = 312: NSAIDs, omega-3, N-acetylcysteine, pioglitazone) found a moderate antidepressant effect in bipolar depression (SMD −0.40, 95% CI −0.14 to −0.65) with no mood switches. The trials were few and small (Early evidence) [[Rosenblat2016]].
- In a 12-week, 2 × 2 factorial trial of 266 adults with bipolar depression in Pakistan, neither minocycline nor celecoxib beat placebo on HAMD-17 [[Husain2020,NCT02703363]].
- In that trial, the adjusted difference for minocycline was 1.48 (95% CI −0.41 to 3.36) and for celecoxib −0.74 (−2.61 to 1.14). The authors say it "casts doubt" on anti-inflammatory add-ons for acute bipolar depression [[Husain2020]].
- In a 24-week RCT (n = 75), N-acetylcysteine 1 g twice daily added to usual treatment improved MADRS scores (difference −8.05, 95% CI −13.16 to −2.95). The benefit was lost after washout, and time to a mood episode was unchanged [[Berk2008NAC]].
- In a maintenance RCT (n = 149), N-acetylcysteine 2 g/day did not differ from placebo on recurrence or symptoms after an 8-week open-label phase [[Berk2012NAC]].
- In the A-Bipolar RCT (250 people newly diagnosed with bipolar disorder), low-dose aspirin 150 mg did not reduce mood instability at 6 months (difference 0.022, 95% CI −0.056 to 0.1) or any secondary outcome [[Bruun2026,NCT05035316]].

### frontier · Chronotherapy and light
- In 60 inpatients with bipolar I depression, one week of repeated total sleep deprivation plus light therapy (with antidepressants and lithium) brought response in 70% of people without and 44% of people with a history of drug resistance [[Benedetti2005]].
- At 9 months, 57% of responders without drug resistance and 17% of responders with drug resistance were still euthymic [[Benedetti2005]].
- In 141 inpatients with bipolar depression (83% drug-resistant), 3 sleep deprivation cycles plus morning light and lithium gave a 50% fall in HDRS in 70% within 1 week. The response persisted at 1 month in 55%, and suicide scores fell after the first cycle (Early evidence) [[Benedetti2014]].
- In a 6-week RCT (46 adults with bipolar depression on antimanic medication), midday 7,000-lux bright light gave higher remission than 50-lux dim red light (68.2% vs 22.2%; adjusted OR 12.6), with no mood polarity switches [[Sit2018]].
- A meta-analysis of 7 RCTs (259 patients) found adjunctive light therapy improved HAMD scores (SMD 0.43, 95% CI 0.04–0.82) and response (OR 2.32). Remission and affective switches did not differ (switch OR 1.30, 95% CI 0.38–4.44) (Early evidence) [[Lam2020Light]].
- In a small RCT in mania (23 analysed), blue-blocking glasses worn 6 p.m. to 8 a.m. for 7 days cut YMRS scores by 14.1 points vs 1.7 with clear glasses (Cohen's d 1.86) (Early evidence) [[Henriksen2016]].
- Across two cohorts at one centre (1,241 admissions, 1994–1998 and 2015–2024), chronotherapy was associated with shorter hospital stays for bipolar depression. The study was observational, so it cannot show causation [[Dallaspezia2026]].

### frontier · Newer antipsychotics and maintenance trials
- In a 6-week phase 3 trial (377 patients with bipolar I or II depression), lumateperone 42 mg/day improved MADRS by 4.6 points more than placebo (effect size −0.56), with minimal weight or metabolic change [[Calabrese2021Lumateperone]].
- The registry results for that trial (NCT03249376) report least-squares mean MADRS change of −16.70 with lumateperone vs −12.12 with placebo (difference −4.6, 95% CI −6.34 to −2.83) [[NCT03249376]].
- The Caplyta label lists depressive episodes of bipolar I or II disorder in adults, as monotherapy and as an add-on to lithium or valproate [[FDA-Caplyta]].
- In a randomized-withdrawal trial of cariprazine for relapse prevention in bipolar I disorder (901 enrolled), time to first relapse did not differ from placebo for 1.5 mg (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.48–1.43) or 3.0 mg (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.52–1.51). These are registry-posted results (Early evidence) [[NCT03573297]].
- The Vraylar label lists acute mania or mixed episodes (adults and patients 10 and older) and bipolar I depression in adults. Maintenance is not a labelled indication [[FDA-Vraylar]].

### Exam hooks
- Over about 13 years, people with bipolar I disorder had symptoms in about half of all weeks, mostly depressive (31.9%) rather than manic (8.9%) [[Judd2002]].
- In bipolar II disorder, depressive symptoms filled 50.3% of weeks and hypomania only 1.3% [[Judd2003]].
- STEP-BD: 48.5% of recovered patients had a recurrence within 2 years, depressive recurrences more than twice as often as manic ones [[Perlis2006STEPBD]].
- After a first manic hospitalization, syndromal recovery is almost universal (98%) but functional recovery is only 43% [[Tohen2003McLean]].
- Rapid cycling means 4 or more episodes a year. It affects about 16% of clinic patients and is a little more common in women and in bipolar II [[Kupka2003]].
- Nearly half (45%) of people with bipolar disorder have an anxiety disorder in their lifetime [[Pavlova2015]].
- Alcohol use disorder is the most common substance use disorder in clinical bipolar samples (42%) [[Hunt2016Clinical]].
- About 1 in 6 adults with bipolar disorder (17%) also has ADHD [[Sandstrom2021]].
- About 37% of people with bipolar disorder have metabolic syndrome, roughly twice the general-population odds [[Vancampfort2013]].
- All-cause mortality is about twice that of the general population (SMR 2.05), and natural causes such as heart disease contribute, not only unnatural deaths [[Hayes2015SMR,Biazus2023]].
- Postpartum relapse in bipolar disorder: about 66% without preventive medication in pregnancy vs 23% with it [[Wesseloo2016]].
- Postpartum psychosis affects about 1–2 per 1,000 births (reported range 0.89–2.6), and peak onset is in the first weeks after delivery [[VanderKruik2017,MunkOlsen2006]].
- First-trimester lithium: cardiac malformations 2.41% vs 1.15% (aRR 1.65), and the risk is dose-dependent [[Patorno2017]].
- Valproate has the highest malformation rate of the common mood-stabilizing anticonvulsants (10.3% in EURAP) and lowers child IQ in a dose-dependent way [[Tomson2018,Meador2013NEAD]].
- DMDD was added in DSM-5 for children with chronic severe irritability, partly in response to the rise in childhood bipolar diagnoses [[APA2022,Copeland2013DMDD,Moreno2007Youth]].
- Methylphenidate without a mood stabilizer was followed by a higher rate of mania in bipolar disorder (HR 6.7), but not with one (HR 0.6) [[Viktorin2017]].
- A single ketamine infusion can lift bipolar depression within 40 minutes, but the effect fades within days [[Diazgranados2010]].
- Lithium reversed the hyperexcitability of iPSC-derived neurons only in cells from patients who had responded to lithium [[Mertens2015]].

### In plain words
- Bipolar disorder usually comes and goes over many years. Low moods and milder symptoms take up much more time than highs [[Judd2002,Judd2003]].
- After an episode ends, it is common for another one to come within a couple of years. Staying in care and treating leftover symptoms helps lower that risk [[Perlis2006STEPBD]].
- Feeling better and getting back to work or school do not always happen at the same time. Getting back to daily life can take longer [[Tohen2003McLean]].
- Many people with bipolar disorder also live with anxiety, alcohol or drug problems, ADHD or migraine. These can be treated too, so tell your care team about them [[Pavlova2015,Hunt2016Clinical,Sandstrom2021,Fornaro2015]].
- People with bipolar disorder are more likely to develop heart and metabolism problems. Regular check-ups of weight, blood sugar, cholesterol and blood pressure are part of good care [[Vancampfort2013,Biazus2023]].
- If you might become pregnant, plan ahead with your care team. Stopping medicine suddenly can bring an episode back, and some medicines carry more risk to a baby than others [[Viguera2007Pregnancy,Wesseloo2016,NICE2014CG192]].
- The weeks after giving birth are a time of extra risk. A plan made before the birth can protect both parent and baby [[Wesseloo2016,DiFlorio2013,Bergink2012]].
- Postpartum psychosis is rare, but it is an emergency. Sudden confusion, strange beliefs or not sleeping at all after giving birth need urgent help [[VanderKruik2017,MunkOlsen2006,Jones2014Lancet]].
- Valproate is generally avoided in anyone who could become pregnant, unless nothing else works and a pregnancy prevention plan is in place [[FDA-Depakote,NICE2014CG192]].
- Strong anger outbursts and constant irritability in a child do not by themselves mean bipolar disorder. Doctors look for clear, separate mood episodes [[Leibenluft2011,Copeland2013DMDD]].
- Older adults with bipolar disorder can do well with treatment. It helps to watch memory and physical health over time [[Young2017GERIBD,Sajatovic2015]].
- Scientists are testing apps, light therapy, gene tests and fast-acting medicines. Most are still being studied and are not yet routine care [[Kutcher2026,Hou2016ConLiGen,Lam2020Light,Diazgranados2010]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Lithium in pregnancy: large cohorts now put the first-trimester cardiac risk at about 1.65 times baseline and dose-dependent, smaller than older estimates [[Patorno2017,MunkOlsen2018]]. NICE CG192 still says not to offer lithium in pregnancy unless antipsychotics have not worked [[NICE2014CG192]]. Meanwhile, stopping mood stabilizers carries high relapse risk [[Viguera2007Pregnancy,Wesseloo2016]]. Status: individual decision with the care team. No trial compares strategies.
- Lithium and breastfeeding: the US label and NICE advise against breastfeeding on lithium [[FDA-Lithobid,NICE2014CG192]]. A small 10-pair study found low infant levels and no serious events, and urged reassessment [[Viguera2007Breastmilk]]. Status: unresolved; small data.
- Pediatric bipolar disorder: office diagnoses in US youth rose about 40-fold from 1994 to 2003 [[Moreno2007Youth]]. Community prevalence was 1.8% and did not rise over time [[VanMeter2011]]. Non-episodic irritability predicts anxiety and depression, not bipolar disorder [[Leibenluft2011]], which led to DMDD [[Copeland2013DMDD]]. Status: diagnostic boundaries still debated.
- Does bipolar disorder "progress"? Recurrence rates rise with episode number in register data [[Kessing1998]], and episode count predicts dementia [[Velosa2020]]. But cognitive deficits in euthymia may be smaller than once thought and not clearly drug-related [[Bourne2013]]. Status: open (Hypothesis).
- Does lithium protect against dementia? Observational data are consistent [[Kessing2008Dementia,Velosa2020]], but confounding cannot be excluded. Status: Early evidence; no RCT.
- Anti-inflammatory adjuncts: a small-trial meta-analysis suggested benefit [[Rosenblat2016]]. Larger RCTs of minocycline, celecoxib and low-dose aspirin were negative [[Husain2020,Bruun2026]], and NAC findings are mixed [[Berk2008NAC,Berk2012NAC]]. Status: not supported for routine use.
- Digital phenotyping: classification studies report AUCs of 0.78–0.89 [[FaurholtJepsen2016,Fang2026ML]], but a randomized self-monitoring trial and a 12-month prospective passive-sensing study found no clinical benefit or reliable early warning [[FaurholtJepsen2015,Ludwig2025]]. Status: Early evidence; external validation needed.
- Ketamine: effects in bipolar depression are rapid but short-lived, from small crossover trials [[Diazgranados2010,Zarate2012Ketamine,Bahji2021NMA]]. Inhaled esketamine trial results are unpublished [[NCT03965871]]. Status: Early evidence; maintenance and mood-switch safety need larger trials.
- Antidepressants and rapid cycling: 46% of rapid cycling followed antidepressant treatment, but causality is unproven [[Kupka2003]]. Status: Hypothesis.
- Cariprazine maintenance: the registry-posted relapse-prevention trial was negative for both doses [[NCT03573297]]. Maintenance is not a labelled indication [[FDA-Vraylar]]. Status: not established.
