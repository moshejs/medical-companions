# G · Psychological care, suicide safety, living well, families & nursing

Pass date: 2026-11-03. Modules served: therapy, crisis, living, families, nursing.

This pass covers the adjunctive psychological treatments for bipolar disorder (trials and meta-analyses), suicide risk and prevention in a safe-messaging frame, self-management and physical health, families and carers, and nursing care (acute mania, observation, lithium teaching, metabolic monitoring, discharge). Trial and review numbers come from PubMed abstracts (efetch) read in full. Guideline wording comes from the NICE recommendation pages (CG185, last updated 2 September 2025; NG10, published 28 May 2015) read directly on nice.org.uk on 2026-11-03, with each guideline's full NCCMH version validated as a PubMed record. Lithium label wording comes from the current Lithobid label in openFDA (effective 2026-08-24).

## Findings

### therapy · Why psychological care is added to medicines
- NICE CG185 says that to prevent relapse, people should be offered "a structured psychological intervention (individual, group or family), which has been designed for bipolar disorder and has a published evidence-based manual", in addition to medicines (recommendation 1.7.3) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 says relapse-prevention programmes should give information about bipolar disorder, include self-monitoring of mood, thoughts and behaviour, address relapse risk and functioning, and "develop plans for relapse management and staying well" (recommendation 1.7.4) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 says people with bipolar depression should be offered a psychological intervention developed for bipolar disorder, or a choice of CBT, interpersonal therapy or behavioural couples therapy (recommendation 1.2.5) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- The CANMAT/ISBD 2018 guidelines give first-, second- and third-line recommendations for pharmacological and psychological treatments, and also cover comorbid substance use and metabolic disorders and safety monitoring [[Yatham2018]].
- The British Association for Psychopharmacology guideline (third edition, 2016) presents medicines "integrated with a coherent approach to psychoeducation and behaviour change" [[Goodwin2016BAP]].
- A Lancet seminar states that treating medical and psychiatric comorbidity, adding psychosocial treatments and involving caregivers "have been shown to improve health outcomes" for people with bipolar disorders [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].

### therapy · Group psychoeducation (Barcelona programme)
- Design: 120 outpatients with bipolar I or II, in remission for at least 6 months and on standard medication, were randomised to 21 sessions of group psychoeducation or 21 non-structured group meetings, then followed for 2 years [[Colom2003]].
- At 2 years, psychoeducation reduced the number of people who relapsed and the number of recurrences per person. It also lengthened the time to depressive, manic, hypomanic and mixed recurrences and reduced the number and length of hospital stays [[Colom2003]].
- 5-year follow-up (99 of 120 completed): mean recurrences were 3.86 with psychoeducation vs 8.37 in the control group (P<0.0001) [[Colom2009]].
- 5-year follow-up: total time acutely ill was 154 vs 586 days (P=0.0001), and time to any recurrence was longer with psychoeducation (log rank 9.953, P<0.002) [[Colom2009]].
- The authors describe group psychoeducation as "the first psychological intervention showing such a long-term maintained efficacy" in bipolar disorder (Early evidence: single-centre trial) [[Colom2009]].
- Cost-benefit analysis of the same trial: inpatient care made up 40% of estimated total cost in the control group but about 15% in the psychoeducation group. Over 5 years, psychoeducation was "less costly and more effective" [[Scott2009Cost]].
- In a lithium-specific programme, 50 euthymic people taking lithium improved their knowledge of lithium after brief hospital-based psychoeducation, and the gain lasted to 24 months. Hospitalisation showed only a trend towards falling (uncontrolled before-after study) [[Even2010]].

### therapy · Psychoeducation vs peer support, and timing (PARADES)
- PARADES (England, 8 sites) randomised 304 people with remitted bipolar disorder to 21 weekly 2-hour sessions of structured group psychoeducation (n=153) or optimised unstructured peer support (n=151) [[Morriss2016PARADES]].
- At 96 weeks, 58% (89/153) in the psychoeducation group vs 65% (98/151) in the peer-support group had a new bipolar episode. Time to the next episode did not differ (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.62–1.11) [[Morriss2016PARADES]].
- Psychoeducation helped most in people with 1–7 previous episodes (HR 0.28, 95% CI 0.12–0.68). It was also more acceptable: median attendance was 14 vs 9 sessions [[Morriss2016PARADES]].
- Number of previous episodes also changed the effect of CBT in a separate trial (below). Together these suggest structured therapy may work best early in the course of the illness (Early evidence) [[Morriss2016PARADES,Scott2006CBT]].

### therapy · Family-focused therapy (FFT)
- Miklowitz 2003: 101 people recovering from a manic, mixed or depressive episode were randomised to 21 sessions of FFT over 9 months (psychoeducation, communication training and problem-solving) or to crisis management, all with medication, and followed for 2 years [[Miklowitz2003FFT]].
- Over 2 years, 35% (11/31) relapsed with FFT vs 54% (38/70) with crisis management. Mean time without relapse was 73.5 vs 53.2 weeks (HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.20–0.75; intention to treat). FFT also improved medication adherence [[Miklowitz2003FFT]].
- Adolescents (FFT-A, 58 teenagers with bipolar I, II or NOS): FFT-A did not change recovery or recurrence rates, but teenagers recovered from depressive symptoms faster (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.04–3.29) and spent fewer weeks depressed over 2 years [[Miklowitz2008FFTA]].
- High-risk youth (127 people aged 9–17 with mood symptoms and a first- or second-degree relative with bipolar I or II): 12 sessions of FFT lengthened the time from recovery to the next mood episode compared with enhanced care (HR 0.55) [[Miklowitz2020FFT]].
- In that trial FFT delayed depressive episodes (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.31–0.88) but not manic or hypomanic episodes or conversion to bipolar disorder (Early evidence) [[Miklowitz2020FFT]].
- Children aged 8–12 with mood disorders (70% bipolar spectrum): eight 90-minute multifamily psychoeducation sessions plus usual care lowered mood severity scores compared with a wait-list (P=.03) [[Fristad2009]].

### therapy · STEP-BD psychosocial trial (intensive therapy vs collaborative care)
- Design: 293 outpatients with bipolar I or II depression on protocol medication, at 15 STEP-BD sites, were randomised to intensive psychotherapy (n=163; up to 30 sessions in 9 months of FFT, IPSRT or CBT) or collaborative care (n=130; 3 sessions in 6 weeks) [[Miklowitz2007]].
- Year-end recovery rates were 64.4% with intensive psychotherapy vs 51.5% with collaborative care [[Miklowitz2007]].
- Intensive therapy shortened the time to recovery (HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.08–2.00; P=.01) [[Miklowitz2007]].
- People in intensive therapy were 1.58 times (95% CI 1.17–2.13) more likely to be "clinically well" in any study month. The three intensive therapies did not differ from one another [[Miklowitz2007]].
- Drop-out was similar: 35.6% for intensive therapy vs 30.8% for collaborative care [[Miklowitz2007]].
- In a 152-patient subsample, intensive therapy gave better total functioning, relationship functioning and life satisfaction over 9 months, but no improvement in work or role functioning [[Miklowitz2007AJP]].

### therapy · Interpersonal and social rhythm therapy (IPSRT)
- IPSRT adds a social-rhythm component (regular daily routines) to interpersonal psychotherapy. The trial randomised 175 acutely ill people with bipolar I to IPSRT or intensive clinical management, with a 2-year maintenance phase [[Frank2005IPSRT]].
- IPSRT did not shorten the time to stabilisation. But people given IPSRT in the acute phase went longer without a new episode (P=.01), whatever their maintenance treatment [[Frank2005IPSRT]].
- IPSRT made social rhythms more regular by the end of acute treatment (P<.001). People who increased their regularity were less likely to have a recurrence (P=.05) (Early evidence for regular routines as a mechanism) [[Frank2005IPSRT]].

### therapy · Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT): mixed results
- Lam 2003: 103 people with bipolar I and frequent relapses were randomised to about 14 sessions of cognitive therapy (plus 2 booster sessions) or to usual care. Over 12 months the CT group had fewer episodes, fewer days in episode and fewer admissions, and better social functioning [[Lam2003CT]].
- Scott 2006 was a pragmatic multicentre trial in 253 people. More than half had a recurrence by 18 months, with no difference between CBT and usual care (HR 1.05, 95% CI 0.74–1.50) [[Scott2006CBT]].
- In a post hoc analysis of Scott 2006, CBT helped people with fewer than 12 previous episodes but was less effective in those with more (interaction P=0.04) [[Scott2006CBT]].
- Insomnia-focused CBT (CBTI-BP, pilot RCT, n=58 people with bipolar I between episodes): over 6 months, days in a mood episode were 3.3 with CBTI-BP vs 25.5 with psychoeducation (Early evidence) [[Harvey2015CBTI]].
- In the same pilot, hypomania/mania relapse was 4.6% with CBTI-BP vs 31.6% with psychoeducation, and overall relapse was 13.6% vs 42.1% (marginal) [[Harvey2015CBTI]].

### therapy · Meta-analyses and network meta-analyses
- Oud 2016 (55 RCTs, 6010 participants): individual psychological interventions reduced relapse at the end of treatment (RR 0.66, 95% CI 0.48–0.92) and at follow-up (RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.63–0.87) (moderate-quality evidence) [[Oud2016]].
- Oud 2016: collaborative care reduced hospital admissions (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.49–0.94). Low-quality evidence linked group interventions to fewer depressive relapses and family psychoeducation to lower depression and mania symptoms [[Oud2016]].
- Miklowitz 2021 network meta-analysis (39 RCTs, 3863 participants): manualised psychotherapy plus medication had lower recurrence than control treatments (OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.43–0.74; 20 two-group trials) [[Miklowitz2021NMA]].
- Miklowitz 2021: psychoeducation with guided practice of illness-management skills in a family or group format reduced recurrences compared with the same strategies delivered individually (OR 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.94) [[Miklowitz2021NMA]].
- Miklowitz 2021: CBT stabilised depressive symptoms compared with treatment as usual (SMD −0.32, 95% CI −0.64 to −0.01). Family or conjoint therapy (SMD −0.46) and interpersonal therapy (SMD −0.46) pointed the same way, but their confidence intervals crossed zero [[Miklowitz2021NMA]].
- Miklowitz 2021: people were more likely to stay in treatment with family or conjoint therapy (OR 0.46, 95% CI 0.26–0.82) and brief psychoeducation (OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.23–0.85) than with standard psychoeducation [[Miklowitz2021NMA]].
- Chatterton 2017 network meta-analysis: only carer-focused interventions significantly reduced manic or depressive relapse. Psychoeducation, alone or with CBT, reduced non-adherence to medication, and no intervention significantly reduced depression scale scores [[Chatterton2017]].

### therapy · Collaborative and systematic care
- Simon 2006 (441 people; nurse care managers delivering group psychoeducation, monthly phone monitoring and feedback to clinicians over 2 years): time with significant mania symptoms fell (19.2 vs 24.7 weeks; P=.01) [[Simon2006Care]].
- In Simon 2006, time with depressive symptoms did not change (47.6 vs 50.7 weeks; P=.45). The adjusted extra cost was $1251 per person (95% CI $55–$2446) [[Simon2006Care]].
- Bauer 2006 (VA, 11 hospitals, n=306, 3 years): a collaborative chronic care model with group psychoeducation and nurse care coordinators cut time in affective episodes by a net 6.2 weeks, mainly mania. It was cost-neutral, and most benefit came in years 2–3 [[Bauer2006CCM]].
- Kessing 2013 (n=158, after one of a person's first three admissions): treatment in a specialised mood disorder clinic reduced readmission compared with standard care (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.37–0.97) [[Kessing2013Clinic]].

### therapy · Early-warning-sign training and prodromes
- Perry 1999 (n=69, all with a relapse in the past 12 months): 7–12 sessions teaching people to spot early symptoms and get prompt treatment lengthened the time to first manic relapse. At the 25th centile it was 65 weeks vs 17 weeks [[Perry1999BMJ]].
- The same training cut manic relapses over 18 months (median difference 30%, 95% CI 8–52%) and improved social functioning and employment. It had no effect on depressive relapse [[Perry1999BMJ]].
- Jackson 2003 systematic review (17 studies, 1191 people): at least 80% of people with a mood disorder can identify one or more prodromal (early warning) symptoms [[Jackson2003Prodromes]].
- The most robust early sign of mania is sleep disturbance (median prevalence 77%). Manic prodromes lasted more than 20 days on average and depressive prodromes less than 19 days, with wide variation (2–365 days for depression; 1–120 days for mania) [[Jackson2003Prodromes]].
- Lam 2001 (n=40, 18 months): people reported prodromes reliably. A quarter had difficulty detecting depressive prodromes. Using behavioural coping strategies at the prodromal stage was linked with fewer manic and depressive relapses [[Lam2001Prodromes]].
- A Cochrane review protocol (2025) is evaluating early-warning-sign interventions on time to recurrence and hospitalisation. Its results are not yet published [[Gupta2025Cochrane]].

### therapy · Online, mobile and peer support (evidence labels)
- Online psychoeducation (Proudfoot 2012, n=407): all three arms improved, with no significant differences between them. Adding online peer support raised adherence to the programme (Early evidence) [[Proudfoot2012]].
- MoodSwings 2.0 (n=304, three arms): the forum-plus-modules group had fewer depression symptoms than forum-only peer support (P=.05; Cohen's d 0.17–0.43) (Early evidence) [[Gliddon2019]].
- MONARCA I (n=78, 6 months): daily smartphone self-monitoring with clinical feedback did not reduce depressive or manic symptoms compared with a control smartphone. There was a non-significant trend towards more sustained depressive symptoms (B=2.02, 95% CI −0.13 to 4.17) (Early evidence: no benefit shown) [[FaurholtJepsen2015]].
- Peer support for severe mental illness (18 RCTs, 5597 participants): little or no evidence of effects on hospitalisation, symptoms or satisfaction. There was some evidence of benefit for hope, recovery and empowerment (Early evidence) [[LloydEvans2014]].

### crisis · How common suicide is, as population figures
- The ISBD Task Force's pooled suicide rate in bipolar disorder is 164 per 100,000 person-years (95% CI 5–324), with a male:female ratio of 1.7:1 (population statistic) [[Schaffer2015Epi]].
- Epidemiological studies report that 23–26% of people with bipolar disorder attempt suicide, with higher rates in clinical samples [[Schaffer2015Epi]].
- Systematic reviews estimate the suicide rate in bipolar disorder at about 20–30 times that of the general population [[Plans2019,Miller2020Suicide]].
- Meta-analysis of premature mortality: the suicide standardised mortality ratio (SMR) is 14.44 (95% CI 12.43–16.78), and the all-cause SMR is 2.05 (95% CI 1.89–2.23) [[Hayes2015Mortality]].
- Danish national cohort (up to 36 years after first contact): absolute suicide risk was 7.77% (95% CI 6.01–10.05%) for men and 4.78% (95% CI 3.48–6.56%) for women with bipolar disorder, vs 0.72% and 0.26% in men and women without a psychiatric diagnosis (population statistics) [[Nordentoft2011]].
- These figures also mean that most people with bipolar disorder (more than 92% of men and 95% of women in the Danish cohort) did not die by suicide over follow-up. Suicide risk is serious but not inevitable [[Nordentoft2011]].
- Bipolar II: in 15 retrospective studies, lifetime suicide attempts were 32.4% in bipolar II vs 36.3% in bipolar I (OR 1.21, 95% CI 0.98–1.48; not significant). Bipolar II is not a "milder" condition for suicide risk [[Novick2010]].
- Mood disorders including bipolar disorder are among the conditions with the highest suicide risk in a meta-review of 20 mental disorders [[Chesney2014]].

### crisis · When risk is highest: depressive and mixed states
- Jorvi Bipolar Study (176 people, 18 months, life charts): compared with other phases, suicide attempts were 37-fold more frequent during mixed states and 18-fold more frequent during major depressive phases [[Valtonen2008]].
- In that study, being in a mixed or depressive phase, plus prior attempts, accounted for a population-attributable fraction of 86% [[Valtonen2008]].
- Compared with full remission, suicide attempts were 5-fold more frequent in subthreshold depression, 25-fold in major depressive episodes and 65-fold in bipolar mixed states [[Holma2014]].
- People with bipolar disorder spent 35% of follow-up time in major depressive episodes, 39% in subthreshold depression and 4.6% in mixed episodes. Their higher attempt rate than in major depressive disorder came mostly from more time in high-risk states [[Holma2014]].
- Suicide is associated with depressed or mixed states, not with mania [[Miller2020Suicide]].
- Mixed features in either depressive or (hypo)manic phases are associated with "major increases of risk of suicidal behaviors" [[Tondo2020]].
- Teaching implication: shortening time spent in depressive and mixed phases is a prevention target ("diminishing the time spent in high-risk phases is crucial") [[Holma2014,Valtonen2008]].

### crisis · Risk factors and protective factors
- Hawton 2005 meta-analysis: the main risk factors for suicide death in bipolar disorder were a previous suicide attempt and hopelessness [[Hawton2005Bipolar]].
- Hawton 2005: risk factors for non-fatal suicidal behaviour included:
  - family history of suicide
  - early onset
  - more severe depressive symptoms and episodes
  - mixed states and rapid cycling
  - comorbid disorders, and alcohol or drug misuse [[Hawton2005Bipolar]]
- The ISBD Task Force found 141 studies on 20 factors, including sex, age, polarity of first and current episode, predominant polarity, psychosis, comorbidity, family history, past attempts, early-life trauma and psychosocial precipitants [[Schaffer2015Risk]].
- Demographic and clinical risk factors listed in a 2020 review include male gender, living alone, being divorced, unemployment, younger (<35) or older (>75) age, personal or family history of attempts, and predominant depressive polarity [[Miller2020Suicide]].
- In the Danish cohort, comorbid substance abuse increased suicide risk, and deliberate self-harm roughly doubled it [[Nordentoft2011]].
- Protective and modifiable factors include good social support (poor support raised hazard), effective treatment of depression, and regular follow-up after an attempt [[Holma2014,Turecki2016Lancet]].
- Lithium is the medicine most consistently linked to lower suicide rates in mood disorders (pointer to pass E) [[Cipriani2013,Miller2020Suicide]].
- Risk scores do not predict individuals well. Pooled odds of suicide in "high-risk" vs "lower-risk" groups were 4.84, with sensitivity 56% and specificity 79%. Over about 63 months, 5.5% of high-risk and 0.9% of lower-risk patients died by suicide [[Large2016]].
- Because prediction is weak, reviews stress care for everyone rather than relying on risk categories. "There is no effective algorithm to predict suicide in clinical practice" [[Turecki2016Lancet,Bolton2015BMJ]].

### crisis · The high-risk period after discharge
- Meta-analysis of 100 studies (17,857 suicides): the pooled suicide rate after psychiatric discharge was 484 per 100,000 person-years (95% CI 422–555) [[Chung2017JAMAPsych]].
- The rate was highest in the first 3 months after discharge (1132 per 100,000 person-years; 95% CI 874–1467) and among people admitted with suicidal ideas or behaviour (2078; 95% CI 1512–2856) [[Chung2017JAMAPsych]].
- By follow-up length, rates were 654 (3 months–1 year), 494 (1–5 years), 366 (5–10 years) and 277 (>10 years) per 100,000 person-years. Risk falls over time but stays raised for years [[Chung2017JAMAPsych]].
- Active contact and follow-up after an emergency department visit for suicidal behaviour reduced repeat attempts within 6 months (pooled RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.31–0.76; 2 trials, n=984) [[Inagaki2019]].
- The WHO brief intervention and contact was associated with lower odds of suicide in 3 RCTs (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.09–0.42; n=2028) [[Riblet2017]].
- A systematic review of 97 RCTs and epidemiological studies concludes: "Active follow-up of patients after discharge or a suicide-related crisis should be routine" [[Mann2021Suicide]].

### crisis · Safety planning
- The Safety Planning Intervention (SPI) is a brief clinical intervention: a written, prioritised list of coping strategies and sources of support the person can use in a crisis [[Stanley2012SPI,Stanley2018ED]].
- The SPI has six steps:
  - recognising warning signs
  - internal coping strategies
  - people and social settings that provide distraction
  - people to ask for help
  - professionals and services to contact
  - making the environment safer (unverified) [[Stanley2012SPI]]
- Emergency department cohort study (1640 veterans; SPI plus at least 2 follow-up calls): suicidal behaviour over 6 months was 3.03% with SPI+ vs 5.29% with usual care (OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.33–0.95). SPI+ more than doubled outpatient attendance (OR 2.06) [[Stanley2018ED]].
- Meta-analysis of 6 studies (3536 participants): safety-planning-type interventions reduced suicidal behaviour (RR 0.570, 95% CI 0.408–0.795; number needed to treat 16). They had no significant effect on suicidal ideation [[Nuij2021]].
- A systematic review of 26 studies found improvements in suicidality, depression, hopelessness, hospitalisation and treatment attendance with the SPI. It was feasible in paper or digital form, with or without a clinician [[Ferguson2022]].

### crisis · Means safety and safe messaging (general principles only)
- Restricting access to lethal means prevents suicide. Reviews rate the evidence for means restriction as having strengthened since 2005 [[Zalsman2016,Mann2021Suicide]].
- Effective treatment of depression, CBT and dialectical behaviour therapy prevent suicidal behaviour. Active screening alone is not proven better than screening for depression [[Mann2021Suicide,Zalsman2016]].
- Media reports of a celebrity's death by suicide were followed by a 13% rise in suicides (rate ratio 1.13, 95% CI 1.08–1.18). This is why guidelines advise responsible reporting [[Niederkrotenthaler2020]].
- Stories of people coping with a crisis without suicidal behaviour were associated with lower suicide rates (the "Papageno effect"). Repeated reporting and suicide myths were associated with higher rates [[Niederkrotenthaler2010]].
- Companion rule from this evidence: give no method detail, stress that help works and recovery happens, and show the crisis line. "If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact local emergency services or a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123)" [[Niederkrotenthaler2010,Niederkrotenthaler2020]].

### living · Sleep and daily rhythm
- Sleep loss can trigger switches into mania, and mania in turn reduces sleep. The proposed result is a self-reinforcing loop, with sleep reduction as a "final common pathway" (Hypothesis, supported by experimental sleep-deprivation data) [[Wehr1987]].
- Between episodes, people with bipolar disorder still show longer sleep onset latency, more waking after sleep onset and more variable sleep-wake patterns than controls (21 studies) [[Ng2015Sleep]].
- Sleep disturbance is the most common early warning sign of mania, reported in a median 77% of studies' participants [[Jackson2003Prodromes]].
- Regularising daily routines (IPSRT) and treating insomnia (CBTI-BP) have both been associated with fewer recurrences (Early evidence) [[Frank2005IPSRT,Harvey2015CBTI]].

### living · Mood charting and life charts
- The prospective NIMH Life Chart Method (NIMH-LCM-p) rates mood and episode severity every day, based on how much mood impairs functioning [[Denicoff2000]].
- In 270 people from the Stanley Foundation Bipolar Network, LCM depression ratings correlated with clinician-rated depression (IDS-C; r=−0.785). LCM mania ratings correlated with the YMRS (r=0.656), and average LCM severity with the GAF (r=−0.732) [[Denicoff2000]].
- Life charts were the tool used to measure time spent in each phase in the Jorvi suicide-risk studies [[Valtonen2008,Holma2014]].
- Self-monitoring of mood, thoughts and behaviour is a required component of NICE-recommended relapse-prevention therapy [[NICE2014CG185]].

### living · Relapse-prevention and crisis plans
- NICE CG185 says to develop a risk management plan jointly with the person and their carer that covers:
  - triggers and early warning signs
  - a protocol for the person's own coping strategies
  - agreements on how services respond
  - who to contact in a crisis (recommendation 1.4.1) [[NICE2014CG185]]
- NICE CG185 says care plans for return to primary care should include a crisis plan "indicating early warning symptoms and triggers of both mania and depression relapse and preferred response during relapse" (recommendation 1.9.4) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- Joint crisis plans (Henderson 2004, n=160 with psychosis or non-psychotic bipolar disorder): compulsory admission or treatment fell to 13% (10/80) vs 27% (21/80) (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.24–0.95) [[Henderson2004BMJ]].
- The larger CRIMSON trial (n=569) found no significant effect of joint crisis plans on compulsory admission (18% vs 20%; OR 0.90, 95% CI 0.58–1.39), possibly because of incomplete implementation [[Thornicroft2013CRIMSON]].
- Peer-worker-facilitated psychiatric advance directives (n=394; 36% with bipolar I) reduced compulsory admissions at 12 months: 27.0% vs 39.9% (risk difference −0.13, 95% CI −0.22 to −0.04) [[Tinland2022]].
- Meta-analysis of 13 RCTs: advance statements reduced compulsory admissions by 23% (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.60–0.98) [[deJong2016]].
- In a UK survey of people with bipolar disorder, 82% (463/565) of those who explained their view endorsed self-binding directives. Most cited a "determinate shift" in thinking and decisions when unwell [[Gergel2021]].
- NICE CG185 advises discussing a lasting power of attorney when mania or hypomania has caused financial problems (recommendation 1.1.5) [[NICE2014CG185]].

### living · Substance use and smoking
- National surveys: among people with bipolar disorder, mean prevalence was 33% for any substance use disorder, 24% for alcohol use disorder and 17% for illicit drug use disorder [[Hunt2016Surveys]].
- People with an alcohol use disorder had 4.1 times the odds of bipolar disorder (OR 4.09, 95% CI 3.37–4.96), and illicit drug use was associated with 4.96 times the odds (95% CI 3.98–6.17) [[Hunt2016Surveys]].
- Clinical samples: alcohol use disorder was 42%, cannabis 20% and other illicit drugs 17%. Comorbid substance use was linked to earlier onset and more hospitalisations [[Hunt2016Clinical]].
- Current smoking is more common in bipolar disorder than in the general population (OR 3.5, 95% CI 3.39–3.54; 51 studies, 16 countries) [[Jackson2015Smoking]].
- Smoking cessation is less common in bipolar disorder than in the general population (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.31–0.37) [[Jackson2015Smoking]].

### living · Physical health and the mortality gap
- Bipolar disorders are associated with a loss of about 10–20 potential years of life, driven mainly by excess cardiovascular deaths and suicide (population statistic) [[McIntyre2020Lancet]].
- Natural-cause SMR is 1.64 (95% CI 1.47–1.83). Circulatory death SMR is 1.73, respiratory 2.92 and infection 2.25 [[Hayes2015Mortality]].
- Metabolic syndrome affects 37.3% (95% CI 36.1–39.0) of people with bipolar disorder (OR 1.98 vs the general population) [[Vancampfort2013]].
- Metabolic syndrome is more common in people taking antipsychotics (45.3%) than in those not taking them (32.4%; OR 1.72) [[Vancampfort2013]].
- Across severe mental illness, metabolic syndrome risk is raised (RR 1.58, 95% CI 1.35–1.86). Risk was higher with clozapine and olanzapine and lower with aripiprazole than with other antipsychotics [[Vancampfort2015]].
- Severe mental illness (including bipolar disorder) carries higher incidence of cardiovascular disease (HR 1.78, 95% CI 1.60–1.98) and higher cardiovascular death (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.53–2.24). Higher incidence was linked to antipsychotic use and BMI [[Correll2017CVD]].
- The American Heart Association classes bipolar disorder in young people as a tier II moderate-risk condition for accelerated atherosclerosis and early cardiovascular disease [[Goldstein2015AHA]].
- NICE CG185 says the annual physical health check should cover:
  - weight or BMI, diet, nutrition and physical activity
  - pulse and blood pressure
  - fasting glucose or HbA1c and lipids
  - liver, renal and thyroid function, and calcium (recommendation 1.2.12) [[NICE2014CG185]]
- NICE CG185 says people taking antipsychotics and long-term medication should be offered a combined healthy-eating and physical-activity programme (recommendation 1.8.2) [[NICE2014CG185]].

### living · Work, study, driving and stigma
- Employment: across 25 studies (4892 people; mean follow-up 4.9 years), about 40–60% of people with established bipolar disorder were employed. Employment was higher early in the illness [[Marwaha2013]].
- 40–50% of people may slide in occupational status over time, which supports early intervention [[Marwaha2013]].
- Predictors of good employment outcomes (22 studies, n=6301) include better verbal memory (r=0.33) and executive function (r=0.26) and more years of education (r=0.23). Predictors of poorer outcomes include more hospitalisations (r=−0.35), depression (r=−0.25) and personality disorder (r=−0.49) [[Tse2014]].
- Functional recovery after an episode lags behind symptomatic recovery. Subsyndromal depression and persistent cognitive problems are the strongest correlates of functional impairment [[Gitlin2017]].
- NICE CG185 recommends:
  - supported employment programmes for people who wish to work (recommendation 1.9.6)
  - with the person's agreement, talking with education staff, creditors and employers about how the person can be supported (recommendation 1.1.4) [[NICE2014CG185]]
- Driving: a review (German-language) states that people with acute mania are not fit to drive, but driving ability can usually be confirmed after remission under psychiatric supervision. About 17% of people with bipolar disorder in remission had impaired driving performance [[Laux2014]].
- The lithium label cautions that lithium "may impair mental and/or physical abilities" and advises care with activities that need alertness, such as driving [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Stigma: 25 studies show a moderate to high degree of internalised (self-)stigma in bipolar disorder. Public stigma findings are inconsistent: bipolar disorder is viewed more positively than schizophrenia and less positively than depression [[Ellison2013Stigma]].
- GAMIAN-Europe (1182 people with bipolar disorder or depression):
  - 21.7% reported moderate or high self-stigma
  - 71.6% reported moderate or high perceived discrimination
  - 59.7% reported moderate or high stigma resistance [[Brohan2011]]

### families · Expressed emotion and the family climate
- Miklowitz 1988 (23 recently manic inpatients): high family expressed emotion (critical, hostile or over-involved attitudes) and negative affective style predicted relapse over 9 months after discharge, independent of medication adherence [[Miklowitz1988EE]].
- Butzlaff and Hooley meta-analysis: expressed emotion robustly predicts relapse. Mean effect sizes for mood disorders were significantly larger than for schizophrenia [[Butzlaff1998]].
- Caregivers of people with bipolar disorder show high levels of expressed emotion. Caregiver burden is linked to caregiver depression, which can add stress at home [[Ogilvie2005]].

### families · Carer burden and support
- STEP-BD Family Experience Study (caregivers of 500 patients): 89%, 52% and 61% of caregivers reported moderate or higher burden from problem behaviours, role dysfunction and disrupted household routine, respectively [[Perlick2007]].
- In that study, high-burden caregivers reported more physical health problems, depressive symptoms and health-risk behaviours, and less social support [[Perlick2007]].
- Caregiver-only group psychoeducation (Reinares 2008; 113 euthymic patients; 12 × 90-minute sessions for caregivers only) reduced the percentage of patients with any recurrence and lengthened relapse-free time. The effect was mainly on hypomanic and manic recurrence [[Reinares2008]].
- FFT for caregivers (n=46 caregivers) reduced caregiver depressive symptoms and health-risk behaviour and was associated with greater falls in patients' depression (Early evidence) [[Perlick2010]].
- REACT online toolkit for relatives (n=800): distress fell in both arms, with no difference vs a resource directory alone (−1.39, 95% CI −3.60 to 0.83). Cost was £62.27 per person [[Lobban2020REACT]].
- NICE CG185 says to give carers written and verbal information on diagnosis, "positive outcomes and recovery", support and getting help in a crisis (recommendation 1.1.6) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 says to offer all carers an education and support programme "with a positive message about recovery" (recommendation 1.1.8) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 says to negotiate early how information will be shared, stressing the importance of sharing information about risk (recommendation 1.1.7) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 says to offer a family intervention to people living with or in close contact with family (recommendation 1.7.2) [[NICE2014CG185]].

### families · Children of parents with bipolar disorder (brief; genetics is in pass B)
- Family high-risk meta-analysis (33 studies): offspring of parents with severe mental illness had a 32% probability (95% CI 24–42%) of developing a severe mental illness by adulthood, about 2.5 times the risk in control offspring (RR 2.52) [[Rasic2014]].
- Dutch Bipolar Offspring Study, 22-year follow-up (n=100): lifetime bipolar disorder was 11–13%, with no new onsets after the 12-year follow-up. Lifetime prevalence of any mood disorder was 65% [[Helmink2025]].
- So most children of a parent with bipolar disorder (about 87–89% in that cohort) did not develop bipolar disorder themselves. Their risk of depression continues into adulthood, which supports early help [[Helmink2025]].
- NICE CG185 asks services to identify children and adults who depend on or care for a person with bipolar disorder and may need support or safeguarding, and to offer psychological and social support (recommendation 1.1.9) [[NICE2014CG185]].

### nursing · Acute mania: environment, safety and de-escalation
- NICE CG185 says to ensure people with mania or hypomania have "access to calming environments and reduced stimulation". It also advises them not to make important decisions until they recover, and encourages them to keep up relationships with carers (recommendation 1.5.1) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- A Dutch three-round Delphi study (professionals, patients and informal caregivers) reached consensus on all 89 statements for a standard of nursing care in acute mania on a closed ward [[Goossens2022]].
- Project BETA de-escalation consensus sets four objectives:
  1. ensure the safety of patient, staff and others
  2. help the patient manage emotions and regain control
  3. avoid restraint when at all possible
  4. avoid coercive interventions that escalate agitation [[Richmond2012]]
- BETA describes a three-step approach: verbally engage, build a collaborative relationship, then verbally de-escalate [[Richmond2012]].
- NICE NG10: use a restrictive intervention only when de-escalation and other preventive strategies have failed, keep attempting de-escalation throughout, and never use restrictive interventions to punish or establish dominance (recommendations 1.4.5–1.4.6) [[NICE-NG10]].
- NICE NG10 advises against routinely using manual restraint for more than 10 minutes (recommendation 1.4.29) [[NICE-NG10]].
- NICE NG10 says that after rapid tranquillisation, staff should monitor pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, level of hydration and level of consciousness at least every hour until there are no further concerns, and every 15 minutes in higher-risk situations (recommendation 1.4.45) [[NICE-NG10]].
- Safewards cluster RCT (31 wards): ten simple staff-patient relationship interventions reduced conflict events by 15% (95% CI 5.7–23.7%) and containment events by 23.2% (95% CI 9.9–35.5%) [[Bowers2015]].

### nursing · Observation levels
- NICE NG10 defines four observation levels:
  - low-level intermittent: once every 30–60 minutes
  - high-level intermittent: once every 15–30 minutes
  - continuous: within eyesight or arm's length of a designated nurse
  - multiprofessional continuous: within eyesight of 2–3 staff and arm's length of at least 1 (recommendation 1.4.11) [[NICE-NG10]]
- NICE NG10 says that at least once each shift, a nurse should set aside dedicated time to assess mental state and engage positively with the person (recommendation 1.4.9). Policies should require multidisciplinary review when observation continues for 1 week or more (recommendation 1.4.10) [[NICE-NG10]].
- Older literature found that 3–20% of admissions receive special observation. Patients often dislike it and nurses find it stressful, and before 2001 there was no evaluative research on it [[Bowers2001Observation]].
- A 2020 systematic review found no consensus on how to improve constant observation. Teamwork interventions may improve patient experience and safely reduce how often and how intensively it is used [[Reen2020]].

### nursing · Lithium teaching (teaching points as published; details in pass E)
- Lithium label boxed warning: "Lithium toxicity is closely related to serum lithium levels, and can occur at doses close to therapeutic levels" [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Label: toxic concentrations (≥1.5 mEq/L) are close to the therapeutic range (0.8–1.2 mEq/L), and some people show toxic signs within the therapeutic range [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Label: mild neurological signs of toxicity include fine tremor, lightheadedness, lack of coordination and weakness. Moderate signs include drowsiness, ataxia, blurred vision, tinnitus and slurred speech. Severe signs include confusion, seizure and coma. Gastrointestinal signs include nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Label: the risk of toxicity rises with febrile illness, interacting drugs, impaired kidney function, volume depletion or dehydration, and changes in sodium or potassium [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Label: patients should keep a normal diet including salt, with adequate fluid intake (2500–3500 mL) "at least during the initial stabilization period". Protracted sweating or diarrhoea lowers tolerance [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Label: sodium loss caused by diuretics, ACE inhibitors and ARBs may increase serum lithium. Lithium levels should be closely monitored when NSAIDs are started or stopped [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- NICE CG185 tells people taking lithium to:
  - seek medical attention if they develop diarrhoea or vomiting or become acutely ill
  - maintain fluid intake after sweating (exercise, heat, fever) or long immobility
  - talk to their doctor promptly about pregnancy or pregnancy plans (recommendation 1.10.17) [[NICE2014CG185]]
- NICE CG185 says to warn people taking lithium not to take over-the-counter NSAIDs (recommendation 1.10.18) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- Ontario population study (10,615 people aged 66+ on lithium; 3.9% admitted for toxicity): starting a loop diuretic (RR 5.5) or an ACE inhibitor (RR 7.6) raised the risk of admission for lithium toxicity within a month. Thiazides and NSAIDs were not independently significant in this cohort [[Juurlink2004Lithium]].
- Suspected lithium toxicity is an emergency. The label tells patients and families to watch for early toxic symptoms and contact the prescriber, and states that no specific antidote is known [[FDA-Lithobid]].

### nursing · Lithium monitoring schedule (as published, not instructions)
- NICE CG185 says to check plasma lithium 1 week after starting and after every dose change, then weekly until stable. Its initial target range is 0.6–0.8 mmol/L for people prescribed lithium for the first time (recommendation 1.10.15) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 says to check plasma lithium every 3 months in the first year, then every 6 months. Higher-risk groups (older people, interacting drugs, renal or thyroid risk, poor symptom control or poor adherence) stay on 3-monthly checks (recommendations 1.10.19–1.10.20) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 says to check weight or BMI, urea and electrolytes including calcium, eGFR and thyroid function every 6 months (recommendation 1.10.21) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 says to monitor at every appointment for neurotoxicity (paraesthesia, ataxia, tremor, cognitive impairment), which "can occur at therapeutic levels" (recommendation 1.10.24) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 says that if lithium is stopped, it should be reduced gradually over at least 4 weeks and preferably up to 3 months, with close monitoring for early signs of mania and depression for 3 months afterwards (recommendations 1.10.25–1.10.26) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- The label says samples for serum lithium should be drawn just before the next dose (8–12 hours after the previous dose). Maintenance levels "should be monitored at least every two months" in uncomplicated cases [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- UK audit (3373 patients): among people on lithium for a year or more, NICE standards were met in only 30% for lithium levels, 55% for renal function and 50% for thyroid function. Monitoring gaps are common [[Collins2010Lithium]].

### nursing · Metabolic monitoring for antipsychotics
- NICE CG185 says to record weight or BMI, pulse, blood pressure, fasting glucose or HbA1c and lipids before starting an antipsychotic (recommendation 1.10.5) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 says to monitor pulse and blood pressure after each dose change, weight or BMI weekly for the first 6 weeks and then at 12 weeks, and glucose/HbA1c and lipids at 12 weeks (recommendation 1.10.8) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- In routine practice (39 pre-guideline studies, n=218,940), baseline screening was:
  - blood pressure 69.8%
  - weight 47.9%
  - glucose 44.3%
  - cholesterol 41.5% [[Mitchell2012Monitoring]]
- After guidelines were introduced, monitoring rose (weight 75.9%, glucose 56.1%). Lipid monitoring stayed low at 28.9% [[Mitchell2012Monitoring]].

### nursing · Discharge planning and NCLEX-style priorities
- Discharge is a high-risk window. Suicide rates are highest in the first 3 months after discharge, so follow-up contact should be arranged before the person leaves (population statistics) [[Chung2017JAMAPsych,Mann2021Suicide]].
- NICE CG185 says discharge and transfer plans should include:
  - recovery goals
  - a crisis plan with early warning signs and triggers
  - a mental-state assessment
  - a medication plan with review dates and monitoring
  The person and GP each get a copy, and the person is encouraged to share it with carers (recommendation 1.9.4) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- NICE CG185 suggests considering intensive case management for people likely to disengage from services (recommendation 1.9.2) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- Nursing priority order used in teaching: physical and psychological safety first, then physiological needs (sleep, nutrition and hydration in a person too active to eat or rest), then a low-stimulation environment and de-escalation, then teaching and discharge planning. This order is built from NICE CG185 recommendation 1.5.1, the NG10 safety and hydration monitoring rules and the label's fluid and salt advice [[NICE2014CG185,NICE-NG10,FDA-Lithobid]].

### Exam hooks
- Group psychoeducation (Colom): 5-year recurrences 3.86 vs 8.37, and days acutely ill 154 vs 586 [[Colom2009]].
- In STEP-BD, intensive psychotherapy (FFT, IPSRT or CBT) gave 64.4% year-end recovery vs 51.5% with 3-session collaborative care [[Miklowitz2007]].
- FFT 2-year relapse: 35% vs 54% with crisis management (HR 0.38) [[Miklowitz2003FFT]].
- IPSRT's added ingredient is regular social rhythms (daily routines) [[Frank2005IPSRT]].
- A large pragmatic CBT trial (n=253) was negative overall (HR 1.05). Benefit was confined to people with fewer than 12 prior episodes [[Scott2006CBT]].
- Manualised psychotherapy + medication vs control: recurrence OR 0.56 [[Miklowitz2021NMA]].
- Mixed states carry the highest suicide-attempt incidence: 37-fold vs other phases [[Valtonen2008]], or 65-fold vs remission [[Holma2014]].
- Suicide is associated with depressive or mixed states, not pure mania [[Miller2020Suicide]].
- Highest-risk time after discharge: the first 3 months, at 1132 per 100,000 person-years [[Chung2017JAMAPsych]].
- Strongest risk factors for suicide death in bipolar disorder: previous attempt and hopelessness [[Hawton2005Bipolar]].
- Safety-planning-type interventions: RR 0.57 for suicidal behaviour, NNT 16 [[Nuij2021]].
- Most robust early warning sign of mania: sleep disturbance (median 77%) [[Jackson2003Prodromes]].
- High expressed emotion in families predicts relapse in bipolar disorder [[Miklowitz1988EE]].
- Lithium teaching: keep normal salt and adequate fluids, and seek care with vomiting, diarrhoea or fever. Avoid OTC NSAIDs [[FDA-Lithobid,NICE2014CG185]].
- In older adults, starting an ACE inhibitor (RR 7.6) or a loop diuretic (RR 5.5) sharply raises the risk of hospitalisation for lithium toxicity [[Juurlink2004Lithium]].
- NICE lithium levels: every 3 months in year 1, then every 6 months (3-monthly if higher risk) [[NICE2014CG185]].
- Antipsychotic monitoring per NICE: weight weekly for 6 weeks, then at 12 weeks, with glucose and lipids at 12 weeks [[NICE2014CG185]].
- Observation levels per NG10: intermittent every 30–60 or 15–30 minutes, continuous within eyesight or arm's length [[NICE-NG10]].
- Mania nursing: calming environment with reduced stimulation, and no major decisions until recovered [[NICE2014CG185]].
- Metabolic syndrome affects about 37% of people with bipolar disorder (OR about 2 vs the general population) [[Vancampfort2013]].

### In plain words
- Talking therapies work best alongside medicine, not instead of it. Learning about the illness in a group, or with family, helps people stay well longer [[Colom2009,Miklowitz2021NMA]].
- In one study, people who took a 6-month class about bipolar disorder spent about 154 days unwell over 5 years, compared with about 586 days for those who did not [[Colom2009]].
- A regular daily routine (same wake time, meals and bedtime) can help keep moods steadier [[Frank2005IPSRT]].
- Losing sleep is often the first warning sign of a high, so protecting sleep is protecting your health [[Jackson2003Prodromes,Wehr1987]].
- Keeping a daily mood chart helps you and your team spot changes early [[Denicoff2000,Perry1999BMJ]].
- A written plan made when you are well, with your warning signs, what helps and who to call, can help in a crisis [[NICE2014CG185,Henderson2004BMJ]].
- Low moods and mixed moods are the times to ask for extra help, because that is when the risk of suicidal thoughts is highest [[Valtonen2008,Holma2014]].
- If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact local emergency services or a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123). Help works [[Nuij2021,Stanley2018ED]].
- If you take lithium, drink enough fluids, keep your usual salt intake, and get medical advice quickly if you are vomiting, have diarrhoea or a fever. Ask before taking pain relievers like ibuprofen [[FDA-Lithobid,NICE2014CG185]].
- Shaking, unsteadiness, slurred speech or confusion while taking lithium can be signs of toxicity. That is an emergency, so get help straight away [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Looking after your heart matters too. Regular checks of weight, blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol are part of good care [[NICE2014CG185,Correll2017CVD]].
- Many people with bipolar disorder work. Support at work or school can make a real difference [[Marwaha2013,NICE2014CG185]].
- Families matter. Calm, supportive homes and carer education are linked with fewer relapses, and carers need support too [[Miklowitz1988EE,Reinares2008,Perlick2007]].
- Most children of a parent with bipolar disorder do not develop it, though they are at higher risk of mood problems and benefit from early help [[Helmink2025,Rasic2014]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Does CBT prevent relapse? Lam 2003 found fewer episodes and admissions with cognitive therapy [[Lam2003CT]]. The larger pragmatic Scott 2006 trial found no overall effect (HR 1.05), with possible benefit only in people with fewer episodes [[Scott2006CBT]]. Status: CBT now has its clearest support for depressive symptoms, not recurrence [[Miklowitz2021NMA]].
- Is structured psychoeducation better than good peer support? Colom showed large 5-year gains against unstructured groups [[Colom2009]]. PARADES found no overall difference against optimised peer support but a benefit in people with 1–7 prior episodes [[Morriss2016PARADES]]. Status: it may matter most early in the illness (Early evidence).
- Which component matters? The network meta-analysis favours family or group skills practice [[Miklowitz2021NMA]]. Chatterton found only carer-focused interventions reduced relapse [[Chatterton2017]]. Status: heterogeneous trials, low-to-moderate certainty [[Oud2016]].
- Do smartphone monitoring and online programmes help? MONARCA I found no symptom benefit and a trend towards more depressive symptoms [[FaurholtJepsen2015]]. MoodSwings 2.0 found a small depression benefit [[Gliddon2019]]. Status: Early evidence. Digital tools are not a substitute for care.
- Joint crisis plans and advance directives: the early trial was positive [[Henderson2004BMJ]], CRIMSON was null [[Thornicroft2013CRIMSON]], and peer-facilitated directives were positive [[Tinland2022]]. Pooled advance statements give RR 0.77 [[deJong2016]]. Status: implementation quality appears critical.
- Self-binding directives: most surveyed people with bipolar disorder endorsed them [[Gergel2021]], but they raise capacity and human-rights questions. Status: legally unsettled, varies by country.
- Can suicide be predicted for an individual? Risk categorisation has 56% sensitivity and 79% specificity [[Large2016]]. Status: guidelines and reviews favour needs-based care and safety planning for everyone over risk scores [[Turecki2016Lancet,Bolton2015BMJ]].
- NSAIDs and lithium: the label warns that NSAIDs can raise lithium levels and advises monitoring [[FDA-Lithobid]], and NICE advises avoiding OTC NSAIDs [[NICE2014CG185]]. An older-adult cohort did not find NSAIDs independently significant for hospitalisation, unlike ACE inhibitors and loop diuretics [[Juurlink2004Lithium]]. Status: caution stays standard teaching.
- Lithium's specific anti-suicide effect: the meta-analysis supports it (OR 0.13 vs placebo for suicide) [[Cipriani2013]]. RCTs pooled for suicide death alone were non-significant (OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.05–1.02) [[Riblet2017]]. Status: discussed in pass E.
