# E · Lithium: evidence, pharmacokinetics, monitoring, toxicity and interactions

Pass date: 2026-11-03. Modules served: lithium, levels.

This pass covers lithium for bipolar disorder: how well it prevents relapse and treats mania, its link with lower suicide rates, real-world registry comparisons, what happens when it is stopped, how the body handles it, the serum ranges in labels and guidelines, monitoring schedules, toxicity and its treatment, interactions, long-term effects on kidney, thyroid and parathyroid, pregnancy, and older adults and children. Every dose, level and schedule below is quoted as published in a label or guideline. None of it is an instruction. Lithium levels and the toxicity table are teaching material, not a way to read one's own result; suspected lithium toxicity is a medical emergency. Outcome and risk figures are population statistics.

## Findings

### lithium · History, indications and place in guidelines
- John Cade's report "Lithium salts in the treatment of psychotic excitement" appeared in the Medical Journal of Australia in September 1949. It is the founding paper of lithium treatment in psychiatry [[Cade1949]].
- The current US generic label indicates lithium as monotherapy for bipolar I disorder: treatment of acute manic and mixed episodes, and maintenance treatment, in patients 7 years and older [[FDA-Lithium]].
- The LITHOBID label says that, given during a manic episode, lithium "may produce a normalization of symptomatology within 1 to 3 weeks" [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- NICE CG185 (recommendation 1.7.7): "Offer lithium as a first-line, long-term pharmacological treatment for bipolar disorder" [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- If lithium is ineffective, poorly tolerated or not suitable, NICE says to consider an antipsychotic (asenapine, aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine or risperidone). One example of "not suitable" it gives is that "the person does not agree to routine blood monitoring" [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- CANMAT/ISBD 2018 lists lithium among the first-line treatments for acute mania (with quetiapine, divalproex, asenapine, aripiprazole, paliperidone, risperidone and cariprazine) and among the first-line options for bipolar I depression [[Yatham2018]].
- CANMAT/ISBD 2018 calls lithium "the gold standard for maintenance treatment". It prevents both manic and depressive episodes, with a larger effect against mania, and "appears to have a degree of anti‑suicidal effects" [[Yatham2018]].
- In CANMAT/ISBD 2018, lithium is a second-line option for bipolar II depression, "ideally at a serum level of 0.8‐1.2 mEq/L" [[Yatham2018]].
- CANMAT/ISBD 2018 describes the people who usually respond well to lithium. They tend to have an episodic, remitting course; a family history of bipolar disorder (especially lithium-responsive); low comorbidity (especially anxiety and substance use); and a mania–depression–euthymia episode pattern [[Yatham2018]].
- CANMAT/ISBD 2018 also cites data that people with a lithium-responsive relative have a 67% likelihood of responding, against 35% for people without one [[Yatham2018]].
- A 2024 review states that lithium prescribing has declined internationally over recent decades, to about 35% ever-use or less among people with bipolar disorder. It also describes specialised care models that raised lithium use to 70% of patients [[Kessing2024Why]].

### lithium · Maintenance (relapse prevention) evidence
- Geddes 2004 pooled 5 placebo-controlled RCTs (770 participants). Lithium reduced all relapses (random-effects RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.50–0.84) and manic relapses (RR 0.62, 0.40–0.95). The effect on depressive relapses was smaller and uncertain (RR 0.72, 0.49–1.07) [[Geddes2004]].
- The Severus 2014 update pooled 7 placebo-controlled trials (1,580 participants). Lithium reduced overall mood episodes (RR 0.66, 0.53–0.82) and manic episodes (RR 0.52, 0.38–0.71). For depressive episodes the random-effects RR was 0.78 (0.59–1.03) and the fixed-effect RR was 0.73 (0.60–0.88) [[Severus2014E]].
- In the same meta-analysis, more people stopped lithium than placebo for reasons other than a mood episode (RR 1.33, 1.07–1.65). More people on lithium completed the study (RR 1.69, 1.12–2.55) [[Severus2014E]].
- Against anticonvulsants (7 trials, n = 1,305), lithium was better at preventing manic episodes (RR 0.66, 0.44–1.00). It did not differ in overall or depressive episodes [[Severus2014E]].
- BALANCE was an open-label RCT of 330 people with bipolar I disorder in the UK, France, the USA and Italy, followed for up to 24 months. The groups were lithium (target 0.4–1.0 mmol/L), valproate (750–1250 mg) and the two combined [[Geddes2010BALANCE]].
- BALANCE primary-outcome events (a new intervention for an emerging mood episode): 54% with the combination, 59% with lithium and 69% with valproate [[Geddes2010BALANCE]].
- BALANCE hazard ratios:
  - combination vs valproate: HR 0.59 (0.42–0.83)
  - combination vs lithium: HR 0.82 (0.58–1.17), not significant
  - lithium vs valproate: HR 0.71 (0.51–1.00, p = 0.047) [[Geddes2010BALANCE]]
- A 2014 network meta-analysis covered 33 RCTs, 17 treatments and 6,846 participants. Its authors concluded that lithium "should remain the first-line treatment" for relapse prevention because it works against both manic and depressive recurrence and its evidence is of better quality, "notwithstanding its tolerability profile" [[Miura2014]].
- In that network meta-analysis, placebo was better tolerated than lithium, measured as discontinuation for adverse events (RR 2.58, 95% CrI 1.33–5.39) [[Miura2014]].
- Gelenberg 1989 was a double-blind RCT in 94 people. Relapse occurred in 13% (6/47) of those kept at a "standard" level of 0.8–1.0 mmol/L and in 38% (18/47) of those at a "low" level of 0.4–0.6 mmol/L. Relapse risk was 2.6 times higher in the low-range group (95% CI 1.3–5.2) [[Gelenberg1989]].
- In the same trial, tremor, diarrhoea, urinary frequency, weight gain and metallic taste were more frequent in the standard-range group [[Gelenberg1989]].
- Bipolar CHOICE randomised 482 people with bipolar I or II disorder to lithium or quetiapine, each with adjunctive personalised treatment, for 6 months. Primary outcomes did not differ. Lithium had a lower side-effect burden: frequency p = .05, intensity p = .01, impairment p = .01 [[Nierenberg2016CHOICE]].

### lithium · Acute mania
- The Cipriani 2011 multiple-treatments meta-analysis included 68 RCTs (16,073 participants). Lithium beat placebo on mania scales at 3 weeks (SMD −0.37, 95% CI −0.63 to −0.11) [[Cipriani2011]].
- In the same analysis, haloperidol was more effective than lithium (SMD −0.19, −0.36 to −0.01). Olanzapine, risperidone and quetiapine led to fewer discontinuations than lithium [[Cipriani2011]].
- In a 21-day RCT in 179 inpatients with mania, at least 50% improvement was seen in 49% on lithium (target 1.5 mmol/L), 48% on divalproex and 25% on placebo [[Bowden1994]].
- In a pediatric RCT (ages 7–17, lithium n = 53 vs placebo n = 28, 8 weeks), lithium reduced Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) scores by 5.51 points more than placebo (95% CI 0.51–10.50). It caused no significant weight gain but raised TSH (+3.0 vs −0.1 mIU/L) [[Findling2015,NCT01166425]].
- In that pediatric study's 28-week randomised-withdrawal phase (label data), 41.2% (7/17) of children continuing lithium discontinued, against 78.6% (11/14) switched to placebo (HR 0.28, 0.10–0.78) [[FDA-Lithium]].
- GERI-BD randomised 224 people aged 60 or older with mania to lithium (target 0.80–0.99 mEq/L) or divalproex for 9 weeks. YMRS improvement favoured lithium (difference 3.90, 97.5% CI 1.71–6.09). Response rates were similar: 79% vs 73% [[Young2017GERIBD]].

### lithium · Suicide, self-harm and mortality (population statistics)
- Cipriani 2013 pooled 48 RCTs (6,674 participants). Compared with placebo, lithium reduced suicides (OR 0.13, 95% CI 0.03–0.66) and deaths from any cause (OR 0.38, 0.15–0.95). It showed no clear benefit for deliberate self-harm (OR 0.60, 0.27–1.32) [[Cipriani2013]].
- In unipolar depression, lithium was associated with fewer suicides (OR 0.36, 0.13–0.98) and fewer total deaths (OR 0.13, 0.02–0.76) than placebo [[Cipriani2013]].
- A 2006 meta-analysis of 31 studies (85,229 person-years) found suicides and attempts about five times less frequent with lithium than without it (RR 4.91, 3.82–6.31). This equals a roughly 80% lower risk over an average of 18 months [[Baldessarini2006]].
- Song 2017 compared each person's time on and off treatment within a Swedish register cohort of 51,535 people with bipolar disorder (2005–2013). Suicide-related events were 14% lower during lithium treatment (HR 0.86, 0.78–0.95). They were not lower during valproate treatment (HR 1.02, 0.89–1.15) [[Song2017]].
- Song 2017 estimated that 12% (4–20%) of suicide-related events might have been avoided had people taken lithium for the whole follow-up [[Song2017]].
- In UK primary-care records (6,671 people), self-harm rates were 205 per 10,000 person-years on lithium. Rates were higher on valproate (392), olanzapine (409) and quetiapine (582) [[Hayes2016JAMAPsychiatry]].
- After propensity-score adjustment, the other three drugs combined had a higher self-harm hazard than lithium (HR 1.40, 1.12–1.74) [[Hayes2016JAMAPsychiatry]].
- In a Danish nationwide cohort of 13,186 people who bought lithium, buying it at least twice was associated with a lower suicide rate than buying it once (rate ratio 0.44, 0.28–0.70) [[Kessing2005Suicide]].
- A 2017 meta-review concluded that the evidence is "overwhelmingly in favour of lithium as an antisuicidal agent" and that lithium is still under-used in practice [[Smith2017Lithium]].
- A VA randomised trial added lithium or placebo to usual care in 519 veterans with depression or bipolar disorder after a recent suicide-related event. It was stopped for futility, finding no difference in repeat suicide-related events (HR 1.10, 0.77–1.55) [[Katz2022,NCT01928446]].
- In that trial, mean lithium levels at 3 months were low: 0.54 mEq/L in bipolar disorder and 0.46 mEq/L in major depression [[Katz2022]].

Safe-messaging note for builders (not a finding): if you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact local emergency services or a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).

### lithium · Real-world registry comparisons
- In a Finnish cohort of 18,018 people hospitalised for bipolar disorder (mean follow-up 7.2 years), lithium was associated with one of the lowest risks of psychiatric rehospitalisation (HR 0.67, 0.60–0.73) and the lowest risk of all-cause hospitalisation (HR 0.71, 0.66–0.76) [[Lahteenvuo2018]].
- In the same Finnish cohort, quetiapine, the most-used antipsychotic, showed only modest effects (psychiatric rehospitalisation HR 0.92, 0.85–0.98) [[Lahteenvuo2018]].
- In UK records (5,089 people on monotherapy), 75% of people on lithium had stopped it or needed an add-on drug by 2.05 years. The same point came at 0.76 years for quetiapine, 0.98 years for valproate and 1.13 years for olanzapine [[Hayes2016WorldPsychiatry]].
- In Danish registers (4,268 people), valproate compared with lithium carried a higher rate of switching or add-on treatment (HR 1.86, 1.59–2.16) and of psychiatric admission (HR 1.33, 1.18–1.48) [[Kessing2011BJP]].

### lithium · Stopping lithium and adherence
- Suppes 1991 pooled 14 studies (257 people with bipolar I disorder) who stopped lithium after an average of 30 months. More than 50% of new episodes came within 10 weeks of stopping [[Suppes1991]].
- In those studies, the median time to the end of remission was 5.0 months after stopping. 25% had a manic recurrence by 2.7 months, against 14 months for a depressive recurrence [[Suppes1991]].
- NICE: "If stopping lithium, reduce the dose gradually over at least 4 weeks, and preferably up to 3 months." It advises close monitoring for early signs of mania and depression during the reduction and for 3 months after stopping [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- When starting lithium, NICE advises telling the person that poor adherence or rapid discontinuation may increase the risk of relapse [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- A 2008 review found that an abrupt fall in serum level of more than 0.2 mmol/L was associated with a higher relapse risk [[Severus2008]].

### levels · Pharmacokinetics (how the body handles lithium)
- Lithium is reported to be completely absorbed in the upper gastrointestinal tract. Peak serum concentration (Tmax) comes 0.25–3 hours after immediate-release and 2–6 hours after sustained-release preparations [[FDA-Lithium]].
- A 2009 review gives similar peaks: 1.0–2.0 hours for standard-release and 4–5 hours for sustained-release forms. It reports bioavailability of 80–100% and total clearance of 10–40 mL/min [[Grandjean2009PartII]].
- Sustained-release formulations lower peak plasma concentration by 30–50% without major change in the area under the curve [[Grandjean2009PartII]].
- Lithium's distribution space approximates total body water and it binds negligibly to plasma proteins. The apparent volume of distribution is 0.7–1 L/kg [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Lithium is not metabolised. It is excreted mainly in urine, in proportion to its serum concentration. Faecal excretion is insignificant [[FDA-Lithium]].
- The glomerulus filters lithium and about 80% is reabsorbed by passive diffusion in the proximal tubule [[FDA-Lithium]].
- The elimination half-life is about 18–36 hours in the generic label [[FDA-Lithium]] and "approximately 24 hours" in the LITHOBID label [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Lithium "may take up to 24 hours to distribute into brain tissue", so acute toxicity symptoms may appear late [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Measured by 7Li magnetic resonance spectroscopy, brain lithium is about half the serum level, occasionally reaching 75–80%. Brain and serum levels correlate only weakly [[Grandjean2009PartII]].
- Lithium reduces sodium reabsorption in the renal tubules. A low-sodium state raises lithium retention, so the labels stress a normal salt intake [[FDA-Lithium,FDA-Lithobid]].
- During the stabilisation period, the labels advise adequate fluid intake: 2500–3000 mL in the generic label [[FDA-Lithium]] and 2500–3500 mL in LITHOBID [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Lithium clearance falls with age [[Grandjean2009PartII]].
- In children, the estimated plasma clearance was 0.59, 0.79 and 1.17 L/h at body weights of 20, 30 and 50 kg [[FDA-Lithium]].
- In late pregnancy, lithium clearance rises by 30–50% because glomerular filtration rises [[Grandjean2009PartII]].
- In a large Dutch dataset (41,102 levels), mean levels peaked in summer (0.761 mmol/L) and were lowest in winter (0.748 mmol/L). The authors judged the difference "statistically significant but therapeutically irrelevant" [[Wilting2007]].

### levels · Target serum ranges as published (not instructions)
- Generic US label: titrate to 0.8–1.2 mEq/L for acute manic or mixed episodes and 0.8–1.0 mEq/L for maintenance (patients 7 years and older) [[FDA-Lithium]].
- LITHOBID label: acute mania doses "normally produce" 1.0–1.5 mEq/L; desirable long-term levels are 0.6–1.2 mEq/L. It states that levels should not exceed 2.0 mEq/L during the acute phase [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Both labels give ≥1.5 mEq/L as the toxic concentration, "close to the therapeutic range (0.8 to 1.2 mEq/L)" [[FDA-Lithium,FDA-Lithobid]].
- NICE: aim for 0.6–0.8 mmol/L in people prescribed lithium for the first time [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- NICE suggests considering 0.8–1.0 mmol/L for a trial of at least 6 months in people who relapsed on lithium before, or who have subthreshold symptoms with functional impairment [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- CANMAT/ISBD 2018: "The target serum level for lithium in acute treatment is 0.8‐1.2 mEq/L (0.4‐0.8 mEq/L in older adults) while in maintenance treatment, serum levels of 0.6‐1 mEq/L may be sufficient" [[Yatham2018]].
- The ISBD/IGSLI Delphi consensus (2019) for adults: standard level 0.60–0.80 mmol/L, lowered to 0.40–0.60 if response is good but tolerance poor, or raised to 0.80–1.00 if response is insufficient and tolerance good [[Nolen2019]].
- For older adults, the majority (not a consensus) of that panel endorsed 0.40–0.60 mmol/L usually, with a maximum of 0.70–0.80 at ages 65–79 and 0.70 over age 80 [[Nolen2019]].
- An earlier review placed the minimum efficacious maintenance level at 0.4 mmol/L, with optimal response at 0.6–0.75 mmol/L. Levels above 0.75 may add control of manic symptoms between episodes [[Severus2008]].
- A 2026 ISBD task-force review covered 14 observational studies of 8,808 adults aged 60 or older taking lithium. Mean or median levels were 0.47–0.67 mmol/L; levels of 0.82–1.00 were linked to more side effects or early toxicity, and 1.20–1.25 were overtly toxic [[Nunes2026]].
- An ISBD initiative recommends laboratory-reported ranges of 0.4–0.8 mmol/L at ages 60–79 and 0.4–0.7 mmol/L at 80 and over. So far 18 dissemination attempts have led to 3 implementations [[Shulman2026]].
- The generic label warns that some patients "abnormally sensitive to lithium may exhibit toxic signs" within the therapeutic range. Older patients may show toxicity at levels other patients tolerate [[FDA-Lithium]].

### levels · When and how levels are measured
- Generic label: draw the serum level immediately before the next dose, about 12 hours after the previous dose, when concentrations are relatively stable. Do not rely on levels alone; assessment must be clinical as well as laboratory [[FDA-Lithium]].
- The LITHOBID label gives the sampling window as 8–12 hours after the previous dose [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- For once-daily dosing, a 2009 review says the 24-hour level should serve as the control value [[Grandjean2009PartII]].
- Generic label: obtain the first level after 3 days, 12 hours after the last oral dose, and regularly until stable. Recheck after any change in dose, after starting or stopping interacting drugs (diuretics, NSAIDs, renin-angiotensin system antagonists, metronidazole), after a marked change in strenuous exercise, and during intercurrent illness [[FDA-Lithium]].
- LITHOBID label: check levels twice weekly in the acute phase until stable, then at least every two months in uncomplicated maintenance [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- CANMAT/ISBD 2018: draw levels about 5 days after the most recent dose change. It recommends two consecutive in-range levels in the acute phase, then checks every 3–6 months [[Yatham2018]].
- NICE: measure the level 1 week after starting and 1 week after every dose change, and weekly until stable. Then every 3 months for the first year [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- After the first year, NICE advises every 6 months. It keeps 3-monthly checks for older people, people on interacting drugs, people at risk of renal or thyroid problems or raised calcium, people with poor symptom control or adherence, and people whose last level was 0.8 mmol/L or higher [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- NICE: check weight or BMI, urea and electrolytes including calcium, eGFR and thyroid function every 6 months, and more often if problems appear [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- NICE: at every appointment, monitor for neurotoxicity (paraesthesia, ataxia, tremor, cognitive impairment), which "can occur at therapeutic levels" [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- Generic label baseline checks: renal function, vital signs, serum electrolytes, thyroid function, concurrent medicines and pregnancy status. Thyroid function is checked before starting, at 3 months and every 6–12 months [[FDA-Lithium]].
- NICE's baseline set: weight or BMI, urea and electrolytes including calcium, eGFR, thyroid function and a full blood count. It adds an ECG for people with cardiovascular disease or its risk factors [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- A UK national audit (3,373 patients, 38 trusts) found that for patients on lithium for a year or more, NICE standards were met for serum levels in 30%, renal function in 55% and thyroid function in 50% [[Collins2010]].

### levels · Toxicity: signs, patterns and risk factors
- Boxed warning: "Lithium toxicity is closely related to serum lithium concentrations, and can occur at doses close to therapeutic concentrations." Facilities for prompt serum measurement should be available before treatment starts [[FDA-Lithium,FDA-Lithobid]].
- The generic label grades neurological toxicity:
  - mild: fine tremor, light-headedness, lack of coordination, weakness
  - moderate: giddiness, apathy, drowsiness, hyperreflexia, muscle twitching, ataxia, blurred vision, tinnitus, slurred speech
  - severe: clonus, confusion, seizure, coma and death [[FDA-Lithium]]
- Other manifestations listed in the label: QT prolongation, ST/T changes and myocarditis; urine concentrating defect, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and renal failure; nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and bloating [[FDA-Lithium]].
- LITHOBID level bands:
  - mild to moderate reactions may occur at 1.5–2.5 mEq/L
  - moderate to severe reactions at 2.0 mEq/L and above
  - above 3.0 mEq/L, a complex multi-organ picture [[FDA-Lithobid]]
- Diarrhoea, vomiting, drowsiness, muscle weakness and lack of coordination may be early signs of intoxication below 2.0 mEq/L [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- Generic label: above 3 mEq/L, patients "may progress to seizures, coma, and irreversible brain damage" [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Labelled risk factors for toxicity:
  - recent febrile illness
  - drugs that raise lithium levels or affect the kidney
  - acute ingestion
  - impaired renal function
  - volume depletion or dehydration
  - significant cardiovascular disease
  - electrolyte changes, especially sodium and potassium [[FDA-Lithium]]
- Lithium poisoning has three patterns: acute, acute-on-chronic and chronic. Chronic poisoning is the most common and is usually unintentional; intake outstrips elimination, often because the kidneys clear less during volume depletion, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, intercurrent illness or interacting drugs [[BairdGunning2017]].
- In 97 poisoning cases at an Australian regional centre, 26 of 28 severe neurotoxicity cases arose from chronic therapeutic use. Chronic poisoning carried a much higher risk of severe neurotoxicity than overdose (OR 136, 95% CI 23–1300) [[Oakley2001]].
- In that series, independent risk factors for chronic poisoning were nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (adjusted OR 26.96), age over 50 (6.20) and thyroid dysfunction (9.30) [[Oakley2001]].
- Severe neurotoxicity was linked to a longer hospital stay (12 vs 2 days) and higher peak levels (2.3 vs 1.6 mmol/L) [[Oakley2001]].
- In a Swedish cohort of 1,340 people (1997–2013), the incidence of a level ≥1.5 mmol/L was 0.01 per patient-year. Of 91 episodes, 34% needed intensive care and 13% haemodialysis, with no deaths [[Ott2016]].
- The Swedish authors concluded that physicians "should not withhold lithium for fear of intoxication" but should have a low threshold to screen for toxicity [[Ott2016]].
- In Ontario, 413 of 10,615 adults aged 66 or older taking lithium (3.9%) were admitted at least once for lithium toxicity over 1992–2001 [[Juurlink2004]].
- SILENT (syndrome of irreversible lithium-effectuated neurotoxicity) means sequelae lasting at least 2 months after lithium stops. A 2005 review found 90 published cases; persistent cerebellar dysfunction was the most common [[Adityanjee2005]].
- The label also notes that rare neurological sequelae may persist and "may be associated with cerebellar atrophy" [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Combining lithium with an antipsychotic has been linked to an encephalopathic syndrome that may resemble neuroleptic malignant syndrome, sometimes followed by irreversible brain damage [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Lithium can precipitate serotonin syndrome, especially with other serotonergic drugs (SSRIs, SNRIs, triptans, tricyclics, tramadol, St John's wort and others) [[FDA-Lithium]].

### levels · Treating toxicity
- There is no specific antidote. Mild toxicity can usually be managed by lowering or stopping the dose. Activated charcoal is not recommended because it does not bind lithium ions significantly [[FDA-Lithium]].
- The generic label calls haemodialysis "the treatment of choice" for severe toxicity. Levels can rebound as lithium diffuses out of tissues, so they need close monitoring [[FDA-Lithium]].
- The EXTRIP workgroup reviewed 418 patients from 166 articles and graded lithium as dialyzable (level of evidence A) [[Decker2015]].
- EXTRIP recommends extracorporeal treatment in these cases:
  - impaired kidney function with a level above 4.0 mEq/L
  - decreased consciousness, seizures or life-threatening dysrhythmias, at any level (1D) [[Decker2015]]
- EXTRIP suggests extracorporeal treatment in these cases:
  - a level above 5.0 mEq/L
  - significant confusion
  - an expected time to fall below 1.0 mEq/L of more than 36 hours (2D) [[Decker2015]]
- EXTRIP: continue until clinical improvement or a level below 1.0 mEq/L, or for at least 6 hours if levels cannot be measured. Haemodialysis is preferred; continuous renal replacement therapy is an acceptable alternative [[Decker2015]].
- The evidence behind the EXTRIP recommendations is very low quality, mostly case reports. No randomised trials of dialysis for lithium poisoning exist [[Decker2015,BairdGunning2017]].
- NICE advises people taking lithium to seek medical attention if they develop diarrhoea or vomiting or become acutely ill for any reason [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- NICE also advises maintaining fluid intake after sweating, in hot climates, with fever, during long immobility, or with a chest infection [[NICE2014CG185E]].

### levels · Interactions and situations that change lithium levels
- Thiazide diuretics have the greatest potential to raise lithium: concentrations often rise by 25–40% after a thiazide starts. Loop and potassium-sparing diuretics have minor, variable effects [[Finley1995]].
- In 20 hypertensive patients stable on lithium, starting an ACE inhibitor raised steady-state lithium by 36.1% and cut lithium clearance by 25.5% [[Finley1996]].
- In Ontario adults aged 66 or older, hospital admission for lithium toxicity rose within a month of starting a loop diuretic (RR 5.5, 95% CI 1.9–16.1) or an ACE inhibitor (RR 7.6, 2.6–22.0). Thiazides and NSAIDs were not independently associated in this study [[Juurlink2004]].
- NSAIDs reduce renal blood flow and lithium clearance and raise serum lithium. Indomethacin and piroxicam significantly raise steady-state levels, and COX-2 inhibitors appear to do the same [[FDA-Lithium,FDA-Lithobid]].
- In healthy volunteers, celecoxib 200 mg twice daily raised mean steady-state lithium by about 17% [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- NICE warns people taking lithium not to take over-the-counter NSAIDs [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- If an NSAID is prescribed, NICE says it should be taken regularly (not as needed), with monthly level checks until stable and then every 3 months [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- Renin-angiotensin system antagonists (ACE inhibitors, ARBs) and nitroimidazole antibiotics such as metronidazole can raise lithium levels [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Acetazolamide, urea, xanthines and alkalinising agents such as sodium bicarbonate lower lithium by increasing its excretion. SGLT2 inhibitors may also lower serum lithium [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Calcium-channel blockers may increase neurological adverse effects (ataxia, tremor, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, tinnitus). Iodide preparations may cause hypothyroidism [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Lithium may prolong the effect of neuromuscular blocking agents [[FDA-Lithium]].
- In a Dutch case-control study, starting a potentially interacting drug was seen in 15.7% of patients whose level rose to ≥1.3 mmol/L, and in no stable controls (OR 20.13, 1.13–359). Antibiotics contributed heavily [[Wilting2005]].
- In the same study, irregular lithium dispensing was associated with elevated levels (OR 3.14, 1.15–8.61) [[Wilting2005]].
- Protracted sweating, diarrhoea or fever lower tolerance to lithium [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Mild hyponatraemia (sodium above 120 mEq/L) can be asymptomatic. Below about 115 mEq/L, stupor, seizures and coma can occur [[FDA-Lithium]].

### lithium · Kidney
- McKnight 2012 meta-analysis (385 studies): mean GFR reduction −6.22 mL/min (95% CI −14.65 to 2.20, not significant) [[McKnight2012]].
- The same analysis found urinary concentrating ability reduced by 15% of the normal maximum (−158 mOsm/kg). 18 of 3,369 patients (0.5%) received renal replacement therapy [[McKnight2012]].
- In Oxford laboratory data, lithium in serum was associated with stage 3 CKD (HR 1.93, 1.76–2.12) [[Shine2015]].
- Risk was greatest in women under 60 and in people with above-median lithium levels. Adverse effects tended to occur early in treatment [[Shine2015]].
- In UK records, valproate (HR 0.56), olanzapine (HR 0.57) and quetiapine (HR 0.62) all had lower rates of CKD stage 3+ than lithium. CKD stage 4+ did not differ [[Hayes2016PLoSMed]].
- In UK primary-care data on 6,360 people, ever-use of lithium was associated with renal failure (HR 2.5, 1.6–4.0). The absolute risk was small, with a number needed to harm of 44 (21–150) in people aged 50 or older [[Close2014]].
- Tondo 2017 followed 312 people on lithium for 8–48 years (mean 18). eGFR fell 0.71% per year of age and 0.92% per year of treatment. 29.5% had at least one eGFR below 60 and 18.1% had two or more; none developed end-stage renal failure [[Tondo2017]].
- In that cohort, the drop in eGFR was about 30% steeper than ageing alone would explain [[Tondo2017]].
- In northern Sweden (785 patients vs 1,549 people from the general population), eGFR fell 0.57 mL/min/1.73 m² per year in both groups. Lithium added 0.54 mL/min/1.73 m² per year of treatment [[Fransson2022]].
- After more than 10 years on lithium, the decline was clearly steeper than in all other groups, though individual trajectories varied widely [[Fransson2022]].
- In a mirror-image study of 168 people who stopped lithium after at least 4.5 years, mean annual eGFR change went from −1.58 to −0.02 mL/min/1.73 m². Restarting lithium returned the decline to −1.71 [[Fransson2025]].
- After stopping, the improvement was largest with eGFR below 30 (+3.03 mL/min/1.73 m² per year) and smaller with eGFR above 60 (+0.77) [[Fransson2025]].
- In Gothenburg laboratory data, the lifetime risk of CKD stage 4 or worse was 13.9–18.6% for people starting lithium at ages 55–74 and 5.4% for those starting at 75 or older. The 10-year risk for those starting at 18–54 was 0–0.7% [[Golic2023]].
- In the same data, 20 or more years of lithium raised the odds of CKD stage 4+ compared with 1–5 years (OR 6.14, 2.65–14.26) [[Golic2023]].
- A 2026 expert algorithm lists these renal risk factors: a rapid early fall in GFR, starting young, cumulative dose, mean serum level and episodes of intoxication. It reports that newer studies show less end-stage kidney disease than older ones, especially with monitoring (Early evidence) [[Strandhave2026]].
- The label describes lithium-induced CKD mainly as chronic tubulointerstitial nephropathy. It advises re-evaluating treatment if renal function changes progressively or suddenly, "even within the normal range" [[FDA-Lithium]].
- The label recommends against lithium in severe renal impairment (creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min). With clearance of 30–89 mL/min, it advises lower starting doses and slow titration [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (polyuria, polydipsia) can begin within weeks. It is usually reversible but may be only partly reversible after long-term use; the label names amiloride as a possible treatment [[FDA-Lithium]].
- NICE: if eGFR falls over 2 or more tests, monitor more often and assess the rate of decline. Decisions on continuing lithium may need input from a renal specialist [[NICE2014CG185E]].

### lithium · Thyroid and parathyroid
- McKnight 2012: clinical hypothyroidism was more common with lithium than with placebo (OR 5.78, 2.00–16.67), and TSH rose by 4.00 on average [[McKnight2012]].
- Shine 2015: lithium was associated with hypothyroidism (HR 2.31, 2.05–2.60) but not with hyperthyroidism (HR 1.22, 0.96–1.55) [[Shine2015]].
- UK records: hypothyroidism was less common with valproate (HR 0.60) and olanzapine (HR 0.48) than with lithium [[Hayes2016PLoSMed]].
- In a Danish target-trial emulation, sustained lithium use raised the absolute risk of myxoedema by 7–10% compared with lamotrigine or valproate. It did not raise the risk of other physical disorders studied (stroke, myocardial infarction, diabetes, dementia, CKD, cancer and others). The authors note possible detection bias [[Kessing2024Physical]].
- Lithium is concentrated in the thyroid and inhibits hormone synthesis and release. Hyperthyroidism (Graves' disease, toxic multinodular goitre, silent thyroiditis) occurs paradoxically and rarely [[FDA-Lithium,Kibirige2013]].
- Hypothyroidism and goitre are the most common thyroid problems on long-term lithium. Lithium-associated hypothyroidism is usually treated with thyroid hormone replacement [[Kibirige2013,FDA-Lithium]].
- McKnight 2012: lithium raised blood calcium by 0.09 mmol/L and parathyroid hormone by 7.32 pg/mL. The authors advise checking calcium before and during treatment [[McKnight2012]].
- In a Swedish outpatient population of 423 people on lithium (mean 13.5 years), 18% had lithium-associated hyperparathyroidism and a further 21% leaned towards hypercalcaemia. About 1% had parathyroid surgery [[Meehan2015]].
- In a second Swedish cohort, lithium-associated hypercalcaemia had a prevalence of 26%. Its odds were higher on lithium than in people with bipolar disorder not taking lithium (adjusted OR 13.45, 3.09–58.55) [[Meehan2018]].
- The label says lithium-associated hyperparathyroidism is more often multiglandular, and hypercalcaemia may persist after lithium stops [[FDA-Lithium]].

### lithium · Other adverse effects
- Common early effects in adults: fine hand tremor, polyuria, mild thirst, nausea and general discomfort. These may settle, or ease with food or a temporary dose reduction [[FDA-Lithium]].
- In the pediatric RCT (label table), these were more frequent on lithium than placebo:
  - nausea/vomiting: 57% vs 29%
  - polyuria: 38% vs 14%
  - tremor: 32% vs 7%
  - raised TSH: 25% vs 0%
  - fatigue: 26% vs 4% [[FDA-Lithium]]
- A 2009 review reports tremor in 4–20% of patients, made worse by caffeine and other psychotropics. It also reports weight gain of up to 4–10 kg in about 30% [[Grandjean2009PartIII]].
- McKnight 2012: more weight gain than placebo (OR 1.89, 1.27–2.82) but less than olanzapine. No increased risk of alopecia or skin disorders was found [[McKnight2012]].
- UK records: weight gain of more than 15% was more frequent with valproate (HR 1.62), olanzapine (HR 1.84) and quetiapine (HR 1.67) than with lithium [[Hayes2016PLoSMed]].
- Patients find weight gain and cognitive effects the most distressing and the most linked to stopping lithium. Thirst, urination, nausea, diarrhoea and tremor are common but usually manageable through dose timing, levels at the lower end of range and, sometimes, antidotes [[Gitlin2016]].
- Lithium can unmask Brugada syndrome. The label advises avoiding lithium in known or suspected Brugada syndrome and seeking a cardiology opinion for unexplained fainting or palpitations [[FDA-Lithium]].
- The label also lists pseudotumour cerebri, sinus-node dysfunction, psoriasis onset or flare, and DRESS [[FDA-Lithium]].

### lithium · Pregnancy, birth and breastfeeding (plan with the care team)
- In 1,325,563 US Medicaid pregnancies, cardiac malformations occurred in 2.41% of infants exposed to lithium in the first trimester, against 1.15% of unexposed infants (adjusted RR 1.65, 1.02–2.68) [[Patorno2017]].
- Risk rose with dose: RR 1.11 at 600 mg/day or less, 1.60 at 601–900 mg and 3.22 above 900 mg [[Patorno2017]].
- Right ventricular outflow tract defects, which include Ebstein's anomaly, occurred in 0.60% vs 0.18% (RR 2.66, 1.00–7.06). The authors concluded the effect was smaller than previously thought [[Patorno2017]].
- An individual-data meta-analysis of six cohorts (727 lithium-exposed pregnancies) found first-trimester exposure associated with major malformations: 7.4% vs 4.3% (aOR 1.71, 1.07–2.72) [[MunkOlsen2018]].
- Major cardiac malformations in that analysis were 2.1% vs 1.6%, not significant. Neonatal readmission within 28 days was 27.5% vs 14.3% [[MunkOlsen2018]].
- The generic label says later studies indicate the increase in cardiac malformation risk "is likely to be small". It suggests considering fetal echocardiography at 16–20 weeks after first-trimester exposure [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Background rates: the US general-population risk of major birth defects is 2–4%, and of miscarriage 15–20% [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Around delivery, the label advises lowering or pausing lithium 2–3 days before the due date and restarting at the preconception dose with close level checks [[FDA-Lithium]].
- A Dutch–US observational study (233 maternal levels, 29 neonates) found no change in the level-to-dose ratio after delivery and no link between neonatal levels and outcomes. Its authors did not recommend lowering the dose before delivery [[Molenaar2021]].
- Neonates exposed late in pregnancy can show lithium toxicity ("floppy baby": hypotonia, cyanosis, poor feeding, bradycardia), which may take up to 14 days to clear [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Lithium passes freely into breast milk [[Grandjean2009PartII]].
- The LITHOBID label advises against nursing during lithium therapy "except in rare and unusual circumstances" [[FDA-Lithobid]].
- McKnight 2012 recommends weighing the balance of risks before stopping lithium in pregnancy, because the malformation risk is uncertain [[McKnight2012]].
- NICE advises people taking lithium to talk to their doctor as soon as possible if they become pregnant or plan a pregnancy [[NICE2014CG185E]].

### lithium · Research landscape
- A genome-wide association study by ConLiGen (2,563 patients) found a chromosome 21 locus linked with lithium response. Two long non-coding RNA genes are in the region (Early evidence) [[Hou2016]].
- In a prospective cohort of 73 people, carriers of the response-associated alleles from that locus had fewer relapses (HR 3.8, 1.1–13.0) (Early evidence) [[Hou2016]].
- Kidney function improves after lithium is stopped (Early evidence; one mirror-image cohort) [[Fransson2025]].
- Age-specific lower laboratory ranges for older adults are being rolled out internationally (Early evidence) [[Shulman2026,Nunes2026]].
- That lithium specifically prevents suicide is (Established) for the placebo-controlled trial evidence base [[Cipriani2013]]. It is contested for adding lithium after a recent suicidal event in actively treated veterans [[Katz2022]].

### Exam hooks
- The boxed warning: lithium toxicity is closely related to serum concentration and can occur at doses close to therapeutic ones [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Draw the level as a trough, about 12 hours after the last dose [[FDA-Lithium,Yatham2018]].
- Half-life is 18–36 hours. CANMAT checks levels about 5 days after a dose change [[FDA-Lithium,Yatham2018]].
- NICE target for a first prescription: 0.6–0.8 mmol/L. Consider 0.8–1.0 after a relapse on lithium [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- NICE checks levels every 3 months in year 1, then every 6 months. eGFR, thyroid function, calcium and weight are checked every 6 months [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- Toxic threshold in US labels: ≥1.5 mEq/L [[FDA-Lithium,FDA-Lithobid]].
- Lithium is renally excreted, not metabolised. About 80% of filtered lithium is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Thiazides raise lithium levels by 25–40% [[Finley1995]]. ACE inhibitors raised them by 36% in one series [[Finley1996]].
- Loop diuretics (RR 5.5) and ACE inhibitors (RR 7.6) increased toxicity admissions in older adults within a month of starting [[Juurlink2004]].
- Severe neurotoxicity comes mostly from chronic, not acute, poisoning [[Oakley2001]].
- EXTRIP: extracorporeal treatment if the level is above 4.0 mEq/L with impaired kidneys, or with seizures, reduced consciousness or dysrhythmia at any level [[Decker2015]].
- Activated charcoal does not bind lithium [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Lithium vs placebo for relapse prevention: RR 0.66 overall and 0.52 for mania. The effect is stronger against mania than depression [[Severus2014E]].
- BALANCE: lithium alone or with valproate beat valproate alone [[Geddes2010BALANCE]].
- Lithium vs placebo for suicide: OR 0.13 [[Cipriani2013]].
- Stopping lithium: more than 50% of recurrences come within 10 weeks; NICE tapers over at least 4 weeks [[Suppes1991,NICE2014CG185E]].
- Hypothyroidism on lithium: OR 5.78 [[McKnight2012]]. Check calcium for hyperparathyroidism [[McKnight2012,FDA-Lithium]].
- Ebstein's anomaly and RVOT defects: 0.60% vs 0.18%. The absolute risk is lower than once believed [[Patorno2017]].
- SILENT means neurological sequelae, most often cerebellar, lasting at least 2 months after toxicity [[Adityanjee2005]].

### In plain words
- Lithium is one of the oldest and best-studied medicines for bipolar disorder, and many guidelines list it first for long-term treatment [[NICE2014CG185E,Yatham2018]].
- Long term, lithium lowers the chance of new mood episodes, especially highs [[Severus2014E]].
- People taking lithium have lower suicide rates in many studies [[Cipriani2013,Song2017]]. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 in the US, or call Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK and Ireland.
- The amount of lithium in the blood matters. Too little may not work, and too much can be harmful, so regular blood tests are part of the treatment [[FDA-Lithium,NICE2014CG185E]].
- The blood test is usually taken about 12 hours after the last dose, often in the morning before that day's dose [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Being sick with vomiting or diarrhoea, sweating a lot, or not drinking enough can push lithium levels up. Contact your care team if this happens [[NICE2014CG185E]].
- Some common medicines, such as some painkillers (NSAIDs like ibuprofen), water pills and some blood pressure pills, can raise lithium levels. Always tell every prescriber and pharmacist you take lithium [[FDA-Lithium,NICE2014CG185E]].
- Shaking that gets worse, being very unsteady, confusion, or severe vomiting or diarrhoea can be signs of too much lithium. This needs urgent medical help [[FDA-Lithium]].
- Lithium can slow the thyroid. A simple blood test finds this, and it can be treated [[Kibirige2013,FDA-Lithium]].
- Over many years, lithium can lower kidney function in some people. Regular kidney blood tests help catch changes early [[Tondo2017,NICE2014CG185E]].
- Stopping lithium suddenly raises the chance of a quick relapse. Changes should be planned with your care team and done slowly [[Suppes1991,NICE2014CG185E]].
- If you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, talk with your care team early. Do not stop or start lithium on your own [[NICE2014CG185E,Patorno2017]].
- Many people take lithium for years with few problems and stay well [[Tondo2017,Ott2016]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Best maintenance level: NICE starts at 0.6–0.8 mmol/L [[NICE2014CG185E]]. The generic US label titrates maintenance to 0.8–1.0 mEq/L [[FDA-Lithium]]. ISBD/IGSLI recommends 0.60–0.80 with individual adjustment [[Nolen2019]]. The only direct RCT favoured 0.8–1.0 over 0.4–0.6 for relapse but found more side effects [[Gelenberg1989]]. Status: consensus-based, with little modern trial evidence.
- Lithium and suicide: meta-analyses of RCTs and registers favour lithium [[Cipriani2013,Baldessarini2006,Song2017]]. The VA trial of adding lithium after a recent suicidal event found no benefit, at mean levels around 0.5 mEq/L [[Katz2022]]. Status: established in general mood-disorder populations; uncertain as an add-on for high-risk people already in treatment.
- Lithium and the kidney: older and laboratory cohorts show more CKD stage 3 and renal failure [[Shine2015,Close2014]]. Newer cohorts show a modest added eGFR decline, no end-stage renal failure in long-term users, and a low 10-year risk before age 55 [[Tondo2017,Fransson2022,Golic2023]]. Stopping lithium slows the decline [[Fransson2025]]. Status: real but usually slow; the trade-off is individual.
- Dosing around delivery: the label advises lowering or pausing lithium 2–3 days before delivery [[FDA-Lithium]]. A 2021 observational study found no level rise after delivery and advises stable dosing to protect against postpartum relapse [[Molenaar2021]]. Status: unresolved; decided with the care team.
- Ebstein's anomaly: early registry reports suggested a large risk. A large cohort found a smaller, dose-related risk [[Patorno2017]], and a pooled cohort analysis found no significant rise in major cardiac malformations but more major malformations overall [[MunkOlsen2018]]. Status: a small absolute risk, weighed against relapse risk.
- Under-use: trials and registries favour lithium [[Miura2014,Lahteenvuo2018,Hayes2016WorldPsychiatry]], yet prescribing has fallen to about 35% ever-use [[Kessing2024Why]]. Bipolar CHOICE found lithium and quetiapine similar over 6 months [[Nierenberg2016CHOICE]]. Status: an implementation gap, with suggested solutions in specialised clinics.
- Thiazides vs loop diuretics: pharmacokinetic reviews rank thiazides as the largest diuretic effect on levels [[Finley1995]]. A population study of older adults found admissions linked with loop diuretics and ACE inhibitors but not thiazides [[Juurlink2004]]. Status: both need monitoring; the clinical risk depends on context.
- Predicting response: clinical predictors are in CANMAT [[Yatham2018]]. The genetic locus from ConLiGen [[Hou2016]] is not yet clinically usable. Status: early research.

## Provenance
- All 60 journal and guideline records were confirmed with a single PubMed esummary batch: authors, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, PMCID and DOI were taken from the record. Abstracts were read through Europe PMC (resultType=core) for every paper that supplied a number.
- Label statements come from openFDA JSON (generic Hikma label and the LITHOBID label), read section by section. The FDA-Lithobid source line keeps the borderline personality disorder companion's text unchanged; this pass also read its dosage, warnings, precautions, adverse reactions and overdosage sections.
- Trials NCT01928446 and NCT01166425 were confirmed on the ClinicalTrials.gov v2 API.
- NICE CG185 recommendations (1.7.7, 1.7.8, 1.10.14–1.10.26) were read on nice.org.uk. CANMAT/ISBD 2018 text was read in the Europe PMC full text (PMC5947163).
- The steady-state fractions in the level-over-time series are a derived calculation (standard first-order kinetics with the labelled 24-hour half-life), not a measured curve. The Suppes 1991 time points are rounded readings of the abstract's statements.
- Brief-requested items handled differently:
  - "Kessing registry" is represented by Kessing2005Suicide, Kessing2011BJP, Kessing2024Physical and Kessing2024Why. Kessing2017 was not used because the Alzheimer's companion already uses that key for a different work (drinking-water lithium and dementia).
  - No single verified source gave a time-to-steady-state figure in days. The "about 5 days" check comes from CANMAT's wording, and the steady-state curve is derived.
- Dropped for lack of a verifiable number in an abstract: the prevalence of nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and of tremor from primary studies (only review-level figures kept), and thiazide effect sizes other than Finley 1995's 25–40%.
- No source is marked unverified.
