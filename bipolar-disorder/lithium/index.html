Lithium: how it works and the evidence

Lithium is a single small ion, used in psychiatry since John Cade's report in 1949. Step inside a brain cell to see the three leading ideas about how it works, each with its evidence label, then watch the trial evidence build up row by row. 63

Schematic cell, published numbers. The neuron is a drawing, not anatomy, and no mechanism shown is proven to be why lithium works. Forest-plot squares and lines are the published point estimates and 95% confidence intervals on a log scale; the acute-mania row uses a different scale (standardised mean difference). All outcome figures are population statistics from trials and meta-analyses. 4,82,83,161,163,251,453

Mechanism 1 of 3 · inside the cell

Inositol depletion

Lithium is one small ion, Li⁺. Its label says it alters sodium transport in nerve and muscle cells and shifts catecholamine metabolism, "but the specific biochemical mechanism of lithium action in mania is unknown". No single mechanism has been proven or ruled out: lithium acts on many steps of cell signalling, usually enhancing basal and damping stimulated activity. 4,133 The first candidate: lithium blocks inositol monophosphatase (IMPase), the enzyme that recycles inositol; brain enzyme was 50% inhibited at 0.8 mM, and inositol 1-phosphate piles up while myo-inositol falls. 203 Berridge's inositol-depletion hypothesis says this damps phosphoinositide signalling most in the busiest cells Hypothesis. 44,209 In 12 people, frontal myo-inositol fell about 30% within 5–7 days, while their clinical state had not yet changed. 349

In plain wordsLithium slows a recycling step inside brain cells, so the busiest signalling pathways run down a little. Whether this is why it helps is still unproven.
Exam hookIMPase is 50% inhibited at 0.8 mM. Brain myo-inositol falls about 30% within a week, before any clinical change, which argues against a simple inositol explanation. 203,349

Out of 100 people: what the trial numbers look like

Relative risks are hard to picture. Pick a comparison and the icons fill in as published. Each figure describes a group of trial participants over that trial's own follow-up, not what will happen to one person.

Applying the pooled risk ratio for any new mood episode, RR 0.66 (95% CI 0.53–0.82), to a placebo group in which 50 of 100 have one gives about 33 of 100 on lithium (range 27–41). The placebo figure is yours to set: trials differed in length and baseline risk, and this is an illustration of a pooled relative effect, not a trial result. 453

Why guidelines still put lithium first

NICE CG185

First-line, long-term

"Offer lithium as a first-line, long-term pharmacological treatment for bipolar disorder."

If lithium is ineffective, poorly tolerated or not suitable, NICE says to consider an antipsychotic. One example of "not suitable" it gives: "the person does not agree to routine blood monitoring". 372

CANMAT/ISBD 2018

"The gold standard for maintenance"

CANMAT/ISBD calls lithium "the gold standard for maintenance treatment": it prevents both manic and depressive episodes, with a larger effect against mania, and "appears to have a degree of anti‑suicidal effects". It is first-line for acute mania and for bipolar I depression. 525

Network meta-analysis

Better evidence, harder to tolerate

Across 33 RCTs and 17 treatments, the authors concluded lithium "should remain the first-line treatment" for relapse prevention, "notwithstanding its tolerability profile": placebo was better tolerated (stopping for adverse events RR 2.58, 95% CrI 1.33–5.39). 344

Under-used

Prescribing has fallen

A 2024 review describes lithium prescribing falling internationally to about 35% ever-use or less among people with bipolar disorder, and specialised care models that raised it to 70%. A 2017 meta-review called the evidence "overwhelmingly in favour of lithium as an antisuicidal agent", and lithium still under-used. 271,460

Who tends to do well on lithium

The classic responder profile

CANMAT/ISBD 2018 describes people who usually respond well:

  • an episodic course with full remission between episodes
  • a family history of bipolar disorder, especially lithium-responsive
  • low comorbidity, especially anxiety and substance use
  • a mania, then depression, then well (euthymia) episode pattern

Relatives of lithium responders responded unequivocally in 67% of cases, against 35% in a clinic comparison group. 195,525

Full, partial and non-response

Research rates response with the Alda scale; in 1,308 patients it suggested three groups: full, partial and non-responders. Non-response is estimated at up to 30%. A chromosome 21 locus and a low schizophrenia polygenic score are linked with better response, but no genetic test is ready for clinical use. Early evidence 5,230,308

Lithium is also used in children: the US label covers bipolar I disorder from age 7, and in older adults (GERI-BD, age 60 and over) it improved mania scores more than divalproex. 132,530

Common early side effects

What the label lists first

Fine hand tremor, passing more urine (polyuria), mild thirst, nausea and general discomfort are common early in treatment. They may settle, or ease with food or a temporary dose reduction made by the prescriber. 132

In a children's trial

More frequent on lithium than placebo: nausea or vomiting 57% vs 29%, polyuria 38% vs 14%, tremor 32% vs 7%, fatigue 26% vs 4%, and a raised thyroid-stimulating hormone 25% vs 0%. 132

Weight and thinking

Weight gain was more common than with placebo (OR 1.89) but less than with olanzapine. People find weight gain and cognitive effects the most distressing and the most linked to stopping; thirst, urination, nausea and tremor are usually manageable. Higher levels bring more side effects. 164,177,321

Fewer side effects than feared?

In Bipolar CHOICE (482 people, 6 months), lithium and quetiapine did equally well on primary outcomes, and lithium carried a lower side-effect burden. Longer-term effects on kidney, thyroid and calcium are covered in Lithium levels, toxicity & interactions. 377

Real-world registers

Trials are short; national registers follow thousands of people for years. They are observational, so they show associations, and these are population statistics.

Stopping lithium

In 14 studies of 257 people who stopped lithium after an average of 30 months, more than half of new episodes came within 10 weeks; the median time to the end of remission was 5 months. Mania tended to come back sooner than depression. 479 An abrupt fall in level of more than 0.2 mmol/L was linked with a higher relapse risk. 451

NICE wording, quoted as published and not as an instruction: "If stopping lithium, reduce the dose gradually over at least 4 weeks, and preferably up to 3 months", with close monitoring for early signs of mania and depression. Any change is planned with the care team; if pregnancy is a possibility, see Pregnancy & the postpartum period. 372

With treatment and monitoring, many people take lithium for years with few problems and stay well. 391,491
