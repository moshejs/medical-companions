Part 4 · Therapy, safety & pregnancy

Pregnancy & the postpartum period

Forty weeks of pregnancy and the first twelve weeks after birth, with the published relapse figures laid along them. The weeks after birth carry the highest risk, and planning ahead with the care team changes the numbers.

Plan with your care team. Nothing here is advice to stop, start or change a medicine. Stopping suddenly carries its own risk, so any change is planned with the prescriber, ideally before pregnancy. 374,511

Schematic shapes, published end points. The pregnancy curve uses one cohort (89 women well at conception; 71% recurrence, 47% of recurrences in the first trimester). The postpartum curves end at the meta-analysis figures; their early rise follows the finding that most perinatal episodes began in the first month. The admission peak is relative risk for first-time mothers. Postpartum weeks are drawn wider than pregnancy weeks. Population figures, not predictions for one person. 107,358,501,511,519

Medicines in pregnancy, per 1,000 births

Pick a medicine to see the published malformation figures as 1,000 dots. Each figure comes from a different study and outcome, named under the dots, so compare within a panel rather than across them. Every option has to be weighed against the risk of becoming unwell, which is why NICE CG192 asks for an individual decision. 245,374

Before, during and after

Before pregnancy

Preconception planning

Among 89 women who were well at conception, 71% had a recurrence in pregnancy. Stopping mood stabilisers doubled the risk compared with continuing, and abrupt or rapid stopping shortened the median time to recurrence 11-fold compared with gradual stopping. 511

Pregnancy itself was not protective: after stopping lithium, recurrence over 40 weeks was 52% in pregnant and 58% in non-pregnant women. 509 NICE advises people taking lithium to talk to their doctor as soon as possible if they plan a pregnancy or become pregnant. 372

Lithium

What the lithium label says

The Lithobid label states that lithium "may cause fetal harm" and that registry data suggest more cardiac and other anomalies, "especially Ebstein's anomaly". 133 A large cohort found right ventricular outflow tract defects, the group including Ebstein's anomaly, in 0.60% vs 0.18% of infants, and called the effect smaller than previously thought. 399

Babies exposed late in pregnancy can show lithium toxicity (hypotonia, cyanosis, poor feeding, slow heart rate) that may take up to 14 days to clear. 132 Neonatal readmission within 28 days was 27.5% vs 14.3%. 359

Breastfeeding

Breastfeeding, from the labels

Lithium: the label says nursing "should not be undertaken during lithium therapy except in rare and unusual circumstances". In 10 mother–infant pairs, trough lithium was 0.76 mEq/L in mothers, 0.35 in milk and 0.16 in infants, with no serious infant adverse events. The evidence is small and the question is unresolved. 133,510

Valproate: found in milk at 1–10% of maternal serum; the label advises watching breastfed infants for signs of liver damage. Lamotrigine: infant levels can rise if a dose raised in pregnancy is not reduced after delivery. Cariprazine: no human lactation studies. 128,130,137

After birth

Postpartum psychosis is an emergency

It is rare, about 1 to 3 per 1,000 births (reported 0.89–2.6), and starts mostly in the first weeks. A 1987 record-linkage study concluded that most puerperal psychoses are bipolar-spectrum illnesses. 256,358,501

Sudden confusion, strange beliefs or not sleeping at all after giving birth need urgent help. In one clinic, none of 20 women with psychosis limited to the postpartum period who started preventive treatment right after delivery relapsed, against 44.4% of those who declined it. 39,245

CrisisSuicide is a leading cause of maternal death, and the weeks after birth are a time of extra risk. 245 If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact local emergency services or a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123). Bipolar Companion is an educational synthesis of the published literature on bipolar disorder and lithium therapy, made for health-science, medical and nursing courses and for people living with bipolar disorder, their families and carers.

It is not medical advice. It cannot diagnose, and nothing here should start, stop or change a medicine without your own care team. Criteria, screening tools and the lithium level explainer are for learning, not self-diagnosis or reading your own results. Doses, levels and monitoring schedules are quoted as published in labels and guidelines, never as instructions; suspected lithium toxicity is an emergency.
