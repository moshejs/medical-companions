Valproate, lamotrigine & carbamazepine

Three medicines first made for epilepsy also treat or prevent mood episodes. Each has a different strength, its own boxed warning, and its own reasons for careful planning. Watch lamotrigine's slow start as the label publishes it, see why valproate and pregnancy need planning, and compare the three side by side.

What the rules say

The Depakote boxed warning: "Valproate can cause major congenital malformations, particularly neural tube defects (e.g., spina bifida). In addition, valproate can cause decreased IQ scores and neurodevelopmental disorders following in utero exposure." The label says it should not be used in women with bipolar disorder who are pregnant or plan to become pregnant unless other medicines have failed or are otherwise unacceptable. [[FDA-Depakote]]

In April 2018 the UK MHRA banned valproate prescribing for women and girls of childbearing potential who are not in its Pregnancy Prevention Programme. [[Iacobucci2018]] NICE CG185 reproduces the later MHRA measures: do not start valproate for the first time in anyone under 55, male or female, "unless 2 specialists independently agree and document that there is no other effective and tolerated treatment, or there are compelling reasons that the reproductive risks do not apply". [[NICE2014CG185F]]

Plan with your care team

Planning, not stopping suddenly

Guidelines frame valproate and pregnancy as something to plan well ahead with the care team. Stopping valproate abruptly is itself flagged as a risk in the label, so any change is planned with the prescriber, never made alone. [[FDA-Depakote,NICE2014CG185F]]

Over time, EURAP's overall malformation rate fell from 6.1% (1998–2004) to 3.7% (2015–2022) as prescribing moved away from valproate and carbamazepine. [[Battino2024EURAP]] For relapse risk around birth and planning the other medicines, see Pregnancy & the postpartum period.

Stopping

Slowly, and planned

NICE CG185 says that when valproate, lamotrigine or an antipsychotic is stopped, the dose should be reduced "gradually over at least 4 weeks to minimise the risk of relapse". [[NICE2014CG185F]]

Blood levels

Not checked routinely

Unlike lithium, NICE says plasma valproate and lamotrigine levels should not be measured routinely "unless there is evidence of ineffectiveness, poor adherence or toxicity". In the label's mania trials, valproate was dosed to a trough between 50 and 125 mcg/mL. [[NICE2014CG185F,FDA-Depakote]] For lithium's own levels, see Lithium levels.

Compared with lithium

Where lithium fits

NICE asks clinicians to "explain that lithium is the most effective long‑term treatment for bipolar disorder". In BALANCE, lithium alone or with valproate prevented relapse better than valproate alone. [[NICE2014CG185F,Geddes2010BALANCE]] See Lithium: how it works.

Early evidence

Valproate in men

NICE (2025) reproduces MHRA advice that men use effective contraception during valproate and for 3 months after, noting a "possible association" with neurodevelopmental disorders in children. A 2026 Taiwanese cohort of 2.58 million births, including sibling comparisons, found no association (autism HR 0.86, 0.61–1.22). Regulators have kept their precautions. [[NICE2014CG185F,Feng2026]]

With the right medicine, many people stay well for long stretches: in the pooled lamotrigine trials, the median time before needing treatment for a new episode more than doubled compared with placebo. These are group averages. [[Goodwin2004Lamotrigine]]
