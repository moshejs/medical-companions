Psychoeducation & talking therapies

Talking therapies are added to medicines, not used instead of them. Watch two landmark trials unfold over follow-up, compare the main therapies side by side, and try the daily-routine idea behind social rhythm therapy.

NICE recommends that, to prevent relapse, people with bipolar disorder are offered "a structured psychological intervention (individual, group or family), which has been designed for bipolar disorder and has a published evidence-based manual", alongside medicines. [[NICE2014CG185]] A Lancet seminar states that adding psychosocial treatments and involving caregivers "have been shown to improve health outcomes". [[McIntyre2020Lancet]]

Social rhythms: try a week

Interpersonal and social rhythm therapy (IPSRT) adds one ingredient to interpersonal psychotherapy: regular daily routines. In its trial, IPSRT made routines more regular, and people who became more regular were less likely to have a recurrence (Early evidence for routine as the mechanism). [[Frank2005IPSRT]] Switch each routine between steady and irregular and watch the week's regularity change.

More approaches, with evidence labels

Early evidence

Early-warning-sign training

Seven to 12 sessions teaching people to spot their early symptoms and get prompt treatment lengthened the time to a first manic relapse (65 vs 17 weeks at the 25th centile) and cut manic relapses over 18 months. It did not change depressive relapse. [[Perry1999BMJ]] Coping early at the prodromal stage went with fewer relapses in a naturalistic study. [[Lam2001Prodromes]] A Cochrane review of these interventions is under way. [[Gupta2025Cochrane]]

See your own warning signs on a mood chart in Living well.

Established

Collaborative and specialist care

Nurse care managers with group psychoeducation and phone monitoring cut time with significant mania symptoms (19.2 vs 24.7 weeks), but not depressive time. [[Simon2006Care]] A VA collaborative chronic care model cut time in episodes by a net 6.2 weeks over 3 years and was cost-neutral. [[Bauer2006CCM]] A specialised mood clinic early in the illness reduced readmission (HR 0.60). [[Kessing2013Clinic]]

Early evidence

Online programmes and apps

An online psychoeducation trial found all three arms improved equally; adding online peer support raised adherence. [[Proudfoot2012]] MoodSwings 2.0 gave a small benefit for depression symptoms. [[Gliddon2019]] Daily smartphone self-monitoring in MONARCA I did not reduce symptoms. [[FaurholtJepsen2015]] Digital tools are not a substitute for care.

Early evidence

Children and young people

In teenagers with bipolar disorder, family-focused therapy did not change recurrence but sped recovery from depressive symptoms (HR 1.85). [[Miklowitz2008FFTA]] In high-risk youth, 12 sessions of FFT delayed the next mood episode (HR 0.55), mainly depressive episodes. [[Miklowitz2020FFT]] Multifamily psychoeducation lowered mood severity in children aged 8–12. [[Fristad2009]] More on families in Families, work & school.

Open question

Does timing matter?

Group psychoeducation helped most in people with 1–7 previous episodes (HR 0.28), and CBT helped people with fewer than 12 previous episodes but not those with more. Together these suggest structured therapy may work best early in the illness. Early evidence [[Morriss2016PARADES,Scott2006CBT]]

Open question

Which ingredient matters?

The network meta-analysis favours family or group skills practice; another found only carer-focused interventions reduced relapse. Trials differ a lot and certainty is low to moderate. [[Miklowitz2021NMA,Chatterton2017,Oud2016]]

In plain words. Learning about the illness in a group, or with family, helps people stay well longer. In one study, people who took a 6-month class about bipolar disorder spent about 154 days unwell over 5 years, compared with about 586 days for those who did not. [[Colom2009,Miklowitz2021NMA]]
