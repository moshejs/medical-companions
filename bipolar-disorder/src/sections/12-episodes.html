Part 1 · Understanding bipolar disorder

Mania, hypomania & depression

Bipolar disorder is defined by episodes: stretches of days or weeks when mood, energy, sleep, speech and thinking all change together. Scrub through one illustrative course to watch a hypomanic, a manic, a mixed and a depressive stretch build, and see when each one is long enough to count.

Schematic. One illustrative course, not a prediction for anyone; real courses vary widely. The height of the line and the band edges are drawing conventions: DSM-5-TR separates mania from hypomania by duration, impairment, hospitalisation and psychosis, not by how "high" mood goes. The side panels are pictures, not measurements. [[APA2022,Yatham2018]]

Mania and hypomania side by side

Both need the same core: a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive or irritable mood plus abnormally and persistently increased activity or energy, with at least 3 of the same 7 symptoms (4 if the mood is only irritable). What separates them is how long they last and how much they disrupt life. [[APA2022,Yatham2018]]

Things worth knowing

Mania

Mania is not always happy

Among 316 inpatients with mania, the commonest signs were motor activation, accelerated thinking, pressured speech and decreased sleep. Irritability, dysphoric mood and mood lability were also prominent, so mania is "not a purely euphoric state". [[Cassidy1998]]

Mixed

Mixed features are common

DSM-5 replaced the old "mixed episode" with a "with mixed features" specifier. In a meta-analysis, mixed features were present in 11.6% of major depressive episodes and 26.8% of manic or hypomanic episodes. ICD-11 still keeps a mixed episode lasting at least 2 weeks. [[Mason2016,Na2021,Stein2020BMC]]

Depression

Bipolar depression looks like other depression

There are no separate criteria for "bipolar depression": it is a major depressive episode in someone with bipolar disorder, and no single feature reliably tells it apart from unipolar depression. That is why asking about past highs matters. [[Yatham2018,Mitchell2008]]

Psychosis

Losing touch with reality

A meta-analysis of 54 studies found lifetime psychotic symptoms (delusions or hallucinations) in 63% of people with bipolar I and 22% with bipolar II. Grandiose delusions are the commonest kind. Psychosis can mask the mood symptoms. [[Aminoff2022,Dunayevich2000]]

Course

Highs and lows often come in pairs

Predominant polarity: about 30.0% of people mainly have manic or hypomanic episodes and 28.5% mainly depressive ones; a depressive onset was strongly linked to depressive predominance (OR 12.09). In 206 people with bipolar II, 80% had a "biphasic" course, one episode followed straight away by the opposite pole. [[Bartoli2024,Benazzi2006]]

Established

Energy joined mood in 2013

DSM-5 made increased activity or energy a required core symptom alongside the mood change. Applied to 907 patients, it cut the number with a hypomanic or manic visit by 34%; in STEP-BD it lowered prevalence but did not change longitudinal outcomes. [[Mason2016,Fredskild2019,MachadoVieira2017]]

Recovery

Episodes end, and most people recover

In the McLean-Harvard first-episode study, 98% of people hospitalised for a first manic episode reached syndromal recovery within 2 years, half of them by 5.4 weeks. Functional recovery was slower: 43% regained their previous work and living status within 2 years, which is why ongoing care matters. [[Tohen2003]]

Safety

When a low feels unbearable

Depressive episodes can bring thoughts of death. These are symptoms of an illness that is treatable, and help works. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact local emergency services or a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123). See Suicide risk & crisis safety.
