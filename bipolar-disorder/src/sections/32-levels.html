Lithium levels, toxicity & interactions

The amount of lithium in the blood matters: too little may not work and too much can harm, so blood tests are part of the treatment. Watch a week of dosing build to a steady level, see how the kidney handles lithium, and learn what the published ranges mean. [[FDA-Lithium,NICE2014CG185]]

Teaching tool, not medical adviceEvery range, dose and schedule here is quoted as published in a label or guideline, never as an instruction. Nothing on this page can interpret your own result: that needs your care team, who look at how you are as well as the number. Worsening shaking, being very unsteady, confusion, or severe vomiting or diarrhoea can be signs of too much lithium: this needs urgent medical help, so contact emergency services. [[FDA-Lithium]]

The kidney: lithium is handled like sodium

Lithium is not broken down by the liver. The kidney filters it and about 80% of what is filtered is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule, much as sodium is; when the body is short of sodium or water, it holds on to more lithium. Switch on a situation or medicine to see the published size of its effect on a steady level. [[FDA-Lithium,FDA-Lithobid]]

Toxicity: signs by severity

The boxed warning: "Lithium toxicity is closely related to serum lithium concentrations, and can occur at doses close to therapeutic concentrations." [[FDA-Lithium,FDA-Lithobid]]

Pattern

Chronic poisoning is the common one

Lithium poisoning is acute, acute-on-chronic or chronic. Chronic poisoning is the most common and usually unintentional: intake outstrips what the kidneys clear, often during volume depletion, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, illness or a new interacting drug. In 97 cases, 26 of 28 with severe neurotoxicity came from chronic therapeutic use (OR 136 against overdose). [[BairdGunning2017,Oakley2001]]

Who is at risk

Older age, kidneys, thyroid

Independent risk factors for chronic poisoning were nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (adjusted OR 26.96), thyroid dysfunction (9.30) and age over 50 (6.20). In Ontario, 3.9% of adults aged 66 or over taking lithium were admitted at least once for toxicity over 1992–2001. [[Oakley2001,Juurlink2004]]

Perspective

Rare with monitoring

In a Swedish cohort of 1,340 people, levels of 1.5 mmol/L or more occurred 0.01 times per patient-year; of 91 episodes, 34% needed intensive care and 13% haemodialysis, with no deaths. The authors said physicians "should not withhold lithium for fear of intoxication" but should screen for it readily. [[Ott2016]]

Lasting harm

SILENT

The syndrome of irreversible lithium-effectuated neurotoxicity means sequelae lasting at least 2 months after lithium stops; a 2005 review found 90 published cases, most often with persistent cerebellar problems. [[Adityanjee2005,FDA-Lithium]]

How toxicity is treated (as published)

No antidote; charcoal does not help

There is no specific antidote. Mild toxicity can usually be managed by lowering or stopping the dose. Activated charcoal is not recommended because it does not bind lithium ions significantly. [[FDA-Lithium]]

Haemodialysis

The generic label calls haemodialysis "the treatment of choice" for severe toxicity; levels can rebound as lithium leaves the tissues. EXTRIP graded lithium as dialyzable. [[FDA-Lithium,Decker2015]]

EXTRIP thresholds

Recommended with impaired kidney function and a level above 4.0 mEq/L, or with decreased consciousness, seizures or life-threatening dysrhythmias at any level; suggested above 5.0, with significant confusion, or if the level is expected to take more than 36 hours to fall below 1.0. The evidence is very low quality, mostly case reports. [[Decker2015,BairdGunning2017]]

Sick days

NICE advises people taking lithium to seek medical attention if they develop diarrhoea or vomiting or become acutely ill for any reason, and to keep up fluids after sweating, in hot climates, with fever, during long immobility or with a chest infection. [[NICE2014CG185]]

Interactions

Medicine or situationEffect on lithium (as published)
Thiazide diureticsThe greatest potential among diuretics: levels often rise 25–40% after a thiazide starts. Loop and potassium-sparing diuretics have minor, variable effects on levels. [[Finley1995]]
ACE inhibitors, ARBsStarting an ACE inhibitor raised steady-state lithium by 36.1% and cut clearance by 25.5% in 20 people. Renin-angiotensin system antagonists can raise levels. [[Finley1996,FDA-Lithium]]
Older adults (Ontario)Admission for toxicity rose within a month of starting a loop diuretic (RR 5.5) or an ACE inhibitor (RR 7.6); thiazides and NSAIDs were not independently associated in this study. [[Juurlink2004]]
NSAIDsReduce renal blood flow and lithium clearance; indomethacin and piroxicam significantly raise levels, COX-2 inhibitors appear to; celecoxib raised levels by about 17% in volunteers. NICE warns people taking lithium not to take over-the-counter NSAIDs, and says a prescribed NSAID should be taken regularly, not as needed, with monthly level checks until stable. [[FDA-Lithium,FDA-Lithobid,NICE2014CG185]]
MetronidazoleNitroimidazole antibiotics can raise levels. Starting any potentially interacting drug, often an antibiotic, was common in people whose level rose to 1.3 mmol/L or more. [[FDA-Lithium,Wilting2005]]
Lower levelsAcetazolamide, urea, xanthines and alkalinising agents such as sodium bicarbonate increase excretion; SGLT2 inhibitors may lower serum lithium. [[FDA-Lithium]]
Other effectsCalcium-channel blockers may increase neurological effects; iodide may cause hypothyroidism; lithium may prolong neuromuscular blockers; serotonin syndrome with serotonergic drugs; an encephalopathic syndrome with antipsychotics. [[FDA-Lithium]]
Sweating, fever, low saltProtracted sweating, diarrhoea or fever lower tolerance; a low-sodium state raises lithium retention, so the labels stress a normal salt intake. Irregular dispensing was also linked with high levels (OR 3.14). [[FDA-Lithium,FDA-Lithobid,Wilting2005]]

People taking lithium are advised to tell every prescriber and pharmacist that they take it. [[FDA-Lithium,NICE2014CG185]]

Long-term effects: kidney, thyroid, parathyroid

OrganWhat the studies foundWhat it means
Kidney: concentrating urineUrinary concentrating ability reduced by 15% of the normal maximum. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (thirst, passing a lot of urine) can begin within weeks; usually reversible, perhaps only partly after long use. [[McKnight2012,FDA-Lithium]]Thirst and passing more urine are common; amiloride is named in the label as a possible treatment.
Kidney: filtration (eGFR)Meta-analysis: mean GFR −6.22 mL/min, not significant; 0.5% received renal replacement therapy. Over a mean 18 years, 29.5% had an eGFR below 60 at least once and none developed end-stage kidney failure. Lithium added 0.54 mL/min/1.73 m² of decline per year of treatment. [[McKnight2012,Tondo2017,Fransson2022]]Real but usually slow. Lifetime risk of stage 4 kidney disease was 13.9–18.6% starting at 55–74, with a 10-year risk of 0–0.7% starting at 18–54. [[Golic2023]]
Kidney: after stoppingIn 168 people who stopped after at least 4.5 years, yearly eGFR change went from −1.58 to −0.02 mL/min/1.73 m². Early evidence [[Fransson2025]]Decisions on continuing may need a kidney specialist. [[NICE2014CG185]]
ThyroidClinical hypothyroidism more common than with placebo (OR 5.78); TSH up by 4.00 on average; laboratory data HR 2.31. Hyperthyroidism is rare. [[McKnight2012,Shine2015,Kibirige2013]]A blood test finds it, and lithium-associated hypothyroidism is usually treated with thyroid hormone. [[Kibirige2013,FDA-Lithium]]
Parathyroid, calciumCalcium up 0.09 mmol/L and parathyroid hormone up 7.32 pg/mL; 18% had lithium-associated hyperparathyroidism after a mean 13.5 years; hypercalcaemia prevalence 26% in another cohort. [[McKnight2012,Meehan2015,Meehan2018]]Calcium is checked before and during treatment; it is often multiglandular and may persist after stopping. [[McKnight2012,FDA-Lithium]]
