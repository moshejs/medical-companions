# A · Epidemiology, global burden & history

Pass date: 2026-10-30. Modules served: home, what, history.

This pass covers the global and regional burden of TB from the WHO Global Tuberculosis Report 2025 (2024 data): people who fell ill, deaths, incidence rates, the high-burden countries, sex and age, the gap between notified and estimated cases, treatment success and the trends from 2000 to 2024, including the COVID-19 disruption. It also covers how many people carry TB infection, TB in low-incidence countries, and the history of TB from ancient remains to the 2023 UN high-level meeting. All burden and outcome figures are population statistics. They describe groups, not any one person. TB is a notifiable disease, and anyone with symptoms such as a long cough, fever, night sweats or weight loss should see a clinician.

## Findings

### home · Global burden in 2024 (headline numbers)
- In 2024 an estimated 10.7 million people (95% UI 9.9–11.5 million) fell ill with TB worldwide, a rate of 131 new cases per 100,000 population (95% UI 122–141) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- About 1.23 million people died of TB in 2024 (95% UI 1.13–1.33 million) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Of these deaths, 1.08 million (95% UI 0.99–1.18 million) were among HIV-negative people and 150,000 (95% UI 120,000–183,000) among people with HIV [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- WHO counts TB deaths in people with HIV as HIV deaths, with TB as a contributory cause. This is why "TB deaths" and "deaths caused by TB" can be quoted with or without the 150,000 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- WHO calls TB the world's leading cause of death from a single infectious agent and one of the top 10 causes of death. It is also the leading killer of people with HIV and a major cause of deaths related to antimicrobial resistance [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- On WHO's latest all-cause estimates (2021), TB was the tenth leading cause of death worldwide and second to COVID-19 among single infectious agents. In 2024 only 70,000 COVID-19 deaths were officially reported to WHO, far below TB deaths [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- TB deaths among HIV-negative people in 2024 (1.08 million) were almost double the 630,000 (95% UI 600,000–660,000) deaths caused by HIV/AIDS [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The global case fatality rate (the share of people who develop TB who die from it, counting those never diagnosed) was 11.5% in 2024. It was 11.8% in 2023, 12.6% in 2022 and 13.6% in 2019 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- People living with HIV made up 5.8% (95% UI 5.0–6.7%) of new TB cases in 2024, about 619,000 people. The share has fallen steadily from a peak of 17% in 2000 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- In parts of southern Africa more than 50% of people with a new TB episode are living with HIV [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- WHO estimates that TB treatment averted 83 million deaths from 2000 to 2024 (a modelled population figure) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The 2024 data came from 184 countries and areas, with more than 99% of the world's population and TB cases [[WHO-GTR2025]].

### home · Where TB occurs: regions and countries
- By WHO region, new cases in 2024 were 34% South-East Asia, 27% Western Pacific, 25% Africa, 8.6% Eastern Mediterranean, 3.3% Americas and 1.9% Europe. In WHO's grouping, Indonesia is in the Western Pacific Region [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- HIV-negative TB deaths in 2024 were 40% South-East Asia, 29% Africa, 19% Western Pacific, 7.9% Eastern Mediterranean, 2.1% Americas and 1.4% Europe. Most TB deaths among people with HIV were in the African Region [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- 2024 death numbers by region (HIV-negative / with HIV): Africa 315,000 / 108,000; South-East Asia 433,000 / 13,000; Western Pacific 211,000 / 17,000; Eastern Mediterranean 85,000 / 1,300; Americas 22,000 / 7,800; Europe 15,000 / 3,800 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- HIV-negative TB mortality in 2024 ranged from 25 per 100,000 (African Region) and 24 (South-East Asia) to 2.1 (Americas) and 1.6 (Europe). The global rate was 13 per 100,000 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The 30 high TB burden countries had 87% of the world's new cases in 2024 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Eight countries had two-thirds (67%) of cases: India 25%, Indonesia 10%, the Philippines 6.8%, China 6.5%, Pakistan 6.3%, Nigeria 4.8%, the Democratic Republic of the Congo 3.9% and Bangladesh 3.6%. The top five had 55% [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- National incidence in 2024 ranged from fewer than 10 to more than 500 cases per 100,000 population. Twelve African Region countries had rates above 300 per 100,000 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- 62 countries had a low incidence of TB (<10 per 100,000), mostly in the Americas and Europe, plus a few in the Eastern Mediterranean and Western Pacific [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- In WHO's 2024 country estimates, the highest rates per 100,000 were Kiribati 945, Papua New Guinea 664, the Philippines 625 (95% UI 344–1020) and Lesotho 548. India was 187 (160–218), China 49 (40–59) and South Africa 389 (242–571) (WHO database values) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- WHO's 2024 estimates for selected low-incidence countries, per 100,000: United States 3.2 (2.6–3.9), Germany 5.4, Canada 6.2, Australia 6.9, France 7.8, United Kingdom 9.7 and Japan 9.8 (WHO database values) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The three global TB watchlist countries are Cambodia, the Russian Federation and Zimbabwe [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- In 2024, notifications rose throughout 2019–2024 in the Central African Republic, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Nigeria. They kept falling on a long-term trend in China, the Democratic People's Republic of Korea and the Russian Federation [[WHO-GTR2025]].

### home · Who gets TB: sex and age
- In 2024 an estimated 5.8 million adult men (aged ≥15; 54%), 3.7 million adult women (35%) and 1.2 million children aged 0–14 (11%) fell ill with TB [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Most new cases are in people aged 25–54. The Western Pacific is the exception, with the most cases in people aged 65 and over. WHO attributes this to China's ageing population and to reactivation of infections acquired long ago [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- 614,000 adult men, 442,000 adult women and 174,000 children under 15 died from TB in 2024 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Among HIV-negative people who died of TB in 2024, 49.7% were men, 34.4% women and 15.9% children under 15. Among people with HIV the split was 51.9%, 46.6% and 1.5% [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Of the 172,000 HIV-negative children under 15 who died, 122,000 were under 5 (95% UI 71,000–174,000) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- National TB prevalence surveys consistently find that men make up 66–75% of adults with TB [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- A meta-analysis of 56 prevalence surveys in low- and middle-income countries found a pooled male-to-female ratio of 2.21 (95% CI 1.92–2.54) for bacteriologically positive TB and 2.51 (2.07–3.04) for smear-positive TB [[Horton2016]].
- In the same review, the prevalence-to-notification ratio was 1.55 times higher in men than women (95% CI 1.25–1.91; 34 surveys). Men are more often missed by TB services [[Horton2016]].
- In 31 high-burden countries reporting sex-disaggregated outcomes in 2024, treatment success was slightly higher in women and girls (90%) than in men and boys (87%). The share of people who died after starting treatment was 45% higher in men and boys [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- WHO cites work suggesting that transmission from men accounts for most new infections in women, girls, men and boys. Reaching men is therefore a priority for everyone's health (Early evidence) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Writers on "missing men" argue that men's under-use of TB care stems from structural factors such as work, income and health-service design, not individual blame, and call for multicomponent interventions [[Chikovore2020]].

### home · Notified cases and the "missing" gap
- In 2024, 8.3 million people were newly diagnosed with TB and notified, a small increase from 8.2 million in 2023 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Notifications were 78% (95% UI 72–84%) of estimated new cases. The gap of about 2.4 million people who were undiagnosed or unreported was down from 2.6 million in 2023 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The gap widened to a best estimate of 4.5 million in 2020, during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Notifications covered 79% of estimated cases in adult men and women but only 58% in children aged 0–14 and 50% in children under 5 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Ten countries had 63% of the global gap in 2024. Five had about 40%: Indonesia 10%, India 8.8%, the Philippines 7.5%, Pakistan 7.2% and China 6.9% [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Of people notified in 2024, 84% had pulmonary TB and 16% extrapulmonary TB. 64% of pulmonary cases were bacteriologically confirmed [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Of notified people with a new episode, 55% were men, 36% women and 8.3% children aged 0–14. Children have made up 7–8% of notifications for years [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- In 106 countries with 87% of notifications, 15% of people newly diagnosed in 2024 were found through screening [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- A WHO-recommended rapid test was the first test for 54% of people newly diagnosed in 2024, up from 48% in 2023 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- WHO cautions that notifications can also be inflated by overdiagnosis, especially without bacteriological confirmation. In 2024 bacteriological confirmation was under 50% in Papua New Guinea (45%) and the Philippines (46%) [[WHO-GTR2025]].

### home · Treatment outcomes (population statistics)
- Treatment success for drug-susceptible TB on first-line regimens was 88% for the 2023 cohort, up from 81% in 2016. It ranged from 71% in the Americas to 93% in the Eastern Mediterranean Region [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Success among people with HIV was 79% in 2023, up from 75% in 2017 and 68% in 2012 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Treatment success for MDR/RR-TB has improved to 71% (2022 cohort) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- In 2024, 164,545 people were treated for rifampicin-resistant TB, 42% of the roughly 390,000 who developed it. WHO says only about 2 in 5 people with drug-resistant TB accessed treatment [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- In 2024, 91% of people newly diagnosed with TB and known to have HIV were on antiretroviral therapy. Measured against everyone with HIV estimated to have developed TB, coverage was only 61% [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- In the pre-chemotherapy era, TB case fatality in Cape Town, New York City and London was 45–60% a year. After combination chemotherapy it fell below 10% in all three cities [[Hermans2015]].
- Before drugs, pulmonary TB had about 50% mortality. Chemotherapy turned it into a condition that can be cured [[Mitchison2005]].

### home · Trends 2000–2024 and the COVID-19 disruption
- Notifications of people newly diagnosed with TB fell from 7.1 million in 2019 to 5.8 million in 2020, then recovered to 6.4 million in 2021 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- WHO assumes these shortfalls left more people undiagnosed and untreated, raised transmission and pushed incidence up in 2021–2023 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The number of people falling ill fell in 2024 for the first time since 2020, after three years of increases. The 10.7 million was a 1% fall from 10.8 million in 2023, but still above 10.3 million in 2020 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The incidence rate fell 1.7% between 2023 and 2024, back to the 2020 level (the pre-COVID historic low) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- TB deaths rose with the COVID-related disruptions in 2020–2021, then fell: down 3% between 2023 and 2024 and 13.8% compared with 2021 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The "historically high" notifications of 2022–2024 reflect a backlog of people whose diagnosis COVID-19 delayed, plus a real rise in TB from extra transmission [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- In the African Region notifications fell only slightly in 2020. By 2024 they were 38% above the 2019 level [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- In 2024 the Americas' incidence rate rose for the fourth year in a row. WHO attributes this to 2020 case-detection shortfalls and an incomplete recovery [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Early pandemic models predicted hundreds of thousands of extra TB deaths from service disruption. A 2021 review of country data confirmed large disruptions to TB services and greater vulnerability, but found limited evidence on changes in transmission [[McQuaid2021]].
- In 2019 TB was the most common single-pathogen cause of death. In 2020 COVID-19 displaced it [[Chakaya2021]].
- Summed WHO country estimates show slow progress over 25 years: about 11.6 million cases (188 per 100,000) in 2000 against about 10.6 million (130 per 100,000) in 2024. Deaths fell from about 2.9 million to 1.2 million (WHO database country sums, DPRK excluded) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Older estimates were built with different methods and cannot be joined into one series: about 7.5 million cases and 2.5 million deaths in 1990 [[Raviglione1995]], and 10.4 million cases with 1.3 million HIV-negative deaths in 2016 [[Glaziou2018]].
- In 1995 WHO's authors projected 90 million new cases and 30 million deaths in 1990–1999 if control did not improve. They noted that only 3.8 million cases were reported in 1990 (Hypothesis: projection) [[Raviglione1995]].

### home · End TB milestones: reached or missed
- The End TB Strategy targets were endorsed by all WHO Member States at the World Health Assembly in 2014. They call for 80% lower incidence and 90% fewer deaths by 2030, and 90% and 95% by 2035, compared with 2015 [[Floyd2018]].
- Between 2015 and 2024 the incidence rate fell by a net 12.3%, far from the 50% milestone for 2025 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- TB deaths fell by a net 29% over the same period, far from the 75% milestone for 2025 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The European Region came closest (incidence −39%, deaths −49%), driven mainly by the Russian Federation. The African Region followed (incidence −28%, deaths −46%) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- 101 countries cut incidence by more than 20% (the 2020 milestone), and 30 cut it by at least 50%. 65 countries cut deaths by at least 35% [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- South Africa is the one high-burden country estimated to have reached the 2025 incidence milestone. Kenya, the United Republic of Tanzania and Zambia were close [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- In 37 countries the 2024 incidence rate was more than 5% above 2015. Most are in the Americas, but they include Indonesia, Myanmar and the Philippines [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The Russian Federation cut TB deaths by a net 61% between 2015 and 2024 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The independent GBD 2021 analysis also found the 2020 milestones missed. It estimated incidence down 6.26% and deaths down 11.9% between 2015 and 2020, with 9.40 million cases and 1.35 million deaths in 2021 [[GBD2021TB2024]].
- GBD found the slowest progress in older adults: deaths fell 35.3% in children under 5 but only 3.29% in people aged 70 and over (2015–2020) [[GBD2021TB2024]].

### what · How many people carry TB infection
- WHO's 1999 estimate was that one-third of the world's population had TB infection. This was later revised to about one-quarter [[Cohen2019ERJ]].
- A model of 131 infection surveys (1950–2011) plus WHO prevalence data estimated that 23.0% (95% UI 20.4–26.4%) of the world's population, about 1.7 billion people, had TB infection in 2014 [[Houben2016]].
- In that model about 80% of people with TB infection lived in the South-East Asia, Western Pacific and African regions. 0.8% of the world's population (55.5 million people) had been infected within the past 2 years and were at highest risk [[Houben2016]].
- A meta-analysis of 88 studies from 36 countries (2005–2018) put global TB infection prevalence at 24.8% (95% CI 19.7–30.0%) by IGRA and 21.2% (17.9–24.4%) by a 10-mm skin-test cut-off [[Cohen2019ERJ]].
- The 1.7 billion figure is the basis of WHO's End TB target papers, which call for new tools for "the approximately 1.7 billion people infected" [[Floyd2018]].
- Modelling that allows for self-clearance estimates that 24.4% (95% UI 17.8–32.6%) of people clear the infection within 10 years and 73.1% (64.6–81.7%) over a lifetime (Early evidence) [[Emery2021]].
- If self-clearance is real, far fewer people carry live bacteria. In India, China and Japan the population at risk would be 35–80% smaller, while each truly infected person faces a higher lifetime risk: 17.0% against 12.6% if infection were lifelong (Early evidence) [[Emery2021]].
- Even with no new infections after 2015, existing infection alone would produce about 16.5 cases per 100,000 a year in 2035 and 8.3 per 100,000 in 2050. That is far above the elimination threshold (Hypothesis: model projection) [[Houben2016]].

### what · TB in low-incidence countries (US and Europe)
- In 2023 the United States provisionally reported 9,615 TB cases (2.9 per 100,000). This was 16% more than 2022 (8,320; 2.5 per 100,000), 8% above 2019 (8,895), and the highest count since 2013 [[Williams2024]].
- US TB incidence fell steadily from 1993 to 2019. In 2020, the first pandemic year, it dropped 19.9%, from 2.71 per 100,000 in 2019 to 2.16. It then rose to 2.37 in 2021 (7,860 cases) [[Filardo2022]].
- In 2017 the US reported 9,093 cases (2.8 per 100,000), then the lowest rate and number since national reporting began in 1953 [[CDC2018WTBD]].
- In 2023, 76% of US cases with known birth origin (7,259 of 9,573) were in people born outside the United States. The rate was 15.0 per 100,000 in non-US-born people and 0.8 in US-born people [[Williams2024]].
- CDC stresses that most US residents are at minimal risk and that the rise calls for engaging affected communities and their clinicians [[Williams2024]].
- In New York City, TB cases nearly tripled from 1978 to 1992 and drug-resistant isolates more than doubled. Cases then fell 21% from 1992 to 1994 as directly observed therapy grew from under 100 people to nearly 1,300 [[Frieden1995]].
- In that decline, cases rose among older and foreign-born New Yorkers. The authors read this as reactivation of infections acquired years earlier, not recent spread in the city [[Frieden1995]].
- In several low-incidence countries, more than 75% of notified TB cases are in people of foreign origin [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- About one-third of people with TB in the EU/EEA were born in, or are citizens of, another country [[Vasiliu2025]].
- EU/EEA notifications among people of foreign origin fell 19%, from 13,920 in 2019 to about 11,270 in 2020, then rose to 14,049 in 2023. Notifications in people from Ukraine rose 316.4% between 2019 and 2023 [[Vasiliu2025]].
- In 20 low-incidence European countries (2014–2020), 104,371 migrants with TB were reported. Rates in recently arrived migrants appeared up to 11 times those in their countries of origin [[Domaszewska2025]].
- Framing: these patterns reflect TB infection acquired where TB is common, together with the stresses of migration and barriers to care. The response is accessible, free screening and care, not exclusion. The authors call for tailored prevention and care for migrant communities [[Domaszewska2025,Vasiliu2025]].

### history · Ancient disease, phthisis and consumption
- *M. tuberculosis* DNA and mycolic acid markers were found in the bones of a woman and infant buried at Atlit-Yam, a now-submerged Neolithic village in the Eastern Mediterranean dated to 9,250–8,160 years ago [[Hershkovitz2008]].
- In 85 Egyptian mummies from Thebes (about 2050–500 BC), 25 samples carried *M. tuberculosis* complex DNA. None had an *M. bovis* pattern, which argues against the idea that human TB came from cattle TB [[Zink2003]].
- Egyptian remains from about 2400 BC show the spinal deformity of Pott's disease [[Barberis2017]].
- The ancient Greeks called TB "phthisis". Hippocrates described it as a fatal disease, especially of young adults [[Barberis2017]].
- In 1720 the English physician Benjamin Marten first suggested an infectious cause, in "A new theory of Consumption". Johann Lukas Schönlein coined the word "tuberculosis" in the mid-19th century [[Barberis2017]].
- In 18th-century Western Europe, TB mortality reached as high as 900 deaths per 100,000 people a year [[Barberis2017]].
- In 18th- and 19th-century Europe and North America, TB was called "Captain of All These Men of Death" and caused about one in four deaths [[Barberis2017,Daniel2006]].
- Laennec's early-19th-century work began the understanding of TB pathology. In 1865 Jean-Antoine Villemin showed that TB is transmissible [[Daniel2006,Cambau2014]].

### history · Koch, the bacillus and tuberculin
- Robert Koch presented his isolation of the tubercle bacillus to the Physiological Society in Berlin on 24 March 1882 [[Barberis2017]].
- World TB Day falls on 24 March each year to mark that 1882 announcement [[CDC2018WTBD]].
- Koch's discovery built on Villemin's proof of transmissibility and on two new methods: a staining technique and a solid, serum-based culture medium. The organism was named *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* a year later [[Cambau2014]].
- Koch's tuberculin, offered as a cure in 1890/91, failed spectacularly in practice [[Gradmann2006]]. It later became important as a diagnostic tool [[Sakula1982]].
- Koch received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1905 [[Barberis2017]].
- Clemens von Pirquet developed the tuberculin skin test in 1907. Three years later he used it to show TB infection in children without symptoms [[Daniel2006]].

### history · Sanatoria and the pre-drug decline
- The sanatorium cure began with Hermann Brehmer's 1854 dissertation, "Tuberculosis is a curable disease". He founded an institution in Görbersdorf built on fresh air and good food [[Barberis2017]].
- Sanatoria spread in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Rest was combined with lung-collapse procedures to rest diseased lung and close cavities [[Daniel2006]].
- The sanatorium movement drew attention to people with TB and helped spur public-health action [[Murray2015]].
- In England and Wales, TB mortality fell by an average of 1.71% a year (95% CI 0.77–2.63) from 1853 to 1910, before any effective drug. Total mortality, infant mortality and housing density did not fall significantly over that period [[Davies1999]].
- Those authors argue that social conditions alone do not explain the Victorian decline and propose a role for natural selection (Hypothesis) [[Davies1999]].
- A model for England and Wales estimates that each infectious person effectively contacted about 22 people in 1900, about 10 by 1950 and about 1 by 1990. Falling contact was a major driver of the decline [[Vynnycky1999]].
- Pre-chemotherapy TB mortality and notification rates fell steadily in New York and London but stayed high in Cape Town. The cities' paths had already diverged before combination chemotherapy arrived in 1952 [[Hermans2015]].
- In Cape Town, notification rates returned to pre-chemotherapy levels by 1980 and doubled again with the HIV epidemic of the 1990s. Chemotherapy cut case fatality but not transmission [[Hermans2015]].

### history · BCG
- From 1908, Albert Calmette and Camille Guérin subcultured a virulent bovine strain on bile-glycerine-potato medium. After about 230 subcultures over 11 years, by 1919 it no longer caused progressive TB in animals [[Luca2013]].
- On 18 July 1921 Benjamin Weill-Hallé and Raymond Turpin gave the first human dose of BCG by mouth, to a newborn in Paris whose mother had died of TB [[Luca2013]].
- From 1924 to 1928, 114,000 infants were vaccinated without serious complications [[Luca2013]].
- In the 1930 Lübeck disaster, 73 of 250 vaccinated infants died in the first year. An inquiry blamed contamination of the vaccine with virulent bacilli in the local laboratory, not BCG itself [[Luca2013]].
- After World War II, UNICEF, the new WHO and Scandinavian Red Cross societies promoted BCG campaigns, which spread to low-income countries over the next decade [[Luca2013]].

### history · The drug era: streptomycin to short-course chemotherapy
- Streptomycin was reported in 1944 by Albert Schatz, Elizabeth Bugie and Selman Waksman, from a soil actinomycete [[Schatz1944,Crofton2006]].
- Para-aminosalicylic acid (PAS) was reported by Jörgen Lehmann in The Lancet in January 1946. Combined with streptomycin, it greatly reduced drug resistance [[Lehmann1946,Murray2015]].
- The 1948 MRC streptomycin trial was a landmark randomised controlled trial. Allocation used sealed envelopes ordered by random numbers, and the X-ray readers were blinded to treatment group [[MRC1948,Crofton2006,Yoshioka1998]].
- The trial enrolled people aged 15–30 with acute progressive bilateral pulmonary TB. At 6 months, 4 of 55 people given streptomycin had died, against 15 of 52 given bed rest alone (Crofton's account) [[Crofton2006]].
- At 6 months, 47 of 55 people on streptomycin still had culture-positive sputum, and resistance emerged, especially after month 4. This showed that one drug alone could not be relied on [[Crofton2006]].
- Isoniazid, reported in 1952, opened the modern treatment era: cheap, well tolerated and safe [[MRC1952,Murray2015,Daniel2006]].
- "Triple therapy" with streptomycin, PAS and isoniazid, introduced in 1952, assured cure [[Iseman2002]].
- The 1959 Madras trial compared home with sanatorium treatment. It found domiciliary chemotherapy as effective as sanatorium care despite the sanatorium's rest, diet, nursing and supervision. India then had about 2.5 million people with active TB and only 23,000 TB beds [[TCCMadras1959]].
- Mitchison lists the key trial-proven steps: streptomycin works; combination prevents resistance; home treatment works and does not put family members at risk; supervised intermittent regimens help adherence; and rifampicin plus pyrazinamide shorten treatment [[Mitchison2005]].
- Ethambutol was shown effective in the early 1960s and replaced PAS [[Murray2015]].
- Rifampicin came out of a Dow-Lepetit chemical-modification programme in Milan and entered clinical use in 1968 [[Sensi1983]].
- In the 1970s isoniazid plus rifampicin cut treatment from 18 to 9 months. In the 1980s adding pyrazinamide allowed cure in 6 months [[Iseman2002,Murray2015]].
- The East African/British Medical Research Council trial of 6-month regimens was published in 1972 [[EastAfricaBMRC1972]].
- Intermittent (2–3 times weekly) regimens can cure even far-advanced TB in as few as 62–78 supervised doses over 26 weeks [[Iseman2002]].

### history · Global control: emergency, DOTS and new tools
- WHO's first-phase TB policy (1948–63) was a vertical approach. It worked in wealthier countries but largely failed in resource-poor settings [[Raviglione2002]].
- In 1989–98 WHO built a new control framework, a five-element strategy branded DOTS. By 1999, however, only 23% of all infectious cases were managed under DOTS [[Raviglione2002]].
- WHO declared TB a global emergency in 1993 [[John2013]].
- WHO launched its Stop TB Strategy in 2006 [[Raviglione2006]].
- The Xpert MTB/RIF cartridge test detected 98.2% of smear-positive and 72.5% of smear-negative culture-positive TB in one test, with 99.2% specificity (1,730 people in four countries) [[Boehme2010]].
- WHO endorsed Xpert MTB/RIF in 2010. It gives a TB result and rifampicin resistance within 2 hours [[Lawn2011]].
- Bedaquiline (Sirturo, NDA 204384) received its original FDA approval on 28 December 2012, with priority review, for MDR-TB [[FDA-NDA204384,Cox2014]].
- Pretomanid was approved in the US on 14 August 2019 (NDA 212862). It was used as part of BPaL under the Limited Population Pathway for highly drug-resistant TB [[FDA-NDA212862,Keam2019]].
- In December 2022 WHO recommended the 6-month BPaLM regimen (bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid, moxifloxacin) in place of 9-month or longer regimens for MDR/RR-TB [[Vanino2023,Nyangwa2022]].
- People starting 6-month MDR/RR-TB regimens rose from 1,744 in 2022 to 34,256 in 2024. By the end of 2024 these regimens were used in 97 countries [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The first UN General Assembly high-level meeting on TB was held in New York in September 2018 [[Chakaya2021,Rodi2022]].
- A review of UN health high-level meetings found that after the 2018 TB meeting, financial investment remained insufficient for the burden [[Rodi2022]].
- At the second UN high-level meeting on TB, in 2023, Member States adopted a target that by 2027 at least 90% of people who develop TB receive quality-assured diagnosis and treatment [[WHO-GTR2025]].

### Exam hooks
- 2024: 10.7 million people fell ill with TB (131 per 100,000) and 1.23 million died, including 150,000 people with HIV [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- TB is the world's leading cause of death from a single infectious agent [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Two-thirds of cases are in eight countries: India, Indonesia, the Philippines, China, Pakistan, Nigeria, DR Congo and Bangladesh. India alone has 25% [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The 30 high-burden countries have 87% of cases [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Sex and age split of new cases: men 54%, women 35%, children 11% [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Notified cases are 78% of estimated cases, a gap of about 2.4 million. For children under 5, only 50% are notified [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Treatment success: 88% for drug-susceptible TB and 71% for MDR/RR-TB [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- About a quarter of humanity has TB infection: 23.0% (about 1.7 billion) by modelling, and 24.8% by IGRA meta-analysis [[Houben2016,Cohen2019ERJ]].
- COVID-19 cut notifications from 7.1 million (2019) to 5.8 million (2020) [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- End TB progress by 2024: incidence −12.3% and deaths −29% against 2025 milestones of −50% and −75% [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Koch announced the tubercle bacillus on 24 March 1882, the date of World TB Day [[Barberis2017,CDC2018WTBD]].
- BCG was first given to a baby in Paris on 18 July 1921 [[Luca2013]].
- 1948 MRC streptomycin trial: a landmark randomised trial, with random allocation and blinded X-ray reading. It also showed resistance emerging on a single drug [[Crofton2006]].
- Isoniazid (1952), rifampicin (1968), and the 6-month short course with rifampicin and pyrazinamide [[Murray2015,Sensi1983,Iseman2002]].
- The Madras study (1959): home treatment was as effective as sanatorium treatment [[TCCMadras1959]].
- WHO declared TB a global emergency in 1993 [[John2013]].
- In the US in 2023, 76% of cases were in people born outside the US. The overall rate was 2.9 per 100,000 [[Williams2024]].

### In plain words
- TB is still the infection that kills the most people in the world. About 1.2 million people died of it in 2024 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Each year about 11 million people get sick with TB. Most live in Asia and Africa [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- Most people who get TB are adult men, but anyone can get it, including children [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- About 1 in 4 people in the world may have been infected with TB germs without being sick. This is called TB infection [[Houben2016,Cohen2019ERJ]].
- About 1 in 5 people with TB are never found or reported. Finding them early helps them and protects their families [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- TB can be cured. Worldwide, about 9 in 10 people treated for regular TB finish treatment successfully. Ask your clinic about free treatment and support [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- TB medicines have saved tens of millions of lives since 2000 [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- TB is very old. It has been found in bones about 9,000 years old [[Hershkovitz2008]].
- Robert Koch found the TB germ in 1882. World TB Day, 24 March, marks that day [[CDC2018WTBD]].
- Before medicines, people went to sanatoria for rest and fresh air. A 1959 study showed that treatment at home works as well [[Barberis2017,TCCMadras1959]].
- Doctors learned early that one medicine alone stops working, so TB is always treated with several medicines together [[Crofton2006]].
- In countries with little TB, many cases are in people born elsewhere, often from an old infection that wakes up years later. TB is not about where someone comes from. Anyone with symptoms should see a clinician [[Frieden1995,Williams2024]].

## Open questions and controversies
- How many people really carry TB infection? The "about a quarter" figure comes from modelling (23.0%) [[Houben2016]] and from an IGRA/TST meta-analysis (24.8% and 21.2%) [[Cohen2019ERJ]]. Both count immune memory, not live bacteria. Modelling of self-clearance suggests 73.1% of infected people clear it over a lifetime, so the population at risk could be 35–80% smaller in some countries (Early evidence) [[Emery2021]]. Status: unresolved, because no test separates live infection from cleared infection.
- Is TB the "leading infectious killer"? WHO says yes for 2024 [[WHO-GTR2025]]. But COVID-19 displaced TB in 2020 [[Chakaya2021]], and on WHO's latest all-cause estimates (2021) TB ranked second to COVID-19 [[WHO-GTR2025]]. Status: true for 2023–2024 on reported COVID-19 deaths. The claim depends on the year and on whether TB deaths in people with HIV are counted.
- Which global estimate? WHO estimates 10.7 million cases and 1.23 million deaths for 2024 [[WHO-GTR2025]]. GBD 2021 estimated 9.40 million cases and 1.35 million deaths for 2021 [[GBD2021TB2024]]. Status: the two use different methods and inputs. Quote one source consistently and give its uncertainty interval.
- 2023 deaths, inside WHO's own documents: "Top findings" gives 1.27 million and the fact sheet 1.25 million. Both describe the 2024 total of 1.23 million as a fall [[WHO-GTR2025]]. Status: likely a rounding or version difference. Use the 2024 figure and the 3% fall.
- Why did TB fall in Europe before drugs? One view credits social conditions and fewer effective contacts per case (22 in 1900 to about 1 by 1990) [[Vynnycky1999]]. Another notes that TB mortality fell 1.71% a year while housing density and other mortality did not, and proposes natural selection (Hypothesis) [[Davies1999]]. Status: debated. The comparison of Cape Town with New York and London points to persistent transmission tied to socio-economic conditions [[Hermans2015]].
- Reading notifications versus incidence: the gap mixes underdiagnosis with underreporting, and notifications can be inflated by overdiagnosis where bacteriological confirmation is low [[WHO-GTR2025]]. Status: WHO calls for new prevalence and inventory studies in several countries.
- Migration and TB in low-incidence countries: high rates in recently arrived migrants [[Domaszewska2025]] and the 2022–23 rise in people from Ukraine [[Vasiliu2025]] reflect conditions in places of origin and the stresses of displacement. Status: the evidence supports accessible screening and care. It does not support restrictive or stigmatising policies, which these sources do not recommend.

## Provenance
- Validation: 52 of 53 sources were checked against a live record on 2026-10-30. 49 were confirmed with PubMed E-utilities esummary, with authors, title, journal, year, volume, pages and identifiers matched. CDC2018WTBD was confirmed on Europe PMC, plus Crossref for the DOI. FDA-NDA204384 and FDA-NDA212862 were confirmed on the openFDA drugsfda endpoint. The one remaining source, WHO-GTR2025, is marked unverified because the report has no bibliographic record. It was read on who.int and its headline numbers were cross-checked against the PubMed-indexed Zenner 2027 Lancet Microbe review (PMID 42526488).
- Abstracts were read for every source a number is taken from. Full texts were read for Crofton2006 (1948 trial numbers), Luca2013 (BCG history), Barberis2017 (ancient and 18th–19th century history), Williams2024 (US birth-origin data) and Vasiliu2025 (EU figures).
- Cited for date, discoverers or design only, because no abstract exists: Schatz1944, Lehmann1946, MRC1948, MRC1952, EastAfricaBMRC1972, Raviglione2006 and Cox2014. Yoshioka1998 has no abstract in PubMed and is cited only to support the randomisation statement drawn from Crofton2006.
- Calculated values: database country sums (incidence, deaths, notifications 1995–2018 and 2022), regional rates and some treatment-success cohorts were calculated from WHO's CSVs and are labelled "database sum" or "database calculation". They omit the Democratic People's Republic of Korea and sit 1–3% below report totals. The TB/HIV success series from the database (74% in 2012) did not match the report (68%), so only the report's figures are given for that series.
- Discrepancies noted rather than resolved: 2023 TB deaths (1.27 million in "Top findings" against 1.25 million in the fact sheet). The 1948 trial's bed-rest deaths are given as 15 of 52, as Crofton states. The original BMJ report was not read, because it has no abstract and the scan was not text-searchable here. PAS: Murray2015 says it was "discovered in 1945", while Lehmann's Lancet report is dated January 1946.
- Dropped: Fox, Ellard and Mitchison 1999 (BMRC review; no abstract, pass E also dropped it); Kochi 1991 (no abstract); Sakula 1983 on Calmette and Guérin (no abstract); the Lancet Commission 2019 (abstract not read); Keshavjee and Farmer 2012 (no abstract); Vynnycky and Fine 1997 (natural history, pass B's area).
