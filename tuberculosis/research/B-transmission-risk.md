# B · Transmission, risk factors & host susceptibility

Pass date: 2026-10-30. Modules served: spread, risk, control, tpt.

This pass covers how *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* travels through air: the droplet-nucleus idea and the guinea-pig ward experiments that proved it, the quanta of the Wells-Riley model and the published generation rates, what cough aerosol sampling shows about who actually transmits, how fast infectiousness falls once effective treatment starts, and the three environmental interventions that have been measured in human-to-guinea-pig models (natural ventilation, upper-room germicidal ultraviolet light, masks worn by the person with TB). It then covers the risk of progression after exposure — recent infection, reinfection, age, HIV by CD4 count and on antiretroviral therapy, undernutrition, diabetes, smoking, alcohol, silicosis, TNF inhibitors, transplant and dialysis, prior TB, incarceration — the population attributable fractions, host genetics from twin studies to Mendelian susceptibility and the common TYK2 P1104A variant, and crowding and poverty as social determinants.

Risk ratios, attributable fractions and transmission percentages here are population statistics from cohorts, meta-analyses and transmission models. They describe groups, not individuals, and they are not predictions for any one person. Guideline statements are quoted as published, for teaching, and are not instructions: TB is a notifiable disease, and anyone with symptoms should see a clinician. TB treatment is free in most national programmes and effective for most people.

## Findings

### spread · The airborne route and the droplet nucleus
- The modern understanding of airborne TB transmission rests on 1950s experiments in which all the air leaving a tuberculosis ward was piped over colonies of guinea pigs, which were then skin-tested for infection [[Escombe2008,Riley1957]].
- Wells and colleagues established the mechanics of droplet-nucleus infection experimentally, using quantitative airborne tuberculosis in rabbits [[Wells1948]].
- Riley's pilot-ward work was explicitly quantitative: it measured the infectivity of ward air and tested control of that infectivity in the same ward [[Riley1957]].
- The 1962 extension of the model irradiated the infected air with ultraviolet light and compared the infectiousness of different patients, establishing that infectiousness varies enormously between individuals [[Riley1962]].
- Riley's own later summary of this work was that the essential facts about airborne infection are simple and have been repeatedly ignored in practice [[Riley2001]].
- Cough aerosol particles carrying culturable *M. tuberculosis* are small: 96.4% of cultivable particles collected from people with pulmonary TB in Kampala were 0.65-4.7 µm across [[Fennelly2012]].
- Across respiratory infections, cough and exhaled-breath studies show strikingly similar aerosol size distributions, with pathogens predominantly in particles under 5 µm, which are immediately respirable; there is no evidence that some pathogens travel only in large droplets [[Fennelly2020]].
- Because the infectious particles are respirable rather than large droplets, personal respiratory protection (a respirator, not a surgical mask) is what protects someone working close to a person with a potentially virulent airborne pathogen [[Fennelly2020]].
- TB is transmitted when *M. tuberculosis* in aerosol droplets is deposited on alveolar surfaces; what happens next is decided largely by the host response [[Pai2016]].

### spread · Quanta and the Wells-Riley model
- The Wells-Riley model expresses a person's infectiousness as a rate of production of infectious doses, called quanta, per hour, and converts ventilation and exposure time into a predicted proportion of susceptible people infected [[Escombe2007,Nardell1991]].
- In an office outbreak, 27 of 67 office workers (40%) had documented tuberculin skin-test conversions after an estimated 4 weeks of exposure to one coworker with cavitary TB [[Nardell1991]].
- Fitting the airborne-infection model to that outbreak, the index case was estimated to have added about 13 quanta per hour to the office air — roughly 10 times the average infectiousness reported in a large earlier series of TB cases [[Nardell1991]].
- In the Lima guinea-pig model, people with HIV and pulmonary TB produced a mean of 8.2 infectious quanta per hour, compared with 1.25 quanta per hour for HIV-negative patients in the similar studies of the 1950s [[Escombe2007CID]].
- Mean monthly infectiousness on that ward ranged from 0 to 44 quanta per hour, so the theoretical risk to a health-care worker breathing ward air varied by the same wide margin [[Escombe2007CID]].
- Three individually highly infectious people with MDR-TB and HIV produced 226, 52 and 40 airborne infectious units (quanta) per hour [[Escombe2008]].
- Over 505 study days on that ward, 118 admissions by 97 people with HIV-positive pulmonary TB exposed 292 guinea pigs, of which 144 developed TB disease and a further 30 were skin-test positive only [[Escombe2008]].
- Molecular fingerprinting showed the transmission was extraordinarily concentrated: 8.5% of the 118 admissions caused 98% of the 125 characterised secondary animal TB cases, and 90% of transmission came from inadequately treated MDR-TB [[Escombe2008]].
- In the earlier 16-month run of the same facility, monthly rates of guinea pigs converting their skin test ranged from 0% to 53%, and 159 of 292 exposed animals converted, 129 with laboratory-confirmed TB [[Escombe2007CID]].
- Modelling of the office outbreak predicted diminishing returns from ventilation: raising outdoor air from about 15 to 25 cubic feet per minute per occupant would have cut infections by 33%, and going to 35 cfm by a further 19%, with progressively smaller increments above that [[Nardell1991]].
- The same model predicts that as a source case's infectiousness rises, ventilation offers progressively less protection — the theoretical limit on what building ventilation can achieve [[Nardell1991]].

### spread · What a cough carries: aerosol sampling
- Cough aerosol culture is feasible: in the first study of the method, cough-generated aerosol cultures were positive in 4 of 16 people (25%) with smear-positive pulmonary TB, most of whom had MDR-TB [[Fennelly2004]].
- In that first study, culture-positive cough aerosols were associated with lack of treatment in the previous week (p = 0.007), with a trend for cough frequency (p = 0.08), and the aerosol cultures fell rapidly within the first 3 weeks of effective treatment [[Fennelly2004]].
- In the larger Kampala series, *M. tuberculosis* was cultured from the cough aerosols of 28 of 101 people (27.7%, 95% CI 19.9-37.1) with culture-confirmed TB, with a median of 16 aerosol colony-forming units (range 1-701) in 10 minutes of coughing [[Fennelly2012]].
- Positive aerosol cultures were associated with higher Karnofsky performance scores (p = 0.016), higher sputum smear grade (p = 0.007), shorter time to positivity in liquid culture (p = 0.004), stronger cough (p = 0.016) and fewer days on TB treatment (p = 0.047) [[Fennelly2012]].
- Cough aerosol sampling was reproducible: within-test agreement kappa 0.81 (95% CI 0.68-0.94) and between-day agreement kappa 0.62 (0.43-0.82) [[Fennelly2012]].
- Cough aerosols predicted actual onward infection better than smear or culture did. Among 96 index cases and 442 household contacts, new infection occurred in 69% of contacts of high-aerosol cases (≥10 cfu), 25% of low-aerosol cases (1-9 cfu) and 30% of aerosol-negative cases (p = 0.009) [[JonesLopez2013]].
- Being a high-aerosol index case was the only predictor of new *M. tuberculosis* infection in a household, unadjusted odds ratio 5.18 (95% CI 1.52-17.61) and adjusted 4.81 (1.20-19.23) [[JonesLopez2013]].
- Only 43 of 96 (45%) sputum culture-positive index cases produced any *M. tuberculosis* in cough aerosols, which is why smear microscopy predicts transmission so imprecisely [[JonesLopez2013]].
- The variability of infectious aerosol production, with a minority of "super-emitters" producing far more than the rest, is a plausible explanation for super-spreading events [[Fennelly2020]].

### spread · Cavities, cough frequency and who transmits
- Cough frequency during treatment was twofold higher in people whose baseline cavity volume was large (>7 mL; rate ratio 1.98, p = 0.01) and in those whose cavities lay within 10 mm of an airway (RR 2.44, p = 0.001) [[Proano2018]].
- Cough stopped about three times faster in people with smaller cavities (adjusted HR 2.89, p = 0.06) and cavities farther from the airway (adjusted HR 3.61, p = 0.02); bacillary burden and culture conversion followed the same pattern [[Proano2018]].
- The mechanism is anatomical rather than behavioural: a large cavity connected to an airway supplies both the organisms and the aerosol-generating cough [[Proano2018]].
- Smear microscopy does identify the most infectious people, but it is a crude sort: the relative transmission rate of smear-negative compared with smear-positive patients was 0.22 (95% CI 0.16-0.32) in San Francisco [[Behr1999Lancet]].
- In that study 28 of 71 molecular clusters (39%, 95% CI 28-52) had a smear-negative putative source case, and a minimum of 32 of 183 secondary cases (17%, 95% CI 12-24) were attributed to smear-negative, culture-positive source cases [[Behr1999Lancet]].
- Those estimates survived sensitivity analyses for misclassified and unidentified source cases and stratification by HIV status [[Behr1999Lancet]].
- Prevalence surveys keep finding a substantial share of bacteriologically confirmed but asymptomatic TB, which points to transmission from people who have not presented for care [[Zenner2027]].
- Before chemotherapy, untreated smear-positive pulmonary TB in HIV-negative people lasted on average about 3 years to either death or self-cure, which is the natural duration over which an untreated source case can transmit [[Tiemersma2011]].
- Clinical phenotype shapes transmission: HIV co-infection is associated with less cavitation, diabetes with more cavitation and greater transmission [[Pai2016]].

### spread · How quickly infectiousness falls once treatment starts
- In the guinea-pig ward model, effective treatment appeared to render people with MDR-TB rapidly non-infectious. Across five successive experiments, infection of control guinea pigs ranged from 1% to 77% [[Dharmadhikari2014]].
- In the single experiment where guinea pigs were exposed for 3 months to 27 people with MDR-TB newly started on *effective* treatment, transmission was minimal; the four experiments with high transmission involved people with unsuspected extensively drug-resistant TB who were not on effective treatment [[Dharmadhikari2014]].
- The practical reading is that drug susceptibility, not smear or culture status, governs how fast infectiousness falls — so rapid drug-susceptibility testing is itself an infection-control measure [[Dharmadhikari2014,Escombe2008]].
- Cough aerosol cultures dropped rapidly within the first 3 weeks of effective treatment, and being untreated in the previous week was the strongest correlate of a positive aerosol culture [[Fennelly2004]].
- Smear and culture conversion lag well behind the loss of infectiousness, which is why "still smear-positive" and "still infectious" are not the same statement [[Dharmadhikari2014]].
- A nosocomial XDR-TB outbreak in rural KwaZulu-Natal in which most cases had no prior TB treatment, and 2 of 2 tested health-care workers were affected, showed what happens when effective treatment is delayed in a congregate setting [[Gandhi2006]].

### spread · Settings: wards, prisons, aircraft and households
- Prisons concentrate transmission. The pooled incidence of *M. tuberculosis* infection among people in prison was 15.0 per 100 person-years (95% credible interval 3.8-41.6) [[Cords2021]].
- TB incidence among people in prison was highest in the WHO African region (2190 per 100 000 person-years, 95% CrI 810-4840), South-East Asia (1550, 240-5300) and South America (970, 460-1860), and lowest in North America (30, 20-50) [[Cords2021]].
- TB prevalence exceeded 1000 per 100 000 people in prison in every world region except North America and the Western Pacific, peaking in the WHO South-East Asia region at 1810 per 100 000 (95% CrI 670-4000) [[Cords2021]].
- The incidence rate ratio between prisons and the surrounding general population was 26.9 (95% CrI 17.1-40.1) in South America and above 10 in the African (12.6), Eastern Mediterranean (15.6) and South-East Asian (11.7) regions [[Cords2021]].
- An earlier systematic review found median annual incidence rate ratios of 26.4 (IQR 13.0-61.8) for infection and 23.0 (IQR 11.7-36.1) for TB disease in prisons versus the local general population [[Baussano2010]].
- The fraction of all TB in the general population attributable to transmission inside prisons was estimated at 8.5% (IQR 1.9-17.9) in high-income and 6.3% (IQR 2.7-17.2) in middle- and low-income countries [[Baussano2010]].
- Transmission on a long flight has been documented once in detail: after an 8.75-hour flight, 6 of 15 contacts with positive skin tests had no other risk factor and all 6 had sat in the index patient's section [[Kenyon1996]].
- On that flight, passengers seated within two rows of the index patient were more likely to be skin-test positive than those elsewhere in the section (4 of 13, 30.8%, versus 2 of 55, 3.6%; rate ratio 8.5, 95% CI 1.7-41.3; p = 0.01) [[Kenyon1996]].
- The authors' own conclusion was that the event required the combination of a highly infectious passenger, a long flight and close proximity — not that air travel is generally hazardous [[Kenyon1996]].
- Household and close contacts remain the highest-yield group anywhere. In 95 studies from low- and middle-income settings, the prevalence of TB disease among all contacts was 3.1% (95% CI 2.2-4.4), microbiologically proven TB 1.2% (0.9-1.8) and TB infection 51.5% (47.1-55.8) [[Fox2013]].
- Among household contacts specifically, TB prevalence was 3.1% (95% CI 2.1-4.5); among contacts of people with MDR- or XDR-TB it was 3.4% (0.8-12.6) [[Fox2013]].
- In 108 studies from high-income settings, TB prevalence among contacts was 1.4% (95% CI 1.1-1.8) and infection prevalence 28.1% (24.2-32.4) [[Fox2013]].
- Incidence among contacts is greatest in the first year after exposure, and children under 5 and people living with HIV are the subgroups most at risk [[Fox2013]].

### control · Ventilation
- Opening windows and doors in Lima hospitals gave a median of 28 air changes per hour (ACH) — more than double the 12 ACH recommended for mechanically ventilated high-risk areas, and 18 times the rate with windows and doors closed (p < 0.001) [[Escombe2007]].
- Buildings more than 50 years old, with large windows and high ceilings, ventilated better than modern naturally ventilated rooms: 40 versus 17 ACH (p < 0.001) [[Escombe2007]].
- Even in the lowest quartile of wind speeds, natural ventilation exceeded mechanical ventilation (p < 0.001) [[Escombe2007]].
- Applying the Wells-Riley model to 368 ventilation experiments across 70 clinical rooms, 24 hours of exposure to untreated TB patients of the infectiousness seen in a well-documented outbreak would infect 39% of susceptible people in mechanically ventilated rooms, 33% in modern naturally ventilated rooms and 11% in pre-1950 rooms with windows and doors open [[Escombe2007]].
- The authors' conclusion is a low-cost, maintenance-free one: where respiratory isolation is difficult and the climate permits, windows and doors should be opened [[Escombe2007]].

### control · Upper-room germicidal ultraviolet light and air ionization
- Upper-room UV light was tested against ward air for 535 consecutive days in Lima, with about 150 guinea pigs in each of three air-sampling enclosures on a 2-day cycle [[Escombe2009]].
- TB infection occurred in 35% of control guinea pigs (106/304), falling to 14% (43/303) with negative air ionizers and 9.5% (29/307) with UV lights (both p < 0.0001 versus control) [[Escombe2009]].
- TB disease was confirmed in 8.6% of control animals (26/304), 4.3% with ionizers (13/303) and 3.6% with UV lights (11/307) (both p < 0.03 versus control) [[Escombe2009]].
- On an airborne-infection model analysis, ionizers prevented 60% of infection and 51% of disease, and UV lights prevented 70% of infection and 54% of disease; UV tended to outperform ionization in every analysis [[Escombe2009]].
- The stated precondition is adequate mixing of room air, which is why the trial ran mixing fans with the upper-room lights [[Escombe2009]].
- Irradiating infected ward air with ultraviolet light was already part of Riley's 1962 design, so this is a sixty-year-old idea retested with modern analysis [[Riley1962,Escombe2009]].

### control · Masks worn by the person with TB
- Surgical face masks worn by people with MDR-TB were tested on alternate days over 3 months, with 17 patients on one ward and 90 pathogen-free guinea pigs in each of two chambers breathing the ward's exhaust air [[Dharmadhikari2012]].
- 69 of 90 control guinea pigs (76.6%, 95% CI 68-85) became infected, versus 36 of 90 (40%, 95% CI 31-51) in the mask period — a 56% (95% CI 33-70.5) reduction in transmission risk [[Dharmadhikari2012]].
- The measured benefit is source control: a surgical mask on the person with TB reduces what reaches everyone else, and the authors frame it as an adjunct, not a replacement for ventilation or treatment [[Dharmadhikari2012]].
- Surgical masks may give the wearer some protection from inhaling infectious aerosols, but less than a respirator; worn by patients, they reduce others' exposure [[Fennelly2020]].

### risk · From exposure to infection to disease
- About a quarter of the world's population has immunological evidence of past or current *M. tuberculosis* infection [[Zenner2027,Houben2016]].
- Modelling of global infection prevalence put the figure at 23.0% of the population in 2014, with 1.7 billion people infected [[Houben2016]].
- In 2024 an estimated 10.7 million people developed TB disease and TB caused about 1.23 million deaths, so only a small fraction of those infected progress [[Zenner2027]].
- The groups at increased risk of progression named in the global epidemiological review are people with recent infection, HIV, undernutrition, young age, and other clinical and social vulnerabilities [[Zenner2027]].
- The classic "5-10% lifetime risk" figure comes from modelling of historical cohorts that also estimated the incubation period and serial interval of TB [[Vynnycky2000]].
- That timetable has been re-examined: the long-standing framing of lifelong latent infection with late reactivation overstates how much disease arises long after infection, and most disease in exposed people appears within the first two years [[Behr2018]].
- Among 739 Amsterdam contacts with evidence of infection, the 5-year cumulative TB risk was 9.5% (95% CI 7.5-11.9) — close to the textbook figure, but concentrated early [[Sloot2014]].
- In the subset with infection who did not start preventive treatment, the 5-year risk of *incident* TB (diagnosed more than 180 days after the index case) was 2.4% (95% CI 1.2-4.7); most of the total risk was co-prevalent disease found at screening [[Sloot2014]].
- The practical implication is that contact investigation finds disease as much as it prevents it: 57 of those 739 contacts had co-prevalent TB versus 14 who developed incident TB [[Sloot2014]].

### risk · Recent infection, reinfection and prior TB
- Among child contacts not given preventive treatment, 2-year cumulative TB incidence was far higher in those with a positive infection test than a negative one, and highest under 5 years of age at 19.0% (95% CI 8.4-37.4) [[Martinez2020Lancet]].
- 83% of all children under 5 who developed TB were diagnosed within 90 days of the baseline contact visit, so much of the risk is already present when the household is first screened [[Martinez2020Lancet]].
- Existing infection partially protects against disease after re-exposure: the adjusted incidence rate ratio for TB in people with prior infection versus uninfected people after exposure was 0.21 (95% CI 0.14-0.30), a 79% lower risk [[Andrews2012Reinfection]].
- In that analysis, the weighted mean adjusted incidence attributable to reinfection was 13.5 per 1000 person-years (95% CI 5.0-26.2) versus 60.1 per 1000 person-years (38.6-87.4) attributable to primary infection [[Andrews2012Reinfection]].
- Partial protection is not immunity. In a high-incidence Cape Town community, the TB rate attributable to reinfection after successful treatment was 2.2 per 100 person-years, about four times the age-adjusted incidence of new TB in the same area [[Verver2005]].
- In that cohort, recurrent TB occurred in 108 of 612 people (18%), and 24 of 31 recurrences (77%) after successful treatment were attributable to reinfection with a different strain rather than relapse [[Verver2005]].
- Having had TB once is therefore itself a risk factor for TB again, especially where transmission is intense [[Verver2005]].

### risk · HIV
- HIV is the strongest known risk factor for progression to TB disease [[Suthar2012]].
- In the founding prospective cohort of 520 people in a methadone programme, TB developed in 8 of 217 HIV-seropositive people (4%) and none of 303 seronegative people over a mean 22 months (p < 0.002) [[Selwyn1989]].
- Risk concentrated in those with both infections: 7.9 cases per 100 person-years among HIV-seropositive people with a prior positive tuberculin test versus 0.3 per 100 person-years among seropositive people without one (rate ratio 24.0, p < 0.0001) [[Selwyn1989]].
- Prevalence and incidence of tuberculin positivity were similar in the HIV-positive and HIV-negative groups, so HIV raised the risk of *disease*, not of becoming infected [[Selwyn1989]].
- Antiretroviral therapy substantially reduces TB incidence at every baseline CD4 stratum: hazard ratio 0.16 (95% CI 0.07-0.36) below 200 cells/µL, 0.34 (0.19-0.60) at 200-350, 0.43 (0.30-0.63) above 350, and 0.35 (0.28-0.44) at any CD4 count [[Suthar2012]].
- There was no evidence that the hazard ratio was modified by baseline CD4 count, i.e. ART lowered TB risk across the range [[Suthar2012]].
- At the population level in 2000, 9% (7-12) of new adult TB cases were attributable to HIV, but 31% in the WHO African region and 26% in the United States [[Corbett2003]].
- Of an estimated 1.8 million TB deaths in 2000, 12% (226 000) were attributable to HIV, and TB caused 11% of all adult AIDS deaths [[Corbett2003]].
- In 2024, about 620 000 of the 10.7 million people who developed TB were living with HIV, and about 150 000 of the 1.23 million TB deaths were among people living with HIV [[Zenner2027]].
- Preventive treatment reached 5.3 million people in 2024, including 58% of people living with HIV who were eligible but only 25% of eligible household contacts [[Zenner2027]].

### risk · Undernutrition
- The relationship between body mass index and TB incidence is log-linear and remarkably consistent: across six cohort studies, each 1 kg/m² increase in BMI was associated with a 13.8% lower TB incidence (95% CI 13.4-14.2) within the BMI range 18.5-30 kg/m² [[Lonnroth2010BMI]].
- The dose-response was less certain below 18.5 and above 30 kg/m², where data were sparse [[Lonnroth2010BMI]].
- A 13.8% change per BMI unit means a person at BMI 17 carries several-fold the incidence of an otherwise similar person at BMI 25, which is why undernutrition dominates attributable-risk calculations in South Asia [[Lonnroth2010BMI]].
- About 20% of global TB incidence is attributable to undernutrition, rising to more than a third in India (Established) [[McQuaid2025]].
- RATIONS tested the converse experimentally: monthly food rations for household contacts (750 kcal and 23 g protein per day plus micronutrients) reduced TB incidence from 1.27 to 0.78 per 100 person-years among 10 345 contacts [[Bhargava2023Lancet]].
- Modelling that intervention for India, at 50% coverage of adults on TB treatment and their households (about 23% of TB-affected households), nutritional support could prevent 361 200 TB deaths (95% UI 318 000-437 700) and 880 700 disease episodes (802 700-974 900) from 2023 to 2035 (Hypothesis: model-based) [[McQuaid2025]].
- That would avert about 4.6% of TB deaths and 2.2% of TB episodes nationally, at US$167 (147-187) per disability-adjusted life-year averted, with a median 24.4 households needing support to prevent one TB death and 10.0 to prevent one case [[McQuaid2025]].

### risk · Diabetes
- Diabetes was associated with roughly a threefold risk of TB disease in cohort studies: RR 3.11 (95% CI 2.27-4.26) [[Jeon2008]].
- The policy reading of that evidence is that diabetes triples TB risk and also worsens TB treatment outcomes including death, while diabetes prevalence rises fastest in the low- and middle-income countries that carry the TB burden [[Lonnroth2014DM]].
- The two epidemics therefore interact arithmetically rather than dramatically: a modest relative risk applied to a rapidly growing exposed population produces a large and growing number of cases [[Lonnroth2014DM]].
- Collaborative TB and diabetes service delivery is described as necessary, feasible and synergistic rather than as an established intervention with trial evidence (Early evidence) [[Lonnroth2014DM]].
- Diabetes co-morbidity is associated with more cavitation, which plausibly links it to greater onward transmission as well as greater personal risk [[Pai2016]].

### risk · Smoking and indoor air pollution
- Smoking raises the risk of *infection*: summary RR 1.73 (95% CI 1.46-2.04) across 6 studies of tuberculin positivity [[Bates2007]].
- Smoking raises the risk of TB *disease*: estimates ranged from 2.33 (95% CI 1.97-2.75) to 2.66 (2.15-3.28) across 13 studies, implying an RR of about 1.4 to 1.6 for progression to disease within an already infected population [[Bates2007]].
- TB mortality risk ratios were mostly below the disease risk ratios, suggesting smoking does not add further mortality risk once someone has TB disease [[Bates2007]].
- Passive smoking and indoor air pollution from biomass fuels were also associated with TB disease, but the evidence for these is weaker than for active smoking (Early evidence) [[Lin2007PLoSMed]].
- Across 32 high-burden countries, an estimated 17.6% of incident TB cases (95% CI 8.4-21.4) and 15.2% of TB deaths (1.8-31.9) were attributable to smoking [[Amere2018]].
- The smoking-attributable share differed sharply by sex — 30.3% of cases in men (95% CI 14.7-36.6) versus 4.3% in women (1.7-5.7) — and was highest in Russia at 31.6% of cases (15.9-37.6) and 28.1% of deaths (3.8-51.4) [[Amere2018]].
- Smoking is also an independent risk factor in dust-exposed workers: each tobacco pack-year carried an adjusted rate ratio of 1.02 (95% CI 1.01-1.03) for pulmonary TB in South African gold miners [[Hnizdo1998]].

### risk · Alcohol
- Heavy alcohol use and alcohol use disorders carry a pooled relative risk of TB of 2.94 (95% CI 1.89-4.59) [[Rehm2009]].
- About 10% of TB cases globally were estimated to be attributable to alcohol on that evidence [[Rehm2009]].
- The later and larger meta-analysis separated exposure levels: RR 1.35 (95% CI 1.09-1.68) for alcohol use generally and 3.33 (2.14-5.19) for alcohol-related problems [[Imtiaz2017]].
- TB risk rose with increasing ethanol intake, with evidence of a threshold effect rather than a smooth gradient from zero [[Imtiaz2017]].
- Alcohol consumption was estimated to cause 22.02 incident TB cases (95% CI 19.70-40.77) and 2.35 TB deaths (2.05-4.79) per 100 000 people in 2014 [[Imtiaz2017]].
- Alcohol-attributable TB incidence rose between 2000 and 2014 in most high-burden countries even as alcohol-attributable TB mortality fell [[Imtiaz2017]].
- The pathways are both biological and social: effects on the immune system, altered pharmacokinetics of TB drugs, higher reinfection rates, interrupted treatment and marginalisation [[Rehm2009]].

### risk · Silicosis, silica dust and mining
- In a cohort of 2255 white South African gold miners followed from 1968-71 to the end of 1995, 115 developed pulmonary TB over 39 319 person-years [[Hnizdo1998]].
- Radiologically diagnosed silicosis carried an adjusted rate ratio for pulmonary TB of 3.96 (95% CI 2.59-6.06) [[Hnizdo1998]].
- Silica dust raised the risk even without silicosis, with a dose-response: adjusted rate ratio 1.07 per mg/m³-year of cumulative dust exposure (95% CI 1.04-1.10), and rate ratios by quartile of cumulative exposure of 1.0, 1.51 (0.78-2.91), 2.35 (1.28-4.32) and 3.22 (1.75-5.90) [[Hnizdo1998]].
- Mass preventive therapy was tested at community scale in South African gold mines: 9 months of isoniazid for whole workforces reduced individual TB incidence but produced no detectable population-level impact [[Vynnycky2015Thibela]].
- Modelling of that trial suggested the main reason was that few latent infections among HIV-positive miners were actually cured, and that optimising implementation would have increased impact by only about 10% [[Vynnycky2015Thibela]].
- Adding other interventions — better diagnostics, shorter treatment delay, continuous preventive therapy for HIV-positive people, wider ART coverage — was modelled to cut TB incidence by up to 30% individually and 75% in combination over 10 years (Hypothesis: model-based) [[Vynnycky2015Thibela]].

### risk · Immunosuppressive drugs, transplant and dialysis
- TNF blockade unmasks TB. Of 70 cases reported to the FDA after infliximab, the median onset was 12 weeks of therapy and 40 were extrapulmonary [[Keane2001]].
- In the 3-year French RATIO registry, the sex- and age-adjusted TB incidence on anti-TNF monoclonal antibody therapy was 116.7 per 100 000 patient-years, a standardised incidence ratio of 12.2 (95% CI 9.7-15.5) [[Tubach2009]].
- The risk was drug-class specific: SIR 18.6 (95% CI 13.4-25.8) for infliximab and 29.3 (20.3-42.4) for adalimumab versus 1.8 (0.7-4.3) for etanercept, the soluble receptor [[Tubach2009]].
- In the matched case-control analysis, exposure to infliximab or adalimumab rather than etanercept was an independent risk factor for TB (OR 13.3, 95% CI 2.6-69.0 and OR 17.1, 3.6-80.6) [[Tubach2009]].
- Other independent risk factors were age, the first year of anti-TNF therapy, and being born in a TB-endemic area; none of the 69 cases had received correct chemoprophylaxis [[Tubach2009]].
- End-stage renal disease raises TB risk: the pooled adjusted rate ratio in dialysis populations was 3.62 (95% CI 1.79-7.33, p < 0.001) versus the general population across 12 studies with 71 374 people and 560 TB cases [[AlEfraij2015]].
- In transplant populations in the same review, the pooled unadjusted risk ratio was 11.35 (95% CI 2.97-43.41) versus the general population [[AlEfraij2015]].
- Because these risks are known and the exposure is planned, they are the clearest indication for TB infection testing and preventive treatment before starting therapy [[Sterling2020,Keane2001]].

### risk · Population attributable fractions, side by side
- Published attributable fractions for the main risk factors, each from its own literature: undernutrition about 20% of global incidence (more than a third in India) [[McQuaid2025]]; smoking 17.6% across 32 high-burden countries [[Amere2018]]; alcohol about 10% globally [[Rehm2009]]; HIV 9% of adult cases in 2000, 31% in the WHO African region [[Corbett2003]]; and transmission inside prisons 6.3-8.5% of general-population TB [[Baussano2010]].
- These fractions come from different years, methods and populations and are not additive; they overlap heavily, since the same person may be undernourished, a smoker and incarcerated [[Lonnroth2009]].
- WHO's own framing is that five risk factors — undernutrition, HIV infection, alcohol use disorders, smoking and diabetes — account for a large share of global TB incidence, and it reports a case count attributable to each one each year [[WHO-GTR2025]].
- The population-level risk factors identified in WHO's review are poor living and working conditions that raise transmission, plus the host factors that impair defence: HIV, malnutrition, smoking, diabetes, alcohol misuse and indoor air pollution [[Lonnroth2009]].
- The strategic argument from attributable fractions is that curing patients, while effective and life-saving, has produced less epidemiological impact than predicted, so prevention aimed at vulnerability is also needed [[Lonnroth2009,Lonnroth2010Lancet]].

### risk · Host genetics
- Twin studies gave the first evidence of host genetic control: re-analysis of the Prophit survey found higher TB concordance in monozygotic than dizygotic twin pairs [[Comstock1978]].
- Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) is caused by inborn errors of the interferon-gamma circuit, and the clinical syndrome is childhood-onset disease with weakly virulent mycobacteria including BCG and environmental mycobacteria [[Bustamante2014]].
- IL-12Rβ1 deficiency is the commonest form of MSMD. In an international survey of 141 patients from 102 kindreds in 30 countries, the mean age at first infection was 2.4 years [[deBeaucoudrey2010]].
- Of 78 patients whose first infection was mycobacterial, 65 were caused by BCG, 9 by environmental mycobacteria and 4 by *M. tuberculosis*; 22 of the remaining 24 probands first presented with non-typhoidal extraintestinal salmonellosis [[deBeaucoudrey2010]].
- Penetrance is high but incomplete: 20 of 29 genetically affected siblings had clinical signs (69%) while 8 remained asymptomatic (27%); 99 of 141 patients (70%) survived, with mean age 12.7 ± 9.8 years at last follow-up [[deBeaucoudrey2010]].
- Inherited IL-12Rβ1 and TYK2 deficiencies are each rarer than 1 in 600 000 people, so classical MSMD explains very little TB at population level [[BoissonDupuis2018]].
- The common TYK2 P1104A variant changed that picture. Homozygosity is found in about 1 in 600 Europeans and 1 in 1000 to 1 in 10 000 people outside East Asia, and was enriched in TB patients from endemic areas versus ethnicity-adjusted controls (p = 8.37 × 10⁻⁸; OR 89.31, 95% CI 14.7-1725) [[BoissonDupuis2018]].
- P1104A selectively abolishes TYK2 catalytic activity and so impairs cellular responses to IL-23 — but not to IL-12, IFN-α or IL-10 — making it a common monogenic cause of TB (Established) [[BoissonDupuis2018]].
- The allele frequency of P1104A in Europeans fell from about 9% to 4.2% over the past 4000 years, consistent with purging by endemic tuberculosis (Hypothesis) [[BoissonDupuis2018]].
- Replication in UK Biobank found P1104A homozygotes in 6 of 620 TB patients (1%) versus 228 of 114 473 controls (0.2%), ancestry-adjusted OR 5.0 (95% CI 1.96-10.31, p = 2 × 10⁻³) [[Kerner2019]].
- Homozygosity for TYK2 P1104A may therefore account for about 1% of TB cases in people of European ancestry — and the true odds ratio on infection would be higher, since probably fewer than 10% of the controls were ever infected [[Kerner2019]].
- Heterozygosity for P1104A, and homozygosity or heterozygosity for the TYK2 I684S and V362F variants, showed no such enrichment, so the effect is specific and recessive [[Kerner2019]].

### risk · Poverty, crowding and living conditions
- Household crowding is associated with TB incidence even in a high-income country: in New Zealand census area units, the adjusted incidence rate ratio per unit of crowding was 1.05 (95% CI 1.02-1.08) overall and 1.08 (1.04-1.12) for New Zealand-born people under 40 [[Baker2008Crowding]].
- That association persisted after adjustment for household income, existing TB burden and the proportion of migrants from high-incidence countries, across 1898 notified cases in 2000-04 [[Baker2008Crowding]].
- The authors treat crowding as a modifiable structural exposure — reducing it could reduce TB incidence — rather than as a characteristic of the people affected [[Baker2008Crowding]].
- WHO's review of drivers places living and working conditions upstream of the biological risk factors, acting both by raising the chance of exposure and by clustering host vulnerabilities in the same households [[Lonnroth2009]].
- Thirty high-burden countries accounted for 87% of cases in 2024, led by India, Indonesia, the Philippines, China, Pakistan, Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Bangladesh — a distribution that tracks income and living conditions more closely than any single risk factor [[Zenner2027]].
- Person-first language is a substantive part of this evidence, not a courtesy: terms that assign blame to people affected by TB discourage the care-seeking that interrupts transmission [[Lonnroth2009]].

### tpt · Interrupting transmission: contacts, screening and preventive treatment
- Contact investigation is high-yield prevention because risk is concentrated and early: incidence among contacts is greatest in the first year, and children under 5 and people living with HIV are the highest-risk subgroups [[Fox2013]].
- Preventive treatment was 63% effective against incident TB among all exposed children (adjusted HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.30-0.47) and 91% effective among those with a positive infection test (adjusted HR 0.09, 0.05-0.15) [[Martinez2020Lancet]].
- Because most under-5 cases appeared within 90 days of the first visit, a large share of child disease may not be preventable by prophylaxis started at contact investigation; earlier detection of the adult source case or community-wide screening is the alternative strategy proposed [[Martinez2020Lancet]].
- Community-wide screening was tested directly in Viet Nam and reduced the prevalence of TB infection and disease relative to standard passive case-finding, supporting the idea that finding undiagnosed sources interrupts transmission [[Marks2019]].
- WHO recommends TB preventive treatment for household contacts of people with bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB after TB disease has been excluded, and for people living with HIV, as a core part of prevention [[WHO-Mod1-2024]].
- In the United States, the groups prioritised for testing and treatment of TB infection are recent contacts, people with HIV, people starting TNF inhibitors or other immunosuppression, people with silicosis, dialysis patients and transplant recipients — the same list that the risk literature produces [[Sterling2020]].
- Coverage remains the bottleneck rather than the evidence: preventive treatment reached only 25% of eligible household contacts globally in 2024 [[Zenner2027]].
- Rapid drug-susceptibility testing belongs on the transmission-control list: in the Lima ward model, 90% of transmission came from inadequately treated MDR-TB, and in the guinea-pig series effective treatment rapidly ended infectiousness [[Escombe2008,Dharmadhikari2014]].

### Exam hooks
- Culturable cough aerosol particles from people with pulmonary TB are 0.65-4.7 µm in 96.4% of cases — respirable, not large droplets [[Fennelly2012]].
- A quantum in the Wells-Riley model is one infectious dose; HIV-positive patients in Lima averaged 8.2 quanta/hour versus 1.25 for HIV-negative patients in the 1950s studies [[Escombe2007CID]].
- Smear-negative, culture-positive TB caused about 17% of transmission in San Francisco, with a relative transmission rate of 0.22 versus smear-positive [[Behr1999Lancet]].
- Only 28% of people with culture-confirmed TB produce culturable cough aerosols, and only high-aerosol producers predicted new household infection (adjusted OR 4.81) [[Fennelly2012,JonesLopez2013]].
- Opening windows and doors gave 28 ACH, more than twice the 12 ACH recommended for mechanically ventilated high-risk rooms [[Escombe2007]].
- Upper-room UV light cut guinea-pig TB infection from 35% to 9.5%, and negative ionization to 14% [[Escombe2009]].
- Surgical masks worn by people with MDR-TB cut transmission to guinea pigs by 56% (76.6% to 40% infected) [[Dharmadhikari2012]].
- Effective treatment, not smear conversion, is what ends infectiousness: minimal transmission from 27 people with MDR-TB on effective therapy, high transmission from unsuspected XDR-TB [[Dharmadhikari2014]].
- Large cavities (>7 mL) and cavities within 10 mm of an airway roughly double cough frequency during treatment [[Proano2018]].
- ART reduces TB incidence at every CD4 stratum, with an overall hazard ratio of 0.35 (95% CI 0.28-0.44) [[Suthar2012]].
- In Selwyn's cohort, TB incidence was 7.9 per 100 person-years in people with HIV *and* a positive tuberculin test versus 0.3 without (rate ratio 24.0) [[Selwyn1989]].
- TB incidence falls 13.8% per 1 kg/m² rise in BMI between 18.5 and 30 kg/m² [[Lonnroth2010BMI]].
- Diabetes roughly triples TB risk (RR 3.11, 95% CI 2.27-4.26) [[Jeon2008]].
- Smoking: RR 1.73 for infection, 2.33-2.66 for disease; 17.6% of cases in high-burden countries are attributable to it [[Bates2007,Amere2018]].
- Alcohol use disorders: RR 3.33 (95% CI 2.14-5.19); about 10% of global TB is alcohol-attributable [[Imtiaz2017,Rehm2009]].
- Silicosis carries a rate ratio of 3.96 for pulmonary TB, and silica dust raises risk dose-dependently even without silicosis [[Hnizdo1998]].
- Anti-TNF monoclonal antibodies carry far higher TB risk than the soluble receptor: SIR 18.6 (infliximab) and 29.3 (adalimumab) versus 1.8 (etanercept) [[Tubach2009]].
- Dialysis: pooled adjusted rate ratio 3.62; transplant: pooled unadjusted risk ratio 11.35 [[AlEfraij2015]].
- Prior infection reduces the risk of disease after reinfection by 79% (IRR 0.21), but reinfection TB after cure in Cape Town still ran at 2.2 per 100 person-years [[Andrews2012Reinfection,Verver2005]].
- People in prison have an infection incidence of 15.0 per 100 person-years, and incidence rate ratios above 10 versus the general population in four WHO regions [[Cords2021]].
- Homozygosity for TYK2 P1104A explains about 1% of TB in Europeans (OR 5.0 in UK Biobank) by selectively impairing IL-23-dependent IFN-γ immunity [[Kerner2019,BoissonDupuis2018]].
- IL-12Rβ1 deficiency is the commonest MSMD; mean age at first infection 2.4 years, and BCG was the first pathogen in 65 of 78 mycobacterial cases [[deBeaucoudrey2010]].

### In plain words
- TB spreads through the air. When someone with TB in their lungs coughs, talks or sings, tiny droplets dry into particles small enough to float and be breathed in [[Fennelly2020]].
- Those particles are very small — thousandths of a millimetre — which is why fresh air and good ventilation matter more than cleaning surfaces [[Fennelly2012,Escombe2007]].
- Not everyone with TB passes it on. In one careful study, fewer than a third of people with TB produced any bacteria in their cough, and those few were the ones whose household members caught it [[JonesLopez2013]].
- Opening windows and doors can replace the air in a room more than twice as fast as an expensive machine, and costs nothing [[Escombe2007]].
- A special ultraviolet lamp near the ceiling cut infections in a test ward by about two thirds [[Escombe2009]].
- A simple surgical mask worn by the person who has TB cut the spread of infection by more than half in a hospital study [[Dharmadhikari2012]].
- Once someone starts TB medicines that actually work against their strain, they stop being infectious quickly — much sooner than their sputum tests turn negative [[Dharmadhikari2014]].
- Most people who breathe in TB bacteria never become ill. About a quarter of the world has signs of past infection, and only a small fraction of them ever develop TB disease [[Houben2016,Zenner2027]].
- The risk is highest in the first year or two after being near someone with TB, so checking the household quickly matters [[Fox2013,Sloot2014]].
- Children under 5 who live with someone who has TB are at the highest risk and should be checked and offered preventive medicine straight away [[Martinez2020Lancet]].
- Some conditions make TB more likely: HIV, being underweight, diabetes, smoking, heavy drinking, dust diseases of the lung, kidney failure, and certain arthritis or bowel-disease medicines [[Suthar2012,Lonnroth2010BMI,Jeon2008,Bates2007,Imtiaz2017,Hnizdo1998,AlEfraij2015,Tubach2009]].
- HIV treatment itself lowers the chance of getting TB, by roughly two thirds [[Suthar2012]].
- Enough food is part of TB prevention. Giving food to families of people with TB lowered new cases by about a third in a large Indian trial [[Bhargava2023Lancet]].
- Crowded housing and poverty raise TB rates. That is about conditions, not about the people living in them, and the conditions can be changed [[Baker2008Crowding,Lonnroth2009]].
- A very small number of people inherit a gene change that makes TB much more likely, but for almost everyone the main factors are exposure and health conditions, not genes [[Kerner2019]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Question: how long does someone remain infectious after starting treatment? The guinea-pig data suggest effective treatment ends infectiousness within days to weeks, well before smear or culture conversion [[Dharmadhikari2014]], and cough aerosol cultures fall within 3 weeks [[Fennelly2004]]; but the five-experiment series was retrospective, used only control groups, was confounded by unsuspected XDR-TB, and its own authors call for prospective studies. Status: widely accepted in principle, not settled enough to set universal isolation durations.
- Question: is smear microscopy good enough to identify who transmits? Smear grade does track infectiousness [[Fennelly2012]] and smear-negative cases transmit at about one fifth the rate of smear-positive ones [[Behr1999Lancet]]; but 55% of culture-positive cases produce no culturable cough aerosol at all, and cough aerosol predicted household infection where smear and culture did not [[JonesLopez2013]]. Status: smear remains the operational tool; aerosol sampling is a research method that outperforms it and is not deployable at scale.
- Question: how much transmission comes from people who are asymptomatic or not yet diagnosed? Prevalence surveys repeatedly find substantial bacteriologically confirmed asymptomatic TB [[Zenner2027]], and community-wide screening reduced infection and disease prevalence [[Marks2019]]; the quantitative share of transmission from this group is not established. Status: open, and central to the case for active case-finding.
- Question: how far can building ventilation protect people? Natural ventilation outperformed mechanical ventilation by a wide margin in Lima [[Escombe2007]], but modelling of an office outbreak shows sharply diminishing returns above comfort levels and progressively less protection as source infectiousness rises [[Nardell1991]]. Status: both are right — ventilation is cheap and worth maximising, and it cannot substitute for finding and treating the source.
- Question: does the "5-10% lifetime risk, lifelong latency" framing hold? Modelling of historical cohorts produced it [[Vynnycky2000]] and the Amsterdam cohort's 9.5% 5-year risk in infected contacts is consistent with it [[Sloot2014]]; but the timetable has been challenged on the grounds that late reactivation is rarer than assumed and most disease follows recent infection [[Behr2018]]. Status: actively contested; the practical consequence is to prioritise recent contacts.
- Question: is prior infection protective or not? A meta-analysis of pre-treatment-era cohorts found 79% lower risk of disease after reinfection in already-infected people [[Andrews2012Reinfection]], while in high-transmission Cape Town the reinfection disease rate after cure was four times the rate of new TB [[Verver2005]]. Status: both findings stand; protection is partial and is overwhelmed by repeated heavy exposure.
- Question: should nutritional support be a standard TB prevention measure? RATIONS showed a 39-48% fall in incidence among contacts [[Bhargava2023Lancet]] and modelling finds it cost-effective in India at $167 per DALY averted [[McQuaid2025]]; the trial was open-label and in one Indian state, and the modelled national impact (2.2% of episodes averted) is modest because coverage is partial. Status: strong trial signal, implementation and generalisability unresolved. Pass H covers this as a prevention trial.
- Question: why did mass preventive therapy fail at population level in the gold mines? Individual-level incidence fell but no population-level impact was detected; modelling attributes this chiefly to few latent infections being cured in HIV-positive miners, and estimates that perfect implementation would have added only about 10% [[Vynnycky2015Thibela]]. Status: the negative result is robust; the explanation is model-dependent.
- Question: how much of TB risk is genetic? Twin concordance differed by zygosity [[Comstock1978]]; classical MSMD is vanishingly rare at population level [[BoissonDupuis2018,deBeaucoudrey2010]]; and the single best-characterised common variant, TYK2 P1104A homozygosity, explains about 1% of cases in Europeans [[Kerner2019]]. Status: genetics is mechanistically illuminating and quantitatively small compared with HIV, undernutrition and exposure.
- Question: are the published attributable fractions additive? They sum to well over 60% when listed together [[McQuaid2025,Amere2018,Rehm2009,Corbett2003,Baussano2010]], but they come from different years, methods and populations and overlap within the same people and households [[Lonnroth2009]]. Status: they should be presented side by side with that caveat, never summed.
- Question: do passive smoking and biomass-fuel smoke cause TB? Both were associated with TB disease, but on weaker evidence than active smoking, and no pooled risk ratio is given in the abstract [[Lin2007PLoSMed]] (Early evidence). Status: open; larger studies called for by the authors.

## Provenance
- Every source was checked against a live record before it was written down. All 62 lines but one were confirmed by PubMed E-utilities `esearch`/`esummary` (authors, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, PMID, PMCID and DOI compared field by field against the printed line). WHO Module 1 (2024) was confirmed through Europe PMC `EXT_ID:39298638`, which returns the NCBI Bookshelf record NBK607290 with the title and year as written.
- Abstracts were fetched and read with PubMed `efetch` for every source from which a number is quoted, with these exceptions, each flagged on its own Sources line: Wells1948, Riley1957, Riley1962, Riley2001 and Comstock1978 have no abstract in PubMed or Europe PMC. No number is taken from any of them. The 1950s quanta figure of 1.25 per hour is quoted as Escombe2007CID restates it, and the Prophit twin finding is used only at the level stated in the title and in later reviews.
- Lin2007PLoSMed's abstract reports direction but no pooled risk ratios, and the full text was not retrieved, so it carries no number here; the smoking risk ratios all come from Bates2007.
- One source is marked unverified: WHO-GTR2025, which has no PubMed, Europe PMC or Crossref record. It is kept for one claim only — WHO's own framing of the five risk factors it reports attributable cases for — and every 2024 burden figure in this pass is cited to the PubMed-indexed Zenner2027 instead. This matches how passes F and H handle the same report.
- Dropped for lack of a traceable source: the frequently repeated "1-5 µm droplet nucleus" range (replaced by the measured 0.65-4.7 µm range in Fennelly2012 and the "<5 µm" statement in Fennelly2020); Riley's individual quanta-per-hour figures for named patients; Wells's 1934 and 1935 *American Journal of Hygiene* papers, which returned no PubMed record on title search; Loudon and Spohn's cough-droplet counts, also not indexed; and any laryngeal-TB infectiousness figure, for which no study with a validated record and a usable number was found — laryngeal TB is therefore not given a number in this pass.
- Also searched without a usable result: a documented transmission event with a child as the source case (the title searches returned only unrelated 2026 records), and a WHO population-attributable-fraction table in a PubMed-indexed form.
- All numbers are quoted with the population, comparator, timepoint and confidence interval the source gives. Guinea-pig results are labelled as such; modelled results carry the (Hypothesis: model-based) or (Early evidence) label.
