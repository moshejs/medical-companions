# G · Living with TB, infection control & nursing

Pass date: 2026-10-30. Modules served: control, living, nursing, safety.

This pass covers how TB transmission is prevented in health care (the WHO 2019 and CDC 2005 hierarchy of controls, triage and the FAST strategy, airborne infection isolation rooms, ventilation, upper-room germicidal UV, respirators and patient masks), health-worker risk and screening, when airborne precautions can end, contact investigation and what public-health departments do, and life on treatment: adherence support and the digital-adherence trials, patient education, social protection and food support, stigma and person-first language, depression, alcohol and tobacco, pregnancy and breastfeeding, and rehabilitation after TB. The nursing section covers sputum collection and induction, monitoring as the US labels state it, patient teaching, nurse-led care and transitions to community care.

Guideline statements and label text are quoted as published, for teaching. They are not instructions for any one person. TB is a notifiable disease, and anyone with symptoms should see a clinician. In most national programmes TB treatment is free and effective. Outcome and risk figures are population statistics from trials and cohorts, not predictions for one person.

## Findings

### control · The hierarchy of controls
- WHO and CDC both organise TB infection control as a three-level hierarchy: administrative controls, environmental controls and respiratory protection [[WHO-IPC2019,Jensen2005]].
- Both call administrative controls "the first and most important level" of the hierarchy. These are management measures that cut exposure to people who may have infectious TB [[WHO-IPC2019,Jensen2005]].
- CDC 2005 widened the guideline from hospitals to all "health-care settings": outpatient clinics, TB clinics, correctional health care, home-based care, emergency medical services, long-term care, homeless shelters and laboratories [[Jensen2005]].
- CDC 2005 asks every setting to have a written TB infection-control plan, updated each year, and to run a "problem evaluation" whenever a case is not recognised promptly or a control fails [[Jensen2005]].
- CDC credits the 1994 controls with fewer US health-care outbreaks and less transmission to patients and health workers. It says the remaining risk comes mainly from people with unsuspected, undiagnosed TB [[Jensen2005]].
- A 2026 systematic review (86 studies, 35 698 exposed people) found an overall transmission risk of 7.1% in health-care exposures, and 3.8% in studies published after the 2005 ventilation standards (Established) [[Manzoor2026]].
- In that review, transmission was linked to prolonged contact (median 24 hours), care without an N95 respirator (12.1% of exposures with mask data), frequent direct care (45.1%) and no negative-pressure ventilation (33.6%) [[Manzoor2026]].
- Only 8 of 116 transmissions with documented duration (6.9%) followed less than 8 hours of cumulative exposure, all in poorly ventilated spaces. The authors suggest defining exposure as more than 8 hours without a respirator [[Manzoor2026]].

### control · WHO 2019 recommendations, as published
- Recommendation 1: triage of people with TB signs and symptoms, or with TB disease (conditional, very low certainty) [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- Recommendation 2: respiratory separation or isolation of people with presumed or demonstrated infectious TB (conditional, very low certainty) [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- Recommendation 3: prompt initiation of effective TB treatment for people with TB disease (strong, very low certainty) [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- Recommendation 4: respiratory hygiene, including cough etiquette, in people with presumed or confirmed TB (strong, low certainty) [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- Recommendation 5: upper-room germicidal ultraviolet (GUV) systems (conditional, moderate certainty) [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- Recommendation 6: ventilation systems, including natural, mixed-mode and mechanical ventilation and recirculated air through HEPA filters (conditional, very low certainty) [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- Recommendation 7: particulate respirators within a respiratory protection programme (conditional, very low certainty) [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- WHO defines respiratory hygiene as covering the mouth and nose when breathing, coughing or sneezing: a surgical or cloth mask, tissue, sleeve, flexed elbow or hand, followed by hand hygiene [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- WHO's review of respiratory separation or isolation found an absolute risk reduction of 2% in health workers [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- WHO says triage should fast-track people with presumed TB and minimise their time in the facility, with dialogue with staff and patients so that it does not add to stigma or alienation. It notes that isolation alone is insufficient and that IPC works as a package [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- In 2017, 9299 TB cases among health workers were reported from 60 countries, a notification rate twice that of the general adult population [[WHO-IPC2019]].

### control · Triage and FAST
- FAST stands for "Find cases Actively, Separate safely, Treat effectively". It puts prompt, likely effective treatment, guided by rapid drug-susceptibility results, at the centre of transmission control [[Tierney2022,Le2019IJTLD]].
- At a general hospital in Lima (75 FAST patients against 76 historical controls), drug-susceptibility testing rose from 57.8% to 98.7%. Drug-resistant TB was found in 24.3% against 9%, and 73.3% against 51.3% started TB treatment in hospital (time to treatment HR 2.11, 95% CI 1.39 to 3.21) [[Tierney2022]].
- In a Vietnamese provincial hospital (858 people treated for pulmonary TB, 2016), 57.5% received likely effective treatment. The median time to it was 3 days, and 43.2% started within 2 days [[Le2019IJTLD]].
- In the same hospital, 79.0% of people whose first in-patient test was a positive Xpert started likely effective treatment within 2 days, against 5.7% of those first found smear-negative and later Xpert-positive [[Le2019IJTLD]].
- CDC 2005: people with symptoms of possible TB in settings other than TB clinics should be referred promptly, should stay only as long as needed to arrange transfer to an AII room, and should wear a surgical or procedure mask if possible [[Jensen2005]].
- Immunocompromised people with infectious TB, including people with HIV, should be physically separated from others to protect both themselves and others (CDC 2005) [[Jensen2005]].

### control · Effective treatment and infectiousness
- On an HIV-TB ward in Lima, 8.5% of 118 admissions caused 98% of 125 characterised guinea-pig infections. 90% of transmission came from people with MDR-TB who were not yet on effective treatment (Established) [[Escombe2008]].
- In five guinea-pig studies of air from an MDR-TB ward in South Africa, infection ranged from 1% to 77%. Transmission was minimal when 27 people had just started effective MDR-TB treatment. The high-transmission experiments involved unsuspected XDR-TB not on effective treatment [[Dharmadhikari2014]].
- A WHO-commissioned meta-analysis (44 studies) found smear conversion in 9% at 2 weeks and 82% at 2 months. Solid-culture conversion was 5% at 2 weeks and 88% at 2 months, and liquid-culture conversion 3% and 59% [[Calderwood2021]].
- The authors note that most people stay culture-positive at 2 weeks, which challenges the idea that they are non-infectious by then. Culture is only one part of infectiousness: cough frequency and aerosol output also fall on treatment (Early evidence) [[Calderwood2021]].
- CDC 2005: people with suspected or confirmed TB who are not on treatment are usually considered infectious if they have cough, cavitation on chest X-ray, a positive AFB smear, disease of the lung, airway or larynx, do not cover the mouth and nose when coughing, or undergo cough-inducing procedures [[Jensen2005]].
- CDC 2005: if someone with these features is on standard multidrug therapy with documented clinical improvement, usually with smear conversion over several weeks, their risk of being infectious is reduced [[Jensen2005]].

### control · Environmental controls: AII rooms, ventilation, UV
- CDC 2005 defines an airborne infection isolation (AII) room as under negative pressure, with air flowing from the corridor into the room, preferably exhausted outside. It needs at least 12 air changes per hour (ACH) for construction since 2001, or 6 ACH for older rooms [[Jensen2005]].
- Older AII rooms should be raised to at least 12 ACH where feasible, by changing ventilation or adding HEPA room-air recirculation units or UVGI [[Jensen2005]].
- The negative-pressure difference should be at least 0.01 inch of water gauge against adjacent areas at all times [[Jensen2005]].
- ACH equals the exhaust airflow (cubic feet per minute) divided by room volume (cubic feet), multiplied by 60 [[Jensen2005]].
- In 70 naturally ventilated clinical rooms in Lima, opening windows and doors gave a median of 28 ACH. That was more than double the 12 ACH of mechanically ventilated negative-pressure rooms and 18 times the rate with windows and doors closed [[Escombe2007]].
- Pre-1950 rooms with large windows and high ceilings reached 40 ACH, against 17 ACH in modern naturally ventilated rooms [[Escombe2007]].
- Modelled infection after 24 hours' exposure was 39% in mechanically ventilated rooms, 33% in modern naturally ventilated rooms and 11% in pre-1950 rooms with windows and doors open (Wells-Riley model) [[Escombe2007]].
- Upper-room UV on an HIV-TB ward (535 days, guinea-pig air sampling): TB infection was 35% in controls, 14% with negative ionizers and 9.5% with UV lights. TB disease was 8.6%, 4.3% and 3.6% [[Escombe2009]].
- In an airborne-infection model, UV lights prevented 70% of TB infection and 54% of TB disease. Ionizers prevented 60% and 51%. UV needs adequate room-air mixing [[Escombe2009]].

### control · Respiratory protection and patient masks
- Surgical masks worn by people with MDR-TB on alternate days (17 people, 3 months, South Africa) cut guinea-pig infection from 76.6% (69 of 90) to 40% (36 of 90). That is a 56% reduction in transmission risk (95% CI 33% to 70.5%) [[Dharmadhikari2012]].
- CDC 2005 added annual respirator training, initial fit testing and periodic fit testing for health workers [[Jensen2005]].
- Fit testing confirms which respirator model and size fits the wearer and that it can be put on correctly. CDC says repeat it when transmission risk, facial features, a relevant medical condition, the respirator's physical characteristics, or the model or size change [[Jensen2005]].
- WHO describes qualitative fit testing (the wearer detects a test agent by taste, smell or involuntary cough) and quantitative fit testing (an instrument measures leakage) [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- At least an N95 disposable respirator should be worn by health workers doing sputum induction or inhalation therapy for a person with suspected or confirmed infectious TB. For bronchoscopy or autopsy, a higher level such as a PAPR may be considered (CDC 2005) [[Jensen2005]].

### control · Ending airborne precautions, as published by CDC
- Suspected TB: airborne precautions can stop when TB is considered unlikely and either another diagnosis explains the illness, or the person has three consecutive negative AFB sputum smears [[Jensen2005]].
- The three specimens are collected 8 to 24 hours apart, with at least one early-morning specimen, because secretions pool overnight. This usually allows release from precautions in 2 days [[Jensen2005]].
- Confirmed drug-susceptible TB: a person on standard multidrug treatment with a clear clinical and bacteriological response (less cough, no fever, falling AFB counts) is "probably no longer infectious" [[Jensen2005]].
- While in hospital, CDC keeps people under airborne precautions until all three apply: three consecutive negative smears (8 to 24 hours apart, one early-morning), at least 2 weeks of standard multidrug treatment, and clinical improvement [[Jensen2005]].
- Discharge home before smear conversion is possible when there is a follow-up plan with the local TB programme, treatment and DOT are arranged, and no children under 4 or immunocompromised people live in the household. All immunocompetent household members must already have been exposed, and the person must be willing not to travel outside the home except for health-care visits [[Jensen2005]].
- Health workers with infectious TB are excluded from work. They may return after three consecutive negative sputum samples (8 to 24 hours apart, one early-morning), a response to treatment that is probably effective, and a determination of non-infectiousness by a knowledgeable physician [[Jensen2005]].

### safety · Health-worker risk and screening
- In low- and middle-income countries, an average of 54% of health workers had TB infection (range 33% to 79%). Annual TB disease incidence ranged from 69 to 5780 per 100 000 [[Joshi2006]].
- Higher risk was linked to inpatient TB units, laboratories, internal medicine and emergency departments, and to radiology technicians, patient attendants, nurses, ward attendants, paramedics and clinical officers [[Joshi2006]].
- Pooled annual risk of TB infection in health workers was 3.8%, 6.9% and 8.4% in low-, intermediate- and high-incidence countries. The incidence rate ratio for TB disease was 2.4, 2.4 and 3.7 [[Baussano2011]].
- A 2017 meta-analysis (21 studies, 30 961 health workers, 16 countries): TB infection prevalence 37% and TB disease incidence 97 per 100 000 per year. Against the general population, OR for infection was 2.27 (95% CI 1.61 to 3.20) and IRR for disease 2.94 (1.67 to 5.19) [[Uden2017]].
- NTCA/CDC 2019 for US health care personnel: baseline risk assessment and symptom evaluation; an IGRA or TST for anyone without documented prior TB infection or disease; no routine serial testing without a known exposure or ongoing transmission [[Sosa2019]].
- NTCA/CDC 2019 also encourages treatment for all personnel with untreated TB infection unless contraindicated, an annual symptom screen for those with untreated TB infection, and annual TB education for all personnel [[Sosa2019]].
- The evidence behind the 2019 change: in US studies, 5% (IGRA) and 3% (TST) tested positive at baseline. Serial testing converted 4% (IGRA) and 0.7% (TST), and among 63 975 personnel in eight studies none developed TB disease [[Sosa2019]].
- Among US personnel who tested positive at baseline and were retested by the same method, the second test was negative in 48% by IGRA and 62% by TST [[Sosa2019]].

### control · Contact investigation and the public-health department
- CDC 2005: people with suspected or confirmed TB should be reported immediately to local public health authorities, so that treatment can be tracked to completion through case management, DOT arranged and contact evaluation started [[Jensen2005]].
- NTCA/CDC places contact investigation second only to treating people with TB disease in the US elimination strategy [[NTCA2005]].
- The infectious period is set, by expert opinion, to start 3 months before the TB diagnosis, or earlier if illness was long [[NTCA2005]].
- Each contact's first face-to-face assessment should happen within 3 working days of being listed. High-priority contacts should have a skin test within 7 working days, medium-priority within 14 days. An induration of 5 mm or more is positive for any contact [[NTCA2005]].
- High priority goes to contacts under 5 years, people with HIV, and people taking more than 15 mg prednisone (or equivalent) for over 4 weeks or other immunosuppressants [[NTCA2005]].
- Children under 5 with a negative first skin test and less than 8 weeks since last exposure get "window prophylaxis" once TB disease is excluded. A second test at 8 to 10 weeks decides whether to stop or complete treatment [[NTCA2005]].
- An investigation is expanded beyond high- and medium-priority contacts only when there is evidence of more transmission than expected, for example infection or disease in 10% of high-priority contacts or twice the background rate, secondary cases, or infection in children under 5 [[NTCA2005]].
- The index person is interviewed in their own language, with interpreters provided if needed, and told how site visits work and how confidentiality is protected and where its limits are [[NTCA2005]].
- For contacts who decline evaluation, NTCA/CDC says communicable-disease laws may be considered, "with the least restrictive measures applied first" [[NTCA2005]].
- In 2023, 9615 TB cases were provisionally reported in the US (2.9 per 100 000), a 16% rise on 2022 and the third rise in a row since 2020 [[Williams2024MMWR]].

### control · Yield and effect of contact investigation
- Low- and middle-income countries (95 studies): TB disease was found in 3.1% of all contacts (microbiologically proven 1.2%) and TB infection in 51.5% [[Fox2013]].
- In those settings, prevalence was 3.1% in household contacts and 3.4% in contacts of people with MDR- or XDR-TB. High-income settings (108 studies): 1.4% TB disease and 28.1% TB infection [[Fox2013]].
- An earlier review of household contacts in low- and middle-income countries found all TB in 4.5% (bacteriologically confirmed 2.3%) and TB infection in 51.4% [[Morrison2008]].
- An updated review (244 studies, 2011 to 2019): pooled TB prevalence 3.6%, microbiologically confirmed 3.2%, and TB infection 42.4%. Incidence was 2.0% in the first year after exposure, against 0.5% at 5 years [[Velen2021]].
- In randomised trials pooled in that review, contact investigation increased TB case notification (RR 2.5, 95% CI 2.0 to 3.2) and decreased mortality (RR 0.6, 0.4 to 0.8) [[Velen2021]].
- ACT2 (Vietnam, cluster-randomised, 70 districts, 25 707 household contacts): 1788 per 100 000 contacts were registered with TB under active household investigation, against 703 per 100 000 with passive case finding alone over 2 years (RR 2.5, 95% CI 2.0 to 3.2). Smear-positive TB had RR 6.4 [[Fox2018NEJM]].
- Among 4490 close contacts in the US and Canada, 158 (4%) developed TB. 51% of these cases were diagnosed by 1 month after the index diagnosis, 75% by 3 months, 81% by 6 months and 92% by 12 months [[Reichler2018]].
- TB rates in those contacts were 2644, 115, 46, 69 and 25 per 100 000 in years 1 to 5 after the index diagnosis. Contact investigation found 77% of the contact cases [[Reichler2018]].
- Among 1406 skin-test-positive contacts, TB developed in 9.8% of those who never started TB infection treatment, 1.8% with partial treatment and 0.2% (1 of 517) who completed it [[Reichler2020Risk]].
- Risk factors for TB in contacts included age 5 or under, HIV, induration of 10 mm or more, sharing a bedroom with the index person, exposure to more than one index person, and index-person weight loss [[Reichler2020Risk]].
- TB infection was found in 46% of 3040 close contacts. Prevalence rose by 8.2% per 250 exposure hours, suggesting a possible risk threshold of 250 hours [[Reichler2020Hours]].

### living · What WHO recommends for care and support
- WHO 1.1: health education and counselling on the disease and treatment adherence should be provided to people on TB treatment (strong, moderate certainty) [[WHO-Mod4-2025]].
- WHO 1.2: a package of adherence interventions may be offered together with a suitable treatment administration option (conditional, low certainty) [[WHO-Mod4-2025]].
- WHO 1.3, which may be combined: tracers or digital medication monitors (conditional, very low certainty); material support (conditional, moderate); psychological support (conditional, low); staff education (conditional, low) [[WHO-Mod4-2025]].
- WHO defines material support as food or financial support: meals, food baskets, supplements, vouchers, transport subsidies, living allowances, housing incentives or bonuses. It is meant to cover indirect costs and soften income loss [[WHO-Mod4-2025]].
- WHO 1.4: community- or home-based treatment support over facility-based or unsupervised treatment (conditional, moderate); support by trained lay providers or health workers over family members or no support (conditional, very low); video-supported treatment can replace in-person support where it can be run well (conditional, very low) [[WHO-Mod4-2025]].
- WHO 2.1 and 2.2: people with MDR-TB should be treated mainly in ambulatory care rather than hospital, and in decentralised rather than centralised models (both conditional, very low certainty) [[WHO-Mod4-2025]].
- WHO 3.2: family-centred, integrated models of care may be used for children and adolescents with TB symptoms or exposure (conditional, very low certainty) [[WHO-Mod4-2025]].
- WHO notes that staff education may also reduce stigma that health workers hold towards people with TB [[WHO-Mod4-2025]].

### living · Adherence support: what the trials show
- A meta-analysis of 22 RCTs found cure rates 18% higher with DOTS and 16% higher with education and counselling. Loss to follow-up (reported as "default") fell 49% with DOTS, 26% with financial incentives and 13% with education. None reduced mortality significantly [[Muller2018]].
- Pooled RCTs of psycho-emotional support (RR 1.37, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.73), socio-economic support (RR 1.08) and both combined (RR 1.17) improved treatment success. The evidence was rated very low quality [[vanHoorn2016]].
- Digital adherence technologies (DATs) include phone-based tools, digital pillboxes and ingestible sensors. They can remind, record doses and let programmes triage people by adherence level [[Subbaraman2018]].
- China (cluster RCT, 4173 people): months with at least 20% of doses missed were 29.9% in controls, 27.3% with text messages (not significant), 17.0% with a reminder pillbox (aMR 0.58) and 13.9% with both (aMR 0.49) [[Liu2015PLoSMed]].
- Moldova (RCT, 197 people): video-observed therapy cut unobserved days from 5.24 to 1.29 per 2 weeks. It also saved people about 58 hours and about EUR 25 each [[Ravenscroft2020]].
- New York City (crossover non-inferiority trial, 216 people): 89.8% of doses were completed with electronic DOT, against 87.2% with in-person DOT. Electronic DOT was non-inferior [[Burzynski2022]].
- London: 70% on smartphone video-observed treatment completed at least 80% of observations in the first 2 months, against 31% on DOT (pass E covers this trial) [[Story2019]].
- ASCENT (four cluster-randomised trials, Philippines, South Africa, Tanzania, Ukraine; 25 606 people): smart pillboxes and medication labels did not reduce poor end-of-treatment outcomes in any country (Established) [[Jerene2025]].
- Two incidents of social harm were reported in the pillbox arm, from inadvertent disclosure of treatment status, and both people withdrew [[Jerene2025]].
- ASCENT Ethiopia (78 clinics, 3858 people, 12 months' follow-up): neither pillbox (aOR 1.04) nor labels (aOR 1.14) reduced the composite of death, loss to follow-up, failure or recurrence [[Tadesse2025LDH]].
- Argentina (RCT, 555 people): an interactive app with treatment-supporter messaging, education and weekly urine isoniazid testing raised treatment success from 74% to 82% (RR 1.10, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.20) and cut loss to follow-up from 24% to 17% (Early evidence) [[Rubinstein2026]].
- A 2025 meta-analysis (76 studies, 86 586 people) found DATs linked to a modest, non-significant rise in treatment success in RCTs (OR 1.14, 0.99 to 1.30, very low certainty). Smartphone apps did better (OR 2.17), and 99DOTS showed no benefit [[Mohamed2025]].
- DATs were linked to more reporting of adverse events in RCTs (OR 1.57, moderate certainty). Satisfaction was generally higher with DATs, though few studies measured patient-reported outcomes [[Mohamed2025]].
- 99DOTS, a phone-call-based tool, had suboptimal accuracy against urine isoniazid tests in 597 people in India, partly because of poor engagement [[Thomas2020CID]].

### living · Social protection and food
- Peru (household-randomised, 282 households): cash transfers of up to US$ 230 plus community meetings and home visits raised treatment success from 53% to 64%. TB preventive therapy started in 43% of young contacts against 25% (aOR 2.2) [[Wingfield2017]].
- Brazil, prospective cohort (1239 adults): people in the Bolsa Família cash-transfer programme had a higher cure rate (average effect +0.076) and less dropout (-0.070) [[Oliosi2019]].
- Brazil, matched national data (2167 people): treatment success was 10.58 percentage points higher in people receiving Bolsa Família [[Carter2019BMJGH]].
- Across 146 countries, raising social protection spending from 0% to 1% of GDP was associated with 18.33 fewer prevalent TB cases, 8.16 fewer incident cases and 5.48 fewer deaths per 100 000 (ecological) [[Siroka2016]].
- Modelling: ending extreme poverty could cut global TB incidence by 33.4% by 2035. Expanding social protection could cut it by 76.1%, and both together by 84.3% (Hypothesis, model-based) [[Carter2018LGH]].
- A review of costs to people with TB in low- and middle-income countries: income loss made up 60% of total costs on average, and half the cost came before treatment began. Total cost averaged 58% of annual individual income and 39% of household income [[Tanimura2014]].
- RATIONS (India): adults with pulmonary TB received 1200 kcal and 52 g protein a day for 6 months. 82.4% were underweight at enrolment, and 93.7% had treatment success [[Bhargava2023LGH]].
- In that cohort, gaining 5% of body weight by 2 months was linked to a lower hazard of death (aHR 0.39, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.86). Median weight gain was 4.6 kg, but 54.8% were still underweight at 6 months [[Bhargava2023LGH]].
- RATIONS household contacts (cluster-randomised, 10 345 contacts): monthly food rations cut TB incidence (all forms) with an adjusted IRR of 0.61 (95% CI 0.43 to 0.85), and microbiologically confirmed pulmonary TB with an IRR of 0.52. That is a 39% to 48% relative reduction [[Bhargava2023Lancet]].
- WHO Module 6 (2025) recommends nutritional assessment and counselling for all people with TB and their household contacts, and nutritional interventions for people with TB who have undernutrition, "regardless of age, drug resistance, pregnancy status or severity of undernutrition" [[WHO-Mod6-2025]].
- It also recommends food assistance to prevent TB in household contacts in food-insecure settings, informed by RATIONS [[WHO-Mod6-2025,Bhargava2023Lancet]].

### living · Stigma and person-first language
- Fear of infection is the most common cause of TB stigma. Stigma has serious socio-economic consequences, especially for women, and is perceived to delay diagnosis and disrupt treatment, though attempts to measure that effect are mixed [[Courtwright2010]].
- A review of 83 studies from 35 countries found that stigma shaped decisions about disclosure and about choosing traditional healers or public or private providers. Misconceptions about causes and the link with HIV fed it [[Chang2014IJTLD]].
- In Lusaka, 82% (113 of 138) of people with TB who described TB-related perceptions reported stigma: low self-esteem, insults, exclusion, non-disclosure and difficulty with adherence. Women had more stigma-related problems than men [[Cremers2015]].
- A national survey in Ethiopia (3463 people) found stigma scores highest in people with TB (21.3), then families (20.5), then the general public (18.6). Fear of job loss (32.5%) and of isolation (15.3%) affected disclosure [[Datiko2020]].
- Higher education and more knowledge about TB were linked to lower stigma scores in that survey [[Datiko2020]].
- A 2025 review (17 studies) found stigma linked to lower uptake of testing and poorer adherence to treatment for TB infection. Negative views of TB disease carried over to TB infection even when people understood the difference [[Kilic2025]].
- Authors from TB programmes on five continents proposed replacing "defaulter" with "person lost to follow-up", "TB suspect" with "person with presumptive TB", and "control" with "prevention and care". They called the old terms coercive and blaming [[Zachariah2012]].

### living · Depression, alcohol and tobacco
- Pooled depression prevalence among people with TB was 45.19% (95% CI 38.04 to 52.55; 25 studies, 4903 people). It was 52.34% in MDR-TB and 51.54% in women [[Duko2020]].
- Depressive symptoms were linked to poor TB treatment outcomes (OR 4.26), loss to follow-up (OR 8.70) and death (OR 2.85), based on few studies [[RuizGrosso2020]].
- TB and depression may interact through poverty, undernutrition, inflammation, stigma and drug side effects. Depression often goes unrecognised because the symptoms overlap with TB's (Hypothesis on mechanism) [[Sweetland2017]].
- Alcohol use roughly doubled the odds of poor treatment outcomes: death, failure or loss to follow-up (OR 1.99 in drug-susceptible and 2.00 in MDR-TB; 111 studies) [[Ragan2020]].
- Smoking was linked to poor treatment outcomes (OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.30 to 1.75; 20 studies) [[Burusie2020]].
- Another meta-analysis found that smokers had higher odds of unfavourable outcomes (pOR 1.23), delayed smear or culture conversion (pOR 1.55) and loss to follow-up (pOR 1.35) [[Wang2020IJTLD]].
- In an Indian cohort (455 people), smoking combined with alcohol misuse increased unfavourable outcomes about 3.6- to 4.7-fold (aIRR 3.58 in current smokers, 4.67 in past smokers) [[Thomas2019PLoSOne]].
- Cytisine added to brief behavioural support did not significantly raise 6-month quit rates in people with TB in Bangladesh and Pakistan (32.4% against 29.7%; 2472 people) [[Dogar2020]].
- WHO counts alcohol-cessation counselling, self-help groups and TB clubs as psychological support [[WHO-Mod4-2025]].

### living · Pregnancy, breastfeeding and contraception
- Best-practice guidance says breastmilk is best for newborns in most settings and that there is no absolute contraindication to breastfeeding during TB treatment. Data on drug levels in breastmilk are limited [[Maugans2023]].
- Whatever feeding method a parent chooses, they should be supported to complete therapy. Formula and safe-preparation advice should be provided if they do not breastfeed [[Maugans2023]].
- Infection control should not usually separate a parent from their newborn. Most pregnant people on effective therapy are unlikely to transmit TB, and fear-based practices can deepen stigma and harm adherence [[Maugans2023]].
- People of reproductive age on TB treatment should be offered pregnancy testing and family-planning referral free of charge and without judgment. Rifamycins can reduce the effect of oral hormonal contraception [[Maugans2023]].
- The rifampin label: "the reliability of oral or other systemic hormonal contraceptives may be affected", and alternative contraceptive measures should be considered [[FDA-Rifampin]].
- The rifampin label says that use in the last weeks of pregnancy can cause postnatal bleeding in mother and infant, for which vitamin K may be indicated [[FDA-Rifampin]].
- The isoniazid label says that "the low levels of isoniazid in breast milk do not threaten the neonate". The ethambutol label says the drug is excreted in breast milk [[FDA-Isoniazid,FDA-Ethambutol]].
- The guidance suggests giving pregnant people an "emergency" supply of TB medicines to take during and after delivery, with a referral letter documenting their medication needs, before the due date [[Maugans2023]].

### living · Rehabilitation after TB
- Clinical standards (Delphi, 62 experts): assess everyone for post-TB lung disease at the end of treatment, identify who needs pulmonary rehabilitation, tailor the programme, evaluate it, and provide education and counselling [[Migliori2021PTLD]].
- In a Brazil, Italy and France study (85 rehabilitated, 96 not), a 5-week programme improved FEV1, FVC, DLCO and 6-minute walk distance. Several measures declined over 3 months without rehabilitation [[Silva2025PTLD]].
- Bangladesh (202 people with moderate post-TB lung disease, 6-week programme): 6-minute walk distance rose from 402.8 m to 456.7 m and FEV1 from 1.28 L to 1.36 L (Early evidence, no control group) [[Rifat2026]].

### nursing · Sputum collection and induction
- Simple sputum-submission instructions in Pakistan (RCT, 3055 people) raised smear positivity in women (RR 1.63, 95% CI 1.19 to 2.22). Detection rose from 8% to 13%, and fewer people submitted saliva. Men's results did not change [[Khan2007Lancet]].
- Across 23 studies, giving instructions before or during collection raised microscopy yield (OR 1.6). Pooling samples raised microscopy (OR 1.6) and culture (OR 1.7). Early-morning collection alone did not improve yield significantly (OR 1.5, not significant) [[Datta2017LGH]].
- CDC 2005 technique for induction: an ultrasonic nebuliser at about 5 mL per minute, at least 30 mL of 3% hypertonic saline, deep breaths and intermittent coughing, for up to 15 minutes or until an adequate specimen is produced [[Jensen2005]].
- Induced sputum is often clear and watery. CDC says any material produced should be labelled as expectorated sputum and sent to the laboratory [[Jensen2005]].
- CDC advises considering bronchodilator pre-treatment for people with asthma or COPD, and having medical help and bronchodilators available because of bronchospasm risk [[Jensen2005]].
- Induction should take place in a ventilated booth or AII room, with the health worker wearing at least an N95 [[Jensen2005]].
- Across 23 studies, sputum induction succeeded in 76.4% to 100% of attempts, with infrequent, mild adverse events, in both children and adults regardless of HIV status [[Hepple2012]].
- In a Thai RCT (112 people with smear-negative presumed TB), nebulised salbutamol gave a similar yield to 3% saline. Chest tightness was 5% against 15% and bronchospasm 0% against 11.3% [[Keeratichananont2015]].

### nursing · Monitoring on first-line treatment, as the US labels state it
- Rifampin label: baseline liver enzymes, bilirubin, creatinine, blood count and platelets for adults. Review at least monthly with specific questions about adverse-reaction symptoms. Routine lab monitoring is "generally not necessary" if baseline results are normal [[FDA-Rifampin]].
- Rifampin label teaching points: take it 1 hour before or 2 hours after a meal with a full glass of water, avoid alcohol and liver-toxic drugs or herbs, stress the full course and not missing doses, and do not start other medicines without advice [[FDA-Rifampin]].
- Isoniazid label: monitor and interview monthly. For people 35 and older, also measure AST and ALT before starting and periodically. Isoniazid hepatitis usually occurs in the first 3 months, and daily alcohol raises the risk [[FDA-Isoniazid]].
- Isoniazid label age gradient for hepatitis: 3 per 1000 at ages 20 to 34, 12 per 1000 at 35 to 49 and 23 per 1000 at 50 to 64 [[FDA-Isoniazid]].
- Ethambutol label: test visual acuity before starting and periodically, monthly at doses above 15 mg/kg/day. Test each eye separately and both together with a Snellen chart, and include colour discrimination [[FDA-Ethambutol]].
- The ethambutol label says the vision effect is usually reversible if the drug is stopped promptly, but irreversible blindness has been reported. The drug is contraindicated in people who cannot report changes in vision, such as young children or unconscious patients [[FDA-Ethambutol]].

### nursing · Nurse-led care and transitions
- A 2026 scoping review of 11 nurse-led studies (1044 people, 73% in primary care) found six kinds of intervention: health education, psychological support, respiratory exercises, medication guidance, nutritional support and discharge teaching [[Yulanda2026]].
- Those interventions reported better knowledge, adherence, psychological well-being, respiratory function and nutritional status (Early evidence) [[Yulanda2026]].
- Uganda (RCT, 154 adults with MDR-TB): adding nurse-led palliative care (holistic assessment, symptom control, psychosocial support, home or ward visits alternating with phone calls) improved patient-reported outcomes at 4 months, by 5.12 scale points (effect size 0.61) [[Buyinza2025]].
- Los Angeles Skid Row (single-arm, 50 adults experiencing homelessness): a nurse-led community health worker programme delivering weekly directly observed 3HP achieved 91.8% completion, against a 65% historical clinic benchmark (Early evidence) [[Nyamathi2021]].
- CDC 2005 discharge planning: a specific follow-up plan with the local TB programme, treatment started and DOT arranged before discharge [[Jensen2005]].

### Exam hooks
- Hierarchy of TB infection control: administrative, then environmental, then respiratory protection. Administrative controls come first and matter most [[WHO-IPC2019,Jensen2005]].
- WHO 2019 has 7 IPC recommendations. Two are strong: prompt effective treatment, and respiratory hygiene [[WHO-IPC2019]].
- An AII room needs negative pressure of at least 0.01 inch water gauge and at least 12 ACH (6 ACH for pre-2001 rooms) [[Jensen2005]].
- Ending precautions for suspected TB: three consecutive negative AFB smears, 8 to 24 hours apart, at least one early-morning [[Jensen2005]].
- Confirmed TB in hospital: three negative smears, at least 2 weeks of standard therapy, and clinical improvement [[Jensen2005]].
- FAST = Find cases Actively, Separate safely, Treat effectively [[Tierney2022]].
- Patient surgical masks cut MDR-TB transmission to guinea pigs by 56% [[Dharmadhikari2012]].
- Upper-room UV cut guinea-pig TB infection from 35% to 9.5% [[Escombe2009]].
- Health workers wear fit-tested N95 (or higher) respirators; patients wear surgical masks [[Jensen2005,Dharmadhikari2012]].
- Contacts' infectious period starts 3 months before diagnosis; first contact assessment within 3 working days [[NTCA2005]].
- Children under 5 who are contacts get window prophylaxis after TB disease is excluded, then a retest at 8 to 10 weeks [[NTCA2005]].
- About 3% of contacts in high-burden settings have TB disease and about half have TB infection [[Fox2013]].
- Three-quarters of TB among close contacts appears within 3 months of the index diagnosis [[Reichler2018]].
- US health personnel: baseline test, no routine annual retesting without exposure, annual symptom screen if untreated TB infection [[Sosa2019]].
- Ethambutol: monthly visual acuity and colour testing at doses above 15 mg/kg/day [[FDA-Ethambutol]].
- Rifampin can make hormonal contraception less reliable [[FDA-Rifampin]].
- Instructions on how to cough up sputum raised women's smear positivity 1.63-fold [[Khan2007Lancet]].
- Smart pillboxes and labels did not improve outcomes in the 5-country ASCENT trials [[Jerene2025,Tadesse2025LDH]].
- Say "person with presumptive TB", not "TB suspect"; "lost to follow-up", not "defaulter" [[Zachariah2012]].

### In plain words
- Once you are on the right medicines, you quickly become much less likely to pass TB on.
- Wearing a mask around other people while you are infectious protects them. Staff wear a special fitted mask for the same reason.
- Opening windows and letting fresh air in is one of the simplest ways to make a room safer.
- You can usually stop being in isolation once your tests show it is safe and your doctor agrees. This is often after a few weeks of treatment.
- The health department may ask who you spend time with. That is so they can check and protect your family and friends, and your information is kept private.
- Children under 5 who live with someone with TB may be given medicine to prevent TB, even before tests come back.
- A nurse or helper may watch you take your pills, in person or by video. This is support to help you finish, not a sign that anyone doubts you.
- Food, travel money and other help can make it easier to finish treatment. Ask your TB clinic what support is available.
- Feeling low or worried is common with TB. Tell your nurse, because help is available and it makes treatment easier.
- Drinking alcohol and smoking make TB treatment work less well. Your clinic can help you cut down or quit.
- You can usually keep breastfeeding while you take TB medicine, and you should not have to be separated from your baby.
- Rifampicin can stop the pill and some other hormonal contraceptives from working well. Ask about another method.
- Tell your nurse straight away if your eyesight or colour vision changes while you take ethambutol.
- If breathing is still hard after treatment, lung rehabilitation exercises can help you walk further.
- TB is nobody's fault. It is caused by a germ in the air, and it can be cured.

## Open questions and controversies
- How long to isolate after starting treatment? CDC's hospital rule needs at least 2 weeks of therapy plus three negative smears [[Jensen2005]]. A WHO-commissioned review found most people still culture-positive at 2 weeks [[Calderwood2021]]. Guinea-pig studies suggest effective treatment makes people non-infectious quickly, long before culture conversion [[Dharmadhikari2014,Escombe2008]]. Status: the measure of infectiousness is unsettled, and the evidence points to prompt effective treatment mattering more than a fixed number of days.
- Do digital adherence technologies improve outcomes? Pillboxes and labels did not, in five countries [[Jerene2025,Tadesse2025LDH]]. Medication monitors reduced missed doses [[Liu2015PLoSMed]], video DOT improved observation [[Ravenscroft2020,Burzynski2022,Story2019]], and an interactive app improved success in one trial [[Rubinstein2026]]. Status: better adherence data have not consistently become better clinical outcomes. Engagement and the response to missed doses may matter more than the device [[Mohamed2025]].
- Does serial health-worker testing still make sense? NTCA/CDC 2019 dropped routine annual testing in the US, given low conversion and no TB disease in 63 975 personnel [[Sosa2019]]. In high-burden countries, health-worker risk stays 2 to 3 times the general population [[Uden2017,Baussano2011]]. Status: the answer depends on setting.
- What defines a health-care exposure worth investigating? CDC 2005 uses a broad, risk-based approach [[Jensen2005]]. A 2026 review suggests more than 8 hours of exposure without a respirator would capture most transmission [[Manzoor2026]]. US contact data suggest a 250-hour threshold for household-type exposure [[Reichler2020Hours]]. Status: not yet in guidelines.
- Nutrition for contacts and patients: RATIONS showed fewer TB cases in contacts given food [[Bhargava2023Lancet]], and WHO 2025 now recommends food assistance in food-insecure settings [[WHO-Mod6-2025]]. The patient cohort had no randomised control, so the effect on patients' own outcomes is associational [[Bhargava2023LGH]].
- Stigma's measurable effect on outcomes: it is widely reported and plausible, but quantitative links to delay and adherence are mixed [[Courtwright2010,Kilic2025]].
