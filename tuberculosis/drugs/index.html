How TB drugs work

TB bacteria live in three very different places inside a lung, and no single medicine reaches and kills all of them. Switch the four first-line drugs on and off and watch which bacteria die, which linger and when a lone drug lets resistant mutants take over.

Days 0–2

Isoniazid strikes first

The cavity wall holds the most bacilli, growing fast in air-filled tissue where the immune response has failed locally. Isoniazid, a prodrug switched on by the bacterium's own KatG enzyme, is bactericidal against actively growing organisms, so it does most of the early killing: about 95% of organisms in the first two days. In a study of 100 people given 22 regimens, the fast kill of the first 2 days was driven mainly by isoniazid and rifampin. 156,160,253,260,328

In plain wordsIn the first two days most of the germs die, mainly from isoniazid. That is why people often stop being infectious quite soon, but it is far from a cure.
Exam hookEarly bactericidal activity (EBA): the fall in viable sputum bacilli over the first days. Isoniazid dominates it. 253,328

Where each drug hits the bacillus

Tap a card to flip it. The bacillus on the right lights up the structure that drug attacks. Most targets sit in the thick, waxy envelope or in the machinery that copies genes and makes energy, which is also where resistance mutations arise (see Drug resistance).

Isoniazid is a prodrug. A single gene, katG (catalase-peroxidase), restored isoniazid sensitivity in a resistant mutant, and deleting katG went with resistance in patient isolates. Its target, InhA, is an enoyl reductase in mycolic acid synthesis; a missense mutation in inhA gave resistance to both isoniazid and ethionamide. The US label lists resistance mutations in katG, inhA, kasA and ahpC. 25,160,537

Open questions

Established

A drug has to reach the bacteria

Drugs must cross three barriers in series: from blood into the lesion, through the waxy cell wall, then to the target inside. Imaging of human and animal lesions showed rifampicin and pyrazinamide reaching the lesions well, with rifampicin building up in caseum, while moxifloxacin diffused poorly into caseum. That matches its failure to shorten treatment in trials. 106,200,379

Early evidence

Caseum bacilli are the hardest to kill

In rabbit cavity caseum, bacilli were largely non-replicating and extremely tolerant of many drugs. Only the rifamycins fully sterilised caseum, and pyrazinamide killed there even though it shows no activity in the classic laboratory models of dormancy. 411

Early evidence

Is the rifampicin dose too low?

Over 14 days, sputum bacterial load fell by 0.176 log10 CFU/mL a day at 10 mg/kg and by 0.265 at 30 mg/kg. Exposure rose more than in proportion to dose, and 35 mg/kg was well tolerated for 2 weeks. 46

Early evidence

Does ethambutol get in the way?

In 100 people given 22 different regimens, ethambutol's effect on days 2 to 14 ran opposite to rifampin's, which suggests it may blunt the sterilising activity of the other drugs. 253

Early evidence

Dosing isoniazid by genotype

In a Japanese trial of 172 people, 78% of slow acetylators on standard dosing had isoniazid liver injury, against none on 2.5 mg/kg. Early treatment failure in rapid acetylators fell from 38% to 15.0% at about 7.5 mg/kg. This is not in WHO or US guidance. 18

Established

Food works in opposite directions

A high-fat meal cut rifampin's peak level by 36% and delayed it (Tmax 2.42 to 4.43 h), while the total dose absorbed fell by only 6%. Rifapentine works the other way: food raised its levels by 40 to 50%. That is why Study 31 gave rifapentine after food and rifampin on an empty stomach. 141,165,369
