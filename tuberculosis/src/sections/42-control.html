Part 4 · Prevention & control

Infection control & contacts

Most TB spread in health care comes from people whose TB has not yet been recognised. Follow one person through a clinic as each layer of protection switches on, then run a contact investigation yourself.

WHO and CDC both call administrative controls "the first and most important level" of the hierarchy. WHO adds that triage should be planned with staff and patients so that it does not add to stigma or alienation, and that isolation alone is insufficient: the measures work as a package. Run a contact investigation

A person has just been diagnosed with infectious pulmonary TB. Public health lists the people who shared air with them during the infectious period, which by expert opinion starts 3 months before diagnosis. Give each contact a priority, then move the timeline to see who is assessed, tested and offered preventive treatment. [[NTCA2005]]

What happens next

Reporting

A notifiable disease

CDC asks that suspected or confirmed TB be reported immediately to local public health, so treatment can be followed to completion through case management, DOT arranged and contacts evaluated. The person is interviewed in their own language, with an interpreter if needed, and told how visits work and how confidentiality is protected, and where its limits are. In 2023 the US provisionally reported 9615 cases (2.9 per 100,000), a third yearly rise. [[Jensen2005,NTCA2005,Williams2024MMWR]]

Least restrictive first

Persuasion before law

Contact investigation ranks second only to treating people with TB disease in the US elimination strategy. For contacts who decline evaluation, NTCA/CDC says communicable-disease laws may be considered, "with the least restrictive measures applied first". In randomised trials, contact investigation raised case notification (RR 2.5) and lowered mortality (RR 0.6). [[NTCA2005,Velen2021]]

Ending isolation

When precautions stop (CDC 2005)

  • TB not confirmed: stop when TB is unlikely and another diagnosis explains the illness, or after three consecutive negative AFB smears collected 8–24 hours apart, at least one early-morning; usually about 2 days.
  • Confirmed TB in hospital: all of three negative smears, at least 2 weeks of standard multidrug treatment, and clinical improvement.
  • Home before smear conversion: possible with a TB-programme plan, DOT arranged, no children under 4 or immunocompromised people at home, household members already exposed, and agreement not to travel except for health care.

Guideline text quoted for teaching, not instructions. [[Jensen2005]]

Back to work

Health workers with TB

CDC 2005 excludes health workers with infectious TB from work until three consecutive negative sputum samples (8–24 hours apart, one early-morning), a response to probably effective treatment, and a knowledgeable physician's judgement that they are not infectious. Our sources give no separate criteria for school. [[Jensen2005]]

Health-worker screening

Risk depends on setting

In low- and middle-income countries an average 54% of health workers had TB infection; a meta-analysis found 2.94 times the general population's TB disease rate. WHO counted 9299 TB cases in health workers from 60 countries in 2017, twice the adult rate. In the US, NTCA/CDC 2019 dropped routine annual testing: baseline testing, an annual symptom screen for untreated infection, and annual TB education, after serial testing in 63,975 personnel found no TB disease. [[Joshi2006,Uden2017,WHO-IPC2019,Sosa2019]]

Early evidence

How long is someone infectious?

A WHO-commissioned meta-analysis found smear conversion in 9% at 2 weeks and 82% at 2 months, and liquid-culture conversion in 3% and 59%, so most people are still culture-positive at 2 weeks. Yet guinea-pig studies show effective treatment ends transmission fast. The measure of infectiousness is unsettled; prompt effective treatment matters more than a fixed number of days. [[Calderwood2021,Dharmadhikari2014,Escombe2008]]

Authors from TB programmes on five continents proposed retiring "defaulter", "TB suspect" and "control", calling them coercive and blaming. [[Zachariah2012]]

Fear of infection is the commonest cause of TB stigma, and stigma is perceived to delay diagnosis and disrupt treatment, though attempts to measure that effect are mixed. In an Ethiopian survey, fear of job loss (32.5%) and of isolation (15.3%) affected disclosure; more knowledge about TB went with lower stigma. [[Courtwright2010,Datiko2020]]

A mask on a person with TB is respiratory hygiene, the same courtesy WHO asks of anyone coughing, and a respirator on staff is routine protection, not a judgement. Isolation is a short, practical step that ends once treatment works. [[WHO-IPC2019,Jensen2005]]
