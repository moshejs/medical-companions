# B · Mechanism, pathology & alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

Pass date: 2026-11-07. Modules served: lungs, breathless, aatd.

This pass covers what COPD does to the lung: the small conducting airways (narrowing, loss, mucus, remodelling), emphysema and its subtypes, chronic bronchitis and mucus, the inflammatory cells (macrophages, neutrophils, CD8+ T cells, B-cell follicles, the eosinophilic subset), the protease–antiprotease and oxidant models, why airflow is limited, static and dynamic hyperinflation, the work and neural drive of breathing, ventilation–perfusion mismatch and hypoxaemia, pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale, and lung-function decline by smoking status. The second half covers alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD): SERPINA1 genetics and Z-protein polymerisation, allele and genotype frequencies, PiMZ risk, the effect of smoking, liver disease, testing and diagnostic delay, and the augmentation-therapy trial evidence. Diagnosis by spirometry (pass C), drug treatment (D), exacerbations and blood gases (E), oxygen and AATD augmentation as a therapy choice (F) and new AATD drugs (H) are left to those passes; augmentation trials appear here because they are the main evidence that protease imbalance drives AATD emphysema.

## Findings

### lungs · Definition and the two lesions
- GOLD 2023 defines COPD as "a heterogeneous lung condition" with chronic respiratory symptoms caused by abnormalities of the airways (bronchitis, bronchiolitis) and/or alveoli (emphysema) that cause persistent, often progressive, airflow obstruction [[Agusti2023]].
- GOLD 2023 describes COPD as the result of gene–environment interactions over a lifetime that damage the lungs and/or change their normal development or ageing [[Agusti2023]].
- A 2015 Nature Reviews primer describes COPD as obstruction of the small airways (chronic obstructive bronchiolitis) plus emphysema, which together cause air trapping and breathlessness on exertion. The inflammation is usually corticosteroid resistant, and the disease involves accelerated lung ageing and abnormal repair (Established) [[Barnes2015]].
- Hogg (Lancet 2004) explains airflow limitation as a prolonged time constant for lung emptying: small conducting airways have more resistance, and emphysema makes the lung more compliant [[Hogg2004Lancet]].
- Not all smokers develop COPD, and why some are susceptible is not fully explained [[Barnes2015]]. In the London working-men study, FEV1 fell gradually over life, and most non-smokers and many smokers never developed clinically significant obstruction [[Fletcher1977]].

### lungs · Small airways: the main site of obstruction
- The major sites of obstruction in COPD are small airways under 2 mm in diameter [[McDonough2011]].
- In resected lung from 159 patients across GOLD stages 0–4, progression was strongly linked to thicker small-airway walls (P<0.001) and to inflammatory mucous exudates in the lumen (P<0.001) [[Hogg2004]].
- In the same series, the share of small airways containing neutrophils, macrophages, CD4 cells, CD8 cells, B cells and lymphoid follicles rose as COPD progressed. The authors linked this to a repair and remodelling process that thickens the walls [[Hogg2004]].
- On multidetector CT, airways 2.0–2.5 mm across were fewer than in controls at GOLD 1 (P=0.001), GOLD 2 (P=0.02) and GOLD 3–4 (P<0.001) [[McDonough2011]].
- MicroCT of GOLD 4 lungs removed at transplant showed 72–89% fewer terminal bronchioles and an 81–99.7% smaller total cross-sectional area of terminal bronchioles [[McDonough2011]].
- Narrowing and loss of terminal bronchioles came before emphysematous destruction (P<0.001). This can explain the raised peripheral airway resistance in COPD (Established) [[McDonough2011]].
- Compared with smokers with normal lung function, terminal bronchioles were 40% fewer in GOLD 1 and 43% fewer in GOLD 2. Transitional bronchioles were 56% and 59% fewer. Bronchiolar loss was found even in samples with a normal alveolar surface area [[Koo2018]].
- Alveolar surface area was 33% lower in GOLD 1 and 45% lower in GOLD 2. The surviving small airways had thickened walls and narrowed lumens [[Koo2018]].
- Small-airway thickening and narrowing happen close to tissue undergoing emphysematous destruction. Hogg and Timens call the mechanism behind this pairing "a puzzle" still to be solved (Hypothesis) [[Hogg2009]].
- Hogg describes four processes that narrow small airways: a broken epithelial barrier, failing mucociliary clearance with mucous exudate in the lumen, inflammatory cells in the wall, and connective tissue laid down in the wall. The thicker walls also stop the airway widening normally as the lung inflates [[Hogg2004Lancet]].

### lungs · Emphysema and its subtypes
- The Fleischner Society classes CT emphysema as centrilobular (trace, mild, moderate, confluent, advanced destructive), panlobular, and paraseptal (mild or substantial). It also lists airway-wall thickening, small airways disease, pulmonary arterial enlargement and bronchiectasis as CT features [[Lynch2015]].
- Centrilobular emphysema is most closely linked to smoking. It starts around the respiratory bronchioles, and the separate lesions merge to destroy large volumes of lung. Panacinar (panlobular) emphysema involves the acinus more evenly and is associated with AATD [[Hogg2004Lancet,Hogg2009]].
- In MESA COPD (318 smokers aged 50–79 with at least 10 pack-years), 27% had emphysema on CT. Centrilobular and panlobular emphysema came with more dyspnoea, shorter walk distance, more hyperinflation and lower diffusing capacity. Paraseptal emphysema looked like controls apart from male predominance [[Smith2014]].
- In MESA, centrilobular emphysema was linked to 21 more pack-years and panlobular emphysema to a body mass index 5 kg/m² lower. 17% of smokers with normal spirometry had emphysema [[Smith2014]].
- Comparing CT with lung morphometry, −950 HU was the only attenuation threshold whose relative area did not differ from measured macroscopic emphysema [[Gevenois1995]].
- Smoke-induced emphysema needs macrophage elastase in mice. MME−/− mice exposed to smoke for a long time had no increase in lung macrophages and no emphysema. When monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 was given to recruit macrophages, they still did not develop airspace enlargement (Established in animal models) [[Hautamaki1997]].

### lungs · Chronic bronchitis, goblet cells and mucus
- Chronic bronchitis is caused by mucus overproduction and hypersecretion by goblet cells. It worsens obstruction by plugging the lumen of small airways, remodelling the epithelium, and changing surface tension so that airways collapse more easily [[Kim2013]].
- Kim and Criner list its consequences: faster lung-function decline, more airflow obstruction in smokers, more lower respiratory infections, more exacerbations and higher mortality. Most patients lie between "pure" emphysema and "pure" chronic bronchitis [[Kim2013]].
- Smokers with both chronic bronchitis and airflow limitation had more goblet cells, CD45+ leukocytes, macrophages and CD8+ cells in the peripheral airway epithelium than non-smokers [[Saetta2000]].
- In the Copenhagen City Heart Study (5,354 women and 4,081 men), mucus hypersecretion at both surveys went with an excess FEV1 decline of 22.8 mL/yr in men and 12.6 mL/yr in women. The adjusted relative risk of COPD hospitalisation was 5.3 in men and 5.1 in women [[Vestbo1996]].
- In the 1946 British birth cohort, chronic mucus hypersecretion in smokers rose from 7.6% at age 36 to 13.0% at 43. Symptoms at these ages predicted later airflow limitation (odds ratios 3.70 and 4.11) [[Allinson2016]].
- In the same cohort, hypersecretion usually resolved after quitting. Each occasion it was present cost an extra 3.6 mL/yr of FEV1 [[Allinson2016]].
- In SPIROMICS, total sputum mucin was 3,166 µg/mL in smokers with severe COPD versus 1,515 µg/mL in never-smoking controls. It was 4,194 µg/mL in people with two or more exacerbations a year versus 2,458 µg/mL in those with none [[Kesimer2017]].
- In severe COPD, MUC5B was about 3 times and MUC5AC about 10 times the levels in never-smokers [[Kesimer2017]].
- In COPDGene (4,363 analysed; median follow-up 9.5 years), CT mucus plugs that fully blocked medium-to-large airways were found in 0, 1–2, or 3 or more segments in 59.3%, 21.8% and 18.9% of patients. Mortality was 34.0%, 46.7% and 54.1%, respectively [[Diaz2023]].
- After adjustment (including FEV1 and CT emphysema), plugs in 1–2 segments carried a hazard ratio for death of 1.15, and plugs in 3 or more segments 1.24 [[Diaz2023]].

### lungs · Inflammatory cells and mediators
- COPD inflammation mainly affects the parenchyma and peripheral airways. Alveolar macrophages, neutrophils, T lymphocytes (mainly Tc1, Th1 and Th17) and innate lymphoid cells all increase, and structural cells add cytokines, chemokines and lipid mediators [[Barnes2016]].
- Induced sputum from COPD patients had more neutrophils and higher TNF-α and IL-8 than sputum from smoking and non-smoking controls. IL-8, but not TNF-α, was also higher than in asthma [[Keatings1996]].
- Smokers with chronic bronchitis and airflow limitation had more CD8+ T cells and more smooth-muscle area in their peripheral airways than asymptomatic smokers with normal lung function. Neutrophil, macrophage and CD4+ counts were similar in the two groups [[Saetta1998]].
- In severe emphysema, the number of inflammatory cells in tissue and airspaces was raised in absolute terms. CT extent of destruction tracked CD8 cell density in tissue most closely (R²=0.858) [[Retamales2001]].
- In the same study, alveolar epithelial cells expressing adenoviral E1A protein were 5- to 40-fold more common in mild and severe disease, a proposed amplifier of inflammation (Hypothesis) [[Retamales2001]].
- Lymphoid aggregates with follicles became more common as COPD progressed (P=0.003). This is evidence of an adaptive immune response in the small airways [[Hogg2004]].
- Antielastin antibodies and Th1 responses tracked emphysema severity in smokers, so emphysema may have an autoimmune component (Hypothesis; single study) [[Lee2007]].
- Most patients have neutrophilic inflammation. A subset has raised eosinophils, possibly driven by Th2 cells and type 2 innate lymphoid cells after epithelial IL-33 release, and these patients may respond better to corticosteroids [[Barnes2016]].
- In 182 exacerbations from 86 patients, 55% were linked to bacteria, 29% to viruses and 28% to sputum eosinophilia. Peripheral blood eosinophil percentage identified the eosinophilic exacerbations (AUC 0.85) [[Bafadhel2011]].
- GOLD 2023 states that COPD in non-smokers is more common in women and in younger people. It shows more small-airway obstruction, less emphysema, fewer sputum neutrophils and more sputum eosinophils than COPD in smokers [[Agusti2023]].

### lungs · Protease–antiprotease and oxidative stress
- In BAL fluid, the elastase-inhibitory capacity of alpha-1 antitrypsin (α1-PI) was 40% lower in 26 healthy smokers than in 24 non-smokers. Smokers' α1-PI contained methionine sulfoxide (4 mol/mol of inactive inhibitor), and non-smokers' did not [[Carp1982]].
- Carp and colleagues proposed that oxidants in smoke inactivate lung α1-PI and so contribute to emphysema even when the gene is normal (Early evidence) [[Carp1982]].
- The Prolastin-C label describes emphysema through the "protease-antiprotease imbalance" model. In it, α1-PI is the main antiprotease of the lower respiratory tract and inhibits neutrophil elastase, and smoking and infection raise elastase by recruiting neutrophils [[FDA-ProlastinC]].
- Oxidative stress is a major predisposing factor in COPD. Antioxidant capacity is reduced by smoking and exacerbations, and oxidative stress persists long after quitting because the body keeps producing reactive oxygen species [[Kirkham2013]].
- Barnes links oxidative stress to NF-κB activation, weaker antiprotease defences, DNA damage, cellular senescence, autoantibodies and corticosteroid resistance (the last through inactivation of histone deacetylase 2) [[Barnes2016]].
- HDAC activity in peripheral lung fell step by step with GOLD stage, while IL-8 mRNA and histone-4 acetylation at the IL-8 promoter rose. HDAC2 protein was also lower in more severe disease [[Ito2005]].
- Antioxidant strategies have so far failed in COPD trials, and the authors argue that no single "magic bullet" exists [[Kirkham2013]].

### lungs · The pulmonary vessels
- Pulmonary arteries from patients with mild COPD relaxed less to ADP than those of non-smokers and smokers. They also carried an inflammatory infiltrate, mostly T cells, with CD8+ cells raised in both smokers and COPD [[Peinado1999]].
- The vascular infiltrate correlated with impaired endothelium-dependent relaxation and with intimal thickness. This suggests that smoke-induced inflammation starts the vascular disease early (Early evidence) [[Peinado1999,Barbera2003]].
- Structural and functional changes in the pulmonary circulation appear at the early stages of COPD, and some are present in smokers with normal lung function [[Barbera2003]].

### breathless · Why expiration is limited
- Expiratory flow limitation is called the pathophysiological hallmark of COPD, and dyspnoea its most prominent and distressing symptom [[ODonnell2006]].
- Calverley and Koulouris describe expiratory flow limitation and dynamic hyperinflation as linked concepts, now moving from the physiology laboratory to the bedside [[Calverley2005]].
- In 117 COPD outpatients, tidal expiratory flow limitation (measured with negative expiratory pressure) correlated with chronic dyspnoea more strongly (rs>0.5) than FEV1 (rs<−0.4) or FVC (rs<−0.3). It stayed the best predictor after adjusting for FEV1 [[Eltayara1996]].
- Of 52 patients, 29 were flow-limited at rest. Inspiratory capacity was normal in all non-flow-limited patients and reduced in most flow-limited ones, and peak work rate and VO2 max were lower in the flow-limited group [[Diaz2000]].

### breathless · Static and dynamic hyperinflation
- Dynamic hyperinflation is the temporary rise in operating lung volumes above resting values during exertion. It overloads the inspiratory muscles, makes them functionally weaker, and stops tidal volume rising normally [[ODonnell2006]].
- In 105 patients (FEV1 37% predicted), 80% hyperinflated during incremental cycling. Inspiratory capacity fell by a mean 0.37 L (14% predicted), with wide variation [[ODonnell2001]].
- Resting IC best predicted how much it would fall (r=−0.50). Peak VO2 tracked peak tidal volume (r=0.68), which in turn tracked IC at peak exercise (r=0.79) [[ODonnell2001]].
- In flow-limited patients, resting IC was the only predictor of exercise tolerance, correlating with maximum work rate (r=0.73) and VO2 max (r=0.75) [[Diaz2000]].
- Dyspnoea intensity during exercise correlates with end-expiratory lung volume and with the ratio of inspiratory effort to volume moved. O'Donnell calls this neuromechanical dissociation, and says anything that lowers end-expiratory volume should ease breathlessness [[ODonnell2006]].
- Long-acting bronchodilators reduce hyperinflation at rest and during exercise in moderate to severe COPD, so tidal volume can expand further for the same effort [[ODonnell2006]].
- In 689 patients followed for a mean of 34 months, 183 (27%) died. The resting inspiratory-to-total lung capacity ratio (IC/TLC), a marker of static hyperinflation, independently predicted all-cause and respiratory mortality, independent of the BODE index [[Casanova2005]].
- Among 345 patients with mild to very severe COPD, four resting lung-volume phenotypes were found: preserved IC and TLC (168), high TLC with preserved IC (51), low IC without hyperinflation (52), and low IC with hyperinflation (74) [[Berton2025]].
- Hyperinflated patients with low IC had the highest odds of very severe mechanical constraint and dyspnoea on exertion (OR 5.2) [[Berton2025]].

### breathless · Neural drive and the sensation of breathlessness
- Diaphragm EMG relative to maximum (an index of neural respiratory drive) was 9.0% at rest in 100 healthy people and 27.9% in 30 COPD patients. It correlated with FEV1, vital capacity and inspiratory capacity [[Jolley2009]].
- In healthy people, neural drive was higher at ages 51–80 than at 18–50 (11.4% vs 8.2% of maximum) [[Jolley2009]].
- The 2012 ATS statement reports that imaging studies consistently link dyspnoea stimuli to activity in cortico-limbic areas involved in interoception and nociception. Endogenous and exogenous opioids may modulate how dyspnoea is felt [[Parshall2012]].
- The ATS statement distinguishes several qualitatively and mechanistically distinct breathing sensations, and notes that treatment of dyspnoea has lagged behind understanding of its mechanisms [[Parshall2012]].

### breathless · Gas exchange: V/Q mismatch and hypoxaemia
- In 23 patients with advanced COPD studied by multiple inert gas elimination, V/Q mismatch and shunt explained all of the hypoxaemia at rest and on exercise. There was no evidence of diffusion limitation [[Wagner1977]].
- Wagner found three patterns. High-V/Q areas were common where compliance was greatly raised (lost capillary bed). Low-V/Q areas were common in patients with severe cough and sputum (poorly ventilated, obstructed units). Some patients had both [[Wagner1977]].
- Patients with similar arterial gases often had different V/Q patterns, so the pattern cannot be inferred from PaO2 and PaCO2 [[Wagner1977]].
- In 150 patients across GOLD 1–4, the alveolar–arterial PO2 gradient and V/Q mismatch were already clearly abnormal in GOLD 1. They rose only modestly with severity [[RodriguezRoisin2009]].
- Post-bronchodilator FEV1 correlated with PaO2 (r=0.62) and PaCO2 (r=−0.59). Mostly perfusion heterogeneity was disproportionately large in GOLD 1, consistent with early involvement of the smallest airways, parenchyma and vessels [[RodriguezRoisin2009]].
- In AATD emphysema, basal-predominant disease went with worse FEV1 (by 9.9% predicted) but better gas exchange than apical-predominant disease of the same volume. This shows that where emphysema sits shapes the physiology [[Parr2004]].
- GOLD 2023 notes that secondary polycythaemia is associated with pulmonary hypertension, venous thromboembolism and mortality. It became less common after long-term oxygen therapy was introduced, but one study found it in 8.4% of severe COPD patients on oxygen [[Agusti2023]].

### breathless · Pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale
- Weitzenblum and Chaouat say cor pulmonale is best defined as pulmonary hypertension caused by lung disease, which enlarges the right ventricle and may lead to right heart failure. COPD is its leading cause [[Weitzenblum2009]].
- In COPD the pulmonary hypertension is pre-capillary, caused mainly by chronic alveolar hypoxia remodelling the vessels. In stable disease the resting mean pulmonary artery pressure usually lies between 20 and 35 mmHg [[Weitzenblum2009]].
- Pressure can rise further during exercise, sleep and exacerbations. Fewer than 5% of patients have severe or "disproportionate" PH (mean PAP above 40 mmHg) [[Weitzenblum2009]].
- Of 998 COPD patients catheterised for chronic respiratory failure, 27 had a mean PAP of 40 mmHg or more. In only 11 (1.1%) was COPD the sole cause [[Chaouat2005]].
- That group had an unusual profile: mild-to-moderate obstruction, severe hypoxaemia, low PaCO2, very low DLCO, worse dyspnoea and shorter survival [[Chaouat2005]].
- In 131 mildly to moderately hypoxaemic patients without resting PH, catheterised twice about 6.8 years apart, mean PAP rose by an average of 0.4 mmHg per year. 25% developed resting PH, which was usually mild (20–42.5 mmHg) [[Kessler2001]].
- In COPDGene and ECLIPSE, a CT pulmonary artery-to-aorta diameter ratio above 1 was independently associated with future severe exacerbations (OR 3.44 in the trial cohort, 2.80 in the validation cohort). It had the strongest association of all variables tested [[Wells2012]].
- Long-term oxygen is the only treatment shown to slow the progression of PH in COPD, though pressures rarely return to normal. Vasodilators are not recommended because benefit is minimal and they worsen gas exchange [[Barbera2003]].

### breathless · How fast FEV1 falls
- In ECLIPSE (2,163 patients, 3 years), post-bronchodilator FEV1 fell by a mean 33 mL per year, with a between-patient standard deviation of 59 mL per year [[Vestbo2011]].
- Over those 3 years, 38% of ECLIPSE patients lost more than 40 mL/yr, 31% lost 21–40 mL/yr, 23% changed by less than ±20 mL/yr, and 8% gained more than 20 mL/yr [[Vestbo2011]].
- Decline in ECLIPSE was faster by 21 mL/yr in current smokers, by 13 mL/yr with emphysema, and by 17 mL/yr with bronchodilator reversibility [[Vestbo2011]].
- In the Lung Health Study (3,926 smokers with mild-to-moderate obstruction), quitters gained an average of 47 mL (2%) in FEV1 the year after quitting. Their later decline was half that of continuing smokers (31 vs 62 mL/yr), similar to never-smokers [[Scanlon2000]].
- At 11 years, sustained quitters lost 30.2 mL/yr (men) and 21.5 mL/yr (women), and continuing smokers lost 66.1 mL/yr (men) and 54.2 mL/yr (women). 38% of continuing smokers had FEV1 below 60% predicted, compared with 10% of sustained quitters [[Anthonisen2002]].
- Fletcher and Peto found that a susceptible smoker who quits does not regain lost function, but the further rate of loss reverts to normal [[Fletcher1977]].
- About half of people with COPD at the end of three cohorts had a normal FEV1 before age 40 followed by rapid decline (53 mL/yr). The other half started with a low FEV1 and declined at 27 mL/yr. Accelerated decline is therefore not obligatory for COPD [[Lange2015]].
- GOLD 2023 states that lungs grow and mature until about 20–25 years of age (earlier in women), plateau briefly, then decline with age. Below-normal trajectories are associated with more multimorbidity and premature death [[Agusti2023]].

### aatd · The protein and the gene
- Alpha-1 antitrypsin is made mainly in the liver, but its main job is to protect the lung against neutrophil elastase [[Stoller2005]].
- The most common severe variant is the Z allele of SERPINA1, a Glu342Lys substitution. Pi*ZZ carriers may develop lung and liver disease [[Hamesch2019,Stoller2005]].
- The PI locus lies on chromosome 14q32.1. The PI*Z allele is the commonest deficiency allele, and most people with severe deficiency are PI type ZZ [[WHO1997]].
- The Prolastin-C label describes AATD as an autosomal co-dominant disorder. It states that about 95% of severely deficient patients are PiZ homozygotes, that PiZZ serum levels are typically below 35% of the average normal level, and that Pi(null)(null) gives undetectable protein [[FDA-ProlastinC]].
- Blanco and colleagues report the Z allele in 98% of severe cases [[Blanco2017]]. The label's 95% figure counts ZZ homozygotes rather than Z-allele carriage [[FDA-ProlastinC]].
- The Z mutation blocks the final stage of processing in the liver, so a ZZ homozygote secretes only 15% of the protein. The other 85% accumulates in the hepatocyte endoplasmic reticulum, where much is degraded and the rest forms insoluble inclusions [[Lomas1992]].
- Z antitrypsin forms polymers by "loop-sheet" polymerisation: the reactive-centre loop of one molecule inserts into the A-sheet gap of the next. This happens spontaneously at 37 °C and is blocked by a peptide inserted into the A-sheet (Established) [[Lomas1992]].
- Z protein made locally in the lung also polymerises. These polymers are found in epithelial lining fluid and attract neutrophils, which may add to lung inflammation beyond simple deficiency (Early evidence) [[Mulgrew2004]].
- Serum levels below a protective threshold of 11 µmol/L increase the risk of emphysema [[Stoller2005]]. The label adds that PiSZ levels run about 9–23 µM and are considered to carry moderately increased risk [[FDA-ProlastinC]].

### aatd · How common it is
- Severe AATD affects about 1 in 2,000–5,000 people [[Stoller2005]]. The WHO memorandum puts its frequency in Europe and North America at about 1 in 2,000 to 1 in 7,000, comparable with cystic fibrosis [[WHO1997]].
- Some 4% of northern Europeans are MZ heterozygotes [[Lomas1992]], and Strnad et al. give 2–4% of Caucasians as Pi*Z carriers [[Strnad2019]].
- An analysis of 224 cohorts from 65 countries estimated 253,404 Pi*ZZ individuals worldwide: 119,594 in Europe, 91,490 in the Americas and Caribbean, 32,154 in Asia, 3,824 in Africa, 4,126 in Australia and 2,216 in New Zealand [[Blanco2017]].
- In surveyed countries with a total population of 4.4 billion, de Serres estimated at least 116 million carriers (PiMS and PiMZ) and 3.4 million deficiency-allele combinations (PiSS, PiSZ, PiZZ). AATD was found in populations of all racial groups, not only white Europeans [[deSerres2002]].
- GOLD 2023 states that PiZZ is found in 0.12% of COPD patients, with prevalence ranging from 1 in 408 in Northern Europe to 1 in 1,274 in Eastern Europe [[Agusti2023]]. Greulich and colleagues put AATD as the cause of about 1% of COPD cases [[Greulich2013]].

### aatd · Lung disease and the effect of smoking
- COPD with a high frequency of panacinar emphysema is the most common clinical disorder in AATD and the leading cause of disability and death. In smokers it generally begins by the third decade of life, much earlier than usual COPD [[WHO1997]].
- In 69 ZZ individuals followed from 1970 to 1983, dyspnoea began at a mean age of 32 in smokers and 51 in non-smokers. Mean age at death (excluding liver deaths) was 48 and 67 years [[Janus1985]].
- In the same cohort, mean FEV1 was 77% predicted in non-smokers and 38% in smokers. FEV1 fell by 80 mL/yr in non-smokers, 317 mL/yr in smokers and 61 mL/yr in ex-smokers [[Janus1985]].
- In 608 Swedish PiZZ adults, adjusted FEV1 decline was 47 mL/yr in never-smokers, 41 mL/yr in ex-smokers and 70 mL/yr in current smokers, with a dose–response to cigarette consumption [[Piitulainen1999]].
- In the NHLBI registry (1,129 adults with levels of 11 µM or less, or ZZ), 5-year mortality was 19%. Mean FEV1 decline was 54 mL/yr and was faster in men, at ages 30–44, in current smokers, and at FEV1 35–79% predicted [[AATDRegistry1998]].
- In 1,339 Swedish PiZZ adults, mortality was raised compared with the general population (SMR 4.80 in smokers, 2.80 in never-smokers). Smokers identified by screening still had an SMR of 3.40 [[Tanash2010]].
- Among 568 never-smoking PiZZ adults, those found by family or population screening had an SMR of 0.70 (95% CI 0.14–2.04). Emphysema (45%) and cirrhosis (28%) were the commonest causes of death [[Tanash2008]].
- Compared with 5,999 random controls, 1,585 PiZZ individuals had a 3.2-fold adjusted risk of death. Never-smoking PiZZ people found by screening had no clear excess risk (HR 1.2, 95% CI 0.6–2.2) [[Tanash2017]].
- In 102 PiZ subjects with emphysema on CT, 65 had a basal-predominant pattern and 37 (36%) an apical-predominant pattern, so AATD emphysema is not always basal [[Parr2004]].
- The ERS 2017 statement notes that AATD emphysema is strongly linked to smoking, but some never-smokers also develop progressive lung disease [[Miravitlles2017]].

### aatd · Heterozygotes (PiMZ)
- In a family-based study, PiMZ relatives had lower post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC than PiMM relatives (0.75 vs 0.78) and an adjusted odds ratio for COPD of 5.18. The odds ratio was 10.65 in ever-smokers, and the effect was abolished in never-smokers [[Molloy2014]].
- In COPDGene (8,271 smokers), white PiMZ subjects had a lower FEV1 (68% vs 75% predicted) and more CT emphysema than non-carriers. African American PiMZ subjects also had lower FEV1 (65% vs 84% predicted) [[Foreman2017]].
- Deep sequencing in SPIROMICS confirmed lower FEV1, lower FEV1/FVC and more CT emphysema in MZ heterozygous smokers. Rarer non-S/Z SERPINA1 variants that lower AAT also added emphysema risk [[Ortega2020]].
- GOLD 2023 states there is no increased COPD risk in MZ or SZ heterozygotes who do not smoke [[Agusti2023]]. A 2023 Alpha-1 Foundation workshop concluded that a single Z allele confers a modest increased risk in some people [[Hersh2026]].

### aatd · Liver disease
- Screening of 200,000 Swedish newborns found 120 Pi Z infants. 14 of them had prolonged obstructive jaundice (9 with severe liver disease), and many apparently healthy Pi Z infants had abnormal liver tests at 3 and 6 months [[Sveger1976]].
- Lomas and colleagues note that about 10% of newborn Z homozygotes develop liver disease, which can lead to fatal childhood cirrhosis [[Lomas1992]].
- The WHO memorandum states that infant cholestasis usually remits by adolescence. AATD is the commonest cause of chronic liver disease in childhood, and cirrhosis and liver carcinoma affect at least 25% of deficient adults over 50 [[WHO1997]].
- In Malmö autopsies, AATD was associated with cirrhosis (odds ratio 7.8) and primary liver cancer (odds ratio 20), significant only in men [[Eriksson1986]].
- In 554 Pi*ZZ adults without known liver disease, non-invasive tests suggested significant fibrosis in 20–36%. Advanced fibrosis was 9 to 20 times more common than in non-carriers, and liver fibrosis was not related to lung function [[Hamesch2019]].
- A single Pi*Z allele raised the odds of cirrhosis in people with fatty liver disease (adjusted OR 7.3) and in people with alcohol misuse (adjusted OR 5.8) [[Strnad2019]].
- Because the liver injury comes from retained Z protein, silencing Z-AAT production is a liver strategy. In a 16-patient phase 2 trial, the RNAi drug fazirsiran cut liver Z-AAT by a median of 83% (Early evidence; pass H covers the pipeline) [[Strnad2022Fazirsiran]].

### aatd · Testing and diagnostic delay
- The 2003 ATS/ERS statement made a strong recommendation to test all symptomatic adults with COPD. The 2016 Alpha-1 Foundation guidance continues to endorse testing all adults with symptomatic fixed airflow obstruction, whether labelled COPD or asthma [[Sandhaus2016]].
- The same guidance says people with unexplained bronchiectasis or liver disease should also be tested, and that testing first-degree relatives is the most efficient way to find cases [[Sandhaus2016]].
- In three European laboratories, measuring the AAT level and genotyping were the main diagnostic steps, with isoelectric focusing (phenotyping) kept for confirmation [[Miravitlles2010]].
- Genotyping only for S and Z can miss rare deficiency alleles. An algorithm that sends low-level samples with selected genotypes on to isoelectric focusing was tested on 50,020 samples [[Bornhorst2007]].
- In US patient surveys, the mean delay from first symptoms to diagnosis was 7.2 years in 1994 and 5.6 years in 2003. Delay was longer in women and older people [[Stoller2005Chest]].
- In Germany and Italy, the median delay was 6 to 7 years, and only 18–25% of surveyed physicians tested all COPD patients [[Greulich2013]].
- The ERS statement says a large share of people with AATD remain undiagnosed and recommends registries and management through expert centres [[Miravitlles2017]].

### aatd · Augmentation therapy: what the trials show
- Weekly infusions of 60 mg/kg plasma-derived AAT in 21 patients raised trough serum levels from 30 to 126 mg/dL. Epithelial lining fluid AAT rose from 0.46 to 1.89 µM six days after infusion, with no change in lung function over 6 months [[Wewers1987]].
- The Prolastin-C label states a dose of 60 mg/kg once weekly by intravenous infusion. It notes that efficacy on exacerbations and emphysema progression "has not been demonstrated" in adequately powered RCTs, and contraindicates the product in IgA-deficient patients with antibodies against IgA [[FDA-ProlastinC]].
- RAPID randomised 180 non-smokers with serum AAT below 11 µM and FEV1 35–70% predicted to 60 mg/kg weekly or placebo for 24 months [[Chapman2015]].
- RAPID's combined primary end point (TLC plus FRC lung density) missed significance (−1.50 vs −2.12 g/L/yr; p=0.06). Lung density loss at TLC alone was slower with treatment (−1.45 vs −2.19 g/L/yr; p=0.03) [[Chapman2015]].
- The RAPID authors concluded that the TLC result should prompt consideration of augmentation to preserve lung tissue in severe AATD emphysema [[Chapman2015]]. The 2016 Alpha-1 Foundation guidance says recent evidence gives strong support for augmentation in appropriate patients where it is available [[Sandhaus2016]].
- In the RAPID open-label extension, delayed-start patients slowed from −2.26 to −1.26 g/L/yr once on treatment. Lung density already lost was never recovered [[McElvaney2017]].
- EXACTLE (77 PiZ subjects, 2–2.5 years) showed a trend to less CT density loss (p=0.049–0.084 across methods). Exacerbation frequency was unchanged but exacerbations were less severe [[Dirksen2009]].
- Pooling the Danish–Dutch trial and EXACTLE (119 patients) gave annual density loss of −1.73 g/L with AAT versus −2.74 g/L with placebo (p=0.006) [[Stockley2010]].
- In the non-randomised NHLBI registry, augmentation was associated with lower mortality (RR 0.64). FEV1 declined 27 mL/yr more slowly only in the FEV1 35–49% subgroup, and confounding could not be excluded [[AATDRegistry1998]].
- The 2016 Cochrane review (3 trials, 283 participants) found less CT lung density loss with augmentation (MD 0.86 g/L). It found no benefit on FEV1, DLCO, quality of life or exacerbations. Because data were sparse, the authors concluded that augmentation "cannot be recommended" [[Gotzsche2016]].
- Standard dosing raises trough levels to about 50% of normal. Doubling the dose to 120 mg/kg/week normalised troughs and further lowered lung elastase activity and markers of elastin breakdown (Early evidence; pilot study) [[Campos2019]].
- SPARTA, a phase 3 placebo-controlled trial of 60 versus 120 mg/kg weekly with whole-lung CT density (PD15) as primary outcome, enrolled 345 people and is listed as completed in 2026. Results were not available in the registry record retrieved for this pass [[NCT01983241]].

### Exam hooks
- Small airways under 2 mm are the main site of obstruction in COPD, and terminal bronchioles are lost before emphysema develops [[McDonough2011,Koo2018]].
- Centrilobular emphysema = smoking, starts at respiratory bronchioles. Panacinar (panlobular) = AATD [[Hogg2004Lancet]].
- The −950 HU threshold on CT best matches macroscopic emphysema [[Gevenois1995]].
- CD8+ T cells and smooth-muscle area separate smokers with COPD from smokers without it [[Saetta1998]].
- Smoke oxidises methionine in α1-PI and cuts its anti-elastase activity by about 40% [[Carp1982]].
- Macrophage elastase knockout mice do not develop smoke-induced emphysema [[Hautamaki1997]].
- Dynamic hyperinflation shows as a fall in inspiratory capacity during exercise (mean 0.37 L) [[ODonnell2001]]. Resting IC/TLC predicts mortality [[Casanova2005]].
- Hypoxaemia in COPD comes from V/Q mismatch, not diffusion limitation [[Wagner1977]].
- PH in COPD is usually mild (mean PAP 20–35 mmHg). Severe PH over 40 mmHg is under 5% and should prompt a search for another cause [[Weitzenblum2009,Chaouat2005]].
- A CT PA:aorta ratio above 1 predicts severe exacerbations [[Wells2012]].
- Quitting halves the subsequent rate of FEV1 decline (31 vs 62 mL/yr) [[Scanlon2000]].
- Z allele = Glu342Lys in SERPINA1. Loop-sheet polymerisation leaves only 15% secreted [[Hamesch2019,Lomas1992]].
- AAT protective threshold is 11 µmol/L [[Stoller2005]].
- ZZ smokers develop dyspnoea about two decades earlier than ZZ non-smokers (32 vs 51 years) [[Janus1985]].
- Test all adults with symptomatic fixed airflow obstruction for AATD, plus unexplained bronchiectasis or liver disease and first-degree relatives [[Sandhaus2016]].
- RAPID: augmentation slowed CT lung density loss at TLC (p=0.03), but the combined primary end point was missed (p=0.06) [[Chapman2015]].

### In plain words
- In COPD, the tiniest airways get narrow, clogged and scarred, and many disappear. This often happens before the air sacs are damaged [[McDonough2011,Koo2018]].
- Emphysema means the walls between air sacs break down. The lung loses its spring, so air gets trapped [[Hogg2004Lancet]].
- Smoke brings in chemicals that make the lung's defence cells release enzymes, and those enzymes eat away lung tissue [[Hautamaki1997,Carp1982]].
- Extra mucus can plug airways. People with many plugs on a scan tend to have worse outcomes [[Diaz2023]].
- When you are active and breathe faster, there is not enough time to breathe all the way out. Air builds up, the lungs get "too full", and each breath feels harder and shorter [[ODonnell2001,ODonnell2006]].
- Your brain has to send a much stronger signal to your breathing muscles than in someone with healthy lungs. That extra effort is part of what breathlessness feels like [[Jolley2009]].
- Low oxygen happens because air and blood no longer meet evenly in the lungs [[Wagner1977]].
- Over time, low oxygen can strain the right side of the heart. In COPD this is usually mild [[Weitzenblum2009]].
- Stopping smoking cannot bring back lost lung function, but it slows further loss to about the normal rate [[Fletcher1977,Scanlon2000]].
- Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency is an inherited condition. The liver makes a faulty protein that gets stuck there, so too little reaches the lungs to protect them [[Lomas1992,Stoller2005]].
- In people with alpha-1, smoking makes lung disease start much earlier, often in their 30s [[Janus1985]].
- Never-smokers with alpha-1 found through family screening can have a near-normal life expectancy [[Tanash2017]].
- A simple blood test can check for alpha-1. Guidelines recommend it for adults with COPD, and say close family members of people with alpha-1 should be tested too [[Sandhaus2016]].
- Weekly infusions of the missing protein slow the loss of lung tissue seen on scans. Studies have not shown that they help breathing test results or quality of life [[Chapman2015,Gotzsche2016]].

### lungs · Step sequence
1. Kicker "Breathe in" · Title "Smoke reaches the small airways": inhaled smoke delivers oxidants that inactivate the lung's own anti-elastase, α1-PI, cutting its capacity by about 40% in smokers [[Carp1982,Kirkham2013]].
2. Kicker "Alarm" · Title "Macrophages and neutrophils arrive": sputum in COPD has more neutrophils and more IL-8 and TNF-α, and macrophage elastase is needed for smoke-induced emphysema in mice [[Keatings1996,Hautamaki1997]].
3. Kicker "Memory" · Title "CD8+ T cells and B-cell follicles": as COPD progresses, more small airways contain CD8 cells, B cells and lymphoid follicles [[Saetta1998,Hogg2004]].
4. Kicker "Narrowing" · Title "Walls thicken, mucus plugs the lumen": small-airway walls thicken and fill with inflammatory mucous exudate, and goblet cells multiply [[Hogg2004,Saetta2000]].
5. Kicker "Vanishing" · Title "Terminal bronchioles disappear": up to 40% are lost even in GOLD 1, before the alveoli are destroyed [[Koo2018,McDonough2011]].
6. Kicker "Destruction" · Title "Alveolar walls break down": centrilobular lesions start at respiratory bronchioles and coalesce, while panacinar emphysema involves the whole acinus in AATD [[Hogg2004Lancet]].

### breathless · Step sequence
1. Kicker "Resistance" · Title "A slow lung to empty": narrowed small airways plus a floppier emphysematous lung lengthen the time constant for emptying [[Hogg2004Lancet]].
2. Kicker "Flow limit" · Title "Breathing out hits a ceiling": tidal expiratory flow limitation tracks chronic dyspnoea better than FEV1 [[Eltayara1996]].
3. Kicker "Air trapping" · Title "Each breath starts a little fuller": on exertion, 80% of patients hyperinflate and inspiratory capacity falls by about 0.37 L [[ODonnell2001]].
4. Kicker "Squeeze" · Title "Tidal volume has nowhere to go": the lower inspiratory capacity caps tidal volume, and peak VO2 falls with it [[ODonnell2001,Diaz2000]].
5. Kicker "Effort" · Title "The brain shouts, the chest barely moves": neural drive is about three times normal at rest, and the mismatch between effort and volume is felt as breathlessness [[Jolley2009,ODonnell2006]].
6. Kicker "Downstream" · Title "Mismatch, low oxygen, a strained right heart": V/Q mismatch causes hypoxaemia, and chronic hypoxia remodels the vessels, giving mild pulmonary hypertension [[Wagner1977,Weitzenblum2009]].

### aatd · Step sequence
1. Kicker "Gene" · Title "One letter in SERPINA1": the Z allele swaps glutamate 342 for lysine (Glu342Lys) [[Hamesch2019]].
2. Kicker "Stuck" · Title "Z protein chains up in the liver": loop-sheet polymerisation traps 85% of Z protein in hepatocytes, and only 15% is secreted [[Lomas1992]].
3. Kicker "Shortfall" · Title "Too little reaches the blood": serum levels below 11 µmol/L raise emphysema risk [[Stoller2005]].
4. Kicker "Unopposed" · Title "Neutrophil elastase runs free": without enough α1-PI, elastase released by neutrophils digests alveolar walls, and smoking adds more neutrophils and oxidises what inhibitor remains [[FDA-ProlastinC,Carp1982]].
5. Kicker "Early emphysema" · Title "Breathless at 32 instead of 51": ZZ smokers develop dyspnoea about two decades before ZZ non-smokers [[Janus1985]].
6. Kicker "Find and slow" · Title "Test, stop smoking, consider augmentation": guidance calls for testing everyone with fixed airflow obstruction, and weekly AAT infusions slowed CT lung density loss in RAPID [[Sandhaus2016,Chapman2015]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Does mucus hypersecretion drive progression? Fletcher and Peto concluded that infection and mucus hypersecretion do not make airflow obstruction progress faster [[Fletcher1977]]. Later cohorts found that persistent hypersecretion goes with excess FEV1 decline (22.8 mL/yr in men) and more hospitalisation [[Vestbo1996,Allinson2016]], and that CT mucus plugs predict death [[Diaz2023]]. Status: the modern view treats mucus as an active contributor. Older and newer cohorts differ in design and measurement.
- Which comes first, small-airway loss or emphysema? Micro-CT shows terminal bronchiolar loss before emphysematous destruction, even in GOLD 1 [[McDonough2011,Koo2018]]. Hogg and Timens call the closeness of airway thickening and nearby tissue destruction an unsolved puzzle [[Hogg2009]]. Status: the order is established in resected lungs, but the cause is not.
- Is emphysema partly autoimmune? Antielastin antibodies and Th1 responses tracked severity in one study [[Lee2007]], and B-cell follicles increase with stage [[Hogg2004]]. Status: Hypothesis, not yet shown to change treatment.
- Does augmentation therapy work? RAPID and the pooled analysis show slower CT lung density loss [[Chapman2015,Stockley2010]], and the extension found lost density was not recovered [[McElvaney2017]]. The Cochrane review found no benefit on FEV1, quality of life or exacerbations and noted the trial sponsors' conflicts of interest [[Gotzsche2016]]. The label version held in openFDA states that efficacy on emphysema progression has not been shown in adequately powered RCTs [[FDA-ProlastinC]], while the Alpha-1 Foundation guidance gives augmentation strong support [[Sandhaus2016]]. SPARTA (60 vs 120 mg/kg) has completed, with results pending in the registry [[NCT01983241]]. Status: disputed. CT density is a sensitive surrogate, but its link to patient outcomes is inferred.
- Should the dose be higher? Standard dosing reaches about 50% of normal trough levels, and double dosing further cut lung elastase and elastin breakdown in a pilot study [[Campos2019]]. Status: Early evidence pending SPARTA.
- How risky is PiMZ? Family and cohort studies show more COPD and emphysema in MZ smokers [[Molloy2014,Foreman2017,Ortega2020]]. GOLD 2023 states no increased risk in non-smoking heterozygotes [[Agusti2023]]. The 2023 workshop concluded the risk is modest and confined to some carriers [[Hersh2026]]. Status: agreed that smoking modifies the risk. Whether MZ smokers should be treated is open.
- How common is AATD among people with COPD? GOLD gives 0.12% PiZZ [[Agusti2023]], while Greulich and colleagues cite about 1% of COPD cases [[Greulich2013]]. The figures probably differ in what they count (ZZ genotype versus any severe deficiency) and in which population was sampled. Status: unresolved and region-dependent.
- Do Z polymers in the lung matter? Polymerised Z protein in lining fluid attracts neutrophils [[Mulgrew2004]], which may explain why replacing the protein does not fully stop inflammation [[Campos2019]]. Status: Early evidence.
