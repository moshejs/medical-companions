# A · Epidemiology, risk factors & history

Pass date: 2026-11-07. Modules served: home, what, causes, history.

This pass covers what COPD is (the GOLD 2023 definition and the newer "etiotype" taxonomy), how many people have it and die of it worldwide and by region, sex and age, how often it goes undiagnosed, the causes and risk factors (tobacco, household and outdoor air pollution, work, early-life events and lung-growth trajectories, infections, genes), the Fletcher–Peto model and its revision, patient FAQ answers for the start page, and a dated history from the first descriptions of "voluminous lungs" to GOLD 2023. Mechanism and alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency in depth belong to pass B, spirometry and grading to pass C, and treatment trials to passes D–F; they appear here only as milestones or FAQ answers.

## Findings

### what · Definition
- GOLD 2023 defines COPD as "a heterogeneous lung condition characterized by chronic respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough, expectoration, and/or exacerbations) due to abnormalities of the airways (bronchitis, bronchiolitis) and/or alveoli (emphysema) that cause persistent, often progressive, airflow obstruction" [[Agusti2023]].
- GOLD 2023 deliberately separates this definition from epidemiology, causes, risk factors and diagnostic criteria, which it discusses on their own [[Agusti2023]].
- Diagnosis needs a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.7 on forced spirometry, in someone with symptoms (dyspnoea, chronic cough or sputum, recurrent lower respiratory infections) and/or exposure to risk factors [[Agusti2023]].
- GOLD 2023 notes that airflow obstruction that is not fully reversible is not specific for COPD; it also occurs in asthma and other diseases, so clinical context and risk factors must be considered [[Agusti2023]].
- If the post-bronchodilator ratio on a single test falls between 0.60 and 0.80, GOLD 2023 advises confirming it with repeat spirometry on a separate occasion [[Agusti2023]].
- GOLD 2023 describes COPD as resulting from dynamic, cumulative and repeated gene–environment interactions over the lifetime that damage the lungs and/or alter their normal development or ageing ("GETomics") [[Agusti2023]].
- The 2001 NHLBI/WHO GOLD workshop summary was the first GOLD global strategy for diagnosis, management and prevention of COPD [[Pauwels2001,Petty2006]].
- The 2006 history review defined COPD (following GOLD 2001) as chronic airflow obstruction that is progressive and only partly reversible, including chronic bronchitis, emphysema and chronic asthmatic bronchitis [[Petty2006]].

### what · Taxonomy: etiotypes, young COPD, pre-COPD and PRISm
- GOLD 2023 proposes a taxonomy of COPD by cause ("etiotypes"), meant to raise awareness of non-smoking COPD and to stimulate research into its mechanisms, prevention and treatment [[Agusti2023]].
- The 2022 Lancet Commission proposed five types: genetically determined COPD, COPD related to early-life events, infection-related COPD, COPD related to smoking or vaping, and environmental-exposure-related COPD (Early evidence; the Commission concedes some aspects are speculative) [[Stolz2022]].
- The Commission states that alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency and telomerase reverse transcriptase mutations are the only two monogenic variants clearly shown to cause COPD, while about 40% of the variability in airflow limitation is attributable to genetics [[Stolz2022]].
- GOLD 2023 proposes calling COPD in people aged 20–50 "young COPD", whether from never reaching normal peak lung function, a shorter plateau or early decline [[Agusti2023]].
- "Pre-COPD" describes people of any age with respiratory symptoms and/or structural or functional abnormalities but a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC above 0.7. They may or may not go on to develop COPD, and there is no evidence yet on how best to treat them [[Agusti2023]].
- "PRISm" (preserved ratio impaired spirometry) means FEV1/FVC ≥0.7 with FEV1 below 80% of reference after bronchodilator. Reported prevalence ranges from 7.1% to 20.3%; it is associated with higher all-cause mortality and can shift to normal, obstructive or restrictive spirometry over time [[Agusti2023]].
- GOLD 2023 recommends that "mild" be used only to describe the spirometric severity of airflow obstruction, not the stage of the disease [[Agusti2023]].

### home · Global burden: how many people
- In 2019, the global prevalence of COPD among people aged 30–79 was 10.3% (95% CI 8.2–12.8) by the GOLD fixed-ratio definition, or 391.9 million people, and 7.6% (5.8–10.1) by the lower-limit-of-normal (LLN) definition, or 292.0 million people [[Adeloye2022]].
- Of those 391.9 million, 315.5 million (80.5%) lived in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) [[Adeloye2022]].
- By WHO region, prevalence (ages 30–79, GOLD definition) was highest in the Western Pacific (11.7%) and lowest in the Americas (6.8%) [[Adeloye2022]].
- Once age and sex structure were taken into account, prevalence was 10.3% in LMICs and 10.1% in high-income countries (HICs) [[Adeloye2022]].
- The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 study counted 212.3 million prevalent COPD cases worldwide, with an age-standardised point prevalence of 2,638.2 per 100,000, which was 8.7% lower than in 1990 [[Safiri2022]].
- The two global counts differ (391.9 million vs 212.3 million) because they use different case definitions and methods: a spirometry-based meta-regression for ages 30–79 versus GBD modelling of all ages [[Adeloye2022,Safiri2022]].
- In GBD 2019, Denmark (4,299.5 per 100,000), Myanmar (3,963.7) and Belgium (3,927.7) had the highest age-standardised prevalence [[Safiri2022]].
- By GBD region in 2019, high-income North America (3,558.4 per 100,000), South Asia (3,298.8) and Australasia (3,192.8) had the highest age-standardised prevalence, and Andean Latin America (1,382.6) the lowest [[Safiri2022]].
- The population-based BOLD study (12 sites, 9,425 people aged 40 or over) found GOLD stage II or higher COPD in 10.1% overall, 11.8% of men and 8.5% of women [[Buist2007]].
- In BOLD, each extra 10 years of age raised the odds of COPD by a pooled factor of 1.94 (95% CI 1.80–2.10) [[Buist2007]].
- A modelling study projected a 23% rise in global COPD cases among people aged 25 and over between 2020 and 2050, approaching 600 million cases by 2050 [[Boers2023]].
- In that projection, cases in women rise by 47.1% compared with 9.4% in men, and cases in LMICs are expected to be more than double those in high-income regions by 2050 [[Boers2023]].
- In GBD 2017, chronic respiratory diseases were the third leading cause of death (7.0% of all deaths), and COPD caused most chronic-respiratory deaths in both sexes [[GBDCRD2020]].

### home · Global burden: deaths and disability
- COPD caused 3.3 million deaths and 74.4 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2019 [[Safiri2022]].
- COPD deaths rose from 2.5 million in 1990 to 3.3 million in 2019, although the age-standardised death rate (42.5 per 100,000) fell by 41.7% over the same period [[Safiri2022]].
- In GBD 2019, Nepal had the highest (182.5 per 100,000) and Japan the lowest (7.4) age-standardised COPD death rate [[Safiri2022]].
- By region, Oceania (112.1 per 100,000), South Asia (93.2) and East Asia (64.6) had the highest age-standardised death rates in 2019 [[Safiri2022]].
- In both 1990 and 2019 the leading causes of age-standardised death worldwide were, in order, ischaemic heart disease, stroke, COPD and lower respiratory infections. In 2021 COVID-19 moved COPD to fourth place [[GBD2021CausesOfDeath]].
- Elderly people and people in LMICs, where 80% of COPD deaths occur, are disproportionately affected [[Stolz2022]].
- Across regions, the burden followed a reversed-V pattern with the sociodemographic index: it was highest in middle-development settings, not the poorest or richest [[Safiri2022]].
- The Commission notes that despite COPD being among the leading causes of death, there were only 780 ongoing therapeutic COPD trials, compared with more than 41,000 cancer treatment trials [[Stolz2022]].

### home · Burden by sex and age
- Worldwide in 2019, GOLD-defined COPD prevalence among people aged 30–79 was more than twice as high in men (14.1%) as in women (6.5%) [[Adeloye2022]].
- Prevalence rises steeply with age: worldwide (GOLD definition) it is 3.8% at ages 30–34 and 27.8% at ages 75–79 [[Adeloye2022]].
- By the LLN definition, prevalence in LMICs was marginally higher in women (7.4%) than in men (7.1%), unlike in HICs [[Adeloye2022]].
- GOLD 2023 states that COPD prevalence in developed countries is now almost equal in males and females [[Agusti2023]].
- At the same severity of airflow limitation, women report more dyspnoea and worse health status and have more exacerbations than men [[Agusti2023]].
- In GBD 2019, more men than women had COPD up to age 70–74, but COPD was more common in women older than 74. The COPD death rate was higher in men in every age group [[Safiri2022]].
- Among people with early-onset COPD (under 60) or low smoking exposure (under 20 pack-years), women had lower FEV1 % predicted (48.7% vs 55.8% in the low-exposure group) and more severe disease than men, which suggests women may be more susceptible to smoke, though other explanations are possible (Early evidence) [[Sorheim2010]].
- In BOLD, the pack-year odds ratio for COPD varied significantly between sites in women (pooled OR 1.28 per 10 pack-years) but not in men (1.16) [[Buist2007]].

### home · Underdiagnosis and overdiagnosis
- Across 44 sites in 27 countries (30,874 adults aged 40 or over), 9.7% had a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below the LLN, but only 5.0% reported a previous COPD diagnosis and only 26.4% had ever had a lung function test [[Lamprecht2015]].
- Overall, 81.4% of spirometry-defined COPD cases were undiagnosed, ranging from 50.0% in Lexington, Kentucky to 98.3% in Ile-Ife, Nigeria [[Lamprecht2015]].
- Underdiagnosis was more likely in men, younger people, never and current smokers, people with less education, those never given spirometry, and those with milder airflow limitation [[Lamprecht2015]].
- A review of population studies found that 10–12% of adults aged 40 or over have persistent airflow limitation, but only 20–30% of them have been diagnosed, so about 70% of COPD worldwide may be undiagnosed [[Diab2018]].
- The same review reports that 30–60% of people with a physician diagnosis of COPD do not actually have it (overdiagnosis) [[Diab2018]].
- GOLD 2023 does not recommend screening spirometry for people without symptoms or risk factors, but says spirometry should be considered for case finding in people with symptoms and/or risk factors (for example more than 20 pack-years, recurrent chest infections or prematurity) [[Agusti2023]].

### causes · Tobacco smoke
- Cigarette smokers have more respiratory symptoms and lung function abnormalities, a faster yearly FEV1 decline and a higher COPD death rate than non-smokers [[Agusti2023]].
- Passive smoke, pipes, cigars, water pipes and marijuana are also listed by GOLD 2023 as risk factors for COPD [[Agusti2023]].
- Smoking in pregnancy can alter fetal lung growth and development [[Agusti2023]].
- In a global meta-analysis, current smoking raised the odds of COPD 3.2-fold (95% CI 2.5–4.0) and ever smoking 2.3-fold (2.0–2.5) [[Adeloye2022]].
- In GBD 2019, smoking accounted for 46.0% of the DALYs due to COPD worldwide [[Safiri2022]].
- In the Copenhagen City Heart Study (8,045 adults with normal lung function at baseline, followed 25 years), 96% of never-smoking men but only 59% of continuously smoking men still had normal lung function; for women the figures were 91% and 69% [[Lokke2006]].
- In that cohort, the absolute risk of COPD among continuous smokers was at least 25%, and 92% of COPD deaths occurred in people who were smoking at baseline [[Lokke2006]].
- GOLD 2023 states that fewer than 50% of heavy smokers develop COPD [[Agusti2023]]. An older review quoted only 15–20% of smokers developing airflow obstruction [[Petty2006]]; longer follow-up gives higher estimates (at least 25%) [[Lokke2006]].
- In 2019 an estimated 1.1 billion people smoked worldwide. The number of smokers fell by about 60 million between 2000 and 2018, but population growth cancelled out the benefit [[Stolz2022]].
- The Commission cites evidence that e-cigarette use in never-tobacco-smokers doubles or triples the risk of self-reported COPD, emphysema or chronic bronchitis, while stating that further studies are needed to confirm a direct effect (Early evidence) [[Stolz2022]].

### causes · Not only smoking
- The ATS estimated that the population-attributable fraction of COPD due to smoking ranged from 9.7% to 97.9% across studies, but was below 80% in most, so a substantial share of COPD comes from other risk factors [[Eisner2010]].
- The ATS concluded that specific genetic syndromes and occupational exposures cause COPD. Outdoor pollution, secondhand smoke, biomass smoke and diet are associated with it but did not meet causation criteria at the time (2010) [[Eisner2010]].
- An estimated 25–45% of people with COPD have never smoked [[Salvi2009]].
- In BOLD, never smokers made up 23.3% (240 of 1,031) of people with GOLD stage II+ COPD (20.5% using the LLN) [[Lamprecht2011]].
- Among 4,291 never smokers in BOLD, 6.6% had GOLD stage I and 5.6% had stage II+ COPD. Predictors included age, education, occupational exposure, childhood respiratory disease, BMI and previous asthma [[Lamprecht2011]].
- GOLD 2023 states that in LMICs COPD in non-smokers may account for up to 60–70% of cases, and that non-smoking risk factors account for over 50% of the global burden [[Agusti2023]].
- Compared with smokers' COPD, COPD in non-smokers is more common in women and younger people, with similar or milder symptoms, more small-airway obstruction, less emphysema and a slower lung function decline [[Agusti2023]].
- Underweight (BMI below 18.5 kg/m²) carried an odds ratio for COPD of 2.2 (95% CI 1.7–2.7) in the global meta-analysis [[Adeloye2022]].
- The Commission expects tobacco-unrelated risk factors to overtake smoking as a share of COPD risk within two decades (Hypothesis) [[Stolz2022]].

### causes · Household air pollution (biomass)
- About 3 billion people, half the world's population, are exposed to biomass smoke, compared with 1.01 billion who smoke tobacco, which suggests biomass may be the biggest COPD risk factor globally (Hypothesis) [[Salvi2009]].
- In a meta-analysis, solid fuel use was associated with COPD (OR 2.80, 95% CI 1.85–4.0) and chronic bronchitis (OR 2.32, 1.92–2.80). Wood smoke during domestic work carried the highest risk [[Kurmi2010]].
- In the global prevalence meta-analysis, biomass exposure had an odds ratio of 1.4 (1.2–1.7) [[Adeloye2022]].
- GOLD 2023 notes that how far household air pollution, rather than other poverty-related exposures, explains this association is still unclear [[Agusti2023]].

### causes · Outdoor air pollution
- In GBD 2019, ambient particulate matter accounted for 20.7% of COPD DALYs worldwide [[Safiri2022]].
- GOLD 2023 states that air pollution causes about 50% of the attributable risk for COPD in LMICs, that the risk is dose-dependent with no apparent "safe" threshold, and that it also raises the risk of exacerbations, hospital admission and death [[Agusti2023]].
- In UK Biobank (303,887 adults aged 40–69), each 5 µg/m³ higher PM2.5 was associated with 83.13 mL lower FEV1 and higher COPD prevalence (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.42–1.62) [[Doiron2019]].
- In 1,759 Southern California children followed from age 10 to 18, the share with low FEV1 (below 80% expected) at age 18 was 4.9 times higher at the highest than the lowest PM2.5 exposure (7.9% vs 1.6%) [[Gauderman2004]].
- As Southern California's air improved, the share of 15-year-olds with low FEV1 fell from 7.9% to 6.3% to 3.6% across three cohorts (1994–2011) [[Gauderman2015]].
- During the London smog of December 1952, weekly excess deaths in Greater London peaked at about 4,500. If the excess deaths in the following months were also due to pollution, the total would be about 12,000 rather than the 3,000–4,000 usually reported [[Bell2004]].

### causes · Work exposures
- An ATS/ERS statement estimated the occupational population-attributable fraction at 14% for COPD and 13% for chronic bronchitis [[Blanc2019]].
- GOLD 2023 cites US NHANES III estimates that 19.2% of COPD overall, and 31.1% among never smokers, is attributable to workplace exposures [[Agusti2023]].
- Occupational exposure to dust or smoke had a pooled odds ratio of 1.4 (1.3–1.6) for COPD [[Adeloye2022]], and occupational particulates, gases and fumes accounted for 15.6% of COPD DALYs in GBD 2019 [[Safiri2022]].

### causes · Early life and lung-function trajectories
- Lungs grow until about age 20–25 (earlier in females), then plateau briefly before a mild age-related decline. Events during gestation, birth, childhood and adolescence can lower peak lung function or speed up the decline [[Agusti2023]].
- GOLD 2023 lists "childhood disadvantage factors" (prematurity, low birth weight, maternal smoking in pregnancy, repeated respiratory infections, poor nutrition) as key determinants of peak lung function in early adulthood [[Agusti2023]].
- In three cohorts, 26% of people with FEV1 below 80% predicted before age 40 had COPD 22 years later, compared with 7% of those with normal FEV1 [[Lange2015]].
- About half of the people with COPD at the end of follow-up had started with normal FEV1 and declined fast (53 ± 21 mL a year). The other half had low FEV1 in early adulthood and a normal-range decline (27 ± 18 mL a year) [[Lange2015]].
- GOLD 2023 summarises this: roughly 50% of patients develop COPD through accelerated FEV1 decline and 50% through abnormal lung growth with normal decline [[Agusti2023,Lange2015]].
- In the Tucson cohort, infants whose airway function was in the lowest quartile at about 2 months still had lower FEV1/FVC (−5.2%) and FEV1 (−233 mL) at age 22, after adjustment for smoking and other factors [[Stern2007]].
- The Tasmanian cohort (lung function from age 7 to 53) found six FEV1 trajectories. Three of them (early below average with accelerated decline, persistently low, below average) accounted for 75% of COPD at age 53 [[Bui2018]].
- The "early below average, accelerated decline" trajectory carried an odds ratio of 35.0 (95% CI 19.5–64.0) for COPD at 53 compared with the average group. Its early-life predictors included childhood asthma, bronchitis, pneumonia, allergic disease, parental asthma and maternal smoking [[Bui2018]].
- Very to moderate preterm birth (28 to under 34 weeks) was associated with COPD at age 53 (OR 2.9, 95% CI 1.1–7.7); late preterm birth was not [[Bui2022]].
- FEV1 below 80% predicted at age 25–40 was found in 4–13% of three general-population cohorts and went with earlier comorbidity and higher all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 2.3 in the Framingham Offspring Cohort) [[Agusti2017]].
- In the Framingham Offspring Cohort, smoking sped up lung function decline in both sexes, susceptibility varied widely, and quitting helped at any age, more so when earlier [[Kohansal2009]].

### causes · Asthma, infections and other diseases
- In the Melbourne asthma cohort followed to age 50, children with severe asthma had an adjusted 32-fold higher risk of COPD (95% CI 3.4–269), and 43% of the COPD group had never smoked [[Tai2014]].
- GOLD 2023 cautions that abnormal lung development can cause asthma-like symptoms in children, so some may have been mislabelled as having asthma [[Agusti2023]].
- A history of tuberculosis was associated with COPD in adults over 40 (pooled OR 3.05, 95% CI 2.42–3.85), most strongly in high-TB countries, never smokers and younger people [[Byrne2015]].
- COPD prevalence among people with HIV was 10.6% (fixed ratio) and remained associated with HIV after adjustment for tobacco (OR 2.58, 95% CI 1.05–6.35) [[Bigna2018]].
- The Commission estimates that 10–42% of people with early-life pneumonia, tuberculosis or HIV develop COPD [[Stolz2022]].
- Poverty and lower socioeconomic status are consistently associated with airflow obstruction and COPD, probably through pollution, crowding, poor nutrition and infections [[Agusti2023]].

### causes · Genes
- The most important known genetic risk factor is SERPINA1 mutation causing alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency. PiZZ is found in 0.12% of people with COPD, and its prevalence ranges from 1 in 408 in Northern Europe to 1 in 1,274 in Eastern Europe (details: pass B) [[Agusti2023]].
- GOLD 2023 states there is no increased COPD risk in MZ and SZ heterozygotes who do not smoke [[Agusti2023]].
- Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency affects about 1 in 2,000–5,000 individuals and causes liver disease and early-onset emphysema [[Stoller2005]].
- Other genetic variants each have small effects on lung function and COPD risk, but together they may increase susceptibility [[Agusti2023]].

### causes · Fletcher–Peto: smoking, susceptibility and quitting
- Fletcher and Peto's study of London working men found that FEV1 falls gradually over life, but most non-smokers and many smokers never develop clinically significant airflow obstruction [[Fletcher1977]].
- In susceptible people, smoking caused irreversible obstruction. A susceptible smoker who stopped did not regain lost function, but further FEV1 loss returned to the normal rate [[Fletcher1977]].
- Fletcher and Peto found that infections and chronic mucus hypersecretion did not speed up airflow obstruction, and described them as two largely unrelated processes [[Fletcher1977]].
- In 2,718 British men followed for 20–25 years, COPD death risk was strongly tied to initial airflow obstruction but not to mucus hypersecretion at similar obstruction [[Peto1983]].
- In the Lung Health Study (3,926 smokers with mild to moderate obstruction), quitting improved FEV1 by an average of 47 mL (2%) in the first year. The later decline in sustained quitters was half that of continuing smokers (31 ± 48 vs 62 ± 55 mL a year), comparable to never smokers [[Scanlon2000]].
- After 14.5 years, all-cause mortality in the Lung Health Study was lower in the smoking-cessation group than in usual care (8.83 vs 10.38 per 1,000 person-years; HR for usual care 1.18, 95% CI 1.02–1.37) [[Anthonisen2005]].
- Among male British doctors, men born 1900–1930 who kept smoking died about 10 years earlier than lifelong non-smokers. Stopping at age 60, 50, 40 or 30 gained about 3, 6, 9 or 10 years of life expectancy [[Doll2004]].
- The Commission argues that Fletcher and Peto's picture of COPD as a self-inflicted disease of smokers with accelerated decline is increasingly undermined by non-smoking risk factors and by people who reach COPD without fast decline [[Stolz2022,Lange2015]].

### home · Life expectancy (population figures)
- In US NHANES III, COPD went with only a modest reduction in life expectancy for never smokers, but a large one for current and former smokers [[Shavelle2009]].
- At age 65, current-smoking men lost an estimated 0.3, 2.2 and 5.8 years of life expectancy for GOLD stage 1, 2 and 3–4, on top of 3.5 years lost to smoking. For never smokers the losses were 0.7 years (stage 2) and 1.3 years (stage 3–4) [[Shavelle2009]].

### what · Patient FAQ answers (cited)
- What is COPD? A long-term lung condition in which damaged airways and/or air sacs make it hard to breathe out fully. It causes breathlessness, cough, phlegm and flare-ups, and the narrowing does not fully go away [[Agusti2023]].
- How common is it? About 1 in 10 adults aged 30–79 worldwide had COPD in 2019 (about 392 million people), most of them in low- and middle-income countries [[Adeloye2022]].
- Can people who never smoked get COPD? Yes. Never smokers made up about a quarter of people with moderate or worse COPD in the BOLD study. Other causes include smoke from cooking fires, polluted air, dust and fumes at work, past tuberculosis and problems with lung growth in early life [[Lamprecht2011,Salvi2009,Agusti2023]].
- Is COPD contagious or inherited? It is not an infection that spreads. A small number of people inherit a strong risk, mainly alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, and smaller gene effects add up in others [[Agusti2023,Stoller2005]].
- Does stopping smoking still help once you have COPD? Yes. In the Lung Health Study, people who quit regained some lung function in the first year, then lost it at about half the rate of people who kept smoking. A quit-smoking programme also lowered deaths over 14.5 years [[Scanlon2000,Anthonisen2005]].
- How is it diagnosed? With a breathing test called spirometry, done after an inhaled reliever (bronchodilator). A ratio of FEV1 to FVC below 0.7 confirms airflow obstruction (details: spirometry module) [[Agusti2023]].
- Can COPD be cured? Lung damage that has already happened does not reverse, but several treatments, including some medicines, oxygen for low blood oxygen and quitting smoking, have been shown to lower the risk of death in selected people (details: treatment modules) [[Agusti2023,Anthonisen2005,NOTT1980]].
- What is the outlook? It varies a lot. In a large US study, COPD shortened life expectancy only modestly in never smokers with mild-to-moderate disease, and more in smokers with severe disease. These are group averages, not predictions for one person [[Shavelle2009]].

### history · Early descriptions
- Early descriptions of emphysema include Bonet's "voluminous lungs" (1679), Morgagni's 19 cases of "turgid" lungs (1769), and Baillie's illustrations of an emphysematous lung, thought to be Samuel Johnson's (1789) [[Petty2006]].
- Badham (1814) used the word "catarrh" for the chronic cough and mucus hypersecretion of chronic bronchitis and described bronchiolitis and chronic bronchitis as disabling disorders [[Petty2006]].
- Laënnec, inventor of the stethoscope, described emphysematous lungs in his treatise on diseases of the chest as hyperinflated, failing to collapse and not emptying well, often with mucus-filled bronchi: an early account of emphysema and chronic bronchitis together [[Petty2006]].

### history · Measuring breath: the spirometer and the FEV1
- John Hutchinson, a surgeon, invented the spirometer and published his method in 1846. He named the volume exhaled from fully inflated lungs the "vital capacity", the capacity to live [[Hutchinson1846,Petty2002]].
- Hutchinson's instrument measured only vital capacity. It took about 100 more years before airflow over time was added [[Petty2006]].
- Tiffeneau and Pinelli in Paris described the forced expiratory manoeuvre in December 1947: the maximal volume exhaled in one second after a deep breath, which they called the CPUE [[Yernault1997]].
- A group of European experts renamed the CPUE "VEMS" on 13 February 1954. The English term "forced expiratory volume" was adopted in 1957 [[Yernault1997]].
- In the US, Gaensler introduced the "air velocity index" (1950) and the "timed vital capacity" (1951), foundations of today's FEV1 and FEV1/FVC [[Gaensler1950,Yernault1997,Petty2006]].

### history · Naming and defining the disease
- The 1959 Ciba Guest Symposium report set out terminology, definitions and classification for chronic pulmonary emphysema and related conditions [[Ciba1959,Petty2006]].
- The ATS Committee on Diagnostic Standards (1962) defined chronic bronchitis clinically (chronic cough for at least three months in at least two years) and emphysema anatomically (enlarged airspaces with loss of alveolar walls), with no physiological criteria [[Petty2006]].
- In 1965 the UK Medical Research Council published a definition and classification of chronic bronchitis for clinical and epidemiological purposes [[MRC1965]].
- Reid (1960) proposed measuring the bronchial mucous gland layer, later called the Reid index, as a diagnostic yardstick in chronic bronchitis [[Reid1960,Petty2006]].
- Before "COPD", names included chronic obstructive bronchopulmonary disease, chronic airflow obstruction and chronic obstructive lung disease. William Briscoe is believed to have first used the term COPD, at the 9th Aspen Emphysema Conference [[Petty2006]].
- The Aspen Emphysema Conferences began in 1958. At the 9th, Burrows and Fletcher presented the emphysematous and bronchial types of chronic airways obstruction seen in London and Chicago, published in 1966 [[Petty2006,Burrows1966]].
- The "Dutch hypothesis" (Orie, 1960) emphasised inherited bronchial hyperreactivity, and the "British hypothesis" emphasised repeated chest infections and air pollution [[Petty2006]].

### history · Causes discovered
- Doll and Hill's 1954 preliminary report linked doctors' smoking habits to their mortality. The British Doctors Study then ran from 1951 to 2001 in 34,439 men [[Doll1954,Doll2004]].
- The London smog of 1952 became one of history's most important air-pollution episodes for science, public perception and regulation [[Bell2004]].
- In 1963 Laurell and Eriksson in Sweden described alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency from the missing α1-globulin band on serum electrophoresis and linked it to emphysema [[Laurell1963,Perciaccante2018]].
- In 1964 Gross produced emphysema in animals by instilling papain, an accidental finding that, together with alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, founded the protease–antiprotease theory [[Petty2006]].
- Fletcher and Peto's 1977 study described the natural history of chronic airflow obstruction and showed the value of quitting in susceptible smokers [[Fletcher1977]].

### history · Treatment milestones
- In the early 1960s, oxygen was considered contraindicated in COPD and exercise was discouraged for fear of straining the right heart. Inhaled isoproterenol came into use in the early 1960s [[Petty2006]].
- The 1980 Nocturnal Oxygen Therapy Trial (203 hypoxaemic patients) found mortality 1.94 times higher with 12-hour nocturnal oxygen than with continuous oxygen (P = 0.01) [[NOTT1980]].
- In the 1981 MRC trial (87 patients), 19 of 42 given oxygen for at least 15 hours a day died over 5 years, compared with 30 of 45 controls [[MRC1981]].
- The 2016 LOTT trial (738 patients with moderate desaturation) found no difference in time to death or first hospitalisation with long-term oxygen (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.79–1.12) [[Albert2016]].
- Lung volume reduction surgery was introduced by Brantigan and Mueller in 1957 and revived in the 1990s [[Petty2006]]. NETT (2003, 1,218 patients) found no overall survival benefit, but lower mortality in upper-lobe emphysema with low exercise capacity (risk ratio 0.47) [[Fishman2003]].
- In 2001 the WHO and NHLBI launched GOLD, which offered a new severity classification [[Pauwels2001,Petty2006]].
- GOLD 2017 separated spirometric grading from the ABCD groups, which were now derived only from symptoms and exacerbation history [[Vogelmeier2017]].
- GOLD 2023 introduced the new definition and taxonomy and merged the former C and D groups into a single "E" (exacerbations) group, creating the ABE tool [[Agusti2023]].

### home · Step sequence
1. Kicker: Breathe. Title: Air in, air out. Normal lungs grow until about age 20–25 and then lose a little function each year with age [[Agusti2023]].
2. Kicker: The condition. Title: Airways and air sacs damaged. In COPD, damaged airways and/or alveoli cause persistent, often progressive airflow obstruction with breathlessness, cough and flare-ups [[Agusti2023]].
3. Kicker: How many. Title: About 1 in 10. In 2019, 10.3% of people aged 30–79 worldwide, about 392 million, had COPD [[Adeloye2022]].
4. Kicker: Unseen. Title: Most cases undiagnosed. About 81% of people whose spirometry showed COPD had never been diagnosed [[Lamprecht2015]].
5. Kicker: Toll. Title: Millions of deaths. COPD caused 3.3 million deaths in 2019 and was the third leading cause of age-standardised death until COVID-19 [[Safiri2022,GBD2021CausesOfDeath]].
6. Kicker: Hope. Title: Prevention works. Quitting smoking halves the later rate of FEV1 decline, and cleaner air improves children's lung growth [[Scanlon2000,Gauderman2015]].

### what · Step sequence
1. Kicker: Healthy. Title: Open airways, elastic sacs. Lungs reach peak function in early adulthood and then follow a short plateau and slow decline [[Agusti2023]].
2. Kicker: Airways. Title: Bronchitis and bronchiolitis. Inflamed, narrowed airways are one of the two abnormalities GOLD 2023 names [[Agusti2023]].
3. Kicker: Alveoli. Title: Emphysema. Loss of alveolar walls is the second; Laënnec saw emphysematous lungs that stayed inflated and did not empty [[Agusti2023,Petty2006]].
4. Kicker: Obstruction. Title: Breathing out is slow. Together these cause persistent airflow obstruction, shown as a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.7 [[Agusti2023]].
5. Kicker: Before COPD. Title: Pre-COPD and PRISm. Some people have symptoms or abnormal lungs with a normal ratio; they may or may not progress [[Agusti2023]].
6. Kicker: Many causes. Title: Etiotypes. GOLD 2023 and the Lancet Commission classify COPD by cause: genes, early life, infection, smoking or vaping, environment [[Agusti2023,Stolz2022]].

### causes · Step sequence
1. Kicker: Genes. Title: Born with a risk. SERPINA1 variants (alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency) and many small-effect variants raise susceptibility [[Agusti2023]].
2. Kicker: Early life. Title: Lungs that grow less. Prematurity, maternal smoking and childhood infections lower peak lung function; low early-adult FEV1 is behind about half of COPD [[Agusti2023,Lange2015,Bui2022]].
3. Kicker: Smoke. Title: Tobacco. Current smoking roughly triples the odds of COPD (OR 3.2) and accounts for 46% of COPD DALYs [[Adeloye2022,Safiri2022]].
4. Kicker: Air. Title: Fires, traffic and work. Solid-fuel smoke (OR 2.80), PM2.5 (OR 1.52 per 5 µg/m³) and work dusts (PAF 14%) add risk [[Kurmi2010,Doiron2019,Blanc2019]].
5. Kicker: Infection. Title: TB, HIV and childhood illness. Past tuberculosis triples the odds of COPD (OR 3.05) [[Byrne2015]].
6. Kicker: Two roads. Title: Fast decline or low start. About half of people reach COPD through fast FEV1 loss and half from a low starting point [[Lange2015]].

### history · Step sequence
1. Kicker: 1679–1821. Title: Voluminous lungs. Bonet, Morgagni and Baillie described over-inflated lungs, and Laënnec linked them to the living patient with his stethoscope [[Petty2006]].
2. Kicker: 1846. Title: The spirometer. Hutchinson measured the vital capacity, "the capacity to live" [[Hutchinson1846,Petty2002]].
3. Kicker: 1947. Title: One second. Tiffeneau and Pinelli timed the forced breath out, the origin of the FEV1 [[Yernault1997]].
4. Kicker: 1959–1965. Title: Naming the disease. The Ciba symposium, the ATS and the MRC defined emphysema and chronic bronchitis; "COPD" entered use at Aspen [[Ciba1959,MRC1965,Petty2006]].
5. Kicker: 1963–1981. Title: Causes and the first life-saving treatment. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (1963), Fletcher–Peto (1977) and the oxygen trials (1980–81) [[Laurell1963,Fletcher1977,NOTT1980,MRC1981]].
6. Kicker: 2001–2023. Title: GOLD. From the 2001 global strategy to the 2023 definition, etiotypes and ABE tool [[Pauwels2001,Agusti2023]].

### Exam hooks
- GOLD 2023 definition: a heterogeneous lung condition with chronic respiratory symptoms due to airway and/or alveolar abnormalities causing persistent, often progressive airflow obstruction [[Agusti2023]].
- Diagnosis needs post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.7; repeat the test if a single result is between 0.60 and 0.80 [[Agusti2023]].
- Global prevalence (ages 30–79, 2019): 10.3% GOLD vs 7.6% LLN; 391.9 million people; 80.5% in LMICs [[Adeloye2022]].
- COPD caused 3.3 million deaths in 2019; deaths rose from 2.5 million in 1990 while the age-standardised death rate fell 41.7% [[Safiri2022]].
- Top attributable risks to COPD DALYs (GBD 2019): smoking 46.0%, ambient PM 20.7%, occupational 15.6% [[Safiri2022]].
- About 81% of spirometry-defined COPD is undiagnosed [[Lamprecht2015]]; 30–60% of physician-diagnosed COPD may be overdiagnosis [[Diab2018]].
- About a quarter of moderate or worse COPD occurs in never smokers [[Lamprecht2011]]; estimates range from 25% to 45% [[Salvi2009]].
- Lange 2015: half of COPD arises from normal early FEV1 with fast decline (53 mL/yr), half from low early FEV1 with normal decline (27 mL/yr) [[Lange2015]].
- Fletcher–Peto: quitting does not restore lost FEV1 but returns its decline to the normal rate [[Fletcher1977]]; Lung Health Study: 31 vs 62 mL/yr [[Scanlon2000]].
- PRISm = FEV1/FVC ≥0.7 with FEV1 below 80% predicted [[Agusti2023]].
- Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency was described by Laurell and Eriksson in 1963 [[Laurell1963,Perciaccante2018]].
- NOTT 1980: mortality 1.94 times higher with nocturnal-only vs continuous oxygen [[NOTT1980]].
- Tiffeneau and Pinelli (1947) introduced the forced expiratory volume in one second [[Yernault1997]].

### In plain words
- COPD is a long-term lung condition that makes it hard to breathe out all the way [[Agusti2023]].
- The tubes that carry air can get swollen and narrow, and the tiny air sacs can get damaged. Many people have both [[Agusti2023]].
- About 1 in 10 adults over 30 around the world has COPD, and many of them do not know it [[Adeloye2022,Lamprecht2015]].
- A simple breathing test called spirometry is the way to find out [[Agusti2023]].
- Smoking is the biggest single cause, but smoke from cooking fires, dirty air, dust at work, old lung infections and being born early can cause it too [[Safiri2022,Salvi2009,Agusti2023]].
- Some people never grow their full lung power as children. They can get COPD later even if their lungs wear out at a normal pace [[Lange2015]].
- Stopping smoking at any age slows the damage. Lung function lost does not come back, but the loss slows down [[Fletcher1977,Scanlon2000]].
- COPD is not contagious. A few people inherit a strong risk, such as alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency [[Agusti2023]].
- The numbers here describe large groups of people. They cannot tell what will happen to any one person [[Shavelle2009]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Fixed ratio vs LLN: the GOLD fixed ratio (FEV1/FVC below 0.7) gives a global prevalence of 10.3%, the LLN 7.6% [[Adeloye2022]]. GOLD 2023 keeps the fixed ratio for diagnosis [[Agusti2023]]. The choice changes who is counted, especially at older ages (detail: pass C). Status: unresolved.
- How many smokers get COPD: "15–20%" (older estimate quoted in [[Petty2006]]); "at least 25%" of continuous smokers over 25 years [[Lokke2006]]; "fewer than 50% of heavy smokers" [[Agusti2023]]. The figure depends on follow-up length and definition.
- Never-smoker share: 23.3% of GOLD II+ COPD in BOLD [[Lamprecht2011]]; 25–45% in a review [[Salvi2009]]; up to 60–70% in LMICs per GOLD 2023 [[Agusti2023]]. Status: varies by setting.
- Biomass as a cause: the meta-analysis finds a consistent association (OR 2.80) [[Kurmi2010]], and Salvi and Barnes suggest it may be the biggest global risk factor (Hypothesis) [[Salvi2009]]. The 2010 ATS review found association but not proven causation [[Eisner2010]], and GOLD 2023 says confounding by other poverty-related exposures is unclear [[Agusti2023]].
- Sex differences: higher prevalence in men globally [[Adeloye2022]] vs near-equal prevalence in developed countries and possible greater female susceptibility [[Agusti2023,Sorheim2010]]; projected faster growth in women [[Boers2023]].
- Is Fletcher–Peto still the model? Accelerated decline explains only about half of COPD [[Lange2015]]; the Lancet Commission calls for a cause-based taxonomy [[Stolz2022]]. Status: the GOLD 2023 taxonomy is a proposal, not a validated classification.
- E-cigarettes and cannabis: the Commission calls the data on e-cigarettes "compelling and concerning" but unconfirmed [[Stolz2022]]; GOLD 2023 lists marijuana as a risk factor [[Agusti2023]]. Status: Early evidence.
- Pre-COPD and PRISm: no trial evidence yet on treatment [[Agusti2023]].
- Counting the burden: GBD (212.3 million) vs spirometry meta-regression (391.9 million) differ by method and age range [[Safiri2022,Adeloye2022]].
