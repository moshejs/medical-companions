# F · Smoking cessation, vaccines, rehabilitation, oxygen & advanced therapies

Pass date: 2026-11-07. Modules served: quitting, rehab, oxygen, advanced.

This pass covers the treatments for COPD that are not inhaled medicines. It includes stopping smoking (the Lung Health Study, varenicline, nicotine replacement, bupropion, cytisine, e-cigarettes), vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, RSV, and GOLD's list including COVID-19, pertussis and zoster), and pulmonary rehabilitation (stable disease, after an exacerbation, at home and by telehealth). It also covers long-term oxygen (NOTT, MRC, LOTT, INOX, REDOX), home non-invasive ventilation (Köhnlein, HOT-HMV), lung volume reduction surgery (NETT), endobronchial valves (LIBERATE, TRANSFORM, IMPACT, STELVIO, BeLieVeR-HIFi, EMPROVE), lung transplantation (ISHLT), alpha-1 antitrypsin augmentation (RAPID, RAPID-OLE, EXACTLE), and palliative care and breathlessness (opioids including BEAMS and MORDYC, palliative oxygen, the handheld fan, an integrated breathlessness service).

This file reports what trials, guidelines and labels say. It does not tell anyone what to do. Doses are quoted from the label or trial that used them.

## Findings

### quitting · Why stopping smoking matters: the Lung Health Study
- The Lung Health Study randomised 5887 smokers aged 35–60 with spirometric signs of early COPD at 10 centres in the United States and Canada. The arms were a smoking intervention plus ipratropium, a smoking intervention plus placebo, or no intervention. The smoking intervention was a 12-session programme of behaviour modification plus nicotine gum, with a 5-year maintenance programme [[Anthonisen1994]].
- Both smoking-intervention groups had significantly smaller FEV1 declines than the control group over 5 years. Most of the difference appeared in the first year and came from quitting, and sustained quitters gained the most [[Anthonisen1994]].
- Ipratropium gave a small, non-cumulative FEV1 benefit that disappeared when the inhaler was stopped. It "did not influence the long-term decline of FEV1" [[Anthonisen1994]].
- In 3818 Lung Health Study participants with analysable results, people who stopped smoking gained an average of 47 mL (2%) in FEV1 in the year after quitting [[Scanlon2000]].
- After that first year, sustained quitters lost FEV1 at half the rate of continuing smokers: 31 ± 48 versus 62 ± 55 mL per year (mean ± SD). The authors described this as comparable to never-smokers [[Scanlon2000]].
- Respiratory symptoms did not predict change in lung function. Smokers with airflow obstruction benefited from quitting "despite previous heavy smoking, advanced age, poor baseline lung function, or airway hyperresponsiveness" [[Scanlon2000]].
- Fletcher and Peto's cohort of London working men found that a susceptible smoker who stops "will not recover his lung function, but the average further rates of loss of FEV1 will revert to normal" [[Fletcher1977]].
- At 5 years, 21.7% of the Lung Health Study special-intervention group had stopped smoking since entry, versus 5.4% of usual care [[Anthonisen2005]].
- After up to 14.5 years, all-cause mortality was 8.83 versus 10.38 per 1000 person-years (special intervention versus usual care; P = 0.03). The hazard ratio for usual care versus intervention was 1.18 (95% CI 1.02–1.37) [[Anthonisen2005]].
- Of the 731 deaths in that follow-up, 33% were from lung cancer, 22% from cardiovascular disease and 7.8% from non-cancer respiratory disease [[Anthonisen2005]].
- The authors conclude that cessation programmes "can have a substantial effect on subsequent mortality, even when successful in a minority of participants" [[Anthonisen2005]].
- GOLD's figure of therapies with evidence of reduced mortality cites the 14.5-year Lung Health Study for smoking cessation, alongside long-term oxygen (NOTT, MRC), pulmonary rehabilitation, triple therapy (IMPACT, ETHOS), NIV and lung volume reduction surgery [[Agusti2023]].
- GOLD 2023 states: "Approximately 40% of people with COPD continue to smoke despite knowing that they have the disease". It adds that "All patients who continue to smoke should be offered help and treatment to quit" [[Agusti2023]].

### quitting · Medicines and support that help people with COPD quit
- A Cochrane review of 16 trials (13,123 participants) in smokers with COPD found high-quality evidence that pharmacotherapy plus high-intensity behavioural treatment beats placebo plus the same behavioural treatment (RR 2.53, 95% CI 1.83–3.50; 4 studies, 1540 participants) [[vanEerd2016]].
- In the same review, varenicline (RR 3.34, 95% CI 1.88–5.92) and nicotine sublingual tablets (RR 2.60, 95% CI 1.29–5.24) increased quit rates over placebo. Bupropion did too (pooled RR 2.03, 95% CI 1.26–3.28) [[vanEerd2016]].
- High-intensity behavioural treatment raised abstinence compared with usual care (RR 25.38, 95% CI 8.03–80.22) and with low-intensity behavioural treatment (RR 2.18). The review found "no convincing evidence for preferring any particular form" of behavioural or drug treatment [[vanEerd2016]].
- In a 27-centre trial of 504 smokers with mild to moderate COPD (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC < 70%, FEV1 ≥ 50% predicted), continuous abstinence over weeks 9–12 was 42.3% with varenicline versus 8.8% with placebo (OR 8.40, 95% CI 4.99–14.14) [[Tashkin2011Chest]].
- Abstinence in that trial stayed higher through weeks 9–52: 18.6% versus 5.6% (OR 4.04). The most common adverse events on varenicline were nausea, abnormal dreams, upper respiratory tract infection and insomnia [[Tashkin2011Chest]].
- In the same trial, post-bronchodilator FEV1 at week 12 had risen by 121.8 mL in continuous abstainers versus 37.9 mL in continuous smokers (P = 0.0069). The difference was no longer significant at weeks 24 or 52 [[Tashkin2011RespirMed]].
- The Cochrane review of nicotine receptor partial agonists (75 trials, 45,049 people) found high-certainty evidence that varenicline helps more people quit than placebo (RR 2.32, 95% CI 2.15–2.51). It also beat bupropion (RR 1.36) and a single form of NRT (RR 1.25) [[LivingstoneBanks2023]].
- The same review found no clear difference between varenicline and dual-form NRT (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.87–1.20; low certainty) [[LivingstoneBanks2023]].
- Cytisine helped more people quit than placebo (RR 1.30, 95% CI 1.15–1.47; moderate certainty) [[LivingstoneBanks2023]].
- People taking varenicline were probably more likely to report serious adverse events (RR 1.23, 95% CI 1.01–1.48; moderate certainty). The estimates for cardiac (RR 1.20) and neuropsychiatric (RR 0.89) serious adverse events had confidence intervals compatible with both benefit and harm [[LivingstoneBanks2023]].
- Across 133 trials (64,640 participants), any form of NRT raised long-term abstinence compared with control (RR 1.55, 95% CI 1.49–1.61). The pooled RRs were gum 1.49, patch 1.64, tablets/lozenges 1.52, inhalator 1.90 and nasal spray 2.02 [[HartmannBoyce2018]].
- The NRT review concluded that NRTs "increase the rate of quitting by 50% to 60%, regardless of setting". NRT can rarely cause non-ischaemic chest pain and palpitations (OR 1.88 for chest pains or palpitations) [[HartmannBoyce2018]].
- Across 52 trials (19,488 participants), combining pharmacotherapy with behavioural support increased quitting compared with usual care or brief advice (RR 1.83, 95% CI 1.68–1.98). The effect was larger when people were recruited in healthcare settings (RR 1.97) [[Stead2016]].
- That review left the Lung Health Study out of pooling because its very intensive programme produced a much larger effect (RR 3.88, 95% CI 3.35–4.50) [[Stead2016]].
- An ERS expert statement notes that many people with pulmonary diseases "are not given specific advice on the benefits or referred for intensive cessation treatments and, therefore, continue smoking". It also describes managing people who find it hard to quit with standard approaches [[JimenezRuiz2015]].

### quitting · Neuropsychiatric safety: EAGLES and the varenicline label
- EAGLES randomised 8144 motivated smokers, with and without psychiatric disorders, at 140 centres in 16 countries. The arms were varenicline 1 mg twice a day, bupropion 150 mg twice a day, nicotine patch 21 mg/day with taper, or placebo, each for 12 weeks [[Anthenelli2016]].
- In the non-psychiatric cohort of EAGLES, moderate and severe neuropsychiatric adverse events occurred in 1.3% (varenicline), 2.2% (bupropion), 2.5% (patch) and 2.4% (placebo) [[Anthenelli2016]].
- In the psychiatric cohort, the rates were 6.5%, 6.7%, 5.2% and 4.9%. None of the varenicline–placebo or bupropion–placebo risk differences was statistically significant [[Anthenelli2016]].
- Varenicline gave higher abstinence than placebo (OR 3.61), nicotine patch (OR 1.68) and bupropion (OR 1.75). Bupropion (OR 2.07) and patch (OR 2.15) each beat placebo [[Anthenelli2016]].
- The most frequent adverse event in EAGLES was nausea on varenicline (25%), insomnia on bupropion (12%), abnormal dreams on the nicotine patch (12%) and headache on placebo (10%) [[Anthenelli2016]].
- The current Pfizer CHANTIX label (openFDA, effective 2026-01-23) has no boxed warning. Under Warnings and Precautions 5.1 it lists postmarketing reports of neuropsychiatric adverse events, including mood changes, psychosis and suicidal ideation. It also lists seizures (5.2) and interaction with alcohol [[FDA-Chantix]].
- The CHANTIX label states the dosing as follows. Start one week before the quit date, or quit between days 8 and 35. Take 0.5 mg once daily on days 1–3, then 0.5 mg twice daily on days 4–7, then 1 mg twice daily for a total of 12 weeks. An additional 12 weeks "is recommended for successful quitters" [[FDA-Chantix]].

### quitting · E-cigarettes
- In a UK NHS stop-smoking-service trial of 886 adults (both groups had weekly behavioural support), 1-year validated abstinence was 18.0% with a refillable e-cigarette versus 9.9% with NRT of the participant's choice (RR 1.83, 95% CI 1.30–2.58) [[Hajek2019]].
- Among people abstinent at 1 year in that trial, 80% in the e-cigarette group were still using their product at 52 weeks, versus 9% in the NRT group [[Hajek2019]].
- The e-cigarette group reported greater declines in cough and phlegm from baseline. Throat or mouth irritation was more common with e-cigarettes (65.3% vs 51.2%) and nausea more common with NRT (37.9% vs 31.3%) [[Hajek2019]].
- The 2024 Cochrane living review (88 studies, 27,235 participants) found high-certainty evidence that nicotine e-cigarettes increase quit rates compared with NRT (RR 1.59, 95% CI 1.29–1.93). That might mean four more quitters per 100 [[Lindson2024]].
- The review found no evidence of serious harm from nicotine e-cigarettes, but "the longest follow-up was two years and the number of studies was small" (Early evidence for long-term safety) [[Lindson2024]].

### quitting · Vaccines
- GOLD 2023 states that, "Depending on local guidelines, patients should be offered vaccination against influenza, pneumococcus, COVID-19, pertussis, and herpes zoster, if they have not already received these" [[Agusti2023]].
- GOLD 2023 also states that people with COPD "should have COVID-19 vaccination in line with national guidelines" [[Agusti2023]].
- In the Cochrane review of influenza vaccine for COPD, inactivated vaccine reduced total exacerbations per vaccinated person compared with placebo (MD −0.37, 95% CI −0.64 to −0.11; 2 RCTs, 180 participants; low-quality evidence) [[Kopsaftis2018]].
- The influenza benefit came from fewer "late" exacerbations, those 3–4 or more weeks after vaccination (MD −0.39). There was no increase in early exacerbations, and local reactions were mild and transient [[Kopsaftis2018]].
- In the Cochrane review of pneumococcal vaccines in COPD (12 RCTs, 2171 participants), vaccination lowered the odds of community-acquired pneumonia (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.43–0.89; NNTB 21) [[Walters2017]].
- Pneumococcal vaccination also lowered the odds of a COPD exacerbation (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.39–0.93; NNTB 8). It did not change all-cause mortality (OR 1.00) or confirmed pneumococcal pneumonia (Peto OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.05–1.31) [[Walters2017]].
- In CAPiTA (84,496 adults aged ≥ 65), PCV13 prevented first episodes of vaccine-type pneumococcal community-acquired pneumonia (vaccine efficacy 45.6%) and vaccine-type invasive pneumococcal disease (75.0%). It did not prevent community-acquired pneumonia from any cause (5.1%, 95% CI −5.1 to 14.2) [[Bonten2015CAPiTA]].
- AReSVi-006 (24,966 adults aged ≥ 60) tested a single dose of the AS01E-adjuvanted RSVPreF3 OA vaccine (Arexvy). Efficacy was 82.6% against RSV lower respiratory tract disease (7 vs 40 cases) and 94.1% against severe disease. High efficacy was seen in participants with coexisting conditions [[Papi2023RSV]].
- RENOIR (34,284 adults aged ≥ 60) tested the bivalent RSVpreF vaccine (Abrysvo). Efficacy was 66.7% against RSV lower respiratory tract illness with ≥ 2 signs or symptoms and 85.7% with ≥ 3 [[Walsh2023RSV]].
- ConquerRSV (35,541 adults aged ≥ 60) tested mRNA-1345 (mResvia). Efficacy was 83.7% against RSV lower respiratory tract disease with ≥ 2 signs or symptoms and 68.4% against RSV acute respiratory disease. Median follow-up was 112 days [[Wilson2023]].
- On 26 June 2024, ACIP recommended a single dose of any FDA-approved RSV vaccine (Arexvy, Abrysvo or mResvia) for all adults aged ≥ 75 and for adults aged 60–74 at increased risk of severe RSV disease. It stated that people already vaccinated "should not receive another dose" [[Britton2024]].

### rehab · What pulmonary rehabilitation is
- The ATS/ERS 2013 statement defines pulmonary rehabilitation as "a comprehensive intervention based on a thorough patient assessment followed by patient-tailored therapies that include, but are not limited to, exercise training, education, and behavior change, designed to improve the physical and psychological condition of people with [chronic respiratory disease] and to promote the long-term adherence to health-enhancing behaviors" (as quoted in the 2023 ATS guideline) [[Spruit2013,Rochester2023]].
- The 2013 statement adds new evidence on rehabilitation in acutely ill people with COPD, its role in chronic disease management, and the role of health behaviour change in keeping the benefits [[Spruit2013]].
- The 2023 ATS guideline makes a strong recommendation for pulmonary rehabilitation in adults with stable COPD and after hospitalisation for an exacerbation (moderate-quality evidence for both) [[Rochester2023]].
- The same guideline makes a strong recommendation to offer a choice of centre-based rehabilitation or telerehabilitation. It makes a conditional recommendation for either supervised maintenance or usual care after the first programme (low-quality evidence) [[Rochester2023]].
- The 2023 ATS guideline states that "Less than 5% of people with COPD who may benefit from PR receive it" [[Rochester2023]].
- GOLD 2023 states that patients with high symptom burden and exacerbation risk (GOLD groups B and E) "should be recommended to take part in a formal PR program". It also says tele-rehabilitation "is safe and has similar benefits to those of center-based PR" [[Agusti2023]].

### rehab · What it achieves in stable COPD
- The 2015 Cochrane review pooled 65 RCTs (3822 participants; mean FEV1 about 37–39% predicted). Most programmes ran 8 or 12 weeks [[McCarthy2015]].
- Rehabilitation improved Chronic Respiratory Questionnaire domains beyond the 0.5-unit minimal important difference: dyspnoea MD 0.79, fatigue 0.68, emotional function 0.56 and mastery 0.71 [[McCarthy2015]].
- SGRQ total score improved by 6.89 points (95% CI −9.26 to −4.52), more than the 4-unit threshold [[McCarthy2015]].
- Six-minute walk distance improved by 43.93 m (95% CI 32.64–55.21; 38 studies, 1879 participants). Maximal exercise improved by 6.77 W [[McCarthy2015]].
- The Cochrane authors concluded that "additional RCTs comparing pulmonary rehabilitation and conventional care in COPD are not warranted" [[McCarthy2015]].
- Exercise-only programmes and programmes with more complex add-ons did not differ significantly in effect. Hospital-based programmes showed larger CRQ effects than community-based ones, but the authors warn this may be confounded [[McCarthy2015]].

### rehab · After an exacerbation, and when to start
- The Cochrane review of rehabilitation after exacerbations (20 studies, 1477 participants) found moderate-quality evidence of fewer hospital readmissions (OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.21–0.91). Results were heterogeneous (I² = 77%) [[Puhan2016]].
- Post-exacerbation rehabilitation improved SGRQ by 7.80 points and 6MWD by 62 m (95% CI 38–86) (high-quality evidence). Mortality did not change significantly (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.28–1.67; low quality) [[Puhan2016]].
- In 197,376 US Medicare beneficiaries hospitalised for COPD in 2014, only 2721 (1.5%) started pulmonary rehabilitation within 90 days of discharge [[Lindenauer2020]].
- One-year mortality was 7.3% in those who started within 90 days versus 19.6% in those who started later or never (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.57–0.69). Every 3 additional sessions was associated with lower risk (HR 0.91). The authors caution that residual confounding is possible (observational) [[Lindenauer2020]].
- An early rehabilitation programme started within 48 hours of admission (389 patients, 82% with COPD) did not reduce 12-month readmissions: 62% versus 58% (HR 1.1, 95% CI 0.86–1.43) [[Greening2014]].
- In that trial, 1-year mortality was higher in the intervention group (OR 1.74, 95% CI 1.05–2.88). The authors suggest that progressive exercise beyond standard physiotherapy "should not be started during the early stages of the acute illness" [[Greening2014]].

### rehab · Home-based and telerehabilitation
- In a 166-person equivalence trial, home-based rehabilitation (one home visit plus seven weekly physiotherapist phone calls) was non-inferior to centre-based rehabilitation for 6MWD at the end of the programme (difference +18.6 m favouring home) [[Holland2017]].
- "Neither group maintained postrehabilitation gains at 12 months" [[Holland2017]].
- The 2021 Cochrane review of telerehabilitation (15 studies, 1904 participants, 99% COPD) found probably little or no difference from centre-based rehabilitation in 6MWD (MD 0.06 m, 95% CI −10.82 to 10.94; moderate certainty) [[Cox2021Cochrane]].
- In that review, 93% of people completed telerehabilitation, compared with 70% for in-person rehabilitation [[Cox2021Cochrane]].
- In a 142-person randomised trial, telerehabilitation was equivalent to centre-based rehabilitation for 6MWD (MD −6 m). Equivalence for CRQ dyspnoea could not be confirmed (MD −1 point, 95% CI −3 to 1). The authors still describe telerehabilitation as safe with clinically meaningful benefits [[Cox2022]].

### oxygen · The published long-term oxygen thresholds
- The ATS 2020 guideline recommends long-term oxygen for at least 15 h/d in adults with COPD and severe chronic resting room-air hypoxaemia (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence) [[Jacobs2020]].
- **ATS 2020 defines "severe hypoxemia" by either of two criteria.** These are the NOTT/MRC-based eligibility thresholds [[Jacobs2020]]:
  - PaO2 ≤ 55 mm Hg (7.3 kPa) or SpO2 ≤ 88%; or
  - PaO2 56–59 mm Hg (7.5–7.9 kPa) or SpO2 89%, plus one of: oedema, haematocrit ≥ 55%, or P pulmonale on an ECG.
- ATS 2020 defines moderate hypoxaemia as SpO2 89–93% and suggests not prescribing long-term oxygen for it (conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence). The corresponding PaO2 was not reported in LOTT, the trial behind this definition [[Jacobs2020,Albert2016]].
- ATS 2020 suggests ambulatory oxygen for adults with COPD and severe exertional room-air hypoxaemia (conditional, moderate-quality evidence). Its systematic review defined that as SpO2 ≤ 88% on exertion [[Jacobs2020]].
- ATS 2020 suggests portable liquid oxygen for people who are mobile outside the home and need continuous flow above 3 L/min during exertion (conditional, very-low-quality evidence) [[Jacobs2020]].
- ATS 2020 makes a best-practice statement that patients and caregivers be trained in oxygen equipment and safety, "including smoking cessation, fire prevention, and tripping hazards" [[Jacobs2020]].
- ATS 2020 reports the entry criteria of the two founding trials:
  - NOTT: PaO2 ≤ 55 mm Hg, or ≤ 59 mm Hg plus oedema, haematocrit ≥ 55% or P pulmonale.
  - MRC: PaO2 40–60 mm Hg (5.3–8.0 kPa) with at least one prior episode of ankle oedema [[Jacobs2020]].
- The Cochrane review of domiciliary oxygen concluded that long-term home oxygen "improved survival in a selected group of COPD patients with severe hypoxaemia (arterial PaO2 less than 55 mm Hg (8.0 kPa))" [[Cranston2005]].
  - The review gives 55 mm Hg as 8.0 kPa. 55 mm Hg is about 7.3 kPa, and ATS 2020 pairs 55 mm Hg with 7.3 kPa [[Jacobs2020]].
- Cochrane found no survival benefit from home oxygen in mild to moderate hypoxaemia, or in people whose only desaturation was at night [[Cranston2005]].
- The BTS 2015 home oxygen guideline covers assessment, follow-up and risk assessment, "particularly in the clinically challenging area of home oxygen users who smoke". Its numeric PaO2 thresholds were not read here (full text not accessed) [[Hardinge2015]].
- GOLD 2023 states that the criteria for prescribing long-term oxygen and ventilatory support "remain unchanged and are described in detail in the GOLD 2023 report" [[Agusti2023]].

### oxygen · NOTT and MRC: the survival trials
- NOTT randomised 203 people with hypoxaemic COPD at six centres. The arms were continuous oxygen or 12-hour nocturnal oxygen, with follow-up for at least 12 months (mean 19.3 months) [[NOTT1980]].
- Overall mortality in the NOTT nocturnal group was 1.94 times that of the continuous group (P = 0.01). The effect was "striking" in patients with carbon dioxide retention [[NOTT1980]].
- NOTT mortality was 11.9% (continuous) versus 20.6% (nocturnal) at 12 months, and 22.4% versus 40.8% at 24 months (as tabulated in a systematic review) [[NOTT1980,Arif2022]].
- That review reports mean use of 17.7 ± 4.8 h/day in the NOTT continuous arm versus 12 ± 2.5 h/day in the nocturnal arm. Continuous oxygen reduced pulmonary vascular resistance at 6 months [[Arif2022]].
- ATS 2020 summarises NOTT as a 2-year mortality risk reduction of 55% (RR 0.45, 95% CI 0.25–0.81) [[Jacobs2020]].
- NOTT subgroups with larger benefit included those below:
  - PaCO2 ≥ 43 mm Hg: 21.7% vs 41.5% mortality.
  - pH < 7.40: 16.0% vs 42.2% [[Jacobs2020]].
- The MRC trial enrolled 87 people under 70 with chronic bronchitis or emphysema, severe hypoxaemia, CO2 retention and a history of heart failure. They were randomised to oxygen by nasal prongs for at least 15 h daily (usually 2 L/min) or no oxygen [[MRC1981]].
- In MRC, 19 of 42 oxygen-treated patients (45%) died over 5 years, versus 30 of 45 controls (67%) [[MRC1981]].
- In the 66 men in MRC, "the survival advantage of oxygen did not emerge until 500 days had elapsed". Of the 12 female controls, 8 were dead at 3 years [[MRC1981]].
- ATS 2020 summarises MRC as a 5-year mortality risk reduction of 59% (RR 0.41, 95% CI 0.17–0.98). It did not pool the two trials because their thresholds, comparators, durations and time points differ [[Jacobs2020]].
- The Cochrane review reports Peto ORs for mortality of 0.45 (95% CI 0.25–0.81) for NOTT at 24 months and 0.42 (95% CI 0.18–0.98) for MRC over 5 years [[Cranston2005]].

### oxygen · Where oxygen did not help: LOTT, INOX, REDOX, palliative oxygen
- LOTT followed 738 people with stable COPD and moderate desaturation at 42 centres for 1–6 years. Moderate desaturation meant resting SpO2 89–93%, or exercise-induced desaturation (SpO2 ≥ 80% for ≥ 5 min and < 90% for ≥ 10 s on the 6-minute walk) [[Albert2016]].
- LOTT found no difference in time to death or first hospitalisation (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.79–1.12). It found no consistent differences in quality of life, lung function or 6-minute walk distance [[Albert2016]].
- ATS 2020 reports LOTT's safety event rates per 100 person-years: 0.08 fires, 0.12 burns from smoking near oxygen, 0.04 burns from open flame, 0.16 burns from liquid-oxygen frost, 0.35 nosebleeds and 0.90 trips or falls over equipment [[Jacobs2020]].
- INOX enrolled people with COPD who spent ≥ 30% of the night below 90% saturation but did not qualify for long-term oxygen. It stopped early after 243 of a planned 600 patients [[Lacasse2020]].
- At 3 years in INOX, 39.0% (nocturnal oxygen) versus 42.0% (sham concentrator) had died or met the NOTT criteria for long-term oxygen (difference −3.0 points, 95% CI −15.1 to 9.1) [[Lacasse2020]].
- REDOX randomised 241 people starting oxygen for chronic severe resting hypoxaemia to 24 or 15 h/day. Median actual use was 24.0 versus 15.0 h/day at 12 months [[Ekstrom2024]].
- In REDOX, 24-hour use did not lower the risk of hospitalisation or death within 1 year: 124.7 versus 124.5 events per 100 person-years (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.72–1.36) [[Ekstrom2024]].
- REDOX notes that the 24 h/day recommendation rested on a nonrandomised comparison (the NOTT-versus-MRC contrast) [[Ekstrom2024]].
- In 239 people with life-limiting illness, refractory breathlessness and PaO2 > 7.3 kPa, oxygen at 2 L/min by nasal cannula gave no more relief than room air through the same concentrator. Morning breathlessness changed by −0.9 versus −0.7 points on a 0–10 scale (P = 0.504) [[Abernethy2010]].

### oxygen · Home non-invasive ventilation (NIV)
- The ATS 2020 guideline uses this definition of chronic hypercapnic COPD: FEV1/FVC < 0.70, resting PaCO2 > 45 mm Hg, not during an exacerbation [[Macrea2020]].
- For that group, ATS 2020 suggests nocturnal NIV in addition to usual care (conditional, moderate certainty) and NIV with "targeted normalization of PaCO2" (conditional, low certainty) [[Macrea2020]].
- ATS 2020 suggests screening for obstructive sleep apnoea before starting long-term NIV [[Macrea2020]].
- It also suggests not starting long-term NIV during an admission for acute-on-chronic hypercapnic failure, and reassessing 2–4 weeks after it resolves [[Macrea2020]].
- The ERS 2019 guideline conditionally suggests long-term home NIV in stable hypercapnic COPD and after an exacerbation that required acute NIV. It also suggests settings that target lower CO2, with fixed pressure support as first-choice mode [[Ergan2019]].
- Köhnlein's trial enrolled 195 people with stable GOLD stage IV COPD, PaCO2 ≥ 7 kPa (51.9 mm Hg) and pH > 7.35 at 36 German and Austrian units. NIV was set to reduce PaCO2 by ≥ 20% or to below 6.5 kPa [[Kohnlein2014]].
- One-year mortality in that trial was 12% (12/102) with NIV versus 33% (31/93) with standard care (HR 0.24, 95% CI 0.11–0.49). Facial skin rash occurred in 14% [[Kohnlein2014]].
- HOT-HMV randomised 116 people with persistent hypercapnia (PaCO2 > 53 mm Hg) 2–4 weeks after an acidotic exacerbation to home oxygen plus home NIV or home oxygen alone. The median ventilator settings were IPAP 24 cm H2O, EPAP 4 cm H2O and backup rate 14/min [[Murphy2017]].
- In HOT-HMV, median time to readmission or death was 4.3 months with NIV plus oxygen versus 1.4 months with oxygen alone (adjusted HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.31–0.77) [[Murphy2017]].
- The 12-month risk of readmission or death was 63.4% versus 80.4% (absolute risk reduction 17.0%). By 12 months, 16 patients in the NIV group and 19 in the oxygen-alone group had died [[Murphy2017]].

### advanced · Lung volume reduction surgery (NETT)
- NETT randomised 1218 people with severe emphysema, all of whom first had pulmonary rehabilitation, to lung volume reduction surgery or continued medical treatment [[Fishman2003]].
- Overall mortality was 0.11 deaths per person-year in both groups (risk ratio 1.01). At 24 months, exercise capacity had improved by more than 10 W in 15% of the surgery group versus 3% of the medical group [[Fishman2003]].
- In people with upper-lobe-predominant emphysema and low baseline exercise capacity, surgery lowered mortality (risk ratio 0.47, P = 0.005) [[Fishman2003]].
- In people with non-upper-lobe emphysema and high exercise capacity, surgery raised mortality (risk ratio 2.06). A high-risk subgroup of 140 patients was identified at an interim analysis [[Fishman2003]].
- At a median 4.3 years of follow-up, the intention-to-treat analysis showed an overall survival advantage for surgery (5-year risk ratio for death 0.86, P = 0.02) [[Naunheim2006]].
- Upper-lobe, low-exercise patients had a 5-year risk ratio of 0.67 after surgery. The authors concluded that "Effects of LVRS are durable" [[Naunheim2006]].
- GOLD 2023 states that in selected patients with heterogeneous or homogeneous emphysema and significant hyperinflation refractory to optimised care, surgical or bronchoscopic lung volume reduction "may be considered". It also says bullectomy is an option for a large bulla [[Agusti2023]].
- GOLD 2023 defines homogeneous emphysema as a < 10% difference in destruction between the target lobe and the ipsilateral non-target lobe on quantitative CT. A difference of more than 10% is heterogeneous [[Agusti2023]].

### advanced · Endobronchial valves
- One-way endobronchial valves work by collapsing a hyperinflated lobe. Collateral ventilation from neighbouring lobes "prevents lobar atelectasis", which explains the modest results of earlier valve trials [[Davey2015]].
- STELVIO enrolled 84 people and excluded 16 (13 for collateral ventilation). The remaining 68 were randomised to valves or standard care [[Klooster2015]].
  - At 6 months, valves improved FEV1 by 140 mL, FVC by 347 mL and 6MWD by 74 m more than control.
  - Pneumothorax occurred in 18% of the valve group [[Klooster2015]].
- BeLieVeR-HIFi was a 50-patient sham-controlled trial in heterogeneous emphysema with intact fissures. Median FEV1 rose by 8.77% with valves versus 2.88% with a sham bronchoscopy (P = 0.0326) [[Davey2015]].
- TRANSFORM (97 patients, heterogeneous emphysema without collateral ventilation): at 3 months, 55.4% with valves versus 6.5% with standard care had an FEV1 improvement of ≥ 12% [[Kemp2017]].
- At 6 months in TRANSFORM, the valve-minus-control differences were residual volume −700 mL, 6MWD +78.7 m and SGRQ −6.5. Pneumothorax occurred in 29.2% [[Kemp2017]].
- LIBERATE (190 patients, 2:1 Zephyr valve versus standard care): at 12 months, 47.7% versus 16.8% had an FEV1 improvement of ≥ 15% [[Criner2018]].
- The 12-month valve-minus-control differences in LIBERATE were FEV1 +0.106 L, 6MWD +39.31 m, SGRQ −7.05, residual volume −522 mL and mMRC −0.8 [[Criner2018]].
- Pneumothorax was the most common serious adverse event in LIBERATE, in 26.6% of valve patients between the procedure and day 45. Four deaths occurred in the valve group during that phase [[Criner2018]].
- IMPACT (93 patients with homogeneous emphysema and no collateral ventilation on Chartis): FEV1 changed by +13.7% with valves versus −3.2% with standard care at 3 months (difference 17.0%). Pneumothorax occurred in 25.6% [[Valipour2016]].
- EMPROVE (172 patients, Spiration valve versus medical management): FEV1 differed by 0.101 L at 6 months and 0.099 L at 12 months. Serious thoracic adverse events were 31.0% versus 11.9%, mainly a 12.4% rate of serious pneumothorax [[Criner2019]].
- The FDA approved the Zephyr Endobronchial Valve System (PMA P180002) on 29 June 2018 for "adult patients with hyperinflation associated with severe emphysema in regions of the lung that have little to no collateral ventilation" [[FDA-Zephyr]].
- The FDA approved the Spiration Valve System (PMA P180007) on 3 December 2018 for adults with breathlessness and hyperinflation from severe emphysema "in regions of the lung that have low collateral ventilation" [[FDA-Spiration]].

### advanced · Lung transplantation
- The 2021 ISHLT consensus calls the BODE index "the most familiar" prognostic model for COPD transplant timing, but notes it overestimated mortality in transplant candidates [[Leard2021]].
  - In OPTN/UNOS candidates, median survival in BODE quartile 4 (7–10) was 59 months, versus 37 months in the original BODE cohort.
- ISHLT 2021 states that a BODE score of 7–10 "still appears to identify a group of transplant-eligible patients whose predicted median survival without transplant is less than the observed median survival of patients with COPD post-transplant (6.0 years)". It "therefore stands as the best guideline for listing patients" [[Leard2021]].
- ISHLT 2021 names FEV1 < 20% predicted as an additional listing consideration. It names pulmonary hypertension, chronic hypercapnia and severe exacerbations as other mortality factors that may influence listing [[Leard2021]].
- ISHLT 2021 suggests referral for consultation in patients with BODE 5–6 and FEV1 20–25% predicted. It also suggests referral for a BODE increase of more than 1 over 24 months or a pulmonary artery-to-aorta diameter ratio > 1 on CT [[Leard2021]].
- In 9883 people with COPD in the ISHLT registry (1987–2006), median survival after lung transplantation was 5.0 years [[Thabut2008]].
- Bilateral transplant had longer median survival than single (6.41 vs 4.59 years), but little benefit for people aged 60 or older (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.81–1.13) [[Thabut2008]].

### advanced · Alpha-1 antitrypsin augmentation as therapy
- RAPID randomised 180 non-smokers aged 18–65 with severe AAT deficiency (serum < 11 μM) and FEV1 35–70% predicted to intravenous A1PI 60 mg/kg per week or placebo for 24 months [[Chapman2015]].
- The combined TLC+FRC CT lung-density endpoint missed significance: −1.50 versus −2.12 g/L per year (P = 0.06) [[Chapman2015]].
- At TLC alone, density loss was slower with A1PI: −1.45 versus −2.19 g/L per year (difference 0.74, 95% CI 0.06–1.42, P = 0.03) [[Chapman2015]].
- In the RAPID open-label extension (140 patients), people who switched from placebo slowed their density loss from −2.26 to −1.26 g/L per year. Early-start patients stayed near −1.5 to −1.6 g/L per year through month 48 [[McElvaney2017]].
- The extension authors note that "Lost lung density was never recovered" (Established for RAPID-OLE; the clinical-outcome benefit is Early evidence) [[McElvaney2017]].
- EXACTLE (77 PiZ subjects, Prolastin 60 mg/kg weekly versus placebo for 2–2.5 years) found a trend toward slower CT density loss (P = 0.049–0.084 across methods) and no change in exacerbation frequency [[Dirksen2009]].

### advanced · Palliative care and breathlessness
- GOLD 2023 states: "All patients with advanced COPD should be considered for end-of-life and palliative care support to optimize symptom control and allow patients and their families to make informed choices about future management" [[Agusti2023]].
- In a randomised trial of 105 people with advanced disease and refractory breathlessness, a breathlessness support service combining palliative care, respiratory medicine, physiotherapy and occupational therapy improved breathlessness mastery (MD 0.58, 95% CI 0.01–1.15) [[Higginson2014]].
- Six-month survival in that trial was 94% (50/53) with the service versus 75% (39/52) with usual care. The survival difference was significant for COPD and interstitial lung disease, and the authors say it "requires further investigation" (Early evidence) [[Higginson2014]].
- A handheld fan directed at the face for 5 minutes reduced breathlessness compared with directing it at the legs (P = 0.003; 50 participants, crossover) [[Galbraith2010]].
- In a crossover trial of 48 opioid-naive people (88% with COPD), 20 mg sustained-release oral morphine for 4 days improved breathlessness by 6.6 mm (morning) and 9.5 mm (evening) on a 100 mm scale compared with placebo. It also improved sleep [[Abernethy2003]].
  - Distressing constipation was more common with morphine (9 vs 1) [[Abernethy2003]].
- The 2016 Cochrane review of opioids for refractory breathlessness (26 studies, 526 participants) found "some low quality evidence" of benefit from oral or parenteral opioids and no evidence supporting nebulised opioids [[Barnes2016]].
  - Nausea or vomiting (4.73-fold), constipation (3-fold) and drowsiness (2.86-fold) were more likely with opioids [[Barnes2016]].
- BEAMS randomised 160 people with COPD and mMRC 3–4 breathlessness at 20 Australian centres to extended-release morphine 8 or 16 mg/day or placebo [[Ekstrom2022]].
  - Worst breathlessness after week 1 did not differ (MD −0.3 for both doses). The authors concluded the findings "do not support the use of these doses" [[Ekstrom2022]].
- MORDYC randomised 124 people with COPD and mMRC 2–4 breathlessness after pulmonary rehabilitation to 10 mg sustained-release morphine twice daily or placebo for 4 weeks [[Verberkt2020]].
  - The CAT score was 2.18 points better with morphine, and PaCO2 did not change significantly (+1.19 mm Hg, P = 0.55).
  - Worst breathlessness improved only in the mMRC 3–4 subgroup (−1.33 points) [[Verberkt2020]].

### Exam hooks
- Only two interventions in the NOTT/MRC era were shown to improve survival in COPD: stopping smoking (Lung Health Study, HR 1.18 for usual care) and long-term oxygen in severe resting hypoxaemia [[Anthonisen2005,NOTT1980,MRC1981]].
- After quitting, FEV1 decline roughly halves, from 62 to 31 mL/yr in Lung Health Study sustained quitters. Lost function is not regained [[Scanlon2000,Fletcher1977]].
- Long-term oxygen thresholds (ATS 2020, from NOTT/MRC): PaO2 ≤ 55 mm Hg (7.3 kPa) or SpO2 ≤ 88%; or PaO2 56–59 mm Hg or SpO2 89% with oedema, haematocrit ≥ 55% or P pulmonale [[Jacobs2020]].
- NOTT: continuous beat 12-hour nocturnal oxygen, with mortality 1.94 times higher on nocturnal. MRC: ≥ 15 h/day beat no oxygen, 45% vs 67% dead at 5 years [[NOTT1980,MRC1981]].
- LOTT: no benefit of oxygen for moderate desaturation (SpO2 89–93% or exercise-only). REDOX: 24 h/day was no better than 15 h/day [[Albert2016,Ekstrom2024]].
- HOT-HMV: home NIV plus oxygen after a hypercapnic admission halved the hazard of readmission or death (HR 0.49) [[Murphy2017]].
- Köhnlein: high-intensity NIV targeting PaCO2 reduction cut 1-year mortality from 33% to 12% [[Kohnlein2014]].
- NETT: lung volume reduction surgery helps survival only in upper-lobe emphysema with low exercise capacity (RR 0.47) [[Fishman2003]].
- Endobronchial valves need absent collateral ventilation (intact fissures). Pneumothorax is the main early risk (about 18–30%) [[Klooster2015,Kemp2017,Criner2018]].
- Varenicline is the most effective single cessation drug (beats bupropion and single NRT). EAGLES found no significant excess of neuropsychiatric events [[LivingstoneBanks2023,Anthenelli2016]].
- Pulmonary rehabilitation in stable COPD raises 6MWD by about 44 m and improves SGRQ by about 7 points [[McCarthy2015]].
- Rehabilitation started within 48 hours of admission did not help and was associated with higher 1-year mortality. Rehabilitation within 90 days after discharge was associated with lower mortality [[Greening2014,Lindenauer2020]].
- Pneumococcal vaccination in COPD: NNTB 21 to prevent one community-acquired pneumonia and 8 to prevent one exacerbation [[Walters2017]].
- Palliative oxygen is no better than room air for breathlessness in people who are not hypoxaemic [[Abernethy2010]].

### In plain words
- Stopping smoking is the single most powerful thing studied for COPD. It slows the loss of lung function to about the speed of someone who never smoked.
- Lungs do not grow back after quitting, but they stop getting worse so fast. Quitting at any age or stage still helped in the studies.
- Medicines like varenicline, nicotine patches and gum, plus regular support, about double the chance of quitting.
- Vaccines against flu, pneumonia germs, RSV and COVID-19 can help prevent chest infections and flare-ups.
- Pulmonary rehabilitation is a class of exercise and education. It helps people walk further and feel less out of breath.
- Starting rehab in the weeks after a hospital stay is linked to living longer. Very hard exercise during the first days of a hospital stay did not help.
- Home oxygen helps people live longer only when blood oxygen is very low at rest. For milder low oxygen it did not help.
- Oxygen is a fire risk. In the trials, burns came from smoking or open flames near the oxygen.
- Some people whose carbon dioxide stays high use a breathing machine with a mask at night. In trials this kept them out of hospital longer.
- For some people with emphysema, surgery or small one-way valves can shrink an over-inflated part of the lung so the rest works better.
- A small fan blowing on the face can ease breathlessness. Palliative care teams can help with breathlessness and with planning ahead.

### quitting · Step sequence
1. Kicker "The damage" — Title: Smoking speeds lung loss. Continuing smokers with airflow obstruction lost about 62 mL of FEV1 a year [[Scanlon2000]].
2. Kicker "The quit" — Title: A small rebound. In the year after quitting, FEV1 rose by an average of 47 mL [[Scanlon2000]].
3. Kicker "The slope changes" — Title: Decline halves. Sustained quitters then lost about 31 mL a year, but lost function was not regained [[Scanlon2000,Fletcher1977]].
4. Kicker "Getting help" — Title: Medicines plus support. Medication with high-intensity behavioural support more than doubled quit rates in smokers with COPD (RR 2.53) [[vanEerd2016]].
5. Kicker "Long term" — Title: Fewer deaths. After 14.5 years, the Lung Health Study's cessation programme lowered all-cause mortality (8.83 vs 10.38 per 1000 person-years) [[Anthonisen2005]].
6. Kicker "Protect" — Title: Vaccines. GOLD 2023 lists influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19, pertussis and zoster vaccines, and ACIP 2024 added RSV vaccination for adults aged ≥ 75 or 60–74 at increased risk [[Agusti2023,Britton2024]].

### rehab · Step sequence
1. Kicker "Assess" — Title: Start with a full assessment. Rehabilitation is defined as a comprehensive intervention built on patient assessment and tailored therapy [[Spruit2013,Rochester2023]].
2. Kicker "Train" — Title: Exercise for 8–12 weeks. Most Cochrane-reviewed programmes ran 8 or 12 weeks of exercise with or without education [[McCarthy2015]].
3. Kicker "Gain" — Title: Walk further, breathe easier. 6MWD rose by about 44 m and SGRQ improved by about 7 points versus usual care [[McCarthy2015]].
4. Kicker "After hospital" — Title: The window that matters. Starting within 90 days of discharge was associated with 1-year mortality of 7.3% versus 19.6% (observational) [[Lindenauer2020]].
5. Kicker "Not too soon" — Title: Avoid the acute phase. Progressive rehabilitation begun within 48 hours of admission did not reduce readmissions [[Greening2014]].
6. Kicker "Keep going" — Title: Home, telehealth and maintenance. Telerehabilitation gave similar 6MWD gains with 93% completion, but gains faded by 12 months without maintenance [[Cox2021Cochrane,Holland2017]].

### oxygen · Step sequence
1. Kicker "Measure" — Title: Is resting oxygen low enough? Severe hypoxaemia per ATS 2020 is PaO2 ≤ 55 mm Hg (7.3 kPa) or SpO2 ≤ 88%, or PaO2 56–59 mm Hg with oedema, haematocrit ≥ 55% or P pulmonale [[Jacobs2020]].
2. Kicker "MRC 1981" — Title: Oxygen vs none. With ≥ 15 h/day of oxygen, 45% died over 5 years versus 67% without, and the curves separated after about 500 days in men [[MRC1981]].
3. Kicker "NOTT 1980" — Title: More hours, fewer deaths. Mortality at 24 months was 22.4% on continuous oxygen versus 40.8% on 12-hour nocturnal oxygen [[NOTT1980,Arif2022]].
4. Kicker "LOTT 2016" — Title: Milder lows don't benefit. In moderate desaturation (SpO2 89–93% or exercise-only), oxygen did not delay death or hospitalisation (HR 0.94) [[Albert2016]].
5. Kicker "REDOX 2024" — Title: 15 hours vs 24. Using oxygen 24 h/day did not lower hospitalisation or death compared with 15 h/day [[Ekstrom2024]].
6. Kicker "High CO2" — Title: Add a ventilator at night. In persistent hypercapnia after an admission, home NIV plus oxygen cut the 12-month risk of readmission or death from 80.4% to 63.4% [[Murphy2017]].

### advanced · Step sequence
1. Kicker "Hyperinflation" — Title: The over-full lung. Valve and surgery candidates have severe emphysema with hyperinflation that persists despite optimal care [[Agusti2023]].
2. Kicker "Surgery" — Title: Remove the worst tissue. NETT found a survival benefit only for upper-lobe emphysema with low exercise capacity (RR 0.47) [[Fishman2003]].
3. Kicker "Valves" — Title: Collapse a lobe from inside. Without collateral ventilation, valves improved FEV1 by 140 mL and 6MWD by 74 m, and pneumothorax occurred in 18% [[Klooster2015]].
4. Kicker "Transplant" — Title: New lungs. ISHLT 2021 uses BODE 7–10 or FEV1 < 20% predicted to guide listing, and median survival after transplant for COPD was 5.0 years in 1987–2006 [[Leard2021,Thabut2008]].
5. Kicker "Alpha-1" — Title: Replace the missing protein. Weekly A1PI slowed CT density loss at TLC (−1.45 vs −2.19 g/L/yr) [[Chapman2015]].
6. Kicker "Comfort" — Title: Palliative care alongside. An integrated breathlessness service improved mastery, and a fan to the face eased breathlessness [[Higginson2014,Galbraith2010]].

## Open questions and controversies
- **24 versus 15 hours of oxygen.**
  - Position A: NOTT's continuous arm beat nocturnal oxygen, and textbooks recommend 24 h/day [[NOTT1980]].
  - Position B: the NOTT continuous arm averaged about 17.7 h/day, and REDOX found 24 h no better than 15 h at 1 year [[Arif2022,Ekstrom2024]].
  - Status: settled for 1-year outcomes. Longer-term survival is not powered in REDOX.
- **Oxygen for isolated nocturnal desaturation.** INOX was underpowered (243 of 600) and showed no effect either way [[Lacasse2020]]. Cochrane also found no survival effect [[Cranston2005]]. Status: unresolved.
- **Does post-exacerbation rehabilitation lower mortality?**
  - The Medicare cohort shows a strong association (HR 0.63), but the authors flag residual confounding [[Lindenauer2020]].
  - The Cochrane pooled mortality OR was 0.68 and not significant, with heterogeneity [[Puhan2016]].
  - Early in-hospital rehabilitation was associated with higher mortality [[Greening2014]].
  - Status: timing and content matter. No definitive RCT exists.
- **Opioids for chronic breathlessness.**
  - Older small trials and MORDYC suggest benefit, mainly in mMRC 3–4 [[Abernethy2003,Verberkt2020]].
  - BEAMS found no benefit at 8–16 mg/day [[Ekstrom2022]].
  - Cochrane rates the evidence low quality [[Barnes2016]].
  - Status: contested.
- **Augmentation therapy's clinical benefit.** RAPID's combined CT endpoint missed significance (P = 0.06) while TLC alone was significant [[Chapman2015]]. No trial has shown a mortality or exacerbation benefit [[Dirksen2009]]. Status: Early evidence for clinical outcomes.
- **E-cigarettes in COPD.** Quit-rate evidence versus NRT is high certainty [[Lindson2024]]. But follow-up is at most 2 years, and 80% of e-cigarette quitters still vaped at 1 year [[Hajek2019]]. Long-term respiratory safety in COPD remains (Hypothesis/Early evidence).
- **The Higginson survival signal.** The 94% versus 75% 6-month survival with a breathlessness service was a safety endpoint in a 105-person trial. The authors say it "requires further investigation" [[Higginson2014]].

  - Records found through PubMed `esearch` were confirmed with `esummary` (authors, title, journal, year, volume/issue/pages, PMCID, DOI). Every abstract was then read from its Europe PMC MEDLINE record (`EXT_ID:<PMID> AND SRC:MED`, resultType=core).
  - NOTT1980, MRC1981, Kohnlein2014, Fishman2003, Klooster2015, Anthonisen1994, Anthenelli2016, LivingstoneBanks2023 and Arif2022 were located and confirmed through Europe PMC only.
  - NCBI E-utilities hit "API rate limit exceeded" intermittently, and calls were retried with pauses.
- **Full texts read (Europe PMC / PMC open access):**
  - GOLD 2023 Executive Summary (PMC10111975): smoking, vaccines, rehabilitation, LVR, palliative care, COVID-19.
  - ATS home oxygen 2020 (PMC7667898): thresholds, NOTT/MRC summaries, NOTT subgroups, LOTT safety rates.
  - ATS NIV 2020 (PMC7427384): hypercapnia definition.
  - ATS PR 2023 (PMC10449064): PR definition, < 5% uptake.
  - ISHLT 2021 (PMC8979471, via NCBI PMC efetch): COPD referral and listing.
  - LOTT (PMC5216457, via NCBI PMC efetch): intervention detail.
  - Arif 2022 (PMC8859503): NOTT 12/24-month mortality and hours of use.
- **Labels and devices.**
  - CHANTIX: openFDA drug/label, Pfizer label effective 2026-01-23 (no boxed warning). An older repackager label in openFDA (effective 2020-02-01) still prints the former boxed warning; it was not used.
  - Zephyr (P180002) and Spiration (P180007): openFDA device/pma.
  - RSV vaccines and alpha-1 proteinase inhibitors (Zemaira, Prolastin-C) are biologics not returned by openFDA drug/label, so their use is described from the trials and ACIP.
- **Not accessed:**
  - The full GOLD 2023 report (goldcopd.org, not indexed). Its detailed LTOT criteria were therefore taken from ATS 2020, which quotes NOTT/MRC.
  - The BTS 2015 home oxygen guideline full text (Thorax supplement, paywalled). Its numeric thresholds are not quoted.
  - The NOTT and MRC full texts (paywalled). Their survival-curve anchor points come from the MRC abstract and from the NOTT table in Arif2022, so NOTT's 12/24-month values are secondary-sourced.
  - The ISHLT registry report 2019 (no abstract; not cited).
  - The BTS 2013 PR guideline (no abstract; not cited).
- **Not checked:** ACIP recommendations issued after the 2024 MMWR (for example any extension of RSV vaccination to younger at-risk adults). Builders should not state them from this file.
- **Discrepancies noted.**
  - Cochrane [[Cranston2005]] writes "PaO2 less than 55 mm Hg (8.0 kPa)". ATS [[Jacobs2020]] gives 55 mm Hg as 7.3 kPa, which is the correct conversion.
  - NOTT continuous-arm use is given as 17.7 h/day in Arif2022. Another secondary review (Gauthier 2019, not cited) says about 19 h/day.
- No fact is marked (unverified). The NOTT 12/24-month figures are cited to the secondary source as well as the trial, as stated above.
