Part 4 · Flare-ups & hospital

Flare-ups (exacerbations)

A flare-up is a spell of days when breathlessness, cough and sputum get clearly worse. Follow one through a small airway, from the cold or chest infection that sets it off to a recovery that is not always complete, then try the Rome severity grades and see what the studies found after a flare-up that needed hospital care.

Recovery

Schematic, not to scale. The curves and meters show a direction of change, not measurements. The marked days come from the studies: treatment start [[Wilkinson2004]], median recovery and day-35 and day-91 recovery [[Seemungal2000]], and the ≤14-day window of the Rome definition [[Agusti2023,Celli2022]].

Get helpA flare-up can become an emergency. GOLD 2023 lists reasons for hospital care that include sudden worsening of breathlessness at rest, a high breathing rate, low oxygen saturation, confusion or drowsiness, and new blue lips (cyanosis) or swollen ankles. [[Agusti2023]] Anyone with these signs needs urgent medical help: in an emergency, call your local emergency number. People who have had high carbon dioxide levels before may carry an oxygen alert card for the ambulance crew. [[ODriscoll2017Summary]]

What counts as a flare-up

GOLD 2023 adopted the Rome proposal, which defines an exacerbation of COPD as "an event characterized by dyspnea and/or cough and sputum that worsen over ≤14 days, which may be accompanied by tachypnea and/or tachycardia and is often associated with increased local and systemic inflammation caused by airway infection, pollution, or other insult to the airways". [[Agusti2023,Celli2022]]

The older definition, an "acute worsening of respiratory symptoms that results in additional therapy", graded severity afterwards by how much healthcare was used. GOLD 2023 calls that definition "highly non-specific" and the after-the-fact grading "useless to guide treatment at the point of care". Rome grades severity at the bedside instead. [[Agusti2023]]

Rome gradeAs published
MildBreathlessness VAS <5, breathing rate <24 breaths/min, heart rate <95 bpm, resting SpO2 ≥92% on air (or the usual oxygen prescription) with a change of ≤3% when known, and CRP <10 mg/L if obtained. [[Jacobson2023,Celli2022]]
ModerateAt least three of five: VAS ≥5, breathing rate ≥24, heart rate ≥95, resting SpO2 <92% and/or a change of >3%, CRP ≥10 mg/L. The blood gas may show low oxygen (PaO2 ≤8 kPa) and/or high carbon dioxide (PaCO2 >6 kPa), but no acidosis. [[Jacobson2023,Celli2022]]
SevereDefined by the arterial blood gas: high carbon dioxide (PaCO2 >6 kPa; >45 mmHg) with acidosis (pH <7.35). [[Jacobson2023,Lee2023,Celli2022]] GOLD 2023 notes the gas must show "new-onset/worsening hypercapnia or acidosis", because a few people have long-standing high carbon dioxide. [[Agusti2023]]

Try it: the Rome severity grades

Teaching toolThis shows how the Rome proposal's published thresholds combine, so you can see how the grades work. It is not for diagnosis or treatment decisions, and it cannot tell anyone how ill they are: GOLD 2023 says Rome still needs prospective validation. [[Agusti2023]] Anyone who is much more breathless than usual needs their care team, or emergency help, whatever any score says.

Examples

In a Korean validation of 740 emergency visits or admissions, Rome classed 30.9% as mild, 58.6% as moderate and 10.4% as severe, and predicted ICU admission (AUROC 0.850) and need for ventilation (AUROC 0.870) better than GesEPOC 2021. [[Lee2023]] In a Swedish telehealth cohort of 125 flare-ups, Rome graded severity lower than GOLD 2022 did, and heart failure did not distort its measures. [[Jacobson2023]]

Look-alikes to rule out

GOLD 2023

Mimic or aggravate

Decompensated heart failure, pneumonia and pulmonary embolism may "mimic or aggravate" a flare-up. Worsening breathlessness without the classic features should prompt a search for them. [[Agusti2023]]

Pulmonary embolism

16.1% of unexplained flare-ups

Across 7 CT-angiography studies (880 patients), a clot in the lungs was found in a pooled 16.1% (95% CI 8.3–25.8%) of unexplained flare-ups; 68% of the clots were in the main, lobar or interlobar arteries. [[Aleva2017]]

Clues

Pain and heart failure

In that meta-analysis, chest pain on breathing and heart failure were more frequent in flare-ups with a clot, and signs of a chest infection were less frequent. [[Aleva2017]] More on similar conditions:

One flare-up predicts the next

ECLIPSE, 2138 people followed for 3 years: first-year flare-ups by GOLD stage Established

"The single best predictor of exacerbations, across all GOLD stages, was a history of exacerbations." The pattern was stable over 3 years and also linked to reflux or heartburn, worse quality of life and a raised white-cell count. [[Hurst2010]]

99,574 UK patients

A graded risk

Around one quarter never had a flare-up in up to 10 years. Year-1 flare-ups predicted the long-term rate (HR 1.71 for one, up to 3.41 for five or more). Frequent moderate flare-ups raised the risk of death (HR 1.10 for two, up to 1.57 for five or more); a hospitalised one carried HR 1.79. [[Rothnie2018]]

Quality of life

Half go unreported

Only 51% of 190 flare-ups were reported to clinicians. People with 3–8 a year scored 14.8 points worse on the St George's Respiratory Questionnaire. [[Seemungal1998]]

Lung function

Faster decline

Over 4 years, people with frequent flare-ups lost FEV1 at −40.1 mL a year, against −32.1 mL in those with few. [[Donaldson2002]]

    GOLD 2023 states that the long-term outlook after a hospital stay for a flare-up is poor, "with a five-year mortality rate of about 50%". [[Agusti2023]] In the Quebec cohort the risk of the next severe flare-up was three times higher after the second and 24 times higher after the tenth, compared with the first.     Prevention, in brief

    Established

    Medicines and more

    A Lancet review lists inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting bronchodilators and their combinations as reducing flare-up frequency and severity, and calls pulmonary rehabilitation, self-management and home ventilatory support "increasingly important". [[WedzichaSeemungal2007]]

    Guideline

    Three groups of options

    The CHEST and Canadian Thoracic Society guideline groups prevention into non-drug, inhaled and oral therapies. [[Criner2015]] The medicines in detail:

    A target

    Delay the second one

    The ECLIPSE authors say the frequent-flare-up tendency "has implications for the targeting of exacerbation-prevention strategies". [[Hurst2010]] Suissa and colleagues propose "delaying the second severe exacerbation" as a strategic target. [[Suissa2012]]
