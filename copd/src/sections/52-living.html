Living with COPD

Daily life with COPD runs on a few skills: knowing your usual day, spotting a flare-up early, acting on a written plan, pacing your breathing, and planning ahead. Step through one written action plan, then try the breathing and flying tools below.

What the self-management trials found

A written action plan is usually one part of a wider self-management programme: education, a plan for flare-ups and contact with a case manager. The average benefit is real but modest, and not every programme helped.

Breathing out slowly: pursed lips

Pursed-lip breathing is something many people with COPD start doing on their own when breathless [[Bianchi2004]]. Switch between ordinary breathing and pursed lips and play the breaths: the slower breath out lets the lungs empty further before the next breath in.

Schematic. The size of the ordinary breath and the breath timings are drawn for illustration. The three changes are the measured averages in 22 people with COPD [[Bianchi2004]].

Breathing exercises

Walking further, not always less breathless

A Cochrane review (16 studies, 1,233 people) found 6-minute walk distance improved after yoga with pranayama breathing (45 m), with similar gains in single trials of pursed-lip (50 m) and diaphragmatic breathing (35 m). Effects on breathlessness and quality of life were not consistent Established [[Holland2012]].

Handheld fan

Air across the face

In a crossover trial in 50 people with advanced disease, a handheld fan blown across the nose and mouth for 5 minutes eased breathlessness more than the same fan aimed at the leg (P = 0.003) [[Galbraith2010]].

Staying active

Moving more, living longer?

In 2,386 people with COPD in Copenhagen, any regular activity above "very low" went with fewer COPD admissions (IRR 0.72) and lower mortality (HR 0.76) [[GarciaAymerich2006]]. Measured activity was the strongest predictor of death in 170 outpatients (C statistic 0.81 for 4-year survival) [[Waschki2011]]. These are associations: sicker people move less Early evidence [[GarciaAymerich2006,Waschki2011]].

Exercise training itself is covered in Pulmonary rehabilitation.

Flying and the hypoxic challenge test

Aircraft cabins are pressurised to a "cabin altitude" that regulators cap at 2,438 m (8,000 ft), chosen because an average healthy person's saturation stays above 90% up to that height; above 3,048 m (10,000 ft) it falls to about 89% [[Coker2022]]. A hypoxic challenge test has the person breathe 15% oxygen, roughly the inspired oxygen at a 2,438 m cabin [[Coker2022]].

BTS 2022

Who the statement considers for testing

People with COPD who have resting SpO2 ≤ 95%, an MRC score of 3 or more, or desaturation below 84% on a walking test, and in whom there are concerns about hypercapnia [[Coker2022]].

Already on oxygen

Long-term oxygen users

For people on long-term oxygen without hypercapnia, the statement says it "seems reasonable" to raise the flow by 2 L/min in flight, if the equipment allows [[Coker2022]]. It advises people with severe COPD to carry a copy of their management plan, specialist assessment before flying after a pneumothorax or with bullous disease, and notes COPD raises the risk of blood clots, especially after an exacerbation [[Coker2022]].

Limits of the test

It predicts low oxygen, not symptoms

In 82 people with moderate to very severe COPD, 76% fell below 6.6 kPa during simulated altitude (mean 6.3 kPa), but the value was the same in those with and without symptoms on earlier flights (6.3 vs 6.3 kPa). Those who flew with oxygen reported symptoms less often (17% vs 48%) [[Edvardsen2013]]. The Norwegian algorithm had 100% sensitivity and 80% specificity [[Edvardsen2012]].

Sex and intimacy

Rarely asked about

In 55 outpatients, men with COPD reported lower sexual quality of life than matched norms on every dimension, and women reported less frequent intimacy. Patients said they did not discuss sex with their doctor [[Kaptein2008]].

Work

Early retirement

An international survey of 2,426 people aged 45–67 with COPD in six countries found about 40% (447) of the working-age group had retired early because of COPD, at an estimated average age of about 54 [[Fletcher2011]].

Carers

A thin evidence base

A review of COPD caregiver research (2000–2014) found 27 studies: 25 descriptive, one intervention study, and only two that measured carer burden over time [[Mansfield2016]]. In a nurse-led advance care planning trial, loved ones' anxiety was lower at follow-up (p = 0.02) [[Houben2019]].

Planning ahead and palliative support

GOLD 2023 states that all patients with advanced COPD should be considered for end-of-life and palliative care support, to optimise symptom control and allow patients and families to make informed choices [[Agusti2023]].

That support has often been missing. Compared with 50 people with inoperable lung cancer, 50 people with end-stage COPD had worse daily living and physical, social and emotional function; 90% had clinically relevant anxiety or depression (vs 52%), and none had access to specialist palliative care (vs 30%) [[Gore2000]].

One structured, nurse-led advance care planning session (1.5 hours) in 165 people with advanced COPD improved the quality of end-of-life communication with physicians and made an advance care planning discussion within 6 months more likely, without raising distress [[Houben2019]]. A South London breathlessness support service improved breathlessness mastery at 6 weeks in 105 people with refractory breathlessness [[Higginson2014]].

SupportTalking about future care can feel hard. In the trial above, a planned conversation with a trained nurse did not add distress, and it helped people and their families talk with their doctors [[Houben2019]].
