Part 1 · Understanding COPD

Causes & risk factors

Tobacco smoke is the biggest single cause of COPD, but not the only one: genes, how the lungs grew in childhood, smoke from cooking fires, polluted air, dust and fumes at work, and past infections all add risk. Not only smoking

Cigarette smokers have more respiratory symptoms and lung function abnormalities, a faster yearly fall in FEV1 and a higher COPD death rate than non-smokers [[Agusti2023]]. In a global meta-analysis, current smoking raised the odds of COPD 3.2-fold and ever smoking 2.3-fold [[Adeloye2022]], and in the Global Burden of Disease 2019 smoking accounted for 46.0% of the disability-adjusted life years lost to COPD [[Safiri2022]].

Yet the American Thoracic Society found that the share of COPD due to smoking ranged from 9.7% to 97.9% between studies and was below 80% in most, so a substantial share comes from other risk factors [[Eisner2010]]. An estimated 25–45% of people with COPD have never smoked [[Salvi2009]]; in the BOLD study it was 23.3% of people with moderate or worse COPD [[Lamprecht2011]].

GOLD 2023 states that in low- and middle-income countries COPD in non-smokers may account for up to 60–70% of cases, and that non-smoking risk factors account for over 50% of the global burden [[Agusti2023]]. Compared with COPD in smokers, COPD in non-smokers is more common in women and younger people, with similar or milder symptoms, more small-airway obstruction, less emphysema and a slower fall in lung function [[Agusti2023]].

Poverty and lower socioeconomic status are consistently linked with airflow obstruction and COPD, probably through pollution, crowding, poor nutrition and infections [[Agusti2023]]. The Lancet Commission expects tobacco-unrelated factors to overtake smoking as a share of COPD risk within two decades Hypothesis [[Stolz2022]].

Stop smoking at age X: the Fletcher–Peto picture

Fletcher and Peto followed London working men and found that FEV1 falls gradually over life, but most non-smokers and many smokers never develop clinically significant airflow obstruction [[Fletcher1977]]. In susceptible people smoking caused irreversible obstruction; a susceptible smoker who stopped did not regain lost function, but further loss returned to the normal rate [[Fletcher1977]].

Exposure by exposure

Tobacco

Smoke, in all its forms

GOLD 2023 also lists passive smoke, pipes, cigars, water pipes and marijuana as risk factors, and notes that smoking in pregnancy can alter fetal lung growth [[Agusti2023]]. In the Copenhagen City Heart Study (25 years), the absolute risk of COPD among continuous smokers was at least 25%, and 92% of COPD deaths occurred in people smoking at baseline [[Lokke2006]].

The Commission cites evidence that e-cigarette use in never-tobacco-smokers doubles or triples the risk of self-reported COPD, emphysema or chronic bronchitis, while stating that further studies are needed Early evidence [[Stolz2022]].

Household air

Cooking and heating fires

About 3 billion people, half the world, are exposed to biomass smoke, against 1.01 billion who smoke tobacco, which suggests biomass may be the biggest COPD risk factor globally Hypothesis [[Salvi2009]]. Solid fuel use was associated with COPD (odds ratio 2.80) and chronic bronchitis (2.32), with wood smoke during domestic work carrying the highest risk [[Kurmi2010]].

GOLD 2023 notes it is still unclear how far household air pollution, rather than other poverty-related exposures, explains this association [[Agusti2023]].

Outdoor air

Traffic and particles

In UK Biobank (303,887 adults), each 5 µg/m³ more PM2.5 was associated with 83.13 mL lower FEV1 and more COPD (odds ratio 1.52) [[Doiron2019]]. GOLD 2023 says the risk is dose-dependent with no apparent safe threshold, and pollution also raises the risk of exacerbations, hospital admission and death [[Agusti2023]].

In Southern California, the share of 18-year-olds with low FEV1 was 4.9 times higher at the highest than the lowest PM2.5 exposure (7.9% vs 1.6%) [[Gauderman2004]]. As the air improved, low FEV1 at age 15 fell from 7.9% to 6.3% to 3.6% across three cohorts [[Gauderman2015]].

Work

Dusts, gases and fumes

The ATS concluded in 2010 that occupational exposures cause COPD, while outdoor pollution, secondhand smoke, biomass smoke and diet were associated with it but had not then met causation criteria [[Eisner2010]]. Occupational exposure to dust or smoke had a pooled odds ratio of 1.4 [[Adeloye2022]].

Early life

Lungs that grew less

Lungs grow until about age 20–25, earlier in females [[Agusti2023]]. GOLD 2023 lists prematurity, low birth weight, maternal smoking in pregnancy, repeated respiratory infections and poor nutrition as key determinants of peak lung function [[Agusti2023]]. In Tucson, infants in the lowest quarter of airway function at about 2 months still had FEV1 233 mL lower at age 22 [[Stern2007]].

FEV1 below 80% predicted at age 25–40 was found in 4–13% of three cohorts and went with earlier illness and higher mortality [[Agusti2017]].

Infections & asthma

TB, HIV and severe childhood asthma

Past tuberculosis was associated with COPD (pooled odds ratio 3.05), most strongly in high-TB countries, never smokers and younger people [[Byrne2015]]. COPD prevalence among people with HIV was 10.6% [[Bigna2018]]. Children with severe asthma had a 32-fold higher adjusted risk of COPD by age 50, and 43% of that COPD group had never smoked [[Tai2014]].

GOLD 2023 cautions that abnormal lung development can cause asthma-like symptoms in children, so some may have been labelled as asthma [[Agusti2023]].

Genes

Alpha-1 and many small effects

The most important known genetic risk factor is SERPINA1 mutation causing alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency. PiZZ is found in 0.12% of people with COPD, and from 1 in 408 people in Northern Europe to 1 in 1,274 in Eastern Europe [[Agusti2023]]. There is no increased COPD risk in MZ and SZ heterozygotes who do not smoke [[Agusti2023]].

Other variants each have small effects, but together may increase susceptibility [[Agusti2023]].

Sex

Are women more susceptible?

Among people with early-onset COPD or under 20 pack-years, women had lower FEV1 (48.7% vs 55.8% predicted in the low-exposure group) and more severe disease than men, which suggests greater susceptibility to smoke, though other explanations are possible Early evidence [[Sorheim2010]].

The answer depends on follow-up length and definition. An older review quoted only 15–20% of smokers developing airflow obstruction [[Petty2006]]; 25 years of follow-up in Copenhagen gave at least 25% [[Lokke2006]]; GOLD 2023 states that fewer than 50% of heavy smokers develop COPD [[Agusti2023]].

In 2019 an estimated 1.1 billion people smoked worldwide; the number of smokers fell by about 60 million between 2000 and 2018, but population growth cancelled out the benefit [[Stolz2022]].
