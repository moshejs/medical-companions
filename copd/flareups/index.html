Part 4 · Flare-ups & hospital

Flare-ups (exacerbations)

A flare-up is a spell of days when breathlessness, cough and sputum get clearly worse. Follow one through a small airway, from the cold or chest infection that sets it off to a recovery that is not always complete, then try the Rome severity grades and see what the studies found after a flare-up that needed hospital care.

Trigger

A virus or new bacteria reaches the airways

Most hospital-treated flare-ups carry an infection: in 64 people admitted with a flare-up, a virus and/or bacteria was found in 78% (viruses 48.4%, bacteria 54.7%). 353 In a London cohort, 64% of flare-ups followed a cold in the 18 days before, and most of the viruses found were rhinoviruses. 406 Catching a new strain of bacteria more than doubled the risk (relative risk 2.15). 408

In plain wordsMost flare-ups start with a cold or a chest infection. 353,406
Exam hookViruses (often rhinovirus) and bacteria trigger most exacerbations: 78% had one or both in Papi 2006. 353

Get helpA flare-up can become an emergency. GOLD 2023 lists reasons for hospital care that include sudden worsening of breathlessness at rest, a high breathing rate, low oxygen saturation, confusion or drowsiness, and new blue lips (cyanosis) or swollen ankles. 12 Anyone with these signs needs urgent medical help: in an emergency, call your local emergency number. People who have had high carbon dioxide levels before may carry an oxygen alert card for the ambulance crew. 348

What counts as a flare-up

GOLD 2023 adopted the Rome proposal, which defines an exacerbation of COPD as "an event characterized by dyspnea and/or cough and sputum that worsen over ≤14 days, which may be accompanied by tachypnea and/or tachycardia and is often associated with increased local and systemic inflammation caused by airway infection, pollution, or other insult to the airways". 12,67

The older definition, an "acute worsening of respiratory symptoms that results in additional therapy", graded severity afterwards by how much healthcare was used. GOLD 2023 calls that definition "highly non-specific" and the after-the-fact grading "useless to guide treatment at the point of care". Rome grades severity at the bedside instead. 12

MildBreathlessness VAS <5, breathing rate <24 breaths/min, heart rate <95 bpm, resting SpO2 ≥92% on air (or the usual oxygen prescription) with a change of ≤3% when known, and CRP <10 mg/L if obtained. 67,203
ModerateAt least three of five: VAS ≥5, breathing rate ≥24, heart rate ≥95, resting SpO2 <92% and/or a change of >3%, CRP ≥10 mg/L. The blood gas may show low oxygen (PaO2 ≤8 kPa) and/or high carbon dioxide (PaCO2 >6 kPa), but no acidosis. 67,203
SevereDefined by the arterial blood gas: high carbon dioxide (PaCO2 >6 kPa; >45 mmHg) with acidosis (pH <7.35). 67,203,248 GOLD 2023 notes the gas must show "new-onset/worsening hypercapnia or acidosis", because a few people have long-standing high carbon dioxide. 12

In a Korean validation of 740 emergency visits or admissions, Rome classed 30.9% as mild, 58.6% as moderate and 10.4% as severe, and predicted ICU admission (AUROC 0.850) and need for ventilation (AUROC 0.870) better than GesEPOC 2021. 248 In a Swedish telehealth cohort of 125 flare-ups, Rome graded severity lower than GOLD 2022 did, and heart failure did not distort its measures. 203

Look-alikes to rule out

GOLD 2023

Mimic or aggravate

Decompensated heart failure, pneumonia and pulmonary embolism may "mimic or aggravate" a flare-up. Worsening breathlessness without the classic features should prompt a search for them. 12

Pulmonary embolism

16.1% of unexplained flare-ups

Across 7 CT-angiography studies (880 patients), a clot in the lungs was found in a pooled 16.1% (95% CI 8.3–25.8%) of unexplained flare-ups; 68% of the clots were in the main, lobar or interlobar arteries. 16

Clues

Pain and heart failure

In that meta-analysis, chest pain on breathing and heart failure were more frequent in flare-ups with a clot, and signs of a chest infection were less frequent. 16 More on similar conditions:

One flare-up predicts the next

ECLIPSE, 2138 people followed for 3 years: first-year flare-ups by GOLD stage Established

"The single best predictor of exacerbations, across all GOLD stages, was a history of exacerbations." The pattern was stable over 3 years and also linked to reflux or heartburn, worse quality of life and a raised white-cell count. 199

99,574 UK patients

A graded risk

Around one quarter never had a flare-up in up to 10 years. Year-1 flare-ups predicted the long-term rate (HR 1.71 for one, up to 3.41 for five or more). Frequent moderate flare-ups raised the risk of death (HR 1.10 for two, up to 1.57 for five or more); a hospitalised one carried HR 1.79. 387

Quality of life

Half go unreported

Only 51% of 190 flare-ups were reported to clinicians. People with 3–8 a year scored 14.8 points worse on the St George's Respiratory Questionnaire. 404

Lung function

Faster decline

Over 4 years, people with frequent flare-ups lost FEV1 at −40.1 mL a year, against −32.1 mL in those with few. 115

After a flare-up that needs hospital care

  • Quebec, 73,106 people from their first COPD hospitalisation: 50% had died by 3.6 years and 75% by 7.7 years. 436
  • SUPPORT, 1016 people admitted with PaCO2 ≥50 mmHg: 11% died in hospital, 20% by 60 days, 33% by 180 days, 43% by 1 year and 49% by 2 years. Only 26% were alive with good-to-excellent quality of life at 6 months. 82
  • Dutch cohort of 171 admissions: 8% died in hospital and 23% by 1 year; 35% by 1 year for those admitted to ICU for respiratory failure. 172
  • US Medicare, 2.3 million COPD hospitalisations, deaths within 1 year: 26.2% overall, 24.4% with no ventilation, 41.8% after non-invasive ventilation and 45.7% after invasive ventilation; 64.2% were readmitted within the year. 253

Prevention, in brief

Established

Medicines and more

A Lancet review lists inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting bronchodilators and their combinations as reducing flare-up frequency and severity, and calls pulmonary rehabilitation, self-management and home ventilatory support "increasingly important". 479

A target

Delay the second one

COPD Companion is an educational synthesis of the published literature on chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), emphysema and chronic bronchitis, made for health-science, medical and nursing courses and for people with COPD, their families and caregivers.

It is not medical advice. It cannot diagnose, and nothing here should start, stop or change an inhaler, a medicine, oxygen or a care plan without your own care team. The spirometry explainer, GOLD grade and group tools, mMRC and CAT scores, the blood-gas explainer and the other calculators are for learning, never for diagnosis, self-triage or treatment decisions. Doses, oxygen targets, inhaler routines and guideline statements are quoted as published (GOLD, NICE, ATS/ERS, BTS and drug labels), never as instructions, and survival and risk figures are population statistics. If breathing becomes much worse than usual, or there is confusion, drowsiness, chest pain or blue lips, follow your action plan or seek urgent care.
