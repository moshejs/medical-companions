Part 3 · Treatment

Long-term oxygen & home ventilation

Two trials from around 1980 showed that oxygen for many hours a day helps people with COPD live longer when blood oxygen is very low at rest. Later trials showed where it does not help. Play the survival curves, then try the threshold explainer.

Only the dots are published values; the dotted lines simply join them, and the shape of each curve between dots is not published. MRC 5-year figures from the trial abstract 296. NOTT 12- and 24-month figures are secondary-sourced: the NOTT abstract gives only the 1.94 mortality ratio, and the anchor points were read from the NOTT row of a 2022 systematic review 26,342. Survival is 100 minus the published mortality. HOT-HMV medians and 12-month risks from the trial 300.

Measure

Is resting oxygen low enough?

ATS 2020 defines severe hypoxaemia as PaO2 ≤ 55 mm Hg (7.3 kPa) or SpO2 ≤ 88%; or PaO2 56–59 mm Hg (7.5–7.9 kPa) or SpO2 89% plus oedema, haematocrit ≥ 55% or P pulmonale on an ECG. For that group it recommends long-term oxygen for at least 15 h/day (strong recommendation) 202.

In plain wordsHome oxygen was studied in people whose oxygen level was very low while resting and breathing room air 202.
Exam hookLTOT threshold (ATS 2020): PaO2 ≤ 55 mm Hg / SpO2 ≤ 88%, or 56–59 mm Hg / 89% with oedema, haematocrit ≥ 55% or P pulmonale 202.

Threshold explainer: the published eligibility bands

The ATS 2020 guideline recommends long-term oxygen for at least 15 hours a day in adults with COPD and severe chronic resting room-air hypoxaemia (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence) 202. It defines severe hypoxaemia by either of two criteria, based on the NOTT and MRC entry criteria. GOLD 2023 says its criteria "remain unchanged and are described in detail in the GOLD 2023 report" 12. Choose a measurement, move the value and tick any findings to see which published band it falls in.

Who oxygen helped

Long-term home oxygen "improved survival in a selected group of COPD patients with severe hypoxaemia"; there was no survival benefit in mild to moderate hypoxaemia or with night-time desaturation only 86. The review writes 55 mm Hg as 8.0 kPa; ATS 2020 pairs 55 mm Hg with 7.3 kPa, the correct conversion 202.

ATS 2020 · other suggestions

Moving about with oxygen

ATS suggests ambulatory oxygen for severe exertional room-air hypoxaemia (SpO2 ≤ 88% on exertion; conditional) and portable liquid oxygen for people mobile outside the home who need continuous flow above 3 L/min on exertion (conditional, very-low-quality evidence) 202.

Open question

24 hours or 15?

Textbooks recommended 24 h/day after NOTT 342, but the NOTT continuous arm averaged about 17.7 h/day 26, and the 24-hour advice rested on a non-randomised NOTT-versus-MRC contrast. REDOX found 24 h no better than 15 h at 1 year; it was not powered for longer-term survival 126.

Oxygen safety at home

Safety events per 100 person-years in LOTT, as reported by ATS 2020 202.

ATS 2020 makes a best-practice statement that patients and caregivers be trained in oxygen equipment and safety, "including smoking cessation, fire prevention, and tripping hazards" 202. In LOTT, the commonest event was tripping or falling over equipment; burns came from smoking near oxygen, from open flames and from liquid-oxygen frost 202.

The BTS 2015 home oxygen guideline covers risk assessment, "particularly in the clinically challenging area of home oxygen users who smoke" 182. Help with stopping smoking is in Stopping smoking & vaccines.

Home non-invasive ventilation (NIV)

Some people with COPD keep a high carbon dioxide level when stable. ATS 2020 defines chronic hypercapnic COPD as FEV1/FVC < 0.70 with resting PaCO2 > 45 mm Hg, outside an exacerbation 266. The ATS guideline discusses nocturnal NIV: a ventilator that delivers breaths through a mask during sleep 266.

COPD Companion is an educational synthesis of the published literature on chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), emphysema and chronic bronchitis, made for health-science, medical and nursing courses and for people with COPD, their families and caregivers.

It is not medical advice. It cannot diagnose, and nothing here should start, stop or change an inhaler, a medicine, oxygen or a care plan without your own care team. The spirometry explainer, GOLD grade and group tools, mMRC and CAT scores, the blood-gas explainer and the other calculators are for learning, never for diagnosis, self-triage or treatment decisions. Doses, oxygen targets, inhaler routines and guideline statements are quoted as published (GOLD, NICE, ATS/ERS, BTS and drug labels), never as instructions, and survival and risk figures are population statistics. If breathing becomes much worse than usual, or there is confusion, drowsiness, chest pain or blue lips, follow your action plan or seek urgent care.
