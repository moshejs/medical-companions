Part 1 · Understanding BPD

Genes & families

Twin, family and register studies agree that genes matter for borderline personality disorder: roughly 35–69% of the differences between people in BPD traits are linked to genes. There is no "BPD gene", and heritability says nothing certain about any one family. [[Torgersen2000,Distel2008,Skoglund2021,Streit2026]]

Design

Polygenic, not one gene

The largest genome-wide study so far (12,339 cases, more than a million controls) found 11 genomic loci. Together, common variants explain about 17% of liability (SNP heritability), and today's polygenic score explains 4.6%. Slide to add more of the common-variant signal and watch how little the curve moves. [[Streit2026]]

Liability-threshold model, illustrative. It assumes a population rate of about 1.7% and a normally distributed liability. At today's 4.6% the model gives a top-versus-bottom-tenth ratio close to the published odds ratio of 6.61 (preprint), which is why it is a fair teaching sketch. It is not a test: a polygenic score cannot diagnose or predict BPD in one person. [[Gunderson2018,Streit2026,Streit2025medRxiv]]

What it means, and what it does not

Established

Why candidate genes failed

Early BPD genetics relied on small, unreplicated single-gene studies. Meta-analyses found no association with the serotonin transporter (5-HTTLPR, intron 2 VNTR), TPH1 or the serotonin 1B receptor gene. In depression, 18 historical candidate genes tested in up to 443,264 people did no better than random genes. Risk comes from thousands of tiny-effect variants that only samples of tens of thousands of cases can detect. [[BassirNia2018,Calati2013,Amad2014,Border2019,Streit2026]]

Early evidence

Genes and environment work together

In 5,083 twins and 1,285 siblings, the genes behind BPD features also raised exposure to break-ups, assault and job loss (gene–environment correlation), and after those events environment mattered more (interaction). In E-Risk, harsh treatment predicted borderline-related traits even within identical-twin pairs, more strongly with a family history of psychiatric illness. A Minnesota discordant-twin study found the abuse link mostly genetic instead. See Causes. [[Distel2011LifeEvents,Belsky2012,Bornovalova2013]]

Early evidence

Epigenetics

Childhood maltreatment went with more methylation of the stress-hormone receptor gene NR3C1, and a site near miR124-3 tracked both BPD and maltreatment. A 2025 review of 19 small studies found the candidate-gene methylation results inconsistent. [[Perroud2011,Prados2015,Dawidowski2025]]

Established

What heritability means for one family

Heritability is a population statistic about differences between people, not a share of one person's illness. Even among relatives of someone with BPD, 14.1% had BPD against 4.9% of other relatives, so most relatives did not. Genetic effects were stable over 10 years and explained 72% of trait stability, yet mean trait levels still fell with age. [[Gunderson2011Family,ReichbornKjennerud2015,Bornovalova2009]]

Established

BPD shares genes with ordinary traits

Borderline traits correlated with neuroticism at .68, and the genes behind neuroticism, agreeableness, conscientiousness and extraversion accounted for all the genetic liability to borderline personality. Across personality disorders, BPD loaded on a broad "negative emotionality" factor and on one shared with antisocial PD (genetic correlation 0.73). [[Distel2009,Kendler2008,ReichbornKjennerud2015]]

Established

ADHD overlap

In 2,113,902 Swedes, ADHD went with an adjusted odds ratio of 19.4 for a BPD diagnosis, and an identical co-twin with ADHD with 11.2. In Dutch and Belgian twins, BPD traits and adult ADHD symptoms had a genetic correlation of 0.72. See BPD or something else? [[KujaHalkola2021,Distel2011ADHD]]

Open question

Sex and ancestry

In the preprint, SNP heritability was 20.5% in women and 10.2% in men, but twin studies found similar heritability in both sexes; 85.4% of Swedish diagnoses were in women, which may reflect who gets diagnosed. The largest GWAS included only people of European ancestry, so results may not transfer. [[Streit2025medRxiv,Distel2008,Skoglund2021,Streit2026]]

Early evidence

Genes across the teenage years

In Minnesota female twins, shared-environment effects at 14 fell to zero by 17–24 while genetic influence tended to rise. Both the level of traits and how fast they changed were heritable (a² = .78 and .76). In E-Risk, borderline symptoms at 12 predicted poorer outcomes at 18, mostly through familial, particularly genetic, risk. [[Bornovalova2009,Wertz2020]]
