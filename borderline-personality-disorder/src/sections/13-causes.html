Causes & the biosocial model

No single thing causes borderline personality disorder. Inherited temperament and hard experiences add up, and they shape each other over the years. Scrub through one schematic childhood to see how the biosocial model describes that loop, then compare what the risk-factor studies actually measured. [[Lieb2004,Bohus2021]]

What the risk-factor studies measured

Odds ratios from meta-analyses and cohorts, on a log scale. Notice how much the size depends on the design: people with BPD compared with non-clinical controls report adversity far more often (OR 13.91), but in cohort and population designs the odds ratio is about 2.6. Most exposed people do not develop BPD, and many people with BPD report no abuse. [[Porter2020,Zanarini1997,Temes2020]]

Models, myths and what follows from them

Hypothesis

The biosocial model

Linehan's theory: BPD arises when a child who is biologically emotionally vulnerable grows up in an invalidating environment. The vulnerability is heightened sensitivity, difficulty regulating intense emotion, and a slow return to baseline. Crowell, Beauchaine and Linehan added impulsivity as one of the earliest traits and described development as a series of child–environment transactions. Its treatment offshoot is dialectical behaviour therapy. [[Crowell2009]]

Hypothesis

The mentalizing and attachment model

Fonagy and Luyten propose a low threshold for switching on the attachment system and switching off controlled mentalizing, so that under stress it is hard to tell one's own mind from someone else's. Later work recast BPD as low resilience linked to a general "p" factor, and framed epistemic hypervigilance (difficulty trusting what others teach) as an adaptation to an adverse social-learning environment. Treatment: mentalization-based treatment. [[FonagyLuyten2009,Fonagy2017a,Fonagy2017b]]

Established association

Attachment

All 13 studies in one review found a strong link between BPD and insecure attachment, most often unresolved, preoccupied or fearful; the direction is unclear. In infancy cohorts, disorganised attachment, maternal withdrawal at 18 months and parental hostility came before later borderline symptoms. Gunderson and Lyons-Ruth proposed an inherited "interpersonal hypersensitivity" that meets the parents' own vulnerabilities (Hypothesis). [[Agrawal2004,Carlson2009,LyonsRuth2013,GundersonLyonsRuth2008]]

Myth

"Bad parenting causes BPD"

Not supported. The transactional model's authors frame it as blaming neither the person with BPD nor their parents. Influence runs both ways: teenage symptoms changed parenting and parents' own symptoms. Genes shared by parent and child explain part of the link between family adversity and BPD. And sexual abuse, often assumed to be the cause, is "neither necessary nor sufficient". [[Fruzzetti2005,Stepp2014,Kaufman2020,Bornovalova2013,Zanarini1997]]

Myth

"Everyone with BPD was abused"

Many people with BPD report abuse, and adversity is a real risk factor, but childhood sexual abuse was reported by 62.4% of adults and only 26.0% of adolescents with BPD in one inpatient sample. An early meta-analysis found a moderate correlation (r = .279) and concluded it was not a major or necessary cause. Retrospective recall, selection and severity may all shape these figures. [[Temes2020,Fossati1999,Temes2017]]

Established

Traits are not fixed

BPD usually takes shape in adolescence, after age 12, often after or alongside depression, anxiety, conduct problems or hyperactivity. Traits then tend to ease: in Minnesota twins the mean score fell from 41.3 at 14 to 35.19 at 24, and in Norwegian twins the criterion count fell 28% over 10 years. See Course & recovery. [[Bohus2021,Bornovalova2009,ReichbornKjennerud2015]]

Early evidence

Invalidation, measured

In 186 community adults, a path from perceived invalidation in close relationships through emotion dysregulation and poor distress tolerance to BPD features explained 38% of the variance. The design was cross-sectional, so it cannot show which came first. [[Sturrock2014]]

Hypothesis

Why this matters for care

If temperament and environment shape each other, care can aim at both: skills for handling strong emotion, and support for families. For mothers with BPD, consistent warmth, monitoring, psychoeducation and mindfulness-based parenting have been suggested as targets. See Families & carers. [[Crowell2009,Stepp2012,Eyden2016]]
