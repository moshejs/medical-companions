# B · Causes: genes, temperament, adversity & the biosocial model

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: causes, genes.

This pass covers why borderline personality disorder (BPD) develops. It covers twin, family and register heritability; family aggregation; genome-wide association studies (GWAS), SNP heritability and polygenic scores; why the old candidate-gene findings did not hold up; gene–environment interplay and epigenetics; temperament (negative affectivity, impulsivity, emotional sensitivity); childhood adversity (abuse, neglect, harsh parenting, bullying, family adversity, low socioeconomic status) and attachment; the biosocial and transactional models (Linehan; Crowell, Beauchaine and Linehan; Fruzzetti); and the mentalizing and epistemic-trust model (Fonagy and Luyten). It also covers what prospective cohorts show: Children in the Community, ALSPAC, E-Risk, the Minnesota twin and longitudinal studies, and the Pittsburgh Girls Study. Every number below was read from the PubMed abstract or, where noted, from the open-access full text in PubMed Central.

## Findings

### causes · The big picture: many causes, no single cause
- Leading reviews describe BPD as having a multifactorial cause in which genetic factors and adverse childhood events both contribute and neither alone explains the disorder (Established) [[Lieb2004,Bohus2021]].
- A coherent BPD syndrome usually begins in adolescence (after age 12). It is often preceded by, or develops alongside, internalising problems (depression, anxiety), externalising problems (conduct problems, hyperactivity, substance use) or both (Established) [[Bohus2021]].
- BPD is found in about 1.7% of the general population and in 15–28% of patients in psychiatric clinics or hospitals. The nature of the disorder is not fully known, which feeds an open debate about its definition [[Gunderson2018]].
- A 2009 review applied Hill's classic criteria for causation to the 1995–2007 literature. It concluded that the evidence supports childhood trauma as a cause, especially when trauma is seen as one part of a multifactorial model (Early evidence) [[BallLinks2009]].
- A long-standing view holds that "plasticity" genes, interacting with both negative and positive life events, may explain why twin studies show high heritability while single-gene association studies come up empty (Hypothesis) [[Amad2014]].

### genes · Twin studies: how much of the variation is genetic?
- In the first interview-based twin study of all personality disorders (Norway; 92 monozygotic and 129 dizygotic twin pairs, SCID-II), the best-fitting model gave borderline PD a heritability of .69. The best-fitting models never included shared family environment, though the authors could not rule out shared familial effects for borderline PD (Early evidence; moderate sample) [[Torgersen2000]].
- In that same study, heritability was .60 for personality disorders in general and .60 for the "emotional" cluster B [[Torgersen2000]].
- A twin study of 5,496 twins from 3,644 families in the Netherlands (n=3,918), Belgium (n=904) and Australia (n=674) used a mailed self-report questionnaire. Additive genetic influences explained 42% of the variation in BPD features, in both men and women and similarly in all three countries. Unique (non-shared) environment explained the remaining 58% (Established) [[Distel2008]].
- In that three-country sample, women scored higher than men on BPD features, and younger adults endorsed more features than older adults [[Distel2008]].
- In 1,386 Norwegian twin pairs aged 19–35 interviewed with the SIDP-IV, heritability was 35% for BPD traits, 38% for antisocial, 31% for histrionic and 24% for narcissistic traits. The best model included no sex or shared family environment effects [[Torgersen2008]].
- In a multivariate twin study of 2,794 Norwegian young adults, the 10 DSM-IV personality disorders were best explained by 3 genetic factors. BPD loaded on the first, broad factor ("negative emotionality") and on a second factor shared only with antisocial PD (high impulsivity/low agreeableness) (Established) [[Kendler2008]].
- In the same study, the genetic structure did not mirror the DSM clusters A, B and C. The individual-specific environmental factors did, suggesting that environmental experience may drive the clustering [[Kendler2008]].
- In 5,533 twins and 1,202 siblings, the 4 BPD feature scales (affective instability, identity problems, negative relationships, self-harm) were best explained by one latent BPD factor with heritability 51%. Scale-specific genetic effects ranged from 4% (affective instability) to 20% (self-harm) [[Distel2010]].
- In a web-based sample of 44,112 people including 542 twin pairs, 4 BPD dimensions reflected one latent liability with heritability 60%. Affective instability had the lowest trait-specific genetic loading, marking it as a core feature [[Kendler2011]].
- Twin and family studies give BPD heritability estimates of roughly 35–69%. The spread reflects differences in measures (interview vs self-report), samples and age (Established) [[Torgersen2000,Torgersen2008,Distel2008,Skoglund2021]].
- A systematic review that pooled family and twin studies estimated BPD heritability at approximately 40% [[Amad2014]].

### genes · Heritability across the life course
- In the Minnesota Twin Family Study (female twins assessed at ages 14, 17, 20 and 24), mean BPD trait scores fell from adolescence to adulthood while rank-order stability stayed high (test–retest r = .53 to .73) [[Bornovalova2009]].
- In that cohort the observed mean BPD-trait score fell from 41.3 at age 14 to 35.19 at age 24 [[Bornovalova2009]].
- At age 14 there were modest additive genetic and shared environmental effects and a large non-shared environmental effect. From ages 17 to 24 the genetic influence tended to rise and shared environmental effects fell to zero [[Bornovalova2009]].
- In a genetically informed growth model, both a person's overall level of BPD traits (intercept a² = .78, 95% CI .53–1.00) and their rate of change (slope a² = .76, 95% CI .69–.82) were highly heritable [[Bornovalova2009]].
- In 2,282 Norwegian twins interviewed in early adulthood and about 10 years later, the mean BPD criterion count fell 28%. Rank-order stability was 0.45 [[ReichbornKjennerud2015]].
- In that study, genetic effects on BPD stayed completely stable over 10 years and accounted for 72% of the stability of BPD traits. Unique environmental effects were mostly transient and were the main source of change (Established) [[ReichbornKjennerud2015]].
- BPD and antisocial PD traits shared much of their genetic liability (genetic correlation 0.73; environmental correlation 0.43) [[ReichbornKjennerud2015]].
- In the E-Risk birth cohort (1,116 same-sex twin pairs), borderline-related characteristics at age 12 were highly heritable. The twin correlation was 0.66 for monozygotic and 0.29 for dizygotic twins [[Belsky2012]].
- For extreme borderline-related scores at 12, the probandwise concordance was 52% in monozygotic twins and 7% in dizygotic twins [[Belsky2012]].
- In E-Risk, borderline symptoms at age 12 predicted poorer personality, mental health and functional outcomes, and more victimisation, at age 18. Familial, particularly genetic, risk accounted for most of these links [[Wertz2020]].

### genes · Family and register studies
- In the McLean family study (368 probands: 132 with BPD, 134 without BPD, 102 with major depression; 885 siblings and parents, all directly interviewed), BPD showed familial aggregation. The risk ratio was 2.9 (95% CI 1.5–5.5) using proband–relative pairs and 3.9 (1.7–9.0) using all family pairs [[Gunderson2011Family]].
- In that study, BPD prevalence was 14.1% in relatives of probands with BPD vs 4.9% in relatives of probands without BPD [[Gunderson2011Family]].
- All 4 sectors of BPD psychopathology (affective, interpersonal, behavioural, cognitive) ran in families, most strongly the affective and interpersonal sectors. A single latent BPD liability best explained them [[Gunderson2011Family]].
- A Swedish total-population register study followed 1,851,755 people born 1973–1993. Of these, 11,665 (0.6%) received a clinical BPD diagnosis between 1997 and 2013, and 85.4% of those diagnosed were female [[Skoglund2021]].
- Familial risk of diagnosed BPD fell with genetic relatedness. Adjusted hazard ratios were:
  - monozygotic twins: 11.5 (95% CI 1.6–83.8)
  - dizygotic twins: 7.4 (1.0–55.3)
  - full siblings: 4.7 (3.9–5.6)
  - maternal half-siblings: 2.1 (1.5–3.0)
  - paternal half-siblings: 1.3 (0.9–2.1)
  - cousins whose parents were full siblings: 1.7 (1.4–2.0) [[Skoglund2021]]
- Heritability of clinically diagnosed BPD in Sweden was 46% (95% CI 39–53). The rest of the variance was individually unique environment, with no shared-environment component retained (Established) [[Skoglund2021]].
- In 2,113,902 Swedes, people with ADHD had an adjusted odds ratio of 19.4 (95% CI 18.6–20.4) of also having a BPD diagnosis. Having a full sibling with ADHD raised BPD risk (aOR 2.8, 2.6–3.1), and a monozygotic co-twin with ADHD gave an aOR of 11.2 (3.0–42.2). This pattern suggests shared genetic factors [[KujaHalkola2021]].
- In 7,233 Dutch and Belgian twins and siblings, BPD traits and adult ADHD symptoms correlated at r = 0.59. Genetic factors explained 49% of that overlap, and the genetic correlation was 0.72 [[Distel2011ADHD]].

### genes · Molecular genetics: GWAS, SNP heritability and polygenic scores
- The first case–control GWAS of BPD (Witt 2017; 998 patients, 1,545 controls) found no single-marker hit after multiple-testing correction. Gene-based tests implicated DPYD and PKP4, and gene-set analysis implicated exocytosis [[Witt2017]].
- That GWAS found genetic correlations of BPD with major depression (rg = 0.57), schizophrenia (rg = 0.34) and bipolar disorder (rg = 0.28). This was the first molecular evidence that BPD shares genetic risk with these disorders [[Witt2017]].
- A GWAS of BPD features in 7,125 Dutch people estimated SNP heritability at 23% for the PAI-BOR total score. SNP heritability was concentrated in affective instability items (42.7%), and a promising signal near SERINC5 was reported (Early evidence) [[Lubke2014]].
- Using the BPD GWAS (N = 2,543), BPD showed positive genetic correlations with neuroticism (rg = 0.34) and openness (rg = 0.24) but not with conscientiousness, extraversion or agreeableness (all |rg| < 0.14) [[Streit2022]].
- The largest BPD GWAS meta-analysis to date (Streit 2026; discovery 12,339 cases and 1,041,717 controls, replication 685 cases and 107,750 controls, all European ancestry) identified 11 independent genomic loci and 9 risk genes (Established for common-variant polygenicity) [[Streit2026]].
- In that study SNP heritability was 17.3% on the liability scale. Polygenic scores (PGS) explained 4.6% of the variance in BPD on the liability scale [[Streit2026]].
- BPD's strongest genetic correlations were with post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, ADHD, antisocial behaviour, and measures of suicide and self-harm. Phenome-wide analyses also linked BPD-PGS to physical conditions such as obstructive pulmonary disease and diabetes [[Streit2026]].
- In the preprint version, the genetic correlations were rg = 0.77 with PTSD, 0.74 with depression and 0.67 with ADHD, compared with 0.35 for bipolar disorder and 0.36 for schizophrenia [[Streit2025medRxiv]].
- In the preprint, people in the top PGS decile had an odds ratio of 6.61 (95% CI 5.20–8.41) for being a BPD case vs the bottom decile, up from 2.09 (1.60–2.72) using PGS from the 2017 GWAS. Against the middle decile the top-decile OR was 2.37 (1.94–2.89) [[Streit2025medRxiv]].
- In the preprint, liability-scale SNP heritability was 20.5% in females and 10.2% in males, a significant difference [[Streit2025medRxiv]].
- In the preprint, FOXP2 had the highest gene-prioritisation score. FOXP2 has also been linked in other GWAS to externalising traits, ADHD and substance use [[Streit2025medRxiv]].
- The authors note that common variants (SNP heritability ≈17%) explain roughly a third of the 46–69% heritability found in twin and family studies, a ratio typical of psychiatric disorders [[Streit2025medRxiv]].
- Even the improved BPD polygenic score explains only a small share of risk (4.6% of variance) and cannot diagnose or predict BPD in an individual (Established) [[Streit2026]].

### genes · Why the candidate-gene findings did not replicate
- A 2013 systematic review found conflicting results across BPD genetic association studies. Meta-analyses showed no association for the serotonin transporter promoter polymorphism (5-HTTLPR), the serotonin transporter intron 2 VNTR, or the TPH1 A218C polymorphism [[Calati2013]].
- A 2014 systematic review and meta-analysis likewise found no significant association of BPD with the serotonin transporter gene, TPH1 or the serotonin 1B receptor gene [[Amad2014]].
- Most early BPD genetic studies were small, unreplicated single-candidate-gene studies. BPD genetics lagged well behind other major psychiatric disorders [[BassirNia2018]].
- In depression, a test of 18 historical candidate genes in samples of 62,138 to 443,264 people found no clear main effects and no gene-by-environment effects. Candidate genes were no more associated with depression than random genes, suggesting most earlier reports were false positives (Established) [[Border2019]].
- The lesson for BPD: risk comes from thousands of common variants of tiny effect, which only samples of tens of thousands of cases can detect, not from a few "BPD genes" (Established) [[Streit2026,Border2019]].

### genes · Gene–environment interplay and epigenetics
- In 5,083 twins and 1,285 siblings, both gene–environment interaction and gene–environment correlation were found for BPD features. The genes that influence BPD features also raised the likelihood of exposure to some life events (divorce/break-up, violent assault, sexual assault, job loss) [[Distel2011LifeEvents]].
- In that study, among people exposed to divorce/break-up, violent or sexual assault, or job loss, environmental variance in BPD features was higher, so heritability was lower [[Distel2011LifeEvents]].
- In the E-Risk cohort, harsh treatment through age 10 (physical maltreatment, maternal negative expressed emotion) predicted borderline-related characteristics at 12. The link held in monozygotic-twin difference analyses, evidence of a true environmental effect (Early evidence) [[Belsky2012]].
- In that cohort, the harsh-treatment effect was stronger in children with a family history of psychiatric illness, consistent with a diathesis–stress model (Early evidence) [[Belsky2012]].
- A Minnesota longitudinal discordant-twin study (1,382 twin pairs, followed from age 11 to 24) found little or no evidence that childhood abuse directly causes BPD traits at age 24. The abuse–BPD association instead reflected shared genetic influences, partly overlapping with internalising and externalising disorders (Early evidence; conflicts with E-Risk) [[Bornovalova2013]].
- In 1,051 low-income maltreated and non-maltreated children, maltreatment × gene × gender interactions involving oxytocin-receptor and FKBP5 variants predicted childhood borderline symptoms. The pattern differed in girls (diathesis–stress) and boys (differential sensitivity) (Hypothesis; single study) [[Cicchetti2014]].
- In 101 adults with BPD and 99 with major depression, childhood sexual abuse, its severity and the number of maltreatment types correlated with higher methylation of the glucocorticoid-receptor gene NR3C1 promoter. This suggests an epigenetic route from early stress to the stress-hormone (HPA) axis (Early evidence) [[Perroud2011]].
- A genome-wide methylation scan (96 BPD, 93 depression) linked maltreatment severity to methylation at several sites. One near miR124-3, a microRNA that targets NR3C1, was associated with both BPD and maltreatment severity (Early evidence) [[Prados2015]].
- A 2025 systematic review of 19 DNA-methylation studies in BPD found inconsistent associations for candidate genes (NR3C1, FKBP5, BDNF, DRD2, HTR2A, COMT). Small, heterogeneous samples prevent firm conclusions (Established that evidence is mixed) [[Dawidowski2025]].

### causes · Temperament and personality traits
- A meta-analysis of five-factor-model studies supports describing personality disorders, including BPD, as maladaptive variants of normal personality traits [[SamuelWidiger2008]].
- In 4,403 MZ twins, 4,425 DZ twins and 1,661 siblings, borderline personality correlated with neuroticism at .68 and was best predicted by high neuroticism plus low agreeableness. The genes behind neuroticism, agreeableness, conscientiousness and extraversion accounted for all the genetic liability to borderline personality [[Distel2009]].
- In that study, 33% of the unique environmental influence on borderline personality was specific to BPD. The authors suggest environment may push people with a given trait profile over a threshold into BPD (Hypothesis) [[Distel2009]].
- The strongest genetic correlations of BPD dimensions were with neuroticism (positive), then conscientiousness and agreeableness (both negative) [[Kendler2011]].
- The biosocial developmental model proposes that the earliest vulnerability on the BPD pathway is impulsivity, followed by heightened emotional sensitivity. Environmental risks then amplify these into emotional, behavioural and cognitive dysregulation (Hypothesis) [[Crowell2009]].
- In the Pittsburgh Girls Study (n = 2,450), harsh punishment, poor self-control and negative emotionality between ages 5 and 14 each predicted BPD symptom severity at 14. Only worsening self-control between 12 and 14 predicted growth in BPD symptoms from 14 to 17 [[Hallquist2015]].
- In that cohort, parent and teacher ratings of emotionality at ages 5–8 predicted new-onset positive BPD screens at ages 14–22. So did parent-rated hyperactivity/impulsivity and depression and teacher-rated inattention (Early evidence) [[Stepp2017]].
- In E-Risk, borderline-related characteristics at 12 were more common in children who at age 5 had poorer cognitive function, more impulsivity, and more behavioural and emotional problems [[Belsky2012]].
- In ALSPAC, children with dysregulated behaviour at ages 4–8 were more likely to experience maladaptive parenting (β = 0.075) and bully victimisation (β = 0.327). Part of their later BPD risk ran indirectly through bullying (indirect β = 0.097) [[Winsper2017]].

### causes · Childhood adversity: how common, how strong
- A meta-analysis of 97 studies found that people with BPD were 13.91 times (95% CI 11.11–17.43) more likely to report childhood adversity than non-clinical controls in case–control studies [[Porter2020]].
- In that meta-analysis, the association was smaller in retrospective cohort studies (OR 2.59, 95% CI 0.93–7.30) and epidemiological studies (OR 2.56, 1.24–5.30) [[Porter2020]].
- By subtype, emotional abuse (OR 38.11, 95% CI 25.99–55.88) and neglect (OR 17.73, 13.01–24.17) showed the largest effects [[Porter2020]].
- People with BPD were 3.15 times (95% CI 2.62–3.79) more likely to report childhood adversity than people with other psychiatric diagnoses [[Porter2020]].
- An early meta-analysis of 21 studies found a moderate pooled correlation (r = .279) between childhood sexual abuse and BPD. The authors concluded this did not support sexual abuse as a major or necessary cause of BPD [[Fossati1999]].
- Among 358 inpatients with BPD, 91% reported abuse and 92% reported neglect before 18 [[Zanarini1997]].
- In that study, compared with 109 patients with other personality disorders, those with BPD more often reported a caretaker who withdrew emotionally, treated them inconsistently, denied their thoughts and feelings, made them act as a parent, or failed to protect them [[Zanarini1997]].
- Its authors concluded that sexual abuse is "neither necessary nor sufficient" for BPD, and that neglect by caretakers of both genders is a significant risk factor (Established) [[Zanarini1997]].
- In a later inpatient sample, 26.0% of adolescents (n = 104) and 62.4% of adults (n = 290) with BPD reported childhood sexual abuse/assault before 18. So a large minority of people with BPD report no sexual abuse [[Temes2020]].
- Adults with BPD reported nearly all forms of childhood adversity more often, and more severely, than adolescents with BPD. Adolescents with BPD still reported 5 of 12 adverse experiences more often than healthy peers [[Temes2017]].
- A youth meta-analysis (61 studies, age ≤19) linked youth BPD to sexual abuse (OR 4.88, 95% CI 3.30–7.21), physical abuse (2.79, 2.03–3.84), maternal hostility/verbal abuse (3.28, 2.67–4.03) and neglect (3.40, 2.27–5.11) [[Winsper2016]].
- In the US NESARC (n = 34,653), childhood abuse and neglect stayed associated with cluster A and B personality disorders after adjustment for other adversities, mood, anxiety and substance-use disorders. The adjusted odds ratios were modest (1.22 to 1.63) [[Afifi2011]].

### causes · Prospective cohorts (adversity measured before BPD)
- Children in the Community (639 New York youths followed 1975–1993): people with documented childhood abuse or neglect were more than 4 times as likely to have a personality disorder in early adulthood, after adjusting for age, parental education and parental psychiatric disorder [[Johnson1999]].
- In the same cohort (ages 10–36, four waves), low family socioeconomic status had a modest but robust independent effect on borderline symptoms across the whole age span. The effect remained after accounting for trauma, life events, IQ, poor parenting and comorbidity [[Cohen2008]].
- In a US cohort of 500 children with court-documented abuse or neglect and 396 matched controls followed into adulthood, more abused and/or neglected children met BPD criteria as adults, notably the physically abused and the neglected. Parental substance problems, education and employment, and adult drug abuse, depression and PTSD mediated the link [[Widom2009]].
- ALSPAC (6,050 mother–child pairs): after adjusting for confounders, peer-bullying victims had higher odds of BPD symptoms at about 11.8 years. Odds ratios were 2.82 (95% CI 2.13–3.72) by self-report, 2.43 (1.86–3.16) by mother report and 1.95 (1.34–2.83) by teacher report [[Wolke2012]].
- In ALSPAC, chronic bullying gave an OR of 5.44 (95% CI 3.86–7.66), and combined relational and overt victimisation gave an OR of 7.10 (4.79–10.51) for BPD symptoms at 11 [[Wolke2012]].
- In ALSPAC, family adversity in pregnancy predicted probable BPD symptoms at 11 in a dose–response way: 1–2 adversities OR 1.34 (95% CI 1.01–1.77); more than 2 adversities OR 1.99 (1.34–2.94) [[Winsper2012]].
- In ALSPAC, each point on a suboptimal-parenting index raised the odds of probable BPD symptoms (OR 1.13, 1.05–1.23), as did parent conflict (OR 1.19, 1.06–1.34) [[Winsper2012]].
- Minnesota Longitudinal Study (high-risk sample, N = 162, followed from infancy to 28): adult BPD symptoms were predicted by early temperament, attachment disorganisation and parental hostility, and by disturbance across domains in middle childhood. The effect of attachment disorganisation was mediated by self-representation [[Carlson2009]].
- In a 56-family study followed from infancy, maternal withdrawal observed at 18 months predicted both borderline symptoms and suicidality/self-injury in late adolescence (mean 19.9 years). The effect was independent of, and added to, the severity of childhood abuse [[LyonsRuth2013]].
- Pittsburgh Girls Study (N = 2,451, ages 14–17): BPD symptoms and harsh punishment/low caregiver warmth moved together over time. There was more support for rises in BPD symptoms leading to harsher parenting than the reverse, a two-way (transactional) effect [[Stepp2014]].
- In 498 Pittsburgh parent–daughter pairs, adolescent BPD symptoms at 16 predicted higher parent BPD symptoms at 17. Parent symptoms did not predict later adolescent symptoms after controls [[Kaufman2020]].
- Among 599 low-income children (314 maltreated), maltreated children had higher borderline-feature scores on every subscale. Chronicity of maltreatment (number of developmental periods affected) predicted higher scores [[Hecht2014]].

### causes · Why most maltreated children do not develop BPD, and why many people with BPD report no abuse
- Even in case–control data where the association is strongest, adversity is reported by both groups. The odds ratio drops sharply (13.91 → about 2.6) in population-based and cohort designs, showing that most exposed people do not develop BPD [[Porter2020]].
- In the population-representative NESARC, adjusted odds ratios linking abuse and neglect to cluster B personality disorders were only 1.22–1.63 [[Afifi2011]].
- Retrospective report differs by age group. Sexual abuse was reported by 62.4% of adults but only 26.0% of adolescents with BPD, so a large share of people with BPD report none [[Temes2020]].
- Genetically informative designs show part of the adversity–BPD association is shared genetic risk (gene–environment correlation) rather than a direct effect of the adversity alone [[Bornovalova2013,Distel2011LifeEvents]].
- Temperament can raise exposure to adversity. Dysregulated children in ALSPAC were more often bullied, and that bullying carried part of their later BPD risk [[Winsper2017]].
- Taken together, the best-supported view is that adversity is a significant risk factor in a multifactorial, probabilistic pathway, not a necessary or sufficient cause (Established) [[Zanarini1997,BallLinks2009,Porter2020]].

### causes · Attachment
- A review of 13 attachment studies found that every one reported a strong association between BPD and insecure attachment. Unresolved, preoccupied and fearful attachment were most characteristic (Established association; direction unclear) [[Agrawal2004]].
- Gunderson and Lyons-Ruth proposed an "interpersonal hypersensitivity" phenotype: an inherited reactivity to interpersonal stress that, together with parents' own vulnerabilities, may escalate into disorganised attachment and later BPD (Hypothesis) [[GundersonLyonsRuth2008]].
- In prospective infancy cohorts, disorganised attachment, maternal withdrawal and parental hostility preceded later borderline symptoms [[Carlson2009,LyonsRuth2013]].
- A review of 33 studies found that mothers with BPD or BPD symptoms more often show insensitive, overprotective or hostile parenting. Their children more often show BPD symptoms, internalising and externalising problems, insecure attachment and emotional dysregulation [[Eyden2016]].
- In mothers with BPD, an oscillation between hostile control and passive aloofness may be a distinctive parenting pattern. Psychoeducation, consistent warmth and monitoring, and mindfulness-based parenting are suggested intervention targets (Hypothesis) [[Stepp2012]].

### causes · The biosocial model and the transactional model
- Linehan's biosocial theory holds that BPD arises when a biologically emotionally vulnerable child grows up in an invalidating environment [[Crowell2009]].
- That emotional vulnerability is described as (a) heightened emotional sensitivity, (b) inability to regulate intense emotional responses and (c) slow return to emotional baseline [[Crowell2009]].
- The invalidating environment is characterised by intolerance of a child's expression of private emotional experience, especially emotions not backed by observable events [[Crowell2009]].
- Crowell, Beauchaine and Linehan (2009) extended the model developmentally. They proposed impulsivity as among the earliest traits on the BPD pathway, a position not in Linehan's original theory, and described development as a series of transactions between child and caregiving environment (Hypothesis) [[Crowell2009]].
- Fruzzetti's transactional model describes how invalidating or conflictual family interactions, and the absence of validating ones, transact with the child's own emotional vulnerabilities. The authors frame it as blaming neither the person with BPD nor their parents [[Fruzzetti2005]].
- In a community sample of 186 adults, a path model running from perceived invalidation in close relationships through emotion dysregulation and poor distress tolerance to BPD features explained 38% of the variance in BPD features (Early evidence; cross-sectional) [[Sturrock2014]].
- Prospective data fit the transactional idea. Child dysregulation and harsh parenting influence each other from ages 5 to 14 [[Hallquist2015]], and adolescent BPD symptoms predict later parenting and parental symptoms as much as or more than the reverse [[Stepp2014,Kaufman2020]].

### causes · The mentalizing, attachment and epistemic-trust model
- Fonagy and Luyten's developmental mentalization model proposes that BPD involves a low threshold for activating the attachment system and switching off controlled mentalizing. Difficulty telling apart one's own and others' mental states then leaves the person hypersensitive to others' states (Hypothesis) [[FonagyLuyten2009]].
- The model links these mentalizing impairments to the core features of BPD: affect dysregulation, impulsivity and unstable relationships [[FonagyLuyten2009]].
- Fonagy and colleagues later recast BPD as a prototype of low resilience linked to a general psychopathology ("p") factor (Hypothesis) [[Fonagy2017a]].
- They further proposed that epistemic hypervigilance, distrust or "freezing" (difficulty trusting socially communicated knowledge) can be an adaptive response to an adverse social-learning environment, and this shifted their view of attachment's role (Hypothesis) [[Fonagy2017b]].

### Exam hooks
- Twin heritability of BPD: about .69 in Torgersen 2000 (92 MZ + 129 DZ pairs, interview) vs 42% in Distel 2008 (5,496 twins, self-report, three countries) [[Torgersen2000,Distel2008]].
- Most of the non-genetic variance in BPD is unique (non-shared) environment, not shared family environment [[Distel2008,Skoglund2021]].
- Swedish register: heritability of diagnosed BPD is 46%, and the full-sibling hazard ratio is 4.7 [[Skoglund2021]].
- First-degree relatives of people with BPD: BPD prevalence 14.1% vs 4.9% (risk ratio 2.9) [[Gunderson2011Family]].
- Genetic factors explain 72% of the 10-year stability of BPD traits in adulthood; environmental effects are mostly transient [[ReichbornKjennerud2015]].
- First BPD GWAS (Witt 2017): 998 cases, no single genome-wide significant SNP. Largest GWAS (Streit 2026): 12,339 cases, 11 loci [[Witt2017,Streit2026]].
- SNP heritability of BPD is about 17%, and polygenic scores explain 4.6% of the variance, too little for individual prediction [[Streit2026]].
- BPD's strongest genetic correlations are with PTSD, depression and ADHD [[Streit2026]].
- 5-HTTLPR and other serotonin candidate genes: no association with BPD in meta-analysis [[Calati2013,Amad2014]].
- BPD shares all its genetic liability with normal traits, chiefly high neuroticism and low agreeableness [[Distel2009]].
- Childhood adversity: OR 13.91 for BPD vs non-clinical controls; the largest effects are for emotional abuse and neglect [[Porter2020]].
- Sexual abuse is "neither necessary nor sufficient" for BPD; caretaker neglect is a key risk factor [[Zanarini1997]].
- Bullying by peers (ALSPAC): chronic victimisation gives OR 5.44 for BPD symptoms at age 11 [[Wolke2012]].
- Biosocial model = emotional vulnerability (sensitivity, reactivity, slow return to baseline) × invalidating environment [[Crowell2009]].
- Crowell 2009 places impulsivity as the earliest trait on the developmental pathway [[Crowell2009]].
- Insecure attachment types most linked to BPD: unresolved, preoccupied, fearful [[Agrawal2004]].

### In plain words
- No single thing causes BPD. It usually grows out of a mix of inherited sensitivity and hard life experiences [[Lieb2004,Bohus2021]].
- Genes matter: studies of twins suggest that roughly 40–70% of the differences between people in BPD traits are linked to genes [[Torgersen2000,Distel2008,Skoglund2021]].
- There is no "BPD gene." Thousands of tiny genetic differences each add a very small amount of risk [[Streit2026]].
- A gene test cannot tell you whether you or your child will develop BPD [[Streit2026]].
- Many people with BPD went through abuse, neglect or bullying as children, and these experiences raise the risk [[Porter2020,Wolke2012]].
- But many people with BPD did not experience abuse, and most children who are hurt do not develop BPD [[Zanarini1997,Temes2020,Porter2020]].
- Some children are born more emotionally sensitive or more impulsive. That is a difference in temperament, not a fault [[Crowell2009,Stepp2017]].
- When a sensitive child's feelings are often dismissed or punished, and the child reacts more strongly in turn, both sides can get stuck in a painful loop [[Crowell2009,Fruzzetti2005]].
- These models do not blame the person with BPD or their family. They describe how the two sides affect each other over time [[Fruzzetti2005]].
- Teen BPD symptoms can even change how parents feel and act. Influence runs both ways [[Stepp2014,Kaufman2020]].
- BPD traits usually ease with age, from the teens into adulthood [[Bornovalova2009,ReichbornKjennerud2015]].
- Knowing the causes helps point care toward skills for handling strong emotions, and toward support for families [[Crowell2009,Stepp2012]].

## Open questions and controversies
- How big is heritability? Interview-based twin data gave .69 in a moderate sample [[Torgersen2000]]. Larger self-report and register studies give 42–46% [[Distel2008,Skoglund2021]], and Norwegian interview data give 35% [[Torgersen2008]]. Status: a moderate (~40–50%) estimate is best supported. Measurement, sample and age explain much of the spread.
- Does shared family environment matter? Most adult twin and register models drop shared environment [[Distel2008,Skoglund2021,Torgersen2008]]. A modest shared-environment effect appears at age 14 and fades by adulthood [[Bornovalova2009]], and Torgersen could not exclude it for BPD [[Torgersen2000]]. Status: unresolved for childhood. Note that "unique environment" includes experiences like abuse that siblings do not share.
- Is childhood abuse a cause? For: MZ-twin difference analyses in E-Risk show an environmentally mediated effect of harsh treatment [[Belsky2012]], prospective cohorts show raised risk [[Johnson1999,Widom2009]], and Hill's criteria are judged met [[BallLinks2009]]. Against: a discordant-twin study found the abuse–BPD link mostly genetic [[Bornovalova2013]], and effect sizes shrink in population designs [[Porter2020,Afifi2011]]. Status: adversity is a risk factor within a multifactorial model. The size of its direct causal share is debated.
- How much do retrospective reports bias the picture? Case–control ORs (13.91) far exceed cohort and epidemiological ORs (~2.6) [[Porter2020]]. Adults report more and more severe abuse than adolescents with BPD [[Temes2017,Temes2020]]. Status: recall, selection and severity differences are all possible explanations, and none is settled.
- Is BPD genetically distinct or part of a general liability? BPD shares its genetic liability with normal traits [[Distel2009]] and with PTSD, depression and ADHD [[Streit2026,KujaHalkola2021]], and it loads on a broad genetic factor shared across personality disorders [[Kendler2008]]. A specific BPD/antisocial factor also exists [[Kendler2008]]. Status: open; it matters for ICD-11's dimensional approach (see pass D).
- Gene × environment interaction: Twin evidence supports G×E and rGE [[Distel2011LifeEvents]]. Specific molecular G×E findings (OXTR, FKBP5) come from single studies [[Cicchetti2014]], and the candidate G×E approach failed broadly in depression [[Border2019]]. Status: (Hypothesis) for specific genes.
- Epigenetics: NR3C1 methylation is linked to maltreatment severity in BPD [[Perroud2011]], but a 2025 systematic review finds inconsistent results across 19 small studies [[Dawidowski2025]]. Status: (Early evidence).
- Sex differences: SNP heritability is higher in females than males in the preprint [[Streit2025medRxiv]], but twin studies found similar heritability in men and women [[Distel2008]]. Status: unresolved. Diagnostic ascertainment bias toward women (85.4% of Swedish diagnoses) may contribute [[Skoglund2021]].
- Ancestry: the largest GWAS includes only people of European ancestry [[Streit2026]]. Status: findings may not transfer to other populations.

## Provenance
- All 65 sources were validated against PubMed E-utilities (esummary). Authors, title, journal, year, volume, pages, PMID, PMCID and DOI were taken directly from the PubMed record by script. None are unverified.
- Every number was read in the PubMed abstract (efetch). Some come from the open-access PMC full text (NCBI BioC or Europe PMC):
  - Skoglund 2021: Swedish cohort size and sex split (Table 1)
  - Gunderson 2011 family study: relatives' prevalence of 14.1% vs 4.9%
  - Bornovalova 2009: test–retest r, mean scores at ages 14 and 24, intercept and slope heritability, age-specific pattern
  - Belsky 2012: MZ/DZ twin correlations and concordance
  - Hecht 2014: sample composition
  - Streit 2025 medRxiv preprint (PMC12363718): rg values, decile ORs, sex-specific SNP heritability, FOXP2
- The Streit 2026 Nature Genetics full text (PMC13447106) could not be retrieved through either service. Its headline numbers come from the PubMed abstract. Detail numbers are attributed to the preprint and should be re-checked against the final paper before quoting as definitive.
- Widom 2009 (J Pers Disord) was paywalled. Only the direction of effect from the abstract is used.
- Linehan's 1993 book has no PubMed/Crossref journal record. Its theory is cited through Crowell 2009 rather than as a separate (unverified) source.
- Candidate-gene evidence for the serotonin transporter (5-HTTLPR) comes from two meta-analyses [Calati2013; Amad2014]. Border 2019 is a depression study, used only to illustrate why candidate-gene findings generally failed.
- Dropped: parent–offspring risk ratios (not reported in Skoglund 2021); numerical BPD rates from Widom 2009 (abstract has none); a practitioner review (Sharp & Fonagy 2015) read but not needed.
- Keys Fonagy2017a, Fonagy2017b, Gunderson2018 and Bohus2021 match the lines already used in pass C (C-mechanisms-neuro.md). Gunderson2011Family is used for the 2011 McLean family study, so that it does not clash with the canonical Gunderson2011 (the CLPS 10-year course paper, not cited here).
