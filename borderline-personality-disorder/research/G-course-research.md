## Findings

### course · The two big cohorts (what was studied)
- MSAD enrolled 290 inpatients who met both DIB-R and DSM-III-R criteria for borderline personality disorder (BPD), plus 72 inpatients with other personality disorders as comparison subjects. All were recruited at McLean Hospital between June 1992 and December 1995 and reassessed every 2 years [[Temes2019,Zanarini2012]].
- At baseline, 77.1% of MSAD participants were female and 87% were white. Their mean age was 27 years (SD 6.3), and their mean GAF score was 39.8, indicating major impairment. The cohort was severely ill, which limits how far its numbers apply to outpatients or community samples [[Zanarini2012]].
- Retention in MSAD was high: 87.5% of surviving participants with BPD (231 of 264) were reinterviewed at all eight follow-up waves to 16 years [[Zanarini2012]].
- The Collaborative Longitudinal Personality Disorders Study (CLPS) followed treatment-seeking patients aged 18–45 years at 19 hospital and outpatient settings in 4 northeastern US cities: 175 with BPD, 312 with cluster C personality disorders and 95 with major depressive disorder (MDD) and no personality disorder [[Gunderson2011]].
- CLPS found personality disorders more stable than MDD, but meaningful improvement was "possible and not uncommon." It proposed that personality disorders are hybrids of stable traits and intermittently expressed symptomatic behaviours (Established for the observation, Hypothesis for the hybrid model) [[Skodol2005CLPS]].

### course · Symptomatic remission is common
- In MSAD, 34.5% of people with BPD met remission criteria at 2 years, 49.4% at 4 years and 68.6% at 6 years, and 73.5% remitted at some point over the 6 years [[Zanarini2003Course]].
- Over those 6 years, only 5.9% of people who remitted had a recurrence [[Zanarini2003Course]].
- No comparison subject with another personality disorder developed BPD during 6 years of follow-up [[Zanarini2003Course]].
- By 10 years, 88% of MSAD participants with BPD had achieved remission [[Zanarini2006Prediction]].
- Among the 242 people who remitted by 10 years, 39.3% first remitted by the 2-year follow-up, a further 22.3% by 4 years, 21.9% by 6 years, 12.8% by 8 years and 3.7% by 10 years [[Zanarini2006Prediction]].
- Seven baseline factors independently predicted earlier remission: younger age, no childhood sexual abuse, no family history of substance use disorder, a good vocational record, no anxious-cluster personality disorder, low neuroticism and high agreeableness [[Zanarini2006Prediction]].
- By 10 years, 93% had a symptomatic remission lasting at least 2 years and 86% had a sustained remission lasting at least 4 years [[Zanarini2010Recovery]].
- By 16 years, cumulative remission ranged from 99% for remissions lasting 2 years to 78% for remissions lasting 8 years. The comparison group with other personality disorders reached 97–99% [[Zanarini2012]].
- People with BPD remitted more slowly than comparison subjects. For each length of remission, about 30% of people with BPD remitted at the first possible time point, against about 85% of comparison subjects (hazard ratio for a 2-year remission 0.15, 95% CI 0.08–0.26) [[Zanarini2012]].
- By 24 years, sustained remission rates for BPD were 77%–100%, depending on how long the remission had to last. Comparison subjects reached 97%–100% [[Zanarini2024Remission]].
- In CLPS, 85% of patients with BPD remitted over 10 years. Remission was slower than for MDD (P < .001) and only slightly slower than for other personality disorders (P < .03) [[Gunderson2011]].
- A meta-analysis of 11 prospective studies (837 participants, 9 countries, follow-up of 5 years or more) found that 50%–70% of people with BPD reached long-term remission. Younger age was associated with a higher chance of remission [[AlvarezTomas2019Meta]].
- In the Montreal 27-year follow-up of 64 patients, only 5 still met BPD criteria [[Paris2001]].
- A systematic review of 19 studies (11 cohorts, 1,122 people) concluded that symptomatic remission is common and that recurrence after remission is unlikely, but that recovery including meaningful work was seldom described [[Ng2016Recovery]].
- Within CLPS, 18 of 160 patients improved dramatically within 6 months, falling to 2 or fewer criteria, and only 1 had relapsed by 2 years. Situational change (n = 10) and remission of a co-occurring Axis I disorder (n = 7) were judged the main drivers [[Gunderson2003Remissions]].
- After 2 years of CLPS follow-up, 50%–61% of the personality disorder groups had dropped below the diagnostic threshold, but only 23%–38% met a stricter definition of improvement [[Grilo2004CLPS]].

### course · Recurrence is low after remission and lowest after long remissions
- At 10 years in MSAD, 30% of people with a 2-year remission had a symptomatic recurrence, compared with 15% of those with a sustained (4-year) remission [[Zanarini2010Recovery]].
- By 16 years, recurrence was 36% after a 2-year remission and 10% after an 8-year remission. Comparison subjects ranged from 7% to 4% [[Zanarini2012]].
- After a 2-year remission, recurrence among people with BPD was 16% at 2 years, 21% at 4 years, 30% at 6 years, 33% at 8 years, 34% at 10 years and 36% at 12 and 14 years after the first remission [[Zanarini2012]].
- People with BPD had recurrences faster than comparison subjects after a 2-year remission (HR 6.87, 95% CI 2.45–18.69) [[Zanarini2012]].
- By 24 years, recurrence was 11%–40% for BPD, against 5%–10% for other personality disorders [[Zanarini2024Remission]].
- In CLPS, 12% of patients with BPD relapsed over 10 years. This was less frequent and slower than relapse in MDD (P < .001) and in other personality disorders (P = .008) [[Gunderson2011]].

### course · Recovery (remission plus good social and vocational functioning) is harder
- In MSAD, recovery meant symptomatic remission plus good social and vocational functioning during the previous 2 years. By 10 years, 50% of participants had achieved recovery [[Zanarini2010Recovery]].
- Of those who recovered by 10 years, 34% lost their recovery during follow-up [[Zanarini2010Recovery]].
- By 16 years, cumulative recovery was 60% for recoveries lasting 2 years and 40% for recoveries lasting 8 years. Comparison subjects reached 85% and 75% [[Zanarini2012]].
- Recovery lasting 2 years rose from 14% at the 2-year follow-up to 27% at 4 years, 36% at 6, 43% at 8, 47% at 10, 50% at 12, 56% at 14 and 60% at 16 years [[Zanarini2012]].
- Loss of recovery by 16 years ranged from 44% after a 2-year recovery to 20% after an 8-year recovery. For comparison subjects the range was 28% to 9%, and the between-group difference was not statistically significant [[Zanarini2012]].
- By 24 years, recovery was 37%–60% for BPD and 68%–89% for other personality disorders. Loss of recovery (29%–59%) was more common than symptomatic recurrence (11%–40%) [[Zanarini2024Remission]].
- "Excellent recovery" means remission, good social functioning, full-time work or study, and no impairing Axis I disorder. By 20 years, 39% of people with BPD and 73% of comparison subjects had reached it [[Zanarini2018Excellent]].
- Higher IQ, good childhood work history, good adult vocational record, lower neuroticism and higher agreeableness predicted excellent recovery [[Zanarini2018Excellent]].
- Over 90% of poor psychosocial functioning over 10 years was due to poor vocational, not social, performance [[Zanarini2010Psychosocial]].
- CLPS found severe and persistent impairment in social functioning with only modest improvement over 10 years, even as BPD criteria declined. Falls in criteria count predicted later GAF improvement (P < .001) [[Gunderson2011]].
- On average, 47.2% of MSAD participants with BPD received Social Security Disability Insurance across 24 years, three times the rate in the comparison group. By 24 years, 46% had come off it at some point, and 85% of those later went back on (Established within this cohort) [[Kramer2023SSDI]].
- Recovered participants with BPD were more likely than non-recovered participants to have married or lived with a partner and to have become parents. They were less likely to have divorced or lost custody of a child [[Zanarini2015Marriage]].
- Social isolation persisted over 20 years in "a sizable minority" of people with BPD. It was predicted by lower childhood competence, lower extraversion and lower agreeableness [[Pucker2019Isolation]].
- The adult meta-analysis found significant falls in depression and functional impairment, but only slight functional improvement in the long term. Being female was associated with less functional improvement [[AlvarezTomas2019Meta]].
- Lived-experience accounts describe recovery as non-linear and built on hope, active engagement, engagement with services, and meaningful activities and relationships (qualitative study, 14 women) [[Ng2019LivedRecovery]].

### course · Acute versus temperamental symptoms (which symptoms fade first)
- Over 6 years, impulsive symptoms resolved fastest, affective symptoms were the most chronic, and cognitive and interpersonal symptoms were in between [[Zanarini2003Course]].
- At 10 years, 12 of 24 symptoms had fallen sharply and were reported by fewer than 15% of the participants who reported them at baseline. The other 12 declined substantially but less dramatically [[Zanarini2007Subsyndromal]].
- Impulsive symptoms (self-harm, suicide attempts) and active attempts to manage interpersonal difficulties (demandingness, treatment regressions) resolved fastest. Chronic dysphoria (anger, loneliness and emptiness) and abandonment or dependency concerns were the most stable [[Zanarini2007Subsyndromal]].
- By 16 years, the 12 acute symptoms were more likely than the 12 temperamental symptoms to remit for 2 or 4 years, and less likely to recur afterwards [[Zanarini2016Fluidity]].
- By 16 years, 2-year remission of individual symptoms ranged from 62% to 100% (median 93%). Four-year remission ranged from 34% to 100% (median 74%) [[Zanarini2016Fluidity]].
- Cumulative 2-year remission by 16 years: self-harm 97%, help-seeking suicide attempts 99%, affective instability 96%, serious identity disturbance 99%, chronic/major depression 72%, chronic anger 67%, chronic loneliness/emptiness 71%, chronic anxiety 62% [[Zanarini2016Fluidity]].
- Cumulative 4-year remission by 16 years: self-harm 91%, affective instability 73%, chronic depression 43%, chronic loneliness/emptiness 42%, chronic anger 38%, chronic anxiety 34% [[Zanarini2016Fluidity]].
- Recurrence after a 2-year remission was lowest for impulsive symptoms (self-harm 43%, help-seeking suicide attempts 55%). It was highest for temperamental symptoms (chronic anger 98%, chronic anxiety 93%, chronic loneliness/emptiness 90%). Median recurrence across all 24 symptoms was 75% [[Zanarini2016Fluidity]].
- The authors' summary: BPD symptoms are fluid, with common remissions and recurrences, but the more urgent acute symptoms have a better prognosis than the temperamental ones [[Zanarini2016Fluidity]].
- CLPS found that affective instability and anger were the most prevalent and least changeable BPD criteria over 2 years, while self-injury and frantic efforts to avoid abandonment changed most [[McGlashan2005CLPS]].
- In CLPS, unlike MSAD, all BPD criteria declined at similar rates over 10 years [[Gunderson2011]].
- Behavioural interpersonal features (recurrent breakups, demandingness, treatment regressions, boundary violations) remitted quickly and were rare by 10 years. Distress at being alone and conflicts over dependency and care were the slowest to remit [[ChoiKain2010Interpersonal]].
- Dysphoric affective and cognitive states fell significantly over 10 years but remained an area of vulnerability. They fell faster in people who recovered [[Reed2012Dysphoria]].
- Emptiness was more severe in BPD at baseline but fell over 24 years at a similar rate to the comparison group. A baseline history of PTSD and fewer childhood competencies predicted more severe emptiness [[Hein2025Emptiness]].
- Dissociation fell significantly over 24 years, with a steeper decline in BPD than in the comparison group. Childhood sexual abuse severity, adult rape, adult partner violence and IQ predicted dissociation severity [[Niesten2024Dissociation]].

### course · Adolescence and young adulthood
- BPD is as reliable and valid in adolescents as in adults, and the diagnosis is recognised in classification systems and national guidelines [[Kaess2014Adolescence]].
- A 2025 research review supports diagnosing BPD from age 12, with validated measures available. It calls the evidence for youth psychotherapy promising but limited by few and small RCTs with high risk of bias [[Kaess2025Review]].
- In the community Children in the Community cohort, higher borderline symptoms at a mean age of 14 predicted lower role function, social function and life satisfaction over the next 20 years, independent of adolescent Axis I disorders [[Winograd2008]].
- Those adolescent symptoms also predicted lower academic and occupational attainment, less partner involvement, fewer adult milestones, and adult BPD symptoms and need for services at a mean age of 33 [[Winograd2008]].
- A review of the lifetime course: BPD can be accurately identified in adolescence, and symptoms decline over time in both adolescents and adults, while functional recovery is less consistent [[Biskin2015Course]].
- In the MOBY trial (139 people aged 15–25 years with first-presentation BPD), adolescents (15–17 years) and young adults (18–25 years) showed substantial similarities on 20 key aspects of presentation. The authors argue that detection and intervention should not wait until age 18 [[Chanen2022Age]].
- BPD typically starts in adolescence (after age 12) and is often preceded by, or develops alongside, internalising or externalising problems [[Bohus2021]].
- Continued non-suicidal self-injury from early adolescence (ages 11–13) to ages 18–20 was associated with more BPD symptoms and worse functioning, mediated by emotion regulation deficits (community study, 69 adolescents with self-injury, 61 controls) [[Biskin2021NSSI]].

### course · Comorbidity: how often and how it changes
- In NESARC, 84.8% of adults with BPD had a lifetime anxiety disorder, 82.7% a lifetime mood disorder or episode and 78.2% a lifetime substance use disorder [[Tomko2014]].
- Co-occurrence of mood and anxiety disorders with BPD was high in NESARC Wave 2. After controlling for other comorbidity, the strongest associations were with bipolar disorder and with schizotypal and narcissistic personality disorders (odds ratios ≥ 4.3) [[Grant2008]].
- In NESARC, 30.2% of adults with BPD also had PTSD, and 24.2% of adults with PTSD also had BPD. People with both had poorer quality of life and higher odds of a lifetime suicide attempt than those with either alone [[Pagura2010PTSD]].
- In the NCS-R, all personality disorder clusters were comorbid with a wide range of Axis I disorders, and much of the impairment linked to personality disorders was accounted for by that comorbidity [[Lenzenweger2007]].
- About half of people with BPD have at least one current substance use disorder, most often alcohol use disorder, and about 25% of people with a current substance use disorder meet criteria for BPD (review of 70 studies, 2000–2017) [[Trull2018SUD]].
- A 2025 meta-analysis put eating disorders in 29.7% of people with BPD (95% CI 21.6%–38.4%): anorexia nervosa 9.98%, bulimia nervosa 16.3%, binge eating disorder 16.3% and EDNOS 18.8%. The certainty of this evidence is low [[Paudex2025ED]].
- In the 1998 MSAD baseline sample (379 inpatients with BPD), anxiety disorders were almost as common as mood disorders and separated BPD from other personality disorders better. PTSD was common but not universal [[Zanarini1998AxisI]].
- In that sample, substance use disorders were more common among men with BPD and eating disorders among women with BPD. A lifetime mix of an affect disorder plus an impulse disorder ("complex comorbidity") strongly predicted the BPD diagnosis [[Zanarini1998AxisI]].
- Over 6 years, Axis I disorders declined in people with BPD but stayed high, especially mood and anxiety disorders [[Zanarini2004AxisI]].
- People whose BPD remitted had a substantial decline in all comorbid disorders, while those whose BPD did not remit kept stable rates of comorbidity [[Zanarini2004AxisI,Shah2018Comorbidity]].
- Absence of a substance use disorder was a far stronger predictor of BPD remission than absence of PTSD, mood, other anxiety or eating disorders [[Zanarini2004AxisI]].
- Over 24 years, all 5 classes of symptomatic disorders were more common in BPD than in comparison subjects, but they declined at similar rates in both groups. Absence of substance use disorders was the strongest predictor of recovery (relative risk ratio 2.53, P < .001) [[Zanarini2024Comorbid]].
- Over 10 years, more than 90% of people with BPD who had a substance use disorder at baseline remitted from it. Recurrences (35–40%) and new onsets (21–23%) were less common [[Zanarini2011SUD]].
- Over 24 years, remission from alcohol use disorder in BPD was 64%–91% and from drug use disorder 71%–100%. Recurrence was not uncommon (alcohol 24%–63%, drugs 28%–67%), while new onsets were rarer (alcohol 5%–27%, drugs 5%–21%) [[Zanarini2025SUD]].
- Over 10 years, more than 90% of people with BPD who had anorexia, bulimia or EDNOS at baseline had a stable remission, but migration between eating disorders was common. EDNOS recurred in 52% and newly began in 43% [[Zanarini2010Eating]].
- Anxiety disorders other than PTSD declined over 10 years but remained more common in BPD and followed an intermittent course, with high remission and moderate recurrence and new onset [[Silverman2012Anxiety]].
- In CLPS, BPD and MDD slowed each other's remission (BPD on MDD P = .0004; MDD on BPD P = .0002) and sped each other's relapse. BPD and bipolar disorders were largely independent, except that bipolar II lengthened time to BPD remission (P = .0085) [[Gunderson2014Mood]].
- The CLPS authors argue that the BPD–MDD interaction supports prioritising treatment of BPD, while bipolar disorders need their own treatment (Early evidence for the prioritisation advice) [[Gunderson2014Mood]].
- BPD improvement predicted remission of generalised anxiety disorder (HR 0.65) and PTSD (HR 0.57), and BPD worsening predicted relapse of social phobia (HR 1.87). Worsening PTSD predicted BPD relapse (HR 1.90) [[Keuroghlian2015Anxiety]].

### course · Physical health
- At 6 years, people whose BPD had remitted were less likely than those whose BPD had not to report a "syndrome-like" condition (chronic fatigue, fibromyalgia, temporomandibular joint syndrome), obesity, osteoarthritis, diabetes, hypertension, back pain or urinary incontinence [[Frankenburg2004Medical]].
- Non-remitted participants were also more likely to smoke a pack a day, drink daily, not exercise, use sleep medication daily and use pain medication long term, and to have had a medical emergency visit or medical hospitalisation [[Frankenburg2004Medical]].
- At 6 years, 74 of 264 MSAD participants with BPD (28%) were obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²). Chronic PTSD, lack of exercise, family history of obesity and recent psychotropic polypharmacy were risk factors [[Frankenburg2006Obesity]].
- Over a further decade, participants who never recovered were more likely than those who ever recovered to have a medical syndrome, obesity, osteoarthritis, diabetes, urinary incontinence or multiple medical conditions. They also used more emergency care, hospitalisation and imaging [[Keuroghlian2013Medical]].
- People with BPD who did not recover were significantly more physically inactive than those who recovered, with inactivity stable over 12 years. Obesity and PTSD predicted inactivity [[Glass2024Inactivity]].
- In NESARC, BPD was associated with cardiovascular disease among younger adults and with arthritis and gastrointestinal conditions, after adjustment for sociodemographics and past-year mood, anxiety and substance use disorders [[Quirk2015Physical]].
- In a Spanish 10-year cohort (41 people), 68% reported physical health problems and 32% multiple illnesses. BPD remission was independently linked to better quality of life, while somatic disease worsened quality of life in those not remitted [[AlvarezTomas2024Health]].
- BPD is associated with substantial mental and physical disability in the general population, especially among women [[Grant2008]].

### course · Mortality (population statistics)
- Over 24 years in MSAD, 5.9% of people with BPD and 1.4% of comparison subjects died by suicide. A further 14.0% and 5.5% died of other causes [[Temes2019]].
- In MSAD, the number of prior psychiatric hospitalisations predicted death by suicide (HR 1.62). Male sex (HR 3.56) and more prior hospitalisations (HR 2.93) predicted premature death from other causes [[Temes2019]].
- Most people with BPD in MSAD who died, by suicide (87.5%) or other causes (88%), had not recovered before death. The authors conclude that people who did not recover carried a disproportionately higher risk of early death [[Temes2019]].
- In the Danish nationwide register (10,545 people with a first hospital diagnosis of BPD, 1995–2011, mean follow-up 7.98 years), 547 deaths occurred. The standardised mortality ratio was 8.3 (95% CI 7.6–9.1) [[Kjaer2020Mortality]].
- In that register, more than three inpatient admissions a year and a comorbid substance use disorder were associated with higher mortality [[Kjaer2020Mortality]].
- In a south London register of people with any primary personality disorder in secondary care (n = 1,836), life expectancy at birth was 63.3 years for women and 59.1 years for men, 18.7 and 17.7 years shorter than the general population (SMR 4.2, 95% CI 3.03–5.64) [[Fok2012LifeExpectancy]].
- In the long-term course meta-analysis, mean suicide rates ranged from 2% to 5% [[AlvarezTomas2019Meta]].
- In the Montreal cohort followed for a mean of 27 years, 10.3% of the original cohort had died by suicide and 18.2% had died of any cause [[Paris2001]].
- Personality disorder in general is associated with premature mortality and suicide and is under-recognised in clinical practice [[Tyrer2015Lancet]].

### course · Treatment use over time
- Use of 13 of 16 treatment modalities fell significantly over the first 8 years of MSAD, but use of 15 of 16 did not fall further over the second 8 years [[Zanarini2015Treatment]].
- Most MSAD participants with BPD had received multiple treatments before and during the study, so the MSAD course reflects treated, not untreated, illness [[Zanarini2003Course]].
- Most people gradually reach symptomatic remission, and evidence-based psychosocial treatment can speed it up, but many keep persistent social disability [[Gunderson2018]].

### frontier · Early intervention for young people
- Early intervention fits BPD well: the disorder is common in clinical practice, among the most functionally disabling mental disorders, often linked to help-seeking, and responsive to treatment, and BPD traits are malleable in youth (Established for the rationale) [[Chanen2013Prevention]].
- In the HYPE trial (86 outpatients aged 15–18 with 2–9 BPD criteria), up to 24 sessions of cognitive analytic therapy (CAT) did not differ from manualised good clinical care at 24 months. Both were delivered within a comprehensive service model, and there was some evidence that CAT worked faster [[Chanen2008HYPE]].
- The MOBY trial randomised 139 people aged 15–25 years with recent BPD to HYPE + CAT, HYPE + befriending, or a general youth mental health service + befriending (ACTRN12610000100099) [[Chanen2022MOBY]].
- In MOBY, psychosocial functioning improved by a mean of 19.3%–23.8% from baseline to 12 months in all groups. Neither the service model nor the psychotherapy gave a superior rate of change in functioning (Established for this trial) [[Chanen2022MOBY]].
- The HYPE model improved engagement: median 22 vs 3 contacts and treatment completion of 47.8% (44 of 92) vs 19.2% (9 of 47) for the general youth service [[Chanen2022MOBY]].
- HYPE + CAT also beat befriending on attendance (median 12 vs 3 sessions) and completion (52.2% vs 31.2%) [[Chanen2022MOBY]].
- The MOBY authors conclude that effective early intervention needs youth-oriented case management and psychiatric care, not necessarily specialist psychotherapy [[Chanen2022MOBY]].
- A within-trial cost-utility analysis of MOBY found no significant differences in costs or quality-adjusted life years between arms. At A$96,000 per QALY, the probability of being cost-effective was 36.4% (HYPE + CAT), 30.9% (HYPE + befriending) and 32.7% (general service) [[Lee2026MOBY]].
- A Swiss stepped-care clinic for adolescents offered everyone a brief intervention first. Over 2 years, the 74 who needed nothing more had fewer BPD criteria and better functioning than the 17 who were eligible for, but declined, DBT for adolescents. The study did not show an added benefit of DBT for adolescents, possibly because it lacked statistical power (Early evidence) [[Cavelti2024Stepped]].
- Indicated prevention aimed at subthreshold BPD symptoms "shows promise." Selective and universal prevention still need research (Early evidence) [[Kaess2025Review]].
- A one-day Good Psychiatric Management for Adolescents (GPM-A) workshop for 32 child and adolescent psychiatry staff improved willingness to disclose a BPD diagnosis, reduced negative attitudes and increased confidence, with effects lasting 6 months (Early evidence, uncontrolled) [[Usher2024GPMA]].

### frontier · Shorter and simpler treatments
- In a non-inferiority RCT in Canada (240 suicidal or self-harming adults with BPD), 6 months of comprehensive DBT was non-inferior to 12 months for total self-harm at 6, 12 and 24 months. At 24 months the difference was 0.12 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.36) against a margin of −1.25 (Established for this trial) [[McMain2022]].
- In the same trial, 6 months of DBT gave faster falls in BPD symptoms and general psychopathology, with no difference in dropout [[McMain2022]].
- In a Swiss RCT (76 participants), brief Good Psychiatric Management (10 sessions over 4 months) did not differ from treatment as usual on total ZAN-BPD (d = 0.04). It did reduce relationship problems more (d = 0.52) and improved retention (NCT03717818; Early evidence) [[Kramer2024GPM]].
- In a Montreal stepped-care programme (479 short-term and 145 extended-care patients), a 12-week treatment reduced symptoms. Among patients in extended care, 6–12 months gave the same size of change as 18–24 months (naturalistic, no control group; Early evidence) [[Laporte2018Stepped]].
- Compared with treatment as usual, psychotherapy reduced BPD symptom severity (SMD −0.52, 95% CI −0.70 to −0.33; 22 trials, 1,244 participants, moderate-quality evidence). Subgroup analysis found no clear difference between psychotherapy types [[Storebo2020]].

### frontier · Digital and app-based help
- Fewer than 25% of patients with BPD receive guideline-adherent psychotherapy, which motivates digital tools [[Assmann2025priovi]].
- In the EPADIP-BPD trial (580 adults in Germany), the unguided schema-therapy-based digital therapeutic priovi plus usual care improved BSL-23 scores more than usual care plus self-help material at 3 months (d = 0.24, 95% CI 0.07–0.42) [[Assmann2025priovi]].
- In the same trial, suicide attempts were fewer with priovi (7 vs 21; incidence rate ratio 0.34, 95% CI 0.14–0.79). People with BPD helped develop the program but not design the study (Early evidence) [[Assmann2025priovi]].
- An earlier RCT of priovi (204 participants, 12 months) found no significant difference in the BPDSI slope versus care as usual (between-group d = 0.27, 95% CI 0.00–0.55; NCT03418142) [[Klein2021priovi]].

### frontier · Brain stimulation (TMS and theta-burst)
- A 2021 systematic review found 8 small studies (63 patients in total) of repetitive TMS in BPD. It was well tolerated, but efficacy was not established (Early evidence) [[Konstantinou2021rTMS]].
- In a sham-controlled pilot of intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, added to 8 weeks of inpatient DBT (53 randomised, 40 analysed), iTBS had no significant effect on BSL-23. There was a non-significant trend (d = 0.23), and DBT itself gave large within-group change (d = 0.89–1.12) (Early evidence, null primary) [[Kujovic2025iTBS]].
- In a double-blind sham-controlled trial of 15 sessions of right-DLPFC 10-Hz rTMS in 30 women with BPD, active and sham groups did not differ over 6 months [[Horky2026rTMS]].
- In an 11-person proof-of-concept study, one session of iTBS to an individually targeted right ventrolateral prefrontal site reduced impulsivity on a delay-discounting task without serious adverse events (Hypothesis-generating) [[Tadayonnejad2026rTMS]].
- Registered trials still running include rTMS-enhanced DBT (Masaryk University, NCT06259851, active, not recruiting, est. n = 60) [[NCT06259851]].
- Also running: one-day accelerated iTBS plus D-cycloserine in suicidal patients with BPD (McLean, NCT07460947, recruiting, est. n = 20) [[NCT07460947]].
- Also running: rTMS-enhanced cognitive restructuring for transdiagnostic emotional dysregulation (Duke, NCT05712057, recruiting, est. n = 240) [[NCT05712057]].

### frontier · Oxytocin (lab effects, no clinical benefit shown)
- One intranasal dose of oxytocin (26 IU) normalised faster eye movements toward angry faces and amygdala over-activation in 40 unmedicated women with BPD (laboratory measures; Early evidence) [[Bertsch2013Oxytocin]].
- Oxytocin decreased trust and cooperation in a social dilemma game in people with BPD, driven by anxiously attached, rejection-sensitive participants. This shows oxytocin does not uniformly help (Early evidence) [[Bartz2011Oxytocin]].
- In 51 women with BPD, a single 24 IU dose increased affective empathy and approach motivation compared with placebo [[Domes2019Oxytocin]].
- A single 24 IU dose strengthened avoidance of angry faces in women with BPD on an approach–avoidance task [[Schneider2020Oxytocin]].
- In 53 unmedicated women with BPD, oxytocin did not change resting heart-rate variability, which was reduced in BPD (null result) [[Back2022Oxytocin]].
- A 2024 systematic review of 70 articles concluded that oxytocin's role in BPD treatment "remains uncertain." Some studies suggest benefit for social threat avoidance, while others show adverse effects on nonverbal behaviour and mentalizing [[DiGiacomo2024Oxytocin]].
- A completed oxytocin trial in BPD (n = 138, completed April 2022) has no results posted on ClinicalTrials.gov [[NCT01243658]].
- An RCT of oxytocin plus self-compassion training is listed as recruiting, with an estimated n of 80 and completion in August 2026 (Barcelona, NCT07551882) [[NCT07551882]].

### frontier · Ketamine, psilocybin, MDMA (very early)
- In the first RCT of ketamine in BPD (single 0.5 mg/kg infusion, n = 10, vs midazolam, n = 12), suicidal ideation and depression fell numerically but not significantly versus midazolam. Socio-occupational functioning improved more at day 14 (P = .03) [[Fineberg2023Ketamine]].
- That trial (NCT03395314) was terminated at 22 participants [[NCT03395314,Fineberg2023Ketamine]].
- Dissociation during infusion was more intense with ketamine than midazolam but resolved within 40 minutes [[Fineberg2023Ketamine]].
- A 2025 systematic review (9 studies, n = 281) found that people with depression and comorbid BPD or BPD traits responded to ketamine or esketamine about as well as those without. It advises caution because of case reports of suicidal ideation and self-harm after stopping, and a possibly higher risk of acute dissociation (Early evidence) [[Scott2025Ketamine]].
- Ketamine trials in BPD are planned or running. A Toulouse pilot (NCT07099534, est. n = 38) gives two infusions 24 hours apart and is not yet recruiting [[NCT07099534]].
- A phase II midazolam-controlled trial (NCT07569198, est. n = 120, recruiting) combines ketamine with DBT for suicidality in treatment-resistant depression plus BPD [[NCT07569198]].
- In an open-label single-dose psilocybin study of 9 adults with BPD and MDD (NCT05399498), depression scores fell from 28.56 to 17.22 (Cohen d = 1.41), but BPD scores did not change significantly (Hypothesis-generating) [[Grant2026Psilocybin,NCT05399498]].
- A phase 1 trial of psilocybin-assisted therapy added to DBT (ADEPT, University of Minnesota, NCT07833761, est. n = 18) is not yet recruiting, with a planned start in December 2026 [[NCT07833761]].
- A Yale phase 2 study of MDMA's effects on social cognition in BPD (NCT06683014, est. n = 10) is recruiting [[NCT06683014]].

### frontier · Clozapine and memantine
- The CALMED trial aimed to recruit 222 inpatients with severe BPD for clozapine (up to 400 mg/day) or placebo. It closed early, with 29 participants, because of poor recruitment and COVID-19 (ISRCTN18352058) [[Crawford2022CALMED]].
- In CALMED, the adjusted difference in ZAN-BPD at 6 months was −3.86 (95% CI −10.04 to 2.32). The authors state they "recruited insufficient participants to test the primary hypothesis" [[Crawford2022CALMED]].
- In an 8-week exploratory RCT, adjunctive memantine 20 mg/day (n = 17) reduced ZAN-BPD severity over time compared with placebo (n = 16; p = 0.02), with no excess adverse effects (Early evidence) [[Kulkarni2018Memantine]].
- The memantine pilot cites registration NCT02097706. That record describes an adjunctive "NMDA receptor antagonist" vs placebo trial (est. n = 150), which is still listed as recruiting with an estimated completion of December 2025 and no posted results [[NCT02097706,Kulkarni2018Memantine]].
- Across 46 RCTs (2,769 participants), no medication outperformed placebo on any primary outcome at the end of treatment, and the evidence was of very low certainty. No medication is approved for BPD itself [[StoffersWinterling2022]].

### frontier · Personalised (precision) treatment selection
- In a re-analysis of 156 patients from a DBT vs general psychiatric management trial, a model built on baseline moderators chose each patient's predicted best treatment. Patients randomised to that treatment had better long-term outcomes (d = 0.36, p = 0.028), and more so when the prediction was strong (d = 0.61) (Early evidence, not prospectively tested) [[Keefe2021Selection]].
- The moderators that favoured general psychiatric management were higher symptom severity and impulsivity. Those that favoured DBT were dependent traits, childhood emotional abuse and social adjustment [[Keefe2021Selection]].
- Older age, childhood sexual abuse, family history of substance use, poor vocational record, anxious-cluster comorbidity, high neuroticism and low agreeableness each predicted slower remission. These are candidate stratifiers for care, not yet a validated tool (Hypothesis) [[Zanarini2006Prediction]].

### frontier · Dimensional diagnosis and co-design
- ICD-11 moved toward classifying personality pathology on a single severity dimension, from normal personality to severe disorder, replacing overlapping categories [[Tyrer2015Lancet]].
- A 10-week peer group co-produced with people with lived experience of BPD (51 participants) showed small to moderate gains in recovery, belonging and personality functioning. Some gains persisted at 3 months (uncontrolled; Early evidence) [[Bartsch2026Peer]].
- People with BPD helped build the priovi digital therapeutic but not design its trial, which illustrates how far co-design reaches in current research [[Assmann2025priovi]].

### Exam hooks
- MSAD 6-year remission: 34.5% at 2 years, 49.4% at 4, 68.6% at 6, and only 5.9% of remitters had a recurrence [[Zanarini2003Course]].
- 88% of MSAD inpatients with BPD remitted by 10 years [[Zanarini2006Prediction]].
- By 16 years, 99% had a 2-year remission, but only 60% had a 2-year recovery (remission plus good social and vocational functioning) [[Zanarini2012]].
- Recovery is harder to reach and to keep than remission: 50% recovered by 10 years, and 34% of them lost it [[Zanarini2010Recovery]].
- CLPS 10-year course: 85% remitted, 12% relapsed, social functioning stayed severely impaired [[Gunderson2011]].
- Impulsive (acute) symptoms such as self-harm remit fastest. Affective (temperamental) symptoms such as chronic anger, emptiness and anxiety are the most persistent [[Zanarini2007Subsyndromal,Zanarini2016Fluidity]].
- The strongest comorbidity predictor of BPD remission and recovery is the absence of a substance use disorder [[Zanarini2004AxisI,Zanarini2024Comorbid]].
- When BPD remits, comorbid Axis I disorders decline too. When it does not, they persist [[Zanarini2004AxisI]].
- BPD and MDD slow each other's remission, while BPD and bipolar disorder run largely independent courses [[Gunderson2014Mood]].
- NESARC lifetime comorbidity in BPD: anxiety 84.8%, mood 82.7%, substance use 78.2% [[Tomko2014]].
- About 30% of adults with BPD also have PTSD [[Pagura2010PTSD]].
- MSAD 24-year suicide deaths: 5.9% (BPD) vs 1.4% (comparison). Most people who died had not recovered [[Temes2019]].
- Danish register SMR for hospital-treated BPD: 8.3 [[Kjaer2020Mortality]].
- Failure to remit or recover from BPD is associated with obesity, diabetes, osteoarthritis, chronic pain conditions and costly medical care [[Frankenburg2004Medical,Keuroghlian2013Medical]].
- MOBY: in youth, a dedicated early-intervention service (HYPE) improved attendance and completion, but specialist psychotherapy did not add functional benefit [[Chanen2022MOBY]].
- 6 months of DBT was non-inferior to 12 months for self-harm [[McMain2022]].
- No medication has shown benefit over placebo on core BPD outcomes, and none is approved for BPD [[StoffersWinterling2022]].

### In plain words
- Most people with BPD get much better over time. In the largest long-term study, almost everyone had at least a 2-year stretch without meeting the diagnosis [[Zanarini2012]].
- Once symptoms settle for a long time, they usually stay settled [[Zanarini2012,Gunderson2011]].
- The risky, impulsive behaviours such as self-harm tend to fade first. Feelings like anger, emptiness and loneliness often take longer [[Zanarini2016Fluidity]].
- Getting your life back (work, study, relationships) can take longer than symptoms improving, so support for work and daily life matters [[Zanarini2010Recovery,Zanarini2010Psychosocial]].
- Other conditions such as depression, anxiety, alcohol or drug problems and eating problems are common with BPD, and many of them improve when BPD improves [[Zanarini2004AxisI]].
- Getting help for alcohol or drug use is one of the most useful steps toward recovery from BPD [[Zanarini2024Comorbid]].
- Looking after your body (sleep, activity, weight, smoking) is part of recovery. People who recover from BPD tend to have better physical health too [[Keuroghlian2013Medical,Glass2024Inactivity]].
- BPD can be recognised and helped in teenagers. Getting help early does not have to wait until age 18 [[Kaess2014Adolescence,Chanen2022Age]].
- Shorter treatments can work. Six months of DBT did as well as twelve months on self-harm in one large trial [[McMain2022]].
- Talking therapies are the main treatment. No medicine has been proven to treat BPD itself [[Storebo2020,StoffersWinterling2022]].
- New ideas such as brain stimulation, ketamine and psilocybin are still being tested and are not proven treatments for BPD [[Konstantinou2021rTMS,Fineberg2023Ketamine,Grant2026Psilocybin]].
- People with BPD describe recovery as a journey with ups and downs, driven by hope and by meaningful activities and relationships [[Ng2019LivedRecovery]].
- BPD raises the risk of dying early, mostly when it does not improve. Help works, and if you are in crisis, call or text 988 in the US, or Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK and Ireland [[Temes2019]].

## Open questions and controversies
- How chronic is BPD? MSAD and CLPS show very high remission (85%–99%) and low relapse [[Zanarini2012,Gunderson2011]]. But recovery of functioning stays at 40%–60%, and social impairment persists [[Zanarini2012,Gunderson2011]]. The meta-analytic remission range is 50%–70% [[AlvarezTomas2019Meta]]. Status: remission is common, functional recovery is the unsolved problem.
- Do symptoms fade at different speeds? MSAD found impulsive symptoms remit faster than affective ones [[Zanarini2007Subsyndromal,Zanarini2016Fluidity]]. CLPS found all criteria declined at similar rates over 10 years [[Gunderson2011]], although its 2-year data showed affective instability and anger were the least changeable [[McGlashan2005CLPS]]. Status: the hybrid trait-plus-behaviour model is widely accepted, but how quickly specific criteria change depends on the sample and method.
- How much do the cohorts generalise? MSAD recruited severely ill, mostly white, mostly female inpatients in 1992–1995, using DSM-III-R criteria [[Zanarini2012,Temes2019]]. Community and youth samples may differ [[Winograd2008,Tomko2014]]. Status: open.
- Does treatment change the long-term course? The meta-analysis found no significant association between treatment moderators and long-term outcome [[AlvarezTomas2019Meta]]. Treated MSAD participants reflect usual care [[Zanarini2003Course]]. Short-term trials show psychotherapy beats usual care [[Storebo2020]]. Status: long-term treatment effects are unproven.
- Is specialist psychotherapy needed early on? In MOBY, youth-oriented case management, not specialist psychotherapy, carried the benefit [[Chanen2022MOBY]]. HYPE found CAT and good clinical care similar [[Chanen2008HYPE]]. Status: service model over therapy brand, pending larger trials [[Kaess2025Review]].
- How long must treatment last? 6 months of DBT was non-inferior to 12 months on self-harm [[McMain2022]], and brief GPM was no better than usual care on total symptoms [[Kramer2024GPM]]. Status: brief, structured care is promising, but which patients need longer care is unknown [[Laporte2018Stepped]].
- Do digital therapeutics work? EPADIP-BPD showed a small benefit (d = 0.24) and fewer suicide attempts [[Assmann2025priovi]]. An earlier priovi trial was negative on its primary outcome [[Klein2021priovi]]. Status: early, needs replication and longer follow-up.
- Does oxytocin help? Lab studies show normalised threat processing and empathy [[Bertsch2013Oxytocin,Domes2019Oxytocin]], but also reduced trust in anxiously attached people [[Bartz2011Oxytocin]] and no effect on heart-rate variability [[Back2022Oxytocin]]. No clinical efficacy trial has reported a benefit [[DiGiacomo2024Oxytocin]]. Status: not a treatment.
- Do brain stimulation and fast-acting drugs have a role? Sham-controlled TMS and iTBS trials have been negative or underpowered so far [[Kujovic2025iTBS,Horky2026rTMS]]. Ketamine and psilocybin data are pilot-level and help depressive symptoms more than BPD symptoms [[Fineberg2023Ketamine,Grant2026Psilocybin]]. Status: experimental, with ongoing trials listed above.
- Can precision approaches guide choice between equally effective therapies? A retrospective model matched patients to DBT or GPM with a moderate advantage [[Keefe2021Selection]]. Status: needs prospective validation.

## Provenance
- Validation: all 90 journal sources were matched against their Europe PMC core records (which mirror PubMed: PMID, PMCID, DOI, authors, journal, year, volume, pages). Abstracts were read for every source cited with a number. The 12 trial registrations were read from the ClinicalTrials.gov API v2 on 2026-10-25. NCBI E-utilities were rate-limited during this pass (shared IP), so Europe PMC was the main validation service.
- Full text read: Zanarini 2012 (PMC3509999) Tables 1–4, and Zanarini 2016 (PMC4930411) Tables 1–3, both through PubMed Central. All per-wave anchor points in "Numbers for the animations" come from these tables. Trull 2018 conclusions came from the Europe PMC open-access full text.
- Not accessible: full text of Zanarini 1998, Zanarini 2004, Zanarini 2007, Grant 2008 and the 24-year MSAD papers (paywalled or no open full text). These are cited only for what their abstracts state.
- Dropped: the commonly quoted per-disorder lifetime rates from Zanarini 1998 (for example MDD in about 83% and PTSD in about 56% of inpatients), because I could not confirm them from an accessible source. Also dropped: Chanen 2017 (World Psychiatry; no abstract, letter format) and the Kim 2026 ICD-11 editorial (no abstract). I found no published GPM-A efficacy RCT, so only the uncontrolled GPM-A training study is cited.
- ClinicalTrials.gov statuses are as listed on 2026-10-25. NCT02097706 is still listed as recruiting past its estimated completion date (last update August 2025), so its true status is uncertain. NCT01243658 is complete with no posted results.
- No source is marked unverified.
