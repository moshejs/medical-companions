# C · Mechanisms: emotion dysregulation, neurobiology and mentalizing

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: emotion, brain, home.

This pass covers how emotion dysregulation in borderline personality disorder (BPD) has been measured in daily life and in the laboratory, the cycle that links rejection, emotion, urges, short-term relief and shame, what brain imaging has and has not shown, stress and neuropeptide systems (HPA axis, opioids, oxytocin, vagal tone), pain processing, mentalizing, dissociation and stress-related paranoid or psychotic experiences. Causes (genes, temperament, adversity, the biosocial model as a cause), diagnosis, treatment trials and medication evidence are covered by other passes and are only touched where a mechanism study used them.

## Findings

### emotion · Affective instability in daily life (EMA / e-diaries)
- DSM-5-TR lists affective instability "due to a marked reactivity of mood" as one of the nine BPD criteria; the episodes of dysphoria, irritability or anxiety usually last a few hours and only rarely more than a few days (Established) [[APA2022]].
- In a 24-hour electronic-diary study of 50 women with BPD and 50 healthy women, affective instability for both emotional valence and distress was clearly heightened in the BPD group but not in controls (Established) [[EbnerPriemer2007PsycholMed]].
- In the same study, 48% of declines from a very positive mood in BPD were large enough to reach a negative mood state, compared with 9% in healthy controls; the typical BPD pattern was sudden large drops from positive mood (Established) [[EbnerPriemer2007PsycholMed]].
- Earlier paper-and-pencil diary studies often failed to find instability; the authors attribute the inconsistency to how instability was calculated, not to its absence (Established) [[EbnerPriemer2007PsycholMed]].
- Over 24 hours of ambulatory monitoring, people with BPD reported more negative emotions, fewer positive emotions, more intense negative emotions, more often more than one emotion at once, and (early in treatment) more difficulty identifying which emotion they felt (Established) [[EbnerPriemer2007PsychiatryRes]].
- A subgroup of unmedicated people with BPD showed higher "additional heart rate" (heart-rate rise not explained by movement), a physiological marker of emotional reactivity (Early evidence) [[EbnerPriemer2007PsychiatryRes]].
- In 50 people with BPD and 50 controls monitored for 24 hours, inability to label emotions was linked to distress in the BPD group (p = 0.0009) but not across all participants (p = 0.6492); conflicting emotions were linked to distress in both groups and were more frequent in BPD (Early evidence) [[EbnerPriemer2008]].
- Trull and colleagues followed 34 outpatients with BPD and 26 with a depressive disorder for about 1 month with up to 6 random prompts a day. Mean levels of positive and negative affect did not differ; BPD showed more variability over time and more large successive changes in hostility, fear and sadness (Established) [[Trull2008]].
- In that study people with BPD were more likely to report extreme changes between successive prompts (at or above the 90th percentile of change scores) for hostility (Established) [[Trull2008]].
- The mean squared successive difference (MSSD) and the probability of acute change (PAC) are the preferred instability indices because they capture both the size of change and its timing; the within-person variance alone misses temporal order (Established) [[Jahng2008]].
- In an e-diary study prompting about every 15 minutes for 24 hours (43 BPD, 28 PTSD, 20 bulimia nervosa, 28 healthy controls), people with BPD showed more instability than healthy controls on every index (squared successive differences, probability of acute change, point-by-point change) (Established) [[Santangelo2014]].
- In the same study, people with PTSD or bulimia nervosa showed instability similar to BPD; affective instability was not specific to BPD (Established) [[Santangelo2014]].
- Table values from that study: mean momentary distress on a 0–10 scale was 4.85 (SD 1.83) in BPD versus 0.34 (SD 0.48) in healthy controls; compliance was 94% with about 57–58 ratings per person in 24 hours (Established) [[Santangelo2014]].
- The instability in BPD in that study was mainly made of drops from highly positive valence; mood increases were similar to controls (Established) [[Santangelo2014]].
- Across three e-diary datasets (BPD n = 50, 42 and 43; controls n = 50, 24 and 28), people with BPD had a more negative emotional "home base" and more emotional variability; evidence for a weaker pull back to baseline ("attractor strength") was less consistent and smaller (Early evidence) [[EbnerPriemer2015]].
- With 10 ratings a day for 1 week (40 BPD, 38 major depression, 40 healthy controls), BPD showed more instability after correcting for average emotion level, but not after also correcting for overall variability; the authors conclude BPD differs in the size of departures from baseline, not necessarily in their abruptness (Early evidence) [[Houben2021]].
- With 12 prompts a day for 4 days (71 BPD, 121 anxiety disorders, 74 healthy controls), BPD and anxiety groups did not differ in affective instability, but self-esteem instability was higher in BPD on every index (Early evidence) [[Kockler2022]].
- In 130 women with BPD and 130 healthy women aged 14–53 years, e-diary affective instability declined with older age in BPD, regardless of comorbidity and severity (Early evidence) [[Santangelo2018]].
- People in symptomatic remission from BPD (35, meeting four or fewer criteria) had lower self-esteem instability than people with acute BPD but similar affective instability, which was still higher than in healthy controls (Early evidence) [[Santangelo2020]].
- In an experience-sampling study of 98 women, BPD and ADHD showed similar intensity of anger and irritability and similar instability of sadness, irritability and anger; the authors advise against using emotion dysregulation alone to tell the two apart (Early evidence) [[Moukhtarian2021]].

### emotion · Sensitivity, reactivity and return to baseline (Linehan's three components)
- Linehan's biosocial account describes emotion vulnerability in BPD as high sensitivity, high intensity (reactivity) and a slow return to emotional baseline (Hypothesis, as a theory) [[Kuo2009,Reitz2012]].
- Carpenter and Trull organise BPD emotion dysregulation into four components: emotion sensitivity, heightened and labile negative affect, too few adaptive regulation strategies, and too many maladaptive strategies (Established as a framework) [[Carpenter2013]].
- Rosenthal and colleagues' review notes that self-report studies dominated the early literature and argues for laboratory biobehavioral measures (psychophysiology, neuroimaging) to test emotional responding in BPD (Established) [[Rosenthal2008]].
- In 26 women with BPD, 28 healthy controls and 15 women with depression who rated emotions before, right after, and 3 and 6 minutes after mood-inducing stories, all three groups showed the same rise and fall; BPD differed by a much higher level of negative emotion throughout (Early evidence) [[Jacob2009]].
- Jacob and colleagues conclude that "extreme negative affectivity" may describe BPD better than emotional hyperreactivity (Early evidence) [[Jacob2009]].
- Kuo and Linehan (20 BPD, 20 social anxiety disorder, 20 controls) found lower resting respiratory sinus arrhythmia and higher baseline skin conductance and self-reported negative emotion in BPD, but no greater reactivity to emotion inductions (Early evidence) [[Kuo2009]].
- Table values from Kuo2009: Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale total was 114.65 (SD 20.62) in BPD, 87.79 (SD 19.36) in social anxiety disorder and 71.00 (SD 13.19) in controls (Established for that sample) [[Kuo2009]].
- A second laboratory study (25 BPD, 30 controls) found higher resting heart rate and lower respiratory sinus arrhythmia at baseline in BPD, but no difference in reactivity and no deficit in using mindfulness or distraction to feel less negative (Early evidence) [[Kuo2016]].
- A meta-analysis of 31 laboratory studies (1,675 participants) found null-to-small differences in physiological and behavioural reactivity, a moderate-to-large difference in how negatively people with BPD rated emotional stimuli, and a small difference in self-reported arousal; the hyperreactivity hypothesis was "in general" not supported (Established) [[Bortolla2020]].
- Gratz and colleagues found context-specific reactivity: outpatients with BPD reacted more than non-PD outpatients to a negative-evaluation stressor but not to a general stressor (Early evidence) [[Gratz2010]].
- In that study, shame rose more after negative evaluation in BPD and stayed elevated through the end of the recovery period, the clearest laboratory evidence for slow return to baseline being emotion-specific (Early evidence) [[Gratz2010]].
- In a pilot stress-induction study (14 unmedicated BPD, 18 controls), aversive tension started higher and returned to baseline more slowly in BPD (Early evidence) [[Reitz2012]].
- In daily-life experience sampling, BPD showed a larger rise in negative affect and a larger fall in positive affect after everyday stress than both people with psychosis and healthy controls (44 BPD, 42 psychosis, 49 controls) (Established) [[Glaser2008]].
- In 30 inpatients with BPD and 28 controls, stronger negative-affect reactivity in daily life appeared only in response to disappointment in someone else, not to general appraisals (Early evidence) [[Houben2018]].
- Over 20 days of event recording (38 BPD, 31 controls), negative and positive affect carried over more from one interpersonal event to the next in BPD, a daily-life sign of slower recovery (Early evidence) [[Sadikaj2010]].
- Women with BPD reported aversive tension that was longer and more intense than in healthy controls over the previous 24 hours, and tension correlated strongly with dissociation (Established) [[Stiglmayr2001]].
- Lab measures of dysregulation (resting respiratory sinus arrhythmia; amygdala response) were largely unrelated to daily-life negative emotion, though low resting RSA strengthened the link between daily interpersonal stressors and fear (Early evidence) [[Trull2022]].

### emotion · Rejection sensitivity and interpersonal triggers
- A meta-analysis (31 studies in the meta-analysis) found rejection sensitivity linked to BPD features with pooled r = 0.326, and r = 0.655 when clinical BPD samples were compared with controls (Established) [[Foxhall2019]].
- On the Rejection Sensitivity Questionnaire, people with BPD scored highest of all groups tested and differed significantly even from people with social anxiety disorder (Established) [[Staebler2011CPP]].
- In the Cyberball ball-tossing game, people with BPD felt excluded more readily than controls even when they were included (30 BPD, 30 controls) (Established) [[Renneberg2012]].
- In a separate Cyberball study (35 BPD, 33 controls), people with BPD reported feeling excluded even when included, and when excluded showed fewer positive and more mixed facial expressions (Established) [[Staebler2011PsycholMed]].
- In fMRI Cyberball (20 unmedicated BPD, 20 controls), people with BPD felt more excluded in the inclusion and control conditions and engaged dorsal anterior cingulate and medial prefrontal cortex more in all conditions, which the authors read as hypermentalizing in neutral situations (Early evidence) [[Domsalla2014]].
- In daily life (56 BPD, 60 community adults; 21 days, 6 prompts a day), rejection and disagreement were linked with hostility, sadness and fear; rejection–hostility and rejection–sadness links were stronger in BPD, and rejection predicted later hostility and sadness more strongly in BPD (Established, replicated) [[Hepp2018]].
- People with BPD show more negative affect when they perceive others as less warm, and smaller gains in positive affect when others are warm (Early evidence) [[Sadikaj2010]].
- Gunderson and colleagues describe BPD as marked by "extreme sensitivity to perceived interpersonal slights" alongside intense, volatile emotion and impulsive behaviour (Established) [[Gunderson2018]].
- Shame: 60 women with BPD reported more shame- and guilt-proneness and state shame than 30 women with social phobia and 60 healthy women; their implicit self-concept was also more shame-prone (Established) [[Rusch2007]].
- In that study, after controlling for depression, shame-proneness correlated with lower self-esteem and quality of life and with more anger-hostility (Established) [[Rusch2007]].

### emotion · The cycle: emotion → urge → action → short-term relief → shame
- A review of 18 studies found converging evidence that self-injury serves an affect-regulation function: acute negative affect precedes it, relief often follows, and relieving negative affect is the most common stated reason (Established) [[Klonsky2007]].
- The same review found strong support for a self-punishment function and modest evidence for anti-dissociation, interpersonal-influence, anti-suicide, sensation-seeking and interpersonal-boundaries functions (Established) [[Klonsky2007]].
- The experiential avoidance model proposes that self-harm is maintained mainly by negative reinforcement: escape from unwanted emotional experience (Hypothesis, widely used) [[Chapman2006]].
- In 75 chronically suicidal women with BPD, almost all reported that both suicide attempts and non-suicidal self-injury were intended to relieve negative emotions; non-suicidal acts were also more often meant to express anger, punish oneself, generate normal feelings or distract (Established) [[Brown2002]].
- In 101 women with BPD who self-injure, most reported several motives at once; negative reinforcement was almost always involved, and positive reinforcement played an additional role for about half (Established) [[Kleindienst2008]].
- In daily-life NSSI studies (35 studies reviewed), participants averaged 1.6 acts and 4.3 urges each; urges came with more negative affect and predicted later acts (Established) [[Hepp2020]].
- In that review, 8 studies partly supported rising negative affect before NSSI, but only 4 supported a fall in negative affect afterwards; evidence for interpersonal functions came from only 2 studies and was inconclusive (Established) [[Hepp2020]].
- In 56 people with BPD followed for 21 days (5,750 assessments), urges were more likely with higher negative affect, after rejection, and later in the day; negative affect rose before an urge, peaked during it and then fell (Early evidence) [[Hepp2021PersonalDisord]].
- In 51 women with NSSI disorder (155 NSSI events), more distressing negative interpersonal events were linked with NSSI events and urges; participants rarely endorsed "get help/attention" as a motive, and NSSI did not improve interpersonal events afterwards (Early evidence) [[Hepp2021BRT]].
- In 30 adolescents and young adults, thoughts of NSSI occurred about 5.0 times a week, were mostly of moderate intensity and short duration (1–30 min), and NSSI episodes occurred about 1.6 times a week (Established) [[Nock2009]].
- An experience-sampling study of 30 inpatients with high BPD features found that high negative emotion predicted NSSI in the next interval, but on a scale of hours NSSI was followed by more, not less, negative emotion, which the authors describe as the cyclic nature of NSSI (Early evidence) [[Houben2017]].
- In laboratory models, a painful stimulus after stress induction reduced aversive tension more in BPD than in controls; this was driven by the pain itself rather than tissue damage (57 BPD, 60 controls) (Early evidence) [[Willis2017]].
- After stress induction, a controlled painful stimulus reduced tension and amygdala activity more in women with BPD (21) than controls (17), and amygdala–superior frontal connectivity normalised (Early evidence) [[Reitz2015]].
- These laboratory findings describe why short-term relief can reinforce the behaviour; they do not show that self-injury helps over time, and the daily-life data show negative emotion rising again over the following hours (Established for the short-term effect; Early evidence for the longer course) [[Willis2017,Houben2017,Hepp2020]].
- After 12 weeks of residential dialectical behaviour therapy, the pain-related amygdala deactivation and amygdala–dorsal ACC coupling seen at baseline were reduced compared with treatment as usual (28 DBT, 15 TAU, 23 controls) (Early evidence) [[Niedtfeld2017]].

### brain · Amygdala and limbic reactivity
- Early fMRI: 6 women with BPD showed elevated bilateral amygdala signal to aversive versus neutral pictures, unlike 6 controls (Early evidence, very small) [[Herpertz2001]].
- 15 people with BPD showed greater left amygdala activation to emotional faces than 15 controls; some found neutral faces hard to read or threatening (Early evidence) [[Donegan2003]].
- A multimodal meta-analysis of 19 fMRI studies (281 BPD, 293 controls) found more left amygdala and posterior cingulate activation and blunted bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal responses to negative stimuli in BPD (Established) [[Schulze2016]].
- In the same meta-analysis, the left amygdala combined functional hyperactivity with smaller grey-matter volume (10 structural studies: 263 BPD, 278 controls) (Established) [[Schulze2016]].
- Amygdala hyperactivity in that meta-analysis was moderated by medication: unmedicated samples showed limbic hyperactivity, medicated samples did not (Established) [[Schulze2016]].
- An earlier activation-likelihood meta-analysis (11 studies; 154 BPD, 150 controls) found greater insula and posterior cingulate activation in BPD but less activation than controls from the amygdala through subgenual ACC to dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (Established, conflicting on amygdala) [[Ruocco2013]].
- A comparative meta-analysis (24 BPD studies, 431 BPD vs 436 controls) found limbic hyperactivation in BPD and PTSD but blunted amygdala activation in major depression (Established) [[Schulze2019]].
- Unmedicated BPD (33) showed greater amygdala activation to repeated emotional pictures than schizotypal personality disorder (28) and controls (32), and a prolonged return to baseline of the amygdala signal, while their self-ratings of the pictures were blunted (Early evidence) [[Hazlett2012]].
- Unlike healthy controls, people with BPD did not habituate in emotional-intensity ratings to repeated negative pictures and did not increase dorsal ACC activity; less dorsal ACC increase went with more affective instability (Early evidence) [[Koenigsberg2014]].
- During instructed fear, amygdala activity did not decrease over time in women with BPD as it did in controls (Early evidence) [[Kamphausen2013]].
- Eye-tracking plus fMRI (40 unmedicated women with BPD, 41 controls) showed faster, more frequent first glances to the eyes of angry faces and more amygdala response to angry faces in BPD (Early evidence) [[Bertsch2013AJP]].
- A meta-analysis of 10 studies (266 BPD, 255 controls) found people with BPD less accurate at recognising anger and disgust and, most of all, at recognising neutral faces as neutral (Established) [[Daros2013]].
- Sicorello and Schmahl's review of task fMRI challenges the simple model in which amygdala hyperreactivity causes emotional hyperreactivity and weak prefrontal control causes poor regulation (Established as a critique) [[Sicorello2021]].

### brain · Prefrontal, cingulate and insula: regulation circuits
- The "fronto-limbic imbalance" model proposes that limbic structures generate strong emotion while prefrontal regulatory regions under-engage (Hypothesis, dominant model) [[Sicorello2021,KrauseUtz2014]].
- Krause-Utz and colleagues' review of studies since 2010 describes structural and functional changes in a fronto-limbic network: amygdala and insula for emotion processing; anterior cingulate, medial frontal, orbitofrontal and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex for control (Established) [[KrauseUtz2014]].
- In an emotional go/no-go task (16 BPD, 14 controls), people with BPD showed less ventromedial prefrontal activity (medial orbitofrontal and subgenual cingulate) when inhibiting responses during negative emotion (Early evidence) [[Silbersweig2007]].
- When asked to use psychological distancing (18 BPD, 16 controls), people with BPD engaged dorsal ACC and intraparietal sulcus less and deactivated the amygdala less than controls (Early evidence) [[Koenigsberg2009]].
- During cognitive reappraisal (15 unmedicated women with BPD and affective instability, 15 controls), BPD showed more left amygdala and right insula activation when first viewing aversive pictures, and less left orbitofrontal and more insula activation when trying to decrease emotion (Early evidence) [[Schulze2011]].
- Ruocco's meta-analysis found greater insula activation to negative emotion in BPD, consistent with stronger subjective negative emotion (Established) [[Ruocco2013]].
- In BPD, negative and neutral pictures both produced stronger amygdala, insula and anterior cingulate activation than in controls, and amygdala activation correlated with self-reported emotion-regulation difficulties (Early evidence) [[Niedtfeld2010]].
- After 12 months of standard DBT in unmedicated BPD (11 per group), amygdala activation decreased relative to controls, and improved amygdala habituation tracked improved self-reported emotion regulation (Early evidence) [[Goodman2014]].
- People with BPD (53 medication-free women) showed lower heartbeat-evoked potentials than 60 controls, a marker of weaker cortical representation of body signals that correlated with emotion dysregulation (Early evidence) [[Schmitz2020]].
- Resting vagally mediated heart-rate variability was lower in BPD than controls (5 studies; 95 BPD, 105 controls; Hedges' g = −0.59, 95% CI −1.11 to −0.06, random effects) (Early evidence) [[Koenig2016]].

### brain · Structure: amygdala, hippocampus and grey matter
- A meta-analysis of 11 volumetric MRI studies (205 BPD, 222 controls) found average reductions of 11% in hippocampal volume and 13% in amygdala volume in BPD (Established) [[Ruocco2012]].
- These reductions were not attenuated by psychotropic medication and were unrelated to comorbid depression, PTSD or substance use disorders (Established) [[Ruocco2012]].
- An earlier meta-analysis of 6 studies (104 BPD, 122 controls) found smaller left and right hippocampi and amygdalae in BPD (Established) [[Nunes2009]].
- A voxel-based meta-analysis (13 BPD studies: 395 BPD, 415 controls) found decreased grey matter in bilateral medial prefrontal cortex, bilateral amygdala and right parahippocampal gyrus; the pattern differed from bipolar disorder (Established) [[Yu2019BJP]].
- A meta-analysis of 9 VBM studies (256 BPD, 272 controls) found lower grey matter in right superior/middle temporal gyri; older age was linked with smaller right amygdala volume in BPD (Early evidence) [[Kimmel2016]].
- A dual meta-analysis (15 datasets: 442 BPD, 441 controls) found orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate reductions shared with PTSD, and larger posterior cingulate and precuneus volumes in BPD (Early evidence) [[Lou2021]].

### brain · Replication caveats
- Most early BPD imaging samples were small (6 per group in Herpertz2001; 15 per group in Donegan2003; 11 per group in Goodman2014) (Established) [[Herpertz2001,Donegan2003,Goodman2014]].
- Across neuroscience, low statistical power inflates effect sizes and lowers the chance that a significant finding is true (Established) [[Button2013]].
- In about 50,000 individuals, brain-wide associations between brain measures and mental-health traits were smaller than thought; at the median imaging sample of about 25, studies were underpowered, effect sizes inflated and replication failed; reproducibility needed thousands of participants (Established) [[Marek2022]].
- Meta-analyses disagree on the amygdala: increased activation in Schulze2016 versus decreased activation in Ruocco2013; Schulze2016 shows medication status as one explanation (Established disagreement) [[Schulze2016,Ruocco2013]].
- In large Human Connectome Project samples (870 young adults; 223 adolescents), multivariate functional-connectivity patterns predicted a borderline-trait proxy score in unseen data, with the most predictive regions in emotion-regulation and executive systems (Early evidence; traits, not diagnosed BPD) [[Shafiei2024]].
- Many imaging findings (limbic hyperactivity, prefrontal hypoactivity, smaller grey matter) also appear in PTSD, depression or bipolar disorder, so they are not diagnostic for BPD (Established) [[Schulze2019,Yu2019BJP,Lou2021]].
- Because brain–behaviour effects are small and many BPD imaging findings are shared with other disorders, group-level imaging results cannot serve as a diagnostic test for an individual (Established) [[Marek2022,Schulze2019]].

### brain · Stress systems: HPA axis and cortisol
- A series of meta-analyses (37 studies; 803 BPD, 1,092 controls) found elevated continuous cortisol output and blunted cortisol responses to psychosocial stress in BPD; single cortisol samples and responses to pharmacological challenge did not differ (Early evidence) [[Drews2019]].
- A separate meta-analysis of basal cortisol (12 studies; 278 BPD, 268 non-psychiatric controls) found lower mean cortisol in BPD (Hedges' g = −0.32, 95% CI −0.56 to −0.06) (Early evidence) [[Thomas2019Cortisol]].
- The two meta-analyses point in different directions for "baseline" cortisol, depending on whether single samples or continuous output were measured; both authors cite comorbidity, sampling method and small samples as confounders (Established disagreement) [[Drews2019,Thomas2019Cortisol]].

### brain · Opioid system (Hypothesis)
- Bandelow and colleagues propose that altered opioid-receptor sensitivity or endogenous opioid availability underlies part of BPD; this is a theory built from indirect findings (Hypothesis) [[Bandelow2010]].
- PET imaging (18 unmedicated women with BPD, 14 controls) showed greater baseline mu-opioid receptor availability in orbitofrontal cortex, caudate, nucleus accumbens and left amygdala, and greater endogenous opioid release during induced sadness in several regions (Early evidence) [[Prossin2010]].
- In people with Cluster B personality disorders and a past suicide attempt, those with repeated NSSI (14) had lower cerebrospinal fluid beta-endorphin and met-enkephalin than those without (15) (Early evidence) [[Stanley2010JAD]].
- Stanley and Siever propose that opioids, oxytocin and vasopressin, which regulate attachment and affiliation, may be altered in BPD, and note that clinical data are limited (Hypothesis) [[Stanley2010AJP]].
- In two small double-blind crossover trials (n = 29 total), dissociation was numerically lower on naltrexone than placebo, but the effects were too small to reach significance (Early evidence, null) [[Schmahl2012]].

### brain · Oxytocin (Hypothesis / Early evidence)
- Women with BPD had lower plasma oxytocin than women without BPD in the follicular phase; oxytocin correlated negatively with childhood emotional neglect and abuse (Early evidence) [[Bertsch2013HormBehav]].
- A single intranasal dose of oxytocin (26 IU) normalised the fast first glances to angry eyes and the raised amygdala response to angry faces in women with BPD (randomised, placebo-controlled; 40 BPD, 41 controls) (Early evidence) [[Bertsch2013AJP]].
- In another trial, intranasal oxytocin decreased trust and cooperation in people with BPD, driven by anxiously attached, rejection-sensitive participants (Early evidence) [[Bartz2011]].
- In a pilot crossover trial (14 BPD, 13 controls; 40 IU before a social stress test), oxytocin attenuated stress-induced dysphoria proportionately more in BPD (p = .04) (Early evidence) [[Simeon2011]].
- Oxytocin did not change the reduced heartbeat-evoked potentials in BPD (Early evidence, null) [[Schmitz2020]].
- Herpertz and Bertsch's model links oxytocin to interpersonal hypersensitivity, salience and social reward in BPD and states the mechanisms may not be specific to BPD (Hypothesis) [[Herpertz2015]].
- These were experimental single-dose studies, not treatment trials; in current reviews drug treatment in BPD is reserved for comorbid conditions or crises when psychosocial help is insufficient (Established) [[Bohus2021]].

### brain · Pain processing (Schmahl and colleagues)
- About 60% of people with BPD who self-injure report not feeling pain during self-injury (Established, self-report) [[Bohus2000Pain]].
- In 12 unmedicated women with BPD and 19 controls, pain perception was reduced even during calm, and reduced further during intense distress (Early evidence) [[Bohus2000Pain]].
- Pain thresholds to electric stimulation were higher in BPD than controls (12 vs 12), with no difference in detection thresholds; higher thresholds correlated with dissociation and aversive arousal (Early evidence) [[Ludascher2007]].
- In fMRI (12 women with BPD, 12 controls), identical heat produced less activation in BPD; at equal subjective pain, BPD showed more dorsolateral prefrontal activity and deactivation in perigenual ACC and amygdala, read as an antinociceptive mechanism (Early evidence) [[Schmahl2006]].
- Heat pain reduced amygdala and anterior cingulate activity in BPD regardless of painfulness, supporting an attentional-shift account of pain's soothing effect (Early evidence) [[Niedtfeld2010]].
- Script-induced dissociation in 15 unmedicated women with BPD increased dissociation scores and reduced pain sensitivity (pilot, no control group) (Early evidence) [[Ludascher2010]].
- Hyposensitivity to physical pain sits alongside hypersensitivity to "social pain": after Cyberball, higher temperatures were needed to reach comparable pain in BPD, and rejection sensitivity modulated insula and amygdala pain responses (Early evidence) [[Bungert2015]].

### brain · Mentalizing, hypermentalizing and social cognition
- The mentalizing model proposes that early attachment disruption plus later trauma make mentalizing (understanding self and others in terms of mental states) unstable during emotional arousal, giving rise to BPD symptoms (Hypothesis, influential) [[Fonagy2008]].
- Fonagy and colleagues later reframed BPD as a limitation of resilience linked to the general "p" factor of psychopathology (Hypothesis) [[Fonagy2017a]].
- They propose that epistemic hypervigilance or distrust, learned in the social environment, makes it hard to take in and benefit from social communication (Hypothesis) [[Fonagy2017b]].
- In 111 adolescent inpatients (23% met BPD criteria), borderline traits were linked to hypermentalizing (excessive, inaccurate mental-state attributions) on the Movie for the Assessment of Social Cognition, independent of age, gender and other symptoms (Early evidence) [[Sharp2011]].
- In that study, emotion-regulation difficulties accounted for 43.5% of the hypermentalizing-to-BPD-traits path (Early evidence) [[Sharp2011]].
- A meta-analysis of 36 studies (4,188 people) found hypermentalizing linked to psychopathology in general (r = 0.24, 95% CI 0.17–0.31), not more strongly to BPD (r = 0.26, 95% CI 0.12–0.39) than to other disorders (r = 0.24, 95% CI 0.14–0.33) (Established) [[McLaren2022]].
- A meta-analysis of 34 studies (1,448 BPD, 2,006 controls) found a very large deficit in reflective functioning (d = 1.68, 95% CI 1.17–2.19) but only a modest theory-of-mind deficit (d = 0.36, 95% CI 0.24–0.48) (Established) [[Bora2021]].
- In that meta-analysis, people with BPD underperformed on ToM reasoning (d = 0.44) but not ToM decoding; hyper-ToM errors (d = 0.60) and faux-pas errors (d = 0.62) were the most robust findings (Established) [[Bora2021]].
- Another meta-analysis (17 studies; 585 BPD, 501 controls) found deficits in overall ToM, mental-state reasoning and cognitive ToM, but no deficit in mental-state decoding (Established) [[Nemeth2018]].
- On the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test, 30 people with BPD performed better than 25 controls, especially for neutral expressions (large effect sizes) (Early evidence) [[Fertuck2009]].
- Bora concludes that interpersonal difficulties in BPD fit a maladaptive meta-social-cognitive style better than a primary deficit in basic social cognition (Early evidence) [[Bora2021]].

### brain · Dissociation and stress-related paranoia
- DSM-5-TR criterion 9 is transient, stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociative symptoms (Established) [[APA2022]].
- In 290 people with BPD and 72 Axis II controls on the Dissociative Experiences Scale, 32% of the BPD group had low, 42% moderate and 26% high dissociation (similar to PTSD or dissociative disorders), versus 71%, 26% and 3% in controls (Established) [[Zanarini2000Dissociation]].
- In 21 outpatients with BPD assessed in detail, 24% had no dissociative disorder, 29% a mild one, 24% DDNOS and 24% dissociative identity disorder (Early evidence, small sample) [[Korzekwa2009]].
- Hourly ambulatory monitoring over 48 hours (51 BPD, 51 clinical controls, 40 healthy controls) showed dissociation rising with stress in all groups, more steeply in BPD, and BPD had higher dissociation even after controlling for stress (Established) [[Stiglmayr2008]].
- Higher baseline dissociation predicted less improvement during a 3-month inpatient DBT programme (57 patients; β = −0.017 ± 0.006, p = 0.008) (Early evidence) [[Kleindienst2011]].
- Hazlett and colleagues found fewer dissociative symptoms associated with greater amygdala activation to repeated unpleasant pictures, consistent with dissociation dampening emotional response (Early evidence) [[Hazlett2012]].
- Krause-Utz and colleagues review evidence that dissociation impairs affective-cognitive functioning, body perception and treatment response in BPD, while neural findings remain diverse and need replication (Established) [[KrauseUtz2021]].
- Of 50 people with BPD, 40% reported quasi-psychotic thought in the past 2 years, none true psychotic thought in that period, and 14% ever (Established, historic) [[Zanarini1990]].
- About 20–50% of people with BPD report psychotic symptoms; hallucinations can resemble those in psychotic disorders, and the authors recommend avoiding the terms "pseudo-psychotic" and "quasi-psychotic" (Established) [[Schroeder2013]].
- Psychotic symptoms in BPD are strongly linked to stressful context and appear or intensify in crises (Established) [[DAgostino2019]].
- In daily life, psychotic experiences rose with stress in all patient groups; BPD (56) showed the strongest reactivity and more hallucinatory reactivity than controls and Cluster C patients, while paranoid reactivity did not differ between patient groups (Established) [[Glaser2010]].

### home · Big-picture framing
- BPD was once regarded as untreatable; progress in understanding and management has brought earlier diagnosis and better outcomes, and psychotherapy is the main treatment (Established) [[Bohus2021]].
- Most patients gradually enter symptomatic remission, and evidence-based psychosocial treatments can speed this up; these are population-level statements (Established) [[Gunderson2018]].
- Emotional and brain differences have been shown to change with treatment in small studies (amygdala activity after DBT) (Early evidence) [[Goodman2014,Niedtfeld2017]].
- Many of the mechanisms here (affective instability, limbic hyperactivity, hypermentalizing, low vagal tone) are shared with other conditions; they describe processes, not a fixed "BPD brain" (Established) [[Santangelo2014,Schulze2019,McLaren2022,Koenig2016]].

### Exam hooks
- Linehan's three components of emotion vulnerability: high sensitivity, high intensity (reactivity), slow return to baseline [[Kuo2009,Reitz2012]].
- Preferred EMA instability indices are MSSD and probability of acute change, not within-person variance, because they capture temporal order [[Jahng2008]].
- Typical BPD daily-life pattern: sudden large drops from positive mood (48% of drops from very positive mood reached negative mood vs 9% in controls) [[EbnerPriemer2007PsycholMed]].
- Affective instability is heightened in BPD but also in PTSD and bulimia nervosa: it is transdiagnostic [[Santangelo2014]].
- Self-esteem instability may be more BPD-specific than affective instability [[Kockler2022]].
- Lab meta-analysis: physiological hyperreactivity in BPD is null to small; the big difference is how negatively stimuli are rated [[Bortolla2020]].
- Kuo and Linehan 2009: high baseline negative emotion and low resting RSA in BPD, not greater reactivity [[Kuo2009]].
- Meta-analytic volume reductions in BPD: hippocampus about 11%, amygdala about 13% [[Ruocco2012]].
- Amygdala hyperactivity to negative stimuli appears in unmedicated, not medicated, BPD samples [[Schulze2016]].
- HPA axis: elevated continuous cortisol output but blunted cortisol response to psychosocial stress [[Drews2019]].
- Mentalizing in BPD: very large reflective-functioning deficit (d = 1.68) but modest ToM deficit (d = 0.36) [[Bora2021]].
- Hypermentalizing = excessive, inaccurate attribution of mental states; it is not specific to BPD (r = 0.26 vs 0.24) [[Sharp2011,McLaren2022]].
- DSM-5-TR criterion 9: transient, stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociative symptoms [[APA2022]].
- Dissociation is stress-dependent and predicts poorer DBT response [[Stiglmayr2008,Kleindienst2011]].
- About 20–50% of people with BPD report psychotic symptoms; "pseudo-psychotic" is a misleading label [[Schroeder2013]].
- Self-injury is most often aimed at relieving negative emotion; relief is short-lived and daily-life data show negative emotion rising again over hours [[Klonsky2007,Houben2017]].
- Rejection sensitivity is linked to BPD (pooled r = 0.326; clinical vs control r = 0.655) [[Foxhall2019]].
- People with BPD feel excluded in Cyberball even when included [[Renneberg2012,Staebler2011PsycholMed]].
- Pain thresholds are raised in BPD and rise further under distress and dissociation [[Bohus2000Pain,Ludascher2007]].
- Neuroimaging is not used to diagnose BPD; small samples limit replication [[Marek2022,Button2013]].

### In plain words
- Many people with BPD feel emotions strongly, and their mood can change fast, especially falling from a good mood to a bad one [[EbnerPriemer2007PsycholMed]].
- Studies that ask people to rate their mood on a phone many times a day show these shifts clearly [[Trull2008,Santangelo2014]].
- The biggest difference is often how high painful feelings already are to begin with, not only how fast they jump [[Jacob2009,Kuo2009]].
- Feeling left out or criticised can hit hard and last longer, and shame can linger after the event is over [[Hepp2018,Gratz2010]].
- Some people feel left out even when others are including them; this is a known pattern, not a character flaw [[Renneberg2012]].
- Self-harm can bring a quick sense of relief, which is why it can become a habit, but the relief does not last and bad feelings often come back within hours [[Klonsky2007,Houben2017]].
- Urges to self-harm usually build and then pass, often within minutes; getting through the peak with support and skills matters [[Nock2009,Hepp2021PersonalDisord]].
- Brain scans show differences in areas that handle emotion and control, but they are averages from research groups and cannot diagnose anyone [[Schulze2016,Marek2022]].
- Some brain differences have been seen to shift after therapy such as DBT [[Goodman2014,Niedtfeld2017]].
- Under stress, some people feel unreal, numb or cut off, or become suspicious of others; this usually eases as stress falls [[Stiglmayr2008,APA2022]].
- People with BPD can be very alert to other people's feelings but may read too much into them when upset [[Fertuck2009,Sharp2011]].
- Body stress systems, such as the cortisol response and heart-rate variability, can work differently in BPD [[Drews2019,Koenig2016]].
- Recovery is common, and treatment can help it happen sooner [[Gunderson2018,Bohus2021]].
- If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact local emergency services or a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).

## Open questions and controversies
- Is BPD emotion dysregulation driven by high reactivity or by a high baseline? Linehan's model predicts high reactivity and slow recovery [[Kuo2009,Reitz2012]]; laboratory studies and a meta-analysis mostly find high baseline negative emotion and null-to-small physiological reactivity [[Jacob2009,Kuo2009,Bortolla2020]]; context-specific reactivity (negative evaluation, rejection, shame) is the best-supported middle position [[Gratz2010,Hepp2018]]. Status: unresolved.
- Is "slow return to baseline" real? Evidence for weaker attractor strength in e-diaries is inconsistent and small [[EbnerPriemer2015]]; prolonged amygdala responses and lingering shame support it in specific contexts [[Hazlett2012,Kamphausen2013,Gratz2010]]. Status: Early evidence.
- Is affective instability specific to BPD? It is also heightened in PTSD, bulimia nervosa, anxiety disorders and ADHD [[Santangelo2014,Kockler2022,Moukhtarian2021]]; self-esteem instability may be more specific [[Kockler2022,Santangelo2020]]. Status: transdiagnostic view gaining ground.
- Amygdala hyper- or hypoactivity? Schulze2016 finds hyperactivity (in unmedicated samples) [[Schulze2016]]; Ruocco2013 finds less amygdala activation than controls [[Ruocco2013]]; reviewers question whether amygdala activity maps onto emotional experience at all [[Sicorello2021]]. Status: unresolved; small samples limit all [[Marek2022]].
- HPA axis: higher or lower cortisol? Elevated continuous output and blunted stress response [[Drews2019]] versus lower basal cortisol [[Thomas2019Cortisol]]. Status: method-dependent.
- Opioid and oxytocin models: plausible and partly supported by PET, CSF and single-dose trials [[Prossin2010,Stanley2010JAD,Bertsch2013AJP]], but oxytocin can also reduce trust in rejection-sensitive people [[Bartz2011]] and naltrexone did not significantly reduce dissociation [[Schmahl2012]]. Status: Hypothesis.
- Is hypermentalizing a BPD marker? Strongly linked in adolescents [[Sharp2011]], but not more strongly than in other disorders in meta-analysis [[McLaren2022]]. Status: likely transdiagnostic.
- Does self-injury actually regulate emotion? Retrospective reports and lab models support short-term relief [[Klonsky2007,Kleindienst2008,Willis2017]]; daily-life studies only partly confirm a post-act drop and one finds a later rise [[Hepp2020,Houben2017]]. Status: short-term relief established; longer-term worsening likely.
- Do lab measures predict daily life? Mostly not, apart from moderation by resting vagal tone [[Trull2022]]. Status: Early evidence.
