Risk factors & myths

A few things around pregnancy and birth are linked with a somewhat higher chance of autism; most of the links are small, and none is a reason for blame. Vaccines do not cause autism. See the factors on a forest plot, turn them into absolute numbers, and test the claims.

A "risk factor" is a statistical association in a large population. It is not a cause in any one family, and most children with any listed exposure are not autistic. Genes explain most of the variation in autism; see Genes & heritability. 34,286

Valproate: what the label says

The US Depakote label's boxed warning on fetal risk states that "valproate can cause decreased IQ scores and neurodevelopmental disorders following in utero exposure". Section 8.1 names autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability and ADHD among these risks. The label states valproate should not be used in pregnant women with epilepsy or bipolar disorder unless other medicines have failed or are unacceptable, and that it is contraindicated for migraine prophylaxis in pregnancy. 136

This is quoted as published, not as advice. Anyone taking valproate should not stop it without talking with their prescriber, ideally before a pregnancy. In Danish data the 14-year absolute risk of autism was 4.42% after exposure against 1.53% in all children; childhood autism, the narrower diagnosis, 2.50% against 0.48%. 85,136

Vaccines do not cause autism

This question has been studied in millions of children. MMR, thimerosal-containing vaccines and the number of vaccine antigens are not associated with autism. The 1998 paper that started the scare was retracted and judged fraudulent. Established 117,174,248,249,311,586

Association or cause? Paracetamol (acetaminophen) in pregnancy

A link in a whole population can come from something else that families share: genes, the mother's health, the reason the medicine was taken. Comparing brothers and sisters, who share much of that, is one way to check. Switch between the two analyses.

Autism Companion is an educational synthesis of the published literature on autism, made for health-science, medical and nursing courses, and for autistic people, families and caregivers.

It is not medical advice. It cannot diagnose anyone, and the questionnaires and criteria it explains are teaching tools, not tests to use on yourself or your child. Nothing here should change a support plan or a medicine without your own clinician. Criteria, doses and guideline statements are quoted as published.
