Before the visit

Contact before the visit

In an international survey, 80% of 507 autistic adults against 37% of 157 non-autistic adults reported difficulty visiting a GP. The top barriers were deciding whether symptoms warranted a visit (72%), booking by telephone (62%), not feeling understood (56%), communicating with the doctor (53%) and the waiting room (51%); barriers were linked to untreated conditions and late presentation. Autistic adults asked for online or text booking, sending the reason for the visit in advance, the first or last appointment of the day and a quiet place to wait. 120 After using the AASPIRE toolkit, which produces a personalised accommodations report, mean barriers fell from 4.07 to 2.82 (single-arm). 427

In plain wordsYou can ask for reasonable adjustments, such as booking online, a quiet place to wait or the first appointment of the day.

Pain when words are few: an observational walkthrough

Autistic children do feel and show pain: during venepuncture, 21 autistic children aged 3–7 showed a strong facial pain reaction, larger than non-autistic children's, and parents' global reports matched observed pain less well. 395 Observers judged pain from facial activity. 378 Observational tools help. Below, the five categories of the FLACC scale (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability), each scored 0–2, paraphrased from the published tool. 377 The revised r-FLACC lets parents add the person's own pain behaviours; in 52 children with cognitive impairment, inter-rater ICCs were 0.76–0.90 and scores fell from 6.1 to 1.9 after analgesia. 355

Assessment & documentationRead and update the hospital passport or accommodations report: communication, sensory needs, allergies, risks, needed adjustments, what upsets and what settles. Ask the family what the person's usual behaviour looks like, and record pain with an observational tool.A review of 60 UK passports found wide variation; some omitted allergies, risk assessment or reasonable adjustments. 429 The AASPIRE toolkit's accommodations report was read by 97% of providers. 427 Global parent report alone may miss pain. 395
MedicinesAsk how the person usually takes medicines: liquid or tablet, which drink, what time, from whom. Keep routines where safe. Crisis

Meltdown: de-escalation steps

  1. Treat it as overload, not defiance: autistic adults describe meltdowns as being overwhelmed by sensory, information and social-emotional load. 327
  2. Lower the load: fewer people, less noise and light, offer a quieter space. 346
  3. Fewer words, calm voice, no demands such as eye contact. 457
  4. Give room and time; many people try to get away or be alone to avoid harm. 327
  5. Ask the family what helps; then plan to prevent the next one. 577
Communication

Adapting how you talk

"Respect the way I need to communicate with you": autistic adults asked for written communication, time to process and providers who do not make wrong assumptions. 426 In an international survey, 72% of autistic adults found it hard to decide whether symptoms warranted a visit and 53% found it hard to communicate with the doctor. 120 For people who speak little, offer their own AAC: see Communication, AAC & therapies.

Going home

Discharge teaching

Give the plan in writing, with pictures if helpful: what happened, what to watch for, medicines, who to call and when. Update the passport with what worked today, so the next visit starts there. 427,429 Emergency-department passports were rated helpful and good for communication by staff and families. 217

Emergency

The emergency department

13% of ED visits by autistic children were for a psychiatric problem against 2% in other children (OR 9.13). 265 Autistic adolescents used the ED four times as often. 330 In autistic adults, 15% of ED visits were psychiatric against 4.2%. 615 A crisis-prevention model (START) was followed by fewer hospitalisations and ED visits (pre/post, no control). 266

Theatre

Anaesthesia and sedation

The anaesthesia literature on autism is mostly case reports; knowing each patient's behaviour and reducing stress are the main recommendations. 614 A five-session TEACCH-based programme increased compliance with a dental assessment in children and adults. 437 A social-script iPad app before imaging was linked to fewer externalised behaviours (effect size 0.56). 261

Families

Families as experts

Parents are their child's experts on what agitates and settles them; perioperative reviews recommend talking with them before the day. 577 With adults, agree how information is shared with family, and remember that adults make their own decisions. The AAP frames care as shared decision-making with families. 250
