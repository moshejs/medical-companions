# A · Epidemiology, identity, language & history

Pass date: 2026-11-02. Modules served: home, what, words, history.

This pass covers how common autism is (worldwide, in the US surveillance series and in adults), the sex ratio, why recorded rates rose, the language and identity debate, the double empathy problem, camouflaging (masking), neurodiversity, "profound autism", and the history of the concept from Bleuler (1911) to ICD-11 (2022). It closes with draft answers to nine FAQ questions.

Access notes:
- All numbers come from PubMed or Europe PMC abstracts, or from open full text in Europe PMC (GBD 2021: PMC11750762; Crompton 2020: PMC7545656; Bottema-Beutel 2021: PMC8992888; Keating 2023: PMC10946540).
- The WHO autism fact sheet ("about 1 in 127") was not used as a separate source. Its figure is the GBD 2021 estimate, so the guide cites Santomauro2025 directly.
- The Lancet Commission (Lord2022Lancet) has no PubMed abstract and the Lancet site blocked automated access. Its "profound autism" proposal is described from two validated secondary reports (Siegel2026, Yu2022) and the ADDM analysis that applied it (Hughes2023).
- Books are not indexed by the validation services: Bleuler 1911, Rimland 1964, Bettelheim 1967, the DSM-III and DSM-IV manuals, and Singer's writing on neurodiversity. Their dates are taken from validated secondary sources (Gundel1993, Rosen2021, BarahonaCorrea2015, Botha2024) or from contemporary book reviews validated in Crossref (Cowie1965, Chess1967).
- Kenny 2016 percentages are not in its abstract. The figures given here come from two open-access papers that quote it (BottemaBeutel2021Ableist, Keating2023).

Language note for builders: many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"). Many families and clinicians use person-first language ("person with autism"). This file uses both. "High/low functioning" appears only in quotes or when describing research on those labels.

## Findings

### what · How common is autism worldwide?
- The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021 estimated that 61.8 million people (95% uncertainty interval 52.1–72.7 million) were on the autism spectrum in 2021, or one in every 127 people [[Santomauro2025]].
- GBD 2021 global age-standardised prevalence was 788.3 per 100,000 (663.8–927.2). By sex it was 1,064.7 per 100,000 males (898.5–1,245.7) and 508.1 per 100,000 females (424.6–604.3) [[Santomauro2025]].
- From the GBD 2021 sex-specific rates, the modelled male-to-female prevalence ratio is about 2.1:1. This is our calculation (1,064.7 ÷ 508.1) [[Santomauro2025]].
- Autism accounted for 11.5 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) in 2021 (7.8–16.3 million), or 147.6 DALYs per 100,000 age-standardised. DALYs here measure health loss from living with the condition. They do not judge the worth of autistic lives [[Santomauro2025]].
- GBD 2021 ranked autism among the top ten causes of non-fatal health loss under age 20:
  - 7th for children younger than 5 years (4.3% of years lived with disability)
  - 8th at ages 5–14 (3.4%)
  - 10th at ages 15–19 (2.2%) [[Santomauro2025]]
- Super-region differences in GBD 2021 (per 100,000):
  - highest: high-income super-region, 1,090.2; within it, high-income Asia Pacific 1,559.5 and Japan 1,586.9
  - lowest: southeast Asia, east Asia and Oceania, 669.2; China 655.7
  - lowest region: tropical Latin America, 614.5
  - lowest country estimate: Bangladesh, 588.2 [[Santomauro2025]]
- GBD 2021 age-standardised prevalence was 773.2 per 100,000 in 1990 and 788.3 in 2021, almost flat. Population growth raised total DALYs from 7.9 million (1990) to 11.5 million (2021), while the age-standardised DALY rate stayed about the same (144.5 vs 147.6 per 100,000) [[Santomauro2025]].
- The GBD authors say their US estimate is "more conservative" than the CDC's 1 in 36 for 8-year-olds in 2020. Their prevalence models included only six studies of adults aged 20 or older [[Santomauro2025]].
- The earlier GBD 2010 estimate was 52 million cases (7.6 per 1,000, one in 132 people), with "no clear evidence of a change in prevalence" between 1990 and 2010 after adjusting for methods [[Baxter2015]].
- Zeidan 2022 systematic review (99 estimates from 71 studies published 2012–2021):
  - median global prevalence 100 per 10,000, about 1 in 100 (range 1.09 to 436.0 per 10,000)
  - median male-to-female ratio 4.2
  - median share with co-occurring intellectual disability 33.0% [[Zeidan2022]]
- The previous global review (Elsabbagh 2012) gave a median of 62 per 10,000. It found no clear differences by region, ethnicity or socioeconomic status, but evidence from low- and middle-income countries was thin [[Elsabbagh2012]].
- A three-level meta-analysis of studies from 1994 to 2019 pooled autism spectrum disorder prevalence at 0.72% (95% CI 0.61–0.85). Estimates were higher in North America, in high-income countries, and in studies that used records-review surveillance [[Talantseva2023]].
- Global figures differ mainly because of case-finding methods, awareness and service capacity. Zeidan 2022 attributes the variation to "community awareness, service capacity, help seeking, and sociodemographic factors" (Established) [[Zeidan2022,Elsabbagh2012]].

### what · The US ADDM surveillance series (8-year-olds)
- CDC's Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring (ADDM) Network reviews health records, and at most sites also education records, for 8-year-olds in selected US communities. It is not a nationally representative sample [[Baio2018,ADDM2014SY2010]].
- Prevalence by surveillance year, per 1,000 8-year-olds:
  - 2000: 6.7 (6 sites; about 1 in 150) [[ADDM2007SY2000]]
  - 2002: 6.6 (14 sites; "one of every 152") [[ADDM2007SY2002]]
  - 2006: 9.0 (11 sites; 1 in 110) [[ADDM2009SY2006]]
  - 2008: 11.3 (14 sites; 1 in 88) [[ADDM2012SY2008]]
  - 2010: 14.7 (11 sites; 1 in 68) [[ADDM2014SY2010]]
  - 2012: 14.6 (11 sites; 1 in 68) [[Christensen2016]]
  - 2014: 16.8 (11 sites; 1 in 59) [[Baio2018]]
  - 2016: 18.5 (11 sites; 1 in 54) [[Maenner2020]]
  - 2018: 23.0 (11 sites; 1 in 44) [[Maenner2021]]
  - 2020: 27.6 (11 sites; 1 in 36) [[Maenner2023]]
  - 2022: 32.2 (16 sites; 1 in 31) [[Shaw2025]]
- In 2022, prevalence across the 16 sites ranged from 9.7 per 1,000 in Laredo, Texas, to 53.1 in California, more than a fivefold difference [[Shaw2025]].
- Sites that could review both education and health records found higher prevalence than sites with health records only:
  - 2002: 7.2 vs 5.1 per 1,000 [[ADDM2007SY2002]]
  - 2006: 10.0 vs 7.5 [[ADDM2009SY2006]]
  - 2012: 17.1 vs 10.7 [[Christensen2016]]
- Between 2002 and 2006, average prevalence rose 57% across 10 sites. The CDC wrote that improved ascertainment explains some of the rise but "a true increase in the risk ... cannot be ruled out" [[ADDM2009SY2006]].
- Between 2002 and 2008, prevalence rose an estimated 78%, from 6.4 to 11.4 per 1,000 [[ADDM2012SY2008]].
- When the 2014 records were scored with both a DSM-IV-TR and a DSM-5 surveillance case definition, DSM-IV-TR counts were less than 5% higher. The two definitions overlapped by about 86% (kappa 0.85) [[Baio2018]].
- In 2022, only 34.6% of 8-year-olds counted as autistic had all three elements of the ADDM case definition in their records. Documented autism tests ranged from 24.7% of autistic children in New Jersey to 93.5% in Puerto Rico [[Shaw2025]].
- Parent report gives similar figures: in the 2016 National Survey of Children's Health, parents of 2.50% of US children aged 3–17 (about 1.5 million; 1 in 40) said their child had ever received an autism diagnosis and still had it [[Kogan2018]].

### what · Boys and girls in the ADDM data
- Boy-to-girl prevalence ratio by surveillance year:
  - 2000: 2.8:1 to 5.5:1 across sites [[ADDM2007SY2000]]
  - 2006: 3.2:1 (Alabama) to 7.6:1 (Florida) [[ADDM2009SY2006]]
  - 2012: 23.6 vs 5.3 per 1,000 [[Christensen2016]]
  - 2014: about 4 times as likely [[Baio2018]]
  - 2016: 4.3 times [[Maenner2020]]
  - 2018: 4.2 times [[Maenner2021]]
  - 2020: 3.8 times (43.0 vs 11.4 per 1,000) [[Maenner2023]]
  - 2022: 3.4 times (49.2 vs 14.3 per 1,000) [[Shaw2025]]
- The boy-to-girl gap in ADDM narrowed from about 4.3 in 2016 to 3.4 in 2022. The 2020 report links the continued rise particularly to "non-White children and girls" [[Maenner2020,Shaw2025,Maenner2023]].

### what · Race and ethnicity shifts in the ADDM data
- 2010: non-Hispanic White children were about 30% more likely than non-Hispanic Black children, and almost 50% more likely than Hispanic children, to be identified as autistic [[ADDM2014SY2010]].
- 2012: White 15.5, Black 13.2, Hispanic 10.1 per 1,000 [[Christensen2016]].
- 2016: White, Black and Asian/Pacific Islander (A/PI) prevalence were about equal (18.5, 18.3, 17.9 per 1,000). Hispanic prevalence was lower (15.4) [[Maenner2020]].
- 2020: for the first time among 8-year-olds, prevalence was lower in White children (24.3 per 1,000) than in Black (29.3), Hispanic (31.6) and A/PI children (33.4), "reversing the direction" of earlier differences [[Maenner2023]].
- 2022: White 27.7, multiracial 31.9, Hispanic 33.0, Black 36.6, American Indian or Alaska Native 37.5, A/PI 38.2 per 1,000 [[Shaw2025]].
- The ADDM reports attribute the earlier lower rates in Black and Hispanic children to under-identification and unequal access to evaluation. For example, in 2016 Black children were less likely than White children to have a first evaluation by 36 months (40% vs 45%) (Established) [[Maenner2020,Christensen2016]].

### what · Intellectual disability and age at diagnosis in the ADDM data
- Share of autistic 8-year-olds with intellectual disability (IQ ≤70), among those with IQ data:
  - 2000: 40%–62% (3 sites) [[ADDM2007SY2000]]
  - 2010: 31% [[ADDM2014SY2010]]
  - 2014: 31% (a further 25% borderline at IQ 71–85; 44% IQ >85) [[Baio2018]]
  - 2016: 33% [[Maenner2020]]
  - 2020: 37.9% [[Maenner2023]]
  - 2022: 39.6% [[Shaw2025]]
- Co-occurring intellectual disability differs by group. In 2022 it was recorded in 52.8% of autistic Black children, 50.0% of American Indian or Alaska Native, 43.9% of A/PI, 38.8% of Hispanic, 32.7% of White and 31.2% of multiracial children [[Shaw2025]].
- Swedish register data show the opposite trend in diagnosed populations. From 2001 to 2020, registered autism rose 800% and the share with a recorded intellectual disability fell from 55.8% to 6.7% [[Salkic2026]].
- Median age at earliest known autism diagnosis in ADDM:
  - 2000: 52–56 months [[ADDM2007SY2000]]
  - 2010: 53 months [[ADDM2014SY2010]]
  - 2014: 52 months [[Baio2018]]
  - 2016: 51 months [[Maenner2020]]
  - 2020: 49 months [[Maenner2023]]
  - 2022: 47 months, ranging from 36 months in California to 69.5 months in Laredo [[Shaw2025]]
- Children with intellectual disability were diagnosed earlier: median 43 vs 53 months in 2020 [[Maenner2023]].
- Early identification is improving. Cumulative incidence of diagnosis or eligibility by 48 months was 1.7 times as high in children born in 2018 as in those born in 2014 [[Shaw2025]].
- In 2014, 85% of autistic 8-year-olds had developmental concerns recorded by 36 months, but only 42% had a comprehensive evaluation by then [[Baio2018]].
- Worldwide, a meta-analysis of 56 studies from 40 countries (120,540 autistic people, published 2012–2019) found a mean age at diagnosis of 60.48 months, or 43.18 months in studies limited to children aged 10 years or younger [[vantHof2021]].

### what · Adults: autism is lifelong
- England's 2007 Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey (7,461 adults; 618 diagnostic assessments) estimated adult prevalence at 9.8 per 1,000 (95% CI 3.0–16.5). Prevalence "was not related to the respondent's age" [[Brugha2011]].
- Adding a case-register survey of adults with intellectual disability gave a combined prevalence of 11 per 1,000 adults (about 1.1%; 95% CI 3–19). Autism was far more common in moderate to profound intellectual disability (OR 63.5). Male sex predicted autism only among adults with no or mild intellectual disability (adjusted OR 8.5) [[Brugha2016]].
- Brugha and colleagues read the lack of an age gradient as "consistent with there having been no increase in prevalence". Most autistic adults in the community were "unrecognized" (Early evidence) [[Brugha2011]].
- A CDC modelling study estimated that 2.21% of US adults (95% simulation interval 1.95–2.45%), about 5,437,988 people aged 18 or older, were autistic in 2017. State estimates ranged from 1.97% (Louisiana) to 2.42% (Massachusetts). This figure includes undiagnosed adults [[Dietz2020]].
- In English primary care in 2018:
  - 2.94% of 10–14-year-olds had a recorded autism diagnosis (1 in 34), compared with 0.02% of people aged 70 or older (1 in 6,000)
  - projections suggested 59–72% of autistic people in England were undiagnosed (between 435,700 and 1,197,300 people) [[ONions2023]]
- New diagnoses in English primary care in 2018 were about 1 in 250 5–9-year-olds, 1 in 4,000 20–49-year-olds, and 1 in 18,000 people aged 50 or older [[ONions2023]].
- A JAMA review gives US prevalence as about 2.3% of 8-year-olds and about 2.2% of adults [[Hirota2023]].

### what · Sex ratio: about 3:1, and why clinic figures are higher
- A meta-analysis of 54 prevalence studies (13,784,284 participants; 53,712 autistic) found a pooled male-to-female odds ratio of 4.20 (95% CI 3.84–4.60), with very high heterogeneity (I² 90.9%) [[Loomes2017]].
- High-quality studies gave a lower ratio of 3.32 (2.88–3.84) [[Loomes2017]].
- Studies that screened the general population gave 3.25 (2.93–3.62). Studies that counted only people who already had a diagnosis gave 4.56 (4.10–5.07) [[Loomes2017]].
- The authors conclude the true ratio is "closer to 3:1" than 4:1. They describe a "diagnostic gender bias" in which girls who meet criteria are at disproportionate risk of not being diagnosed (Established for the gap; mechanism debated) [[Loomes2017]].
- In UK primary care, new diagnoses rose faster in females than in males between 1998 and 2018 (exponentiated interaction coefficient 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03) [[Russell2022]].
- Camouflaging is one proposed reason for later recognition in girls and women (Early evidence; see the words section below) [[Hull2020Gender,Lai2017Camouflaging]]. Its link to the "female autism phenotype" is disputed [[Fombonne2020Camouflage,Lai2021Commentary]].

### what · Why recorded rates rose
- Denmark (677,915 children born 1980–1991) attributed recorded prevalence change to two reporting changes: the 1994 change in diagnostic criteria and the 1995 inclusion of outpatient contacts. Together they explained 60% (95% CI 33–87%) of the increase. The criteria change alone explained 33% (0–70%) and outpatient inclusion alone 42% (14–69%) (Established) [[Hansen2015Reporting]].
- California (7,003 patients, 1992–2005): about 26.4% (95% CI 16.25–36.48) of the increased autism caseload was "uniquely associated with diagnostic change". This is the share of people previously diagnosed with intellectual disability ("mental retardation" in the source) who were later re-diagnosed as autistic, through that single pathway [[King2009]].
- Sweden (1993–2002 births): the autism symptom phenotype, measured by parental interview in 19,993 twins, stayed stable (P=0.87 for trend). Over the same years, registered diagnoses among 1,078,975 children rose steadily (P<0.001) (Established) [[Lundstrom2015]].
- In UK primary care, recorded new diagnoses rose 787% between 1998 and 2018 (R² 0.98). The steepest rises were in adults and females. The authors judge "increased reporting and application of diagnosis" more likely than true growth [[Russell2022]].
- Group differences in autism research have shrunk over time. Effect sizes for emotion recognition, theory of mind, planning, P3b amplitude and brain size fell significantly with publication year, but analogous measures in schizophrenia did not. This is consistent with a broadening, more heterogeneous autistic population (Early evidence) [[Rodgaard2019]].
- Reviews list the main drivers of the US rise as changes in diagnostic criteria, better screening and diagnostic tools, and greater public awareness. US prevalence went from 1.1% in 2008 to 2.3% in 2018 [[Hirota2023]].
- Concept change over four decades went from a narrow definition to wide criteria and from rare to common. Autism came to be seen as lifelong, dimensional and often co-occurring with other conditions, and the neurodiversity perspective emerged [[Happe2020]].

### what · Other numbers students meet
- More than 70% of autistic people have at least one co-occurring condition [[Lai2014]].
- Pooled prevalence of diagnosed co-occurring mental health conditions in autistic people:
  - ADHD 28% (95% CI 25–32)
  - anxiety disorders 20% (17–23)
  - sleep-wake disorders 13% (9–17)
  - depressive disorders 11% (9–13)
  - obsessive-compulsive disorder 9% (7–10) [[Lai2019]]
- In a Swedish register cohort, 2.60% of autistic people died during follow-up vs 0.91% of matched controls (OR 2.56, 95% CI 2.38–2.76). This is a population statistic, not a forecast for any one person [[Hirvikoski2016]].

### words · Identity-first or person-first?
- UK survey (Kenny 2016; 3,470 respondents: autistic people, parents, wider family and friends, professionals):
  - the most widely endorsed terms were "autism" and "on the autism spectrum"
  - "autistic" was endorsed by many autistic adults, family members and parents but by fewer professionals
  - "person with autism" was endorsed by almost half of professionals but by fewer autistic adults and parents [[Kenny2016]]
- The UK consensus was not universal. About 40% of autistic respondents in Kenny 2016 did not endorse "autistic", and over 50% did not endorse "autistic person" [[Keating2023]].
- US survey: autistic adults (n = 299) "overwhelmingly" preferred identity-first terms. Professionals (n = 207) were more likely to support and use person-first terms [[Taboas2023]].
- Australia (198 adults with an autism diagnosis): those with a stronger autism identity found identity-first terms more preferable. Those reporting more experience and internalisation of stigma found identity-first terms less preferable and more offensive. Neither factor was associated with views on person-first terms [[Bury2023]].
- Global English-speaking survey (654 autistic adults): "autism", "autistic person", "is autistic", "neurotypical people" and "differences" were consistently favoured across countries. The guiding principle was to respect personal preference [[Keating2023]].
- Systematic review (19 studies, N = 6,350):
  - 10 of 14 studies found more participants preferred identity-first than person-first language, but 4–39% still endorsed person-first
  - the 2 studies that favoured person-first were conducted in Dutch
  - identity-first terms were rated acceptable by about 40–97% of participants, person-first by 5–53%
  - the authors found "no consensus", noted that people with intellectual disability were under-represented, and advised respecting individual preferences [[Schuck2025]]
- In scholarly writing, person-first language is used more often for children with disabilities than for children without. It is used most for the most stigmatised conditions, so it "may actually accentuate stigma" (Early evidence) [[Gernsbacher2017]].

### words · Functioning labels and ableist language
- Functioning-level labels are endorsed by few autistic adults in the UK: "high-functioning" by about 20%, "low-functioning" by under 10% [[BottemaBeutel2021Ableist,Keating2023]].
- In 2,225 children aged 1–18 assessed at diagnosis (1,041 with and 1,184 without intellectual disability), IQ was "a weak predictor" of adaptive behaviour (Vineland) [[Alvares2020]].
- In children without intellectual disability in the same cohort, Vineland adaptive scores fell well below IQ, and the gap stayed large at older ages at diagnosis. The authors argue "'high functioning autism' is an inaccurate clinical descriptor" when based on IQ alone (Established) [[Alvares2020]].
- Bottema-Beutel and colleagues define ableist language as language that "assumes disabled people are inferior to nondisabled people". They suggest these alternatives:
  - "focused" or "intense" interests for "special interests"
  - "co-occurring" for "comorbid"
  - "increased likelihood" for "at risk"
  - "impact" for "suffering from"
  - "support" or "services" for "treatment" [[BottemaBeutel2021Ableist]]
- In 400 members of the general public, puzzle pieces, including autism puzzle-piece logos, evoked negative implicit associations (t(399) = −5.357, p < 0.001) and negative explicit associations (d = 0.491). Participants linked them with "incompleteness, imperfection, and oddity" [[Gernsbacher2018Puzzle]].

### words · Medical, social and neurodiversity models
- The neurodiversity paradigm treats autism as one form of variation in a diversity of minds. It criticises the conventional medical paradigm for an overfocus on deficits, for locating difficulties in the individual rather than their context, and for a narrow perspective [[Pellicano2022]].
- In an online survey (N = 657), identifying as autistic and knowing about neurodiversity were associated with seeing autism as a positive identity "that needs no cure". Groups still overlapped substantially in recognising autism's difficulties, which supports a "deficit-as-difference" view (Early evidence) [[Kapp2013]].
- An autistic researcher describes neurodiversity as accepting autism as natural variation while still supporting people with their disabilities [[denHouting2019]].
- The concept of neurodiversity is often credited to the Australian sociologist Judy Singer (late 1990s). Botha and colleagues present archival evidence that "neurological diversity" was in use earlier and conclude the concept was "collectively developed by neurodivergent people" [[Botha2024]].
- The 2014 Lancet seminar ends with "creation of a supportive environment that accepts and respects that the individual is different is crucial" [[Lai2014]].

### words · The double empathy problem
- Damian Milton, an autistic sociologist, described the "double empathy problem" in 2012. It reframes social difficulty as a two-way mismatch between autistic and non-autistic people, not a one-sided deficit (Hypothesis, with supporting studies below) [[Milton2012,Milton2022]].
- Mitchell and colleagues propose a transactional account. Misperceptions of autistic people by the neurotypical majority lead to their exclusion, which in turn harms autistic people's mental health (Hypothesis) [[Mitchell2021]].
- Crompton 2020 diffusion-chain study (72 adults in nine eight-person chains: 3 autistic, 3 non-autistic, 3 mixed):
  - a 30-detail story was passed along each chain
  - mean details recalled: autistic chains 12.96, non-autistic chains 12.40 (no difference, Tukey p = 0.92), mixed chains 8.92 (significantly fewer)
  - detail was lost faster along mixed chains
  - rapport ratings were lower in mixed chains [[Crompton2020]]
- A larger pre-registered replication (N = 311) found no difference in information transfer between single-neurotype and mixed chains. Non-autistic chains reported higher rapport, and disclosing diagnosis improved rapport. The authors call this a contrast with the earlier result (Early evidence: mixed) [[Crompton2025]].
- In groups of four (143 adults), all-autistic groups had rapport similar to all-non-autistic groups. They rated enjoyment and friendliness higher than mixed groups did. Autistic participants' rapport fell as more non-autistic people joined [[Foster2025]].
- Non-autistic observers formed less favourable first impressions of autistic people from seconds-long video "thin slices" and were less willing to interact with them. The bias disappeared when they judged transcripts alone, suggesting that "style, not substance" drives it (Established across three studies) [[Sasson2017]].

### words · Camouflaging (masking)
- Interviews with 92 autistic adults gave a three-stage model of camouflaging:
  - motivations: fitting in and connecting with others
  - techniques: masking and compensation
  - consequences: exhaustion, challenging stereotypes and threats to self-perception [[Hull2017]]
- The Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire (CAT-Q) has 25 items in three factors. It was developed in 354 autistic and 478 non-autistic adults, with internal consistency α = 0.94 and test-retest r = 0.77. It is a research measure, not a diagnostic test [[Hull2019CATQ]].
- Autistic females reported more camouflaging than autistic males (partial η² = 0.08), with higher Masking and Assimilation subscale scores. There was no gender difference among non-autistic adults [[Hull2020Gender]].
- Measured as the gap between outward presentation and inner traits, camouflaging was higher in autistic women than men (Cohen's d = 0.98), with wide variability in both groups [[Lai2017Camouflaging]].
- A systematic review of 29 studies found three preliminary patterns. Adults with more autistic traits camouflage more. Sex and gender differences exist. Higher self-reported camouflaging goes with worse mental health. Samples were not representative of the whole autistic community (Early evidence) [[Cook2021Camouflaging]].
- In a qualitative survey, 277 autistic adults described camouflaging as both protective (access to social spaces, safety) and harmful (exhaustion, isolation, loss of identity, delayed diagnosis). The time spent camouflaging seemed most damaging [[Bradley2021]].
- In 180 UK undergraduates, camouflaging explained a further 3.2% of the variance in defeat and entrapment after autistic traits (1.1%). Camouflaging, defeat and entrapment together mediated the link between autistic traits and lifetime suicidal thoughts. This was a cross-sectional student sample (Early evidence) [[Cassidy2023Camouflaging]].
- Autistic adults describe autistic burnout as chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to stimuli. Unmasking and acceptance were linked with recovery (Early evidence) [[Raymaker2020]].
- The construct is debated. Fombonne questions camouflaging's construct validity and its use to explain late diagnosis and a "female autism phenotype" [[Fombonne2020Camouflage]]. Lai and colleagues reply that better measurement is warranted, that subjective experience matters for adult diagnosis, and that camouflaging "is not necessarily a feature of autism in female individuals" [[Lai2021Commentary]].

### words · "Profound autism"
- The 2021 Lancet Commission on the future of care and clinical research in autism was written by 32 authors from 6 continents and 13 disciplines, including autistic people and parents. It calls for personalised, stepped and accessible care and for more appreciation of neurodiversity [[Lord2022Lancet,Yu2022]].
- The Commission proposed "profound autism" as an administrative term for autistic people who need 24-hour access to an adult to stay safe. The initial definition included a minimum age and suggested considering IQ and verbal ability [[Lord2022Lancet,Siegel2026]].
- Applying a working definition (nonverbal or minimally verbal, or IQ <50) to ADDM data for 20,135 autistic 8-year-olds (2000–2016), 26.7% had profound autism. Its prevalence in 2016 was 4.6 per 1,000 8-year-olds [[Hughes2023]].
- Compared with others in the ADDM data, children with profound autism were more likely to be female, from racial and ethnic minority groups, of low socioeconomic status, born preterm or with low birth weight, and to have self-injury or seizures [[Hughes2023]].
- A 2026 Delphi study reached 76% agreement on a research definition. Its parts are age 8 or older, adaptive functioning well below age level, needing adult supervision for safety, and IQ below 50 and/or not communicating verbally beyond single words or fixed phrases [[Siegel2026]].
- A 2026 bioRxiv preprint (not peer reviewed) found no genomic, transcriptomic or regulatory evidence that profound autism is a biologically distinct group (Early evidence) [[Eicher2026]].

### history · Before Kanner
- 1911: the Swiss psychiatrist Eugen Bleuler introduced "autism" as a term within schizophrenia [[Gundel1993]]. It was one of his "4 A's" (association, affect, ambivalence, autism) [[Moskowitz2011]].
- 1926: Grunya Efimovna Sukhareva (1891–1981), a child psychiatrist in Kyiv, published detailed descriptions of children with autistic traits in a German journal. She first called this "schizoid (eccentric) psychopathy" and later "autistic (pathological avoidant) psychopathy" [[Manouilenko2015,Ssucharewa1996]].
- Sukhareva's paper appeared almost two decades before Kanner's and Asperger's and was long overlooked. An English translation was published in 1996 [[Pozzi2024,Ssucharewa1996]].
- By 1943, calling children "autistic" or lacking affective contact was not new. What was new in Kanner's proposal was an inborn condition distinct from childhood schizophrenia [[Vicedo2021]].

### history · Kanner and Asperger
- 1943: Leo Kanner in Baltimore published "Autistic disturbances of affective contact", the first systematic description of early infantile autism, based on 11 children [[Kanner1968,Kanner1971,Harris2018Kanner]].
- Kanner wrote that these children "have come into the world with an innate inability to form the usual, biologically provided contact with people" [[Harris2018Kanner]].
- 1938: Kanner examined the first child in his case series [[Olmsted2016]].
- 1971: Kanner published a follow-up of the 11 children originally reported in 1943 [[Kanner1971]].
- 1938 and 1944: Hans Asperger in Vienna first described "autistic psychopaths" in 1938. His full study was published in 1944 [[Czech2018,Asperger1944]].
- Asperger's work found international recognition only in the 1980s [[Czech2018]].
- Czech 2018 examined Asperger's personnel files and his clinical assessments of patients. Findings:
  - Asperger accommodated himself to the Nazi regime and was rewarded with career opportunities
  - he joined several organisations affiliated with the Nazi party (NSDAP), though not the party itself
  - he publicly legitimised race-hygiene policies, including forced sterilisation
  - on several occasions he actively cooperated with the child "euthanasia" programme
  - his diagnostic language was often harsh [[Czech2018]]
- Czech concludes that the story of Asperger as a defender of his patients "does not hold up". Future use of the eponym "should reflect the troubling context of its origins" (Established as a historical finding; interpretation debated in later exchanges) [[Czech2018]].

### history · The "refrigerator mother" era and its reversal
- 1958: the child psychologist Bruno Bettelheim saw Harry Harlow's wire-mother monkeys as a perfect example of the "refrigerator mother", whom he held responsible for her child's autism. His solution was to remove the mother [[vanRosmalen2020]].
- 1967: Bettelheim's book "The Empty Fortress: Infantile Autism and the Birth of the Self" was reviewed in JAMA in 1967 [[Chess1967]].
- Parent-blaming theories are now rejected. Autism is highly heritable: a Swedish cohort reanalysis estimated heritability at about 83% (Established) [[Sandin2017,Lord2018]].
- 1964: the psychologist and parent Bernard Rimland published "Infantile Autism: The Syndrome and Its Implications for a Neural Theory of Behavior". It argued for a biological, neural basis and was reviewed in the British Journal of Psychiatry in 1965 [[Cowie1965]].
- Historians describe the usual US story ("parent-blaming psychogenic causes ... supplanted" by organic research) as simplified. They argue that factors outside science also shaped how the category grew [[Jacobsen2010]].

### history · From Wing to DSM-5 and ICD-11
- 1960s: in Britain, "autism" changed from a term for infant fantasy life, linked with schizophrenia and psychoanalysis, to an epidemiological category. Researchers such as Victor Lotter and Michael Rutter drove the change [[Evans2013]].
- 1979: Lorna Wing and Judith Gould surveyed children under 15 in an area of London (the Camberwell study). They found that a subgroup with "Kanner's early childhood autism" shared many features with other socially impaired children. They proposed classification by quality of social interaction, which laid the groundwork for the spectrum idea [[WingGould1979]].
- 1981: Wing's "Asperger's syndrome: a clinical account" brought Asperger's syndrome to English-language readers. She grouped it with early childhood autism in a wider family of conditions sharing impaired social interaction, communication and imagination [[Wing1981]].
- 1980: autism entered the American Psychiatric Association's DSM-III as an official diagnostic category for the first time. Kanner reviewed the DSM-III criteria that year [[Rosen2021,Harris2018Kanner]].
- 1994: Asperger syndrome was added to DSM-IV [[BarahonaCorrea2015]].
- 2013: DSM-5 merged Asperger syndrome and the other subtypes into one category, autism spectrum disorder [[BarahonaCorrea2015,APA2013]].
- DSM-5 marked "a significant departure" from DSM-IV, swinging from "splitting" into subgroups back to "lumping" [[Volkmar2014,Rosen2021]].
- 2022: DSM-5-TR, a text revision of DSM-5, was published [[APA2022]].
- ICD-11 was approved by the World Health Assembly in May 2019. Health reporting under it began on 1 January 2022 [[Reed2019ICD11]].
- 1998: a 12-child case series in The Lancet suggested a link between MMR vaccination and developmental regression [[Wakefield1998]].
- 2010: The Lancet retracted the 1998 paper [[Lancet2010Retraction]].
- 2011: the BMJ concluded the paper was fraudulent [[Godlee2011]].
- Large studies since then find no link between vaccines and autism (Established):
  - a Danish cohort of 657,461 children: MMR adjusted hazard ratio 0.93 (95% CI 0.85–1.02) [[Hviid2019]]
  - a meta-analysis of 1,256,407 children in cohort studies: OR 0.99 (0.92–1.06) for vaccination and autism [[Taylor2014Vaccine]]

### Exam hooks
- Worldwide, about 1 in 127 people (61.8 million) were autistic in 2021 (GBD 2021) [[Santomauro2025]].
- ADDM 2022: 32.2 per 1,000 8-year-olds (1 in 31), 16 US sites; boys 3.4 times girls [[Shaw2025]].
- ADDM rose from 6.7 per 1,000 (2000; about 1 in 150) to 32.2 per 1,000 (2022) [[ADDM2007SY2000,Shaw2025]].
- ADDM 2020 was the first year that White 8-year-olds had lower identified prevalence than Black, Hispanic and A/PI children [[Maenner2023]].
- 39.6% of autistic 8-year-olds with IQ data had intellectual disability in ADDM 2022 [[Shaw2025]]; the global review median is 33.0% [[Zeidan2022]].
- Median age at earliest known diagnosis in ADDM 2022 was 47 months [[Shaw2025]].
- True male-to-female ratio: about 3:1. Studies of diagnosed cases give about 4.5:1, because girls who meet criteria are missed [[Loomes2017]].
- Danish register: 60% of the rise in recorded prevalence was explained by reporting changes (the 1994 criteria change and the 1995 inclusion of outpatients) [[Hansen2015Reporting]].
- In Swedish children, the symptom phenotype stayed stable while registered diagnoses rose [[Lundstrom2015]].
- Adult prevalence in England is about 1.1% and does not fall with age, which is consistent with autism being lifelong [[Brugha2016,Brugha2011]].
- Bleuler coined "autism" in 1911 to describe a symptom of schizophrenia [[Gundel1993]].
- Sukhareva described autistic children in 1926, before Kanner (1943) and Asperger (1944) [[Manouilenko2015]].
- Kanner's 1943 paper described 11 children [[Kanner1971]].
- Wing and Gould (1979) laid the basis of the spectrum concept. Wing (1981) introduced Asperger's syndrome to English-language readers [[WingGould1979,Wing1981]].
- DSM timeline: DSM-III (1980) first official category; DSM-IV (1994) added Asperger's; DSM-5 (2013) merged the subtypes into autism spectrum disorder [[Rosen2021,BarahonaCorrea2015]].
- Double empathy (Milton 2012): mixed autistic/non-autistic diffusion chains passed on fewer story details (8.92 vs about 12–13 of 30), but a larger 2025 replication found no difference [[Milton2012,Crompton2020,Crompton2025]].
- The CAT-Q has 25 items and three factors (compensation, masking, assimilation). It is a research measure, not a diagnostic tool [[Hull2019CATQ]].
- In an ADDM analysis, 26.7% of autistic 8-year-olds (2000–2016) met a working definition of profound autism [[Hughes2023]].
- Vaccines do not cause autism. Danish MMR cohort: adjusted HR 0.93 (0.85–1.02) in 657,461 children [[Hviid2019]].

### In plain words
- Autism is a lifelong difference in how a person communicates, relates to others and experiences the world. About 1 in 127 people worldwide are autistic [[Santomauro2025,Lord2018]].
- In parts of the US in 2022, about 1 in 31 eight-year-olds had been identified as autistic. Most of the rise over 20 years comes from better recognition, not from something new causing autism [[Shaw2025,Hansen2015Reporting,Lundstrom2015]].
- Girls and women are autistic too. They are more often missed, sometimes because they learn to hide their differences [[Loomes2017,Hull2020Gender]].
- Many adults find out they are autistic later in life. In England most autistic adults may not have a diagnosis [[ONions2023]].
- Some people say "autistic person" and others say "person with autism". Neither is wrong. The respectful thing is to ask what each person prefers [[Kenny2016,Keating2023,Schuck2025]].
- Labels like "high functioning" can hide real needs. A person's IQ does not tell you how much help they need day to day [[Alvares2020]].
- When autistic and non-autistic people misunderstand each other, the gap runs both ways [[Milton2012,Crompton2020]].
- Hiding autistic traits all day ("masking") can be exhausting and is linked to poorer mental health. Places where people can relax and be themselves help [[Cook2021Camouflaging,Bradley2021,Raymaker2020]].
- Vaccines do not cause autism. Very large studies of hundreds of thousands of children show this [[Hviid2019,Taylor2014Vaccine]].
- Parents do not cause autism through how they love or raise their child. That old "refrigerator mother" idea was wrong [[vanRosmalen2020,Sandin2017]].
- Autism was named and renamed many times between 1911 and 2013. Today doctors use one umbrella term, autism spectrum disorder [[Gundel1993,BarahonaCorrea2015,APA2022]].
- An online quiz cannot tell you whether you are autistic. Only a full assessment by trained professionals can [[Hyman2020,Hirota2023]].

### FAQ (draft answers, cited; builders remove citation markers for the FAQ block and JSON-LD)
1. **What is autism?** Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference. It affects social communication and interaction, and it shows in focused interests, repetitive behaviours, routines and sensory differences. It shows up in early childhood and looks very different from person to person [[Lord2018,Hirota2023,APA2022]].
2. **How common is autism?** About 1 in 127 people worldwide (GBD 2021) [[Santomauro2025]]. Global studies give a median of about 1 in 100 children [[Zeidan2022]]. In 16 US communities in 2022, 1 in 31 eight-year-olds had been identified [[Shaw2025]].
3. **Is autism becoming more common?** Diagnoses have risen a lot. In the US monitoring network they went from about 1 in 150 (2000) to 1 in 31 (2022) [[ADDM2007SY2000,Shaw2025]]. Studies find that most of this comes from wider criteria, better reporting and more awareness [[Hansen2015Reporting,Lundstrom2015]]. Global estimates adjusted for these changes are almost flat since 1990 [[Santomauro2025]]. A small true rise cannot be fully ruled out [[ADDM2009SY2006]].
4. **Do vaccines cause autism?** No. A Danish study of 657,461 children found no increased risk after MMR vaccination [[Hviid2019]]. Pooled studies of more than 1.2 million children found no link [[Taylor2014Vaccine]]. The 1998 paper that started the scare was retracted and judged fraudulent [[Lancet2010Retraction,Godlee2011]].
5. **Is autism more common in boys?** Boys are diagnosed about 3 to 4 times as often [[Shaw2025]]. When whole populations are screened, the ratio is closer to 3:1, which suggests many girls are missed [[Loomes2017]].
6. **Can adults be diagnosed?** Yes. About 1 in 100 adults in England are autistic, at every age [[Brugha2016,Brugha2011]]. In England, an estimated 59–72% of autistic people had no diagnosis in 2018 [[ONions2023]]. Adult diagnoses are rising fastest [[Russell2022]].
7. **Should I say "autistic person" or "person with autism"?** Many autistic adults prefer "autistic person". Many professionals and some families use "person with autism". Studies find no single choice that suits everyone, so ask the person [[Kenny2016,Taboas2023,Schuck2025,Keating2023]].
8. **Does autism go away, or can it be cured?** Autism is lifelong. Adult prevalence is similar to that in children and does not fall with age [[Brugha2011]]. Supports aim at communication, wellbeing and participation, and many autistic people now speak, read and live in the community [[Lord2018,Lord2022Lancet]]. A supportive, accepting environment matters [[Lai2014]].
9. **Can an online test tell me if I am (or my child is) autistic?** No. Questionnaires such as the M-CHAT-R/F or the AQ are screening tools that flag who should be assessed. In research they are only a first step before clinical assessment [[Hyman2020,Brugha2011]]. Diagnosis needs a comprehensive evaluation by trained clinicians using direct observation and developmental history [[Hirota2023]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Is there a true rise in autism, beyond better recognition? One position: reporting and diagnostic changes explain most or all of the rise. Danish reporting changes explain 60% [[Hansen2015Reporting]], the Swedish symptom phenotype is stable [[Lundstrom2015]], and GBD age-standardised prevalence is about flat since 1990 [[Santomauro2025,Baxter2015]]. The other position: the CDC says a true increase "cannot be ruled out" [[ADDM2009SY2006]], and the share of the Danish rise left unexplained (40%, with wide CIs) leaves room. Status: open, though most evidence points to recognition.
- Why do US surveillance figures (1 in 31) far exceed GBD estimates for the US? ADDM uses records review in selected communities with wide site variation (9.7 to 53.1 per 1,000) [[Shaw2025]]. GBD models are conservative and rest on few adult studies [[Santomauro2025]]. Status: method-dependent; neither is "the" number.
- Which word to use? Identity-first is favoured by most autistic adults in English-language surveys [[Taboas2023,Keating2023]]. Person-first remains endorsed by 4–39% in those surveys and is favoured in Dutch-language studies [[Schuck2025]]. Status: no consensus; respect personal preference.
- Double empathy: the original diffusion-chain study found a mixed-neurotype penalty [[Crompton2020]]. The larger 2025 replication did not [[Crompton2025]]. Group rapport studies support neurotype-matching effects for autistic participants [[Foster2025]], and first-impression bias by non-autistic observers is robust [[Sasson2017]]. Status: Early evidence, mixed.
- Camouflaging: is it a valid construct, and does it explain late diagnosis in women? Sceptical [[Fombonne2020Camouflage]]; supportive, with better measurement needed [[Lai2021Commentary,Cook2021Camouflaging]]. Links to poor mental health are cross-sectional so far [[Cook2021Camouflaging,Cassidy2023Camouflaging]].
- "Profound autism": supporters say it directs attention and resources to people who need round-the-clock support, who are under-represented in research [[Siegel2026,Hughes2023]]. A preprint finds no distinct biology and suggests studying specific traits such as speech instead [[Eicher2026]]. Status: debated; a research definition reached 76% Delphi agreement in 2026 [[Siegel2026]].
- The Asperger eponym: Czech's archival work shows Asperger cooperated with Nazi race-hygiene policy [[Czech2018]]. DSM-5 had already removed Asperger's disorder as a separate diagnosis [[BarahonaCorrea2015]]. Many people still identify with the term. Status: historical facts established; use of the word is a personal choice.
- Who originated "neurodiversity"? It is commonly credited to Judy Singer. Botha et al. argue for collective origins and earlier use [[Botha2024]]. Status: attribution debated; the concept itself is not.
- Do broader criteria weaken research? Effect sizes for group differences have fallen over time [[Rodgaard2019]], and the share of diagnosed people with intellectual disability has fallen in Sweden [[Salkic2026]]. In the US, the ADDM share with intellectual disability has risen since 2014 [[Baio2018,Shaw2025]]. Status: open; population definitions differ by country and method.
