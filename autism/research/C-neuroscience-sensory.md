# C · Neuroscience, brain development & sensory processing

Pass date: 2026-11-02. Modules served: brain, senses, milestones.

This pass covers how the typical human brain is built (neurogenesis, migration, synapse formation and pruning), what infant-sibling imaging, postmortem tissue and brain transcriptomics show in autism, the main theories (excitation/inhibition balance, predictive coding and Bayesian accounts, connectivity, enhanced perception, monotropism, double empathy), maternal immune activation and the gut microbiome, early social attention, and sensory processing (DSM-5-TR criterion B4, prevalence, Dunn's model, hyperacusis, interoception, meltdown and shutdown, stimming, sensory-friendly settings). It also hands the milestones builder developmental time-course data with ages.

Language: many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"), while many families and clinicians use person-first language ("person with autism"). A UK survey of 3,470 community members found no single term that everyone accepted [[Kenny2016]]. This file uses both.

## Findings

### brain · How the typical brain is built (the time course)
- Human brain development begins in the third gestational week, when neural progenitor cells differentiate, and continues at least through late adolescence (Established) [[Stiles2010]].
- Neuron production begins on embryonic day 42 (E42) and is largely complete by midgestation in most brain areas. Between E25 and E42, progenitors divide symmetrically to enlarge the pool, then switch to asymmetric division (Established) [[Stiles2010]].
- Neurons migrate along radial glial guides and build the six-layered cortex "inside-out": the first arrivals form layer 6, and later cells form progressively more superficial layers (Established) [[Stiles2010]].
- Thalamocortical axons reach the developing cortex around gestational week 22. These key pathways are complete by about gestational week 26 (Established) [[Stiles2010]].
- Typical development overbuilds and then trims. Naturally occurring cell death removes 50% or more of the neurons in a region, and pruning eliminates up to 50% of the initially overproduced connections (Established) [[Stiles2010]].
- Synapse formation in the cortex begins before 27 weeks of conceptional age. Synaptic density peaks near 3 months after birth in auditory cortex, but not until after 15 months in prefrontal cortex (Established) [[Huttenlocher1997]].
- Net synapse elimination ends by about age 12 in auditory cortex and extends to mid-adolescence in prefrontal cortex (Established) [[Huttenlocher1997]].
- In human prefrontal cortex (newborn to 91 years), dendritic spine density in childhood is two to three times the adult value. Spine elimination continues through the third decade of life before stabilising (Established) [[Petanjek2011]].
- In 98 healthy children scanned from birth to age 2, total brain volume rose 101% in the first year and 15% in the second. Grey matter grew 149% in year one, cerebellar volume 240% (Established) [[Knickmeyer2008]].

### brain · Infant-sibling MRI: changes before diagnosis
- In 106 high-familial-likelihood infants and 42 low-likelihood infants, cortical surface-area hyperexpansion at 6–12 months came before brain-volume overgrowth at 12–24 months in the 15 infants diagnosed with autism at 24 months (Early evidence) [[Hazlett2017]].
- In the same study, brain-volume overgrowth was linked to the emergence and severity of autistic social differences (Early evidence) [[Hazlett2017]].
- A deep-learning model using mainly 6–12-month surface-area data predicted a 24-month autism diagnosis in high-likelihood infants. Its positive predictive value was 81% and its sensitivity 88% (Early evidence; research cohort only, not a clinical test) [[Hazlett2017]].
- In 343 infants (221 high-likelihood), the 47 diagnosed at 24 months had 18% more extra-axial cerebrospinal fluid at 6 months than high-likelihood infants without autism (d = 0.54). Extra-axial fluid is fluid in the space around the brain. It stayed elevated through 24 months (d = 0.46) (Early evidence) [[Shen2017]].
- Extra-axial CSF at 6 months predicted a 24-month diagnosis with 69% accuracy (66% sensitivity, 68% specificity), cross-validated in a separate sample (Early evidence) [[Shen2017]].
- The first report of this finding came from 55 infants imaged at 6–9 months. It found excess extra-axial fluid at 6–9 months and larger total cerebral volume at 12–15 and 18–24 months in the 10 infants who developed autism (Early evidence) [[Shen2013]].
- Diffusion imaging of 92 high-likelihood siblings found different fractional anisotropy trajectories in 12 of 15 white-matter tracts in the 28 who were later autistic. Values were higher at 6 months, then changed more slowly, and were lower by 24 months (Early evidence) [[Wolff2012DTI]].
- In 217 high-likelihood infants (44 diagnosed at age 2), fractional anisotropy of the genu of the corpus callosum at 6 months predicted repetitive behaviour and sensory responsiveness at age 2, but not social differences (Early evidence) [[Wolff2017RRB]].
- Resting-state functional connectivity MRI at 6 months correctly classified 9 of 11 high-likelihood infants later diagnosed (sensitivity 81.8%, 95% CI 47.8–96.8). It also correctly classified all 48 who were not diagnosed (specificity 100%, 95% CI 90.8–100). This was a small sample (n = 59) (Early evidence) [[Emerson2017]].
- Event-related potentials to dynamic eye-gaze shifts, recorded at 6–10 months, were associated with an autism diagnosis at 36 months (Early evidence) [[Elsabbagh2012]].

### brain · Head size: "early overgrowth" and its critique
- In a retrospective chart study of 48 autistic children, head circumference at birth was smaller than norms (z = −0.66). It then rose 1.67 SD, reaching the 84th percentile on average by 6–14 months (Hypothesis at the time; later contested) [[Courchesne2003HC]].
- In the same study, accelerated head growth (>2.0 SD) from birth to 6–14 months was seen in 59% of infants with autistic disorder, compared with 6% of healthy infants in longitudinal norm data (Hypothesis at the time; later contested) [[Courchesne2003HC]].
- A study of 51 autistic toddlers (MRI at 18–35 months) and 113 head-circumference histories found head circumference normal at birth. It found a faster growth rate from about 12 months, and enlarged cerebral (not cerebellar) grey and white matter at age 2 (Early evidence) [[Hazlett2005]].
- A 2013 reappraisal found that studies comparing autistic children with locally recruited controls were much less likely to find early overgrowth than studies using population norms (p < .001). In a US community sample of about 75,000 children, typical children showed the same "overgrowth" against classical norms (Established critique) [[Raznahan2013]].
- After correcting for those norm biases, only inconsistent support remained for a subtle, later-emerging overgrowth in a subgroup. The authors concluded that the best-replicated parts of early overgrowth reflect norm bias, not a disease-specific marker (Established critique) [[Raznahan2013]].
- A meta-analysis found macrocephaly in 822 of 5,225 autistic individuals (15.7%) and brain overgrowth in 142 of 1,558 (9.1%). Head and brain size differences were largest in early childhood (Established that a subgroup is affected) [[Sacco2015]].
- The Lancet seminar states that head circumference has been shown to have little usefulness as a predictor of autism (Established) [[Lord2018]].

### brain · No scan or blood test diagnoses autism
- Because there are no reliable biomarkers, autism is diagnosed from behaviour and developmental history (Established) [[Lord2018]].
- A 2020 primer states that diagnosis is made on the basis of behavioural presentation. It adds that MRI needs more work before it can be a reliable biomarker, because replication has been difficult (Established) [[Lord2020NRDP]].
- Gross brain pathology is not characteristic of autism. Only subtle anatomical and functional group differences have been seen in postmortem, imaging and electrophysiology studies (Established) [[Lord2020NRDP]].
- In about 1,000 ABIDE participants (age 6–65), classifiers using anatomical MRI identified group membership with modest accuracy (<60%). Earlier-reported differences in intracranial, cerebellar and amygdala volume were not confirmed (Established) [[Haar2016]].
- Eye-tracking of social visual engagement in 475 children aged 16–30 months at specialty clinics had 71.0% sensitivity and 80.7% specificity against expert diagnosis. It was evaluated as an aid to specialist diagnosis, not a stand-alone test (Early evidence) [[Jones2023]].

### brain · Postmortem tissue: neurons, layers, spines and pruning
- In 7 autistic and 6 control boys aged 2–16, the autistic children had 67% more prefrontal neurons: 1.94 billion (95% CI 1.57–2.31) against 1.16 billion (95% CI 0.90–1.42). The authors call it a small preliminary study (Early evidence) [[Courchesne2011PFC]].
- Focal patches of disorganised cortical layers were found in prefrontal and temporal tissue from 10 of 11 autistic children and 1 of 11 non-autistic children. This points to prenatal differences in layer formation (Early evidence) [[Stoner2014]].
- Golgi-stained pyramidal neurons showed higher dendritic spine densities in autistic than control cortex, mainly in layer II and in temporal layer V (Early evidence) [[Hutsler2010]].
- Layer V pyramidal neurons in postmortem autistic temporal cortex had higher spine density with less developmental pruning. This tracked with overactive mTOR signalling and impaired autophagy (Early evidence) [[Tang2014]].
- In Tsc2+/− mice (constitutively high mTOR), rapamycin corrected spine-pruning defects and autism-like social behaviours. It did not do so in autophagy-deficient mice, which implicates mTOR-dependent autophagy in pruning (Early evidence; animal model) [[Tang2014]].
- In prefrontal areas BA46, BA47 and BA9 from 11 autistic and 10 control brains, parvalbumin-positive (fast-spiking inhibitory) interneurons were fewer in autism. Calbindin and calretinin interneurons were not (Early evidence) [[Hashemi2017]].

### brain · Brain transcriptomics: convergent molecular patterns
- Gene co-expression analysis of postmortem cortex found that the usual frontal-versus-temporal gene-expression differences are attenuated in autism. It also found a downregulated neuronal module enriched for autism genes and an upregulated immune-glial module (Established, replicated) [[Voineagu2011]].
- The neuronal module was enriched for autism GWAS signal, but the immune-glial module was not. This suggests the glial and immune changes are not genetically driven (Early evidence) [[Voineagu2011]].
- A larger cohort replicated the attenuated regional gene-expression differences. It added dysregulated primate-specific lncRNAs and reduced neuron-specific splicing, and found the same core signature in dup15q syndrome (Established) [[Parikshak2016]].
- Across five psychiatric conditions, autism shared transcriptomic changes with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. The degree of sharing paralleled polygenic overlap (Established) [[Gandal2018]].
- RNA-sequencing of 725 samples spanning 11 cortical areas (112 postmortem donors) found autism-related changes across the whole cortex. They were largest in primary visual cortex and followed an anterior-to-posterior gradient (Early evidence) [[Gandal2022]].
- In that study, rare and common autism-linked variants converged on a downregulated synaptic-signalling module. Changes particularly affected excitatory neurons and glia (Early evidence) [[Gandal2022]].
- Single-nucleus RNA sequencing found that upper-layer excitatory neurons (their synaptic signalling) and microglia are preferentially affected. Changes in cortico-cortical projection neurons tracked clinical severity (Early evidence) [[Velmeshev2019]].
- Co-expression networks seeded with high-confidence autism genes converged on midfetal layer 5/6 cortical projection neurons (Early evidence) [[Willsey2013]].
- An integrative analysis found that autism genes cluster in modules for early transcriptional regulation and synaptic development, and are enriched in superficial cortical layers and glutamatergic neurons (Early evidence) [[Parikshak2013]].
- Of 102 autism risk genes found by exome sequencing (11,986 autistic people), most act in gene-expression regulation or neuronal communication and are expressed early in brain development. Their expression is enriched in both excitatory and inhibitory neuron lineages (Established) [[Satterstrom2020]].

### brain · Excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance
- The 2003 model proposed that some forms of autism arise from an increased ratio of excitation to inhibition in sensory, memory, social and emotional systems, through combined genetic and environmental effects (Hypothesis) [[Rubenstein2003]].
- A 2019 update reframed the idea as reduced signal-to-noise in key circuits. It summarised mouse autism-gene mutations that reduce inhibition, and proposed E/I balance as an organising framework rather than one mechanism (Hypothesis) [[Sohal2019]].
- In mice, optogenetically raising E/I balance in medial prefrontal cortex (but not lowering it) impaired information processing and social behaviour. It also raised 30–80 Hz gamma power. Boosting inhibitory cells partly rescued social behaviour (Early evidence; animal) [[Yizhar2011]].
- Across four autism mouse models (Fmr1, Cntnap2, 16p11.2del, Tsc2), feedforward inhibition fell and the E/I conductance ratio rose. Spiking, however, stayed largely normal, which suggests the shift may be homeostatic compensation rather than a cause of hyperexcitability (Early evidence; animal) [[Antoine2019]].
- A review stresses that brain networks compensate homeostatically. This makes it hard to separate a primary E/I defect from compensation, and the evidence points both toward inhibition and toward excitation (Hypothesis) [[Nelson2015]].
- Human support is indirect: reduced parvalbumin interneurons postmortem [[Hashemi2017]], EEG/MEG and modelling studies summarised in the 2020 primer, and "promising but inconclusive" trials of GABA-targeting medicines (Early evidence) [[Lord2020NRDP]].

### brain · Predictive-coding and Bayesian accounts of perception
- Pellicano and Burr proposed "hypo-priors": weaker prior expectations, so autistic perception is less shaped by past experience and more by the raw input. The world can feel "too real" (Hypothesis) [[Pellicano2012]].
- The aberrant-precision account reframes this as too much weight (precision) on sensory evidence relative to prior beliefs, within hierarchical predictive coding (Hypothesis) [[Lawson2014]].
- Van de Cruys and colleagues proposed that prediction errors receive uniformly, inflexibly high weight. Insistence on sameness could then be a way to make the world more predictable (Hypothesis) [[VandeCruys2014]].
- In an experiment with a computational model, autistic adults were less surprised than non-autistic adults when expectations were violated. The model suggested they overestimate how volatile the environment is, and pupil data suggested altered noradrenergic gain (Early evidence) [[Lawson2017]].
- Older cognitive accounts, still taught, include enhanced perceptual functioning (superior low-level discrimination, locally oriented perception) [[Mottron2006]] and weak central coherence, now recast as a detail-focused bias rather than a deficit (Hypothesis; local-bias findings robust) [[Happe2006]].
- Monotropism, co-authored by an autistic researcher, proposes that attention is concentrated on a few interests at a time. Narrow focus would then underlie the other diagnostic features (Hypothesis) [[Murray2005]].

### brain · Connectivity: big data sets, inconsistent answers
- ABIDE pooled 1,112 resting-state scans (539 autistic, 573 controls, age 7–64). Both under- and over-connectivity appeared, with under-connectivity dominant for cortico-cortical and interhemispheric links (Established that both occur) [[DiMartino2014]].
- Across four large cohorts, reproducible under-connectivity was found mainly in sensory-motor regions, and over-connectivity hubs in prefrontal and parietal cortex. The large overlap with controls limits diagnostic use (Early evidence) [[Holiga2019]].
- A multi-site analysis found that no single connectivity method gave reproducible results across sites. Consistent findings emerged only with large samples (Established that findings are inconsistent) [[King2019FC]].
- ENIGMA's mega-analysis (1,571 autistic, 1,651 controls, age 2–64) found smaller pallidum, putamen, amygdala and nucleus accumbens volumes, thicker frontal cortex and thinner temporal cortex. Effect sizes were small (Cohen's d −0.21 to 0.20), with the largest thickness differences around adolescence (Established, small effects) [[vanRooij2018]].

### brain · Maternal immune activation (MIA): animal vs human evidence
- In animal models, MIA alone is sufficient to cause lifelong neuropathology and altered behaviour in offspring (Established in animals) [[Estes2016]].
- In pregnant mice, maternal TH17 cells and interleukin-17a were required for MIA-induced behavioural and cortical changes in pups [[Choi2016]]. Maternal gut bacteria that promote TH17 cells were needed for these effects (Early evidence; mouse) [[Kim2017MIA]].
- In humans, a meta-analysis of 15 studies (>40,000 autism cases) found maternal infection in pregnancy associated with autism (OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.03–1.23). The association was stronger with hospitalisation (OR 1.30, 1.14–1.50) (Established association, small effect) [[Jiang2016Infection]].
- In all Danish births 1980–2005, any maternal infection over the whole pregnancy was not associated with autism (aHR 1.14, 0.96–1.34). First-trimester viral infection needing hospitalisation was (aHR 2.98, 1.29–7.15) (Established) [[Atladottir2010]].
- In Finland, mothers in the highest quintile of early-pregnancy C-reactive protein had a 43% higher risk of an autistic child than those in the lowest quintile (Early evidence) [[Brown2014CRP]].
- In a Norwegian cohort, second-trimester fever was associated with autism (aOR 1.40, 1.09–1.79), and three or more fevers after 12 weeks with aOR 3.12 (1.28–7.63) (Early evidence) [[Hornig2018]].
- Teaching point: the animal work shows a mechanism, while the human data show modest associations. Most children exposed to maternal infection are not autistic (Established) [[Jiang2016Infection,Estes2016]].

### brain · Gut microbiome: "cause" or "diet"?
- Mice colonised with gut microbiota from autistic donors showed autism-like behaviours, and some microbial metabolites changed behaviour in a mouse model (Early evidence; mouse, contested) [[Sharon2019]].
- An open-label Microbiota Transfer Therapy study in 18 autistic children with GI problems reported, at 2 years, a 58% reduction in GI symptom score. CARS-rated autism severity was 47% lower than baseline. There was no control group (Early evidence) [[Kang2019]].
- A metagenomic study of 247 people found negligible direct association between autism diagnosis and the gut microbiome. Restricted interests went with a less diverse diet, which in turn went with lower microbial diversity and looser stools (Established for its design; counters causal claims) [[Yap2021]].
- Yap and colleagues caution against claims that the microbiome drives autism. Microbiome differences may reflect diet preferences linked to autistic traits (Established) [[Yap2021]].
- A multi-dataset Bayesian analysis found an autism-associated microbial and metabolic profile in age- and sex-matched cohorts, but not in sibling-matched cohorts. This pattern is consistent with shared family environment and diet (Early evidence) [[Morton2023]].

### brain · Social attention and social-interaction theories
- In 110 infants (59 high-likelihood, 51 low-likelihood), the 12 later diagnosed with autism showed declining eye fixation from 2 to 6 months. Eye looking began at typical levels and then fell, so it is not absent from birth (Early evidence, single cohort) [[Jones2013Eyes]].
- Full-text slopes from 2 to 6 months: eye fixation rose +3.6% per month in typically developing infants and fell −4.8% per month in infants later diagnosed. By 24 months, eye fixation was about half the typical level (Early evidence) [[Jones2013Eyes]].
- In the same cohort, object fixation by 24 months was about twice the typical level. A steeper decline in eye fixation correlated with more social disability (r = −0.750) (Early evidence) [[Jones2013Eyes]].
- At 6 months, infants later diagnosed attended less to a social scene and to the actress's face. They did not attend more to objects (Early evidence) [[Chawarska2013]].
- Two-year-old autistic children did not orient to point-light biological motion. They oriented instead to non-social physical contingencies (Early evidence) [[Klin2009]].
- A meta-analysis of 38 eye-tracking studies found autistic participants spent less time on social stimuli (mean effect size 0.55), most so with high social content [[ChitaTegmark2016]]. A meta-analysis of 122 studies found the largest effects for eyes (g = 0.47) and whole face (g = 0.50) during human interaction (Established) [[Frazier2017Gaze]].
- In 166 general-population toddler twins, social-scene viewing was highly heritable: monozygotic concordance 0.91, dizygotic 0.35. The most heritable features (attention to eyes and mouth) were the ones reduced in autism (Early evidence) [[Constantino2017]].
- Fixating on geometric images more than 69% of the time identified a subgroup of autistic toddlers (sensitivity 21%, specificity 98%, PPV 86%) with more severe symptoms (Early evidence) [[Pierce2016]].
- The social motivation theory frames autism as reduced drive to seek social reward (Hypothesis, contested) [[Chevallier2012]].
- The double empathy problem reframes social difficulty as a two-way mismatch between autistic and non-autistic people, not a one-sided deficit (Hypothesis) [[Milton2012]].
- Autistic-to-autistic chains passed on story details as well as non-autistic chains did. Mixed chains passed on less, with lower rapport (Early evidence supporting double empathy) [[Crompton2020]].

### milestones · Developmental time course for the milestone builder
- CDC/AAP checklist milestones, the age by which ≥75% of children show the skill (Established) [[Zubler2022]]:
  - looks at your face: 2 months
  - smiles when talked to: 2 months
  - smiles on own to get attention: 4 months
  - laughs: 6 months
  - looks when you call their name: 9 months
  - lifts arms to be picked up: 9 months
  - waves "bye-bye": 12 months
  - plays pat-a-cake: 12 months
  - points to ask for something: 15 months
  - shows you an object they like: 15 months
  - tries 1–2 words besides mama/dada: 15 months
  - points to show you something interesting: 18 months
  - tries to say 3 or more words: 18 months
  - looks at your face to see how to react in a new situation: 24 months
- Gaze to faces, shared smiles and directed vocalisations were similar at 6 months in 25 infants later diagnosed and 25 typical infants. They declined afterwards, with most group differences significant by 12 months (Early evidence) [[Ozonoff2010]].
- In the same study, repeated assessment showed loss of skills in most infants later diagnosed, but most parents did not report regression (Early evidence) [[Ozonoff2010]].
- Response to name, tested at 6–24 months in 156 infant siblings: infants developing autism were more likely to fail to orient from 9 months, persisting to 24 months. At least one failure between 12 and 24 months had sensitivity and specificity of about 0.70 (Early evidence) [[Miller2017Name]].
- At 12 months, 100% of control infants responded to their name by the second call, against 86% of high-likelihood infants. Failure had specificity 0.89 and sensitivity 0.50 for autism, so many autistic infants do respond to their name (Early evidence) [[Nadig2007]].
- By 12 months, siblings later diagnosed could be distinguished on eye contact, visual tracking, disengagement of attention, orienting to name, imitation, social smiling, social interest and sensory-oriented behaviours (Early evidence) [[Zwaigenbaum2005]].
- The brain-change timeline (surface area 6–12 months, volume 12–24 months, extra-axial CSF from 6 months) overlaps the period when behavioural signs first appear (Early evidence) [[Hazlett2017,Shen2017]].

### senses · What DSM-5-TR says (criterion B4)
- Paraphrase of criterion B4: being much more or much less reactive to sensory input, or unusually interested in sensory aspects of the surroundings. Examples include seeming indifferent to pain or temperature, strong reactions to certain sounds or textures, lots of smelling or touching of objects, and visual fascination with lights or movement (Established) [[APA2022]].
- DSM-5 placed sensory differences (hyper- and hypo-responsiveness and sensation seeking) in the restricted and repetitive behaviours domain to reflect how common they are (Established) [[Lord2020NRDP]].
- Atypical sensory behaviour (by Short Sensory Profile and ADI-R) was reported in 92% of 116 autistic children aged 10–14 and in 67% of 72 non-autistic children with special educational needs. So sensory differences are common but not unique to autism (Established) [[Green2016DSM5]].

### senses · How common sensory differences are
- Among 281 autistic children aged 3–6, 95% showed some sensory processing difference on the Short Sensory Profile total score. The largest differences were in under-responsive/seeking, auditory filtering and tactile sensitivity (Established) [[Tomchek2007]].
- On the DISCO interview, over 90% of autistic children had sensory differences across several senses. These persisted across age and ability in children and adults (Established) [[Leekam2007]].
- On the Adult/Adolescent Sensory Profile, 94.4% of autistic adults reported extreme scores on at least one sensory quadrant, with striking variability between individuals (Established) [[Crane2009]].
- The 2009 meta-analysis (14 studies) found the largest autistic–typical difference in under-responsivity, then over-responsivity, then sensation seeking. Differences were greatest at ages 6–9 (Established) [[BenSasson2009]].
- The 2019 update (55 questionnaire studies, 4,606 autistic people) found large effects against typical controls for over-responsivity, under-responsivity and seeking. Only over-responsivity also exceeded every other clinical comparison group (Established) [[BenSasson2019]].
- In an online survey, 221 autistic adults reported more over-responsivity than 181 non-autistic adults across visual, auditory, tactile, smell, taste and proprioceptive domains. Over-responsivity correlated with autistic traits (Established) [[Tavassoli2014]].
- More than half of autistic youth have sensory over-responsivity. Over-responsivity and anxiety commonly co-occur, and three causal explanations remain open (Early evidence) [[Green2015SOR,GreenBenSasson2010]].

### senses · Dunn's model of sensory processing
- Dunn's model crosses neurological threshold (high or low) with self-regulation strategy (passive or active) to give four patterns: low registration, sensation seeking, sensory sensitivity and sensation avoiding (Established model) [[Dunn2001]].
- Every person has some amount of each pattern. The model describes preferences across the general population from birth to 90 years, not a disorder (Established model) [[Dunn2001]].
- In this model, low registration means a high threshold with passive regulation (missing cues), and seeking a high threshold with active regulation. Sensitivity means a low threshold with passive regulation (noticing everything), and avoiding a low threshold with active regulation (Established model) [[Dunn2001]].

### senses · Brain basis of sensory over-responsivity
- In 19 autistic and 19 non-autistic youth (9–17 years), mildly aversive sounds and touch produced stronger responses in primary sensory cortices and the amygdala in autistic youth, correlated with parent-rated over-responsivity after controlling for anxiety (Early evidence) [[Green2015SOR]].
- Youth with over-responsivity showed reduced habituation (the response did not fade with repetition). Autistic youth without over-responsivity showed prefrontal down-regulation of the amygdala (Early evidence) [[Green2015SOR]].
- Sensory differences have been measured with EEG, MEG and fMRI across hearing, touch and vision, including differences in multisensory integration (Established that differences exist; mechanisms open) [[Marco2011]].
- A Nature Reviews Neuroscience review argues that sensory traits are a tractable entry point for animal and computational models of autism (Hypothesis) [[Robertson2017]].
- The 2022 cortex-wide transcriptomic study found its largest changes in primary visual cortex, a sensory region (Early evidence) [[Gandal2022]].

### senses · Hearing: hyperacusis and decreased sound tolerance
- A meta-analysis of 67 studies estimated current hyperacusis in autistic people at 41.42% (95% CrI 37.23–45.84%) and lifetime hyperacusis at 60.58% (50.37–69.76%). Hyperacusis means everyday sounds feel too loud or painful (Established) [[Williams2021Hyperacusis]].
- With objective or observational measures, current prevalence was 27.30% (14.92–46.31%). In ADI-R studies, prevalence rose sharply with age (Established) [[Williams2021Hyperacusis]].
- About 50–70% of autistic people show decreased sound tolerance at some point. A review proposes separating it into hyperacusis (sounds perceived as too loud or painful), misophonia (learned aversion to specific sounds) and phonophobia (fear of sound) (Hypothesis for the framework) [[Williams2021DST]].
- In six children with autism and hyperacusis, noise-attenuating headphones lowered skin-conductance markers of sympathetic arousal. This was a single-subject design (Early evidence) [[Pfeiffer2019EDA]].

### senses · Interoception and alexithymia
- Interoception is sensing the body's internal state, such as heartbeat, hunger or needing the toilet. One study found lower heartbeat-detection accuracy in autistic adults alongside higher self-rated body awareness, and the gap correlated with anxiety (Early evidence) [[Garfinkel2016]].
- A Bayesian meta-analysis (15 studies, 467 autistic, 478 non-autistic) found lower heartbeat-counting performance (g = −0.333) and higher confidence (g = 0.430), but no difference on other measures (Established for counting tasks) [[Williams2023Interoception]].
- A 2025 meta-analysis of five adult studies found no significant difference in cardiac interoceptive accuracy (−0.21, p = .06). Child and self-report findings were inconsistent (Early evidence; findings conflict) [[Klein2025]].
- Alexithymia (difficulty identifying and describing one's own feelings) was found in 49.93% of autistic participants against 4.89% of non-autistic participants. It is common but not universal (Established) [[Kinnaird2019]].
- In 26 autistic and 26 non-autistic adults, alexithymia rather than autism itself mediated the link between interoceptive awareness and empathy (Early evidence) [[Mul2018]].

### senses · Meltdown, shutdown and stimming in autistic people's own words
- In 32 autistic adults, meltdowns were described as being overwhelmed by information, senses, and social and emotional stress. People reported extreme emotion, trouble thinking and remembering, a sense of "not being themselves", and a release of pent-up emotion (Early evidence; qualitative) [[Lewis2023Meltdown]].
- The same adults tried to avoid triggers or to be alone when a meltdown was coming, to prevent harm to their bodies, emotions and relationships (Early evidence; qualitative) [[Lewis2023Meltdown]].
- Autistic youth described meltdowns as "feeling out of control" and shutdown or inertia as "feeling frozen". Shutdowns are more inward, with withdrawal from surroundings, and range from mild to severe (Early evidence; qualitative, n = 8) [[Phung2021]].
- Autistic informants describe meltdowns as responses to overload that feel out of their control, not as chosen behaviour. Youth said compassion and collaboration from trusted adults help, while demands such as eye contact during distress make things worse (Early evidence; qualitative) [[Phung2021,Lewis2023Meltdown]].
- In 32 autistic adults, stimming (repetitive movements or sounds) served as self-regulation to soothe or express intense emotions. Participants objected to treatment that aims to eliminate it (Early evidence; qualitative) [[Kapp2019]].

### senses · Sensory-friendly environments: what has been tested
- In a randomised crossover trial of 162 autistic children aged 6–12, a sensory-adapted dental room (modified visual, auditory and tactile stimuli) lowered physiological stress compared with a regular room. The skin conductance difference was −1.22 μS (95% CI −2.17 to −0.27) (Established for this setting) [[SteinDuker2023]].
- In that trial, video-coded behavioural distress was also lower (Cohen d −0.84 to −1.19), with no withdrawals for adverse effects (Established for this setting) [[SteinDuker2023]].
- An earlier pilot with 22 autistic and 22 typical children found lower physiological anxiety, pain and sensory discomfort in the adapted dental room for both groups (Early evidence) [[Cermak2015]].
- In four students aged 13–20, sound-absorbing walls and halogen lighting were followed by more attending and engagement (Early evidence; very small single-subject design) [[Kinnealey2012]].
- In parent and teacher interviews, noise-attenuating headphones increased participation at home, at school and in the community, and many children learned to request them (Early evidence; qualitative) [[Pfeiffer2019Participation]].
- In a 2-week crossover RCT of 67 autistic children with severe sleep problems, weighted blankets did not increase actigraphy-measured sleep time, though children and parents preferred them. Sensory products are not automatically effective (Established) [[Gringras2014]].
- Autistic adults in participatory focus groups (n = 24) named supermarkets, eateries, town centres, public transport and health-care settings as commonly disabling. They identified six principles of enabling spaces: sensoryscape, space, predictability, understanding, adjustments and recovery (Early evidence; qualitative) [[MacLennan2023]].
- Sensory-integration therapy (pass E) produced short-term gains in sensory and motor skills, and single sensory components (weighted blankets, swings, brushing) have not shown consistent benefit (Established for short-term gains; components inconsistent) [[Lord2018]].

### Exam hooks
- No blood test or brain scan diagnoses autism. Diagnosis is behavioural [[Lord2018,Lord2020NRDP]].
- Neurogenesis in humans starts around embryonic day 42 and is largely complete by midgestation [[Stiles2010]].
- Synaptic density peaks around 3 months in auditory cortex and after 15 months in prefrontal cortex. Pruning runs into adolescence (prefrontal) [[Huttenlocher1997]].
- Total brain volume roughly doubles (+101%) in the first year after birth [[Knickmeyer2008]].
- Infant-sibling MRI: cortical surface-area hyperexpansion at 6–12 months comes before brain-volume overgrowth at 12–24 months [[Hazlett2017]].
- Early head-circumference "overgrowth" was largely an artefact of biased population norms [[Raznahan2013]].
- Eye fixation in infants later diagnosed is typical at 2 months and then declines, so it is not absent from birth [[Jones2013Eyes]].
- Postmortem autistic cortex shows more dendritic spines and reduced pruning, linked to overactive mTOR [[Tang2014]].
- The classic transcriptomic finding is attenuated frontal–temporal gene-expression differences, with downregulated synaptic and upregulated immune-glial modules [[Voineagu2011,Gandal2022]].
- The E/I hypothesis (Rubenstein & Merzenich 2003) proposes increased excitation relative to inhibition. Mouse data suggest the shift may be compensatory [[Rubenstein2003,Antoine2019]].
- Hypo-priors (Pellicano & Burr): perception is less shaped by prior expectations [[Pellicano2012]].
- DSM-5 moved sensory hyper- or hypo-reactivity into criterion B (restricted and repetitive behaviours) [[APA2022,Lord2020NRDP]].
- Over 90% of autistic children and 94.4% of autistic adults report sensory differences [[Leekam2007,Crane2009]].
- Dunn's four quadrants are low registration, seeking, sensitivity and avoiding [[Dunn2001]].
- Current hyperacusis is about 41% in autistic people, and lifetime about 61% [[Williams2021Hyperacusis]].
- About half of autistic people have alexithymia (49.93%), against 4.89% of controls [[Kinnaird2019]].
- In humans, maternal infection is only modestly associated with autism (OR 1.13) [[Jiang2016Infection]].
- Microbiome differences in autism largely track diet diversity, not diagnosis [[Yap2021]].
- Response to name fails in only about half of infants later diagnosed at 12 months (sensitivity 0.50) [[Nadig2007]].

### In plain words
- No scan or blood test can tell whether someone is autistic. Trained clinicians decide by watching, listening and taking a careful history [[Lord2018]].
- Autistic brains are built a little differently, mostly in how brain cells connect. These are differences on average across groups. You could not spot them in one person's scan [[Haar2016,Lord2020NRDP]].
- A baby's brain grows very fast, about doubling in size in the first year [[Knickmeyer2008]].
- The brain makes extra connections early and trims the unused ones over childhood and the teenage years [[Huttenlocher1997,Petanjek2011]].
- Many babies who are later diagnosed make eye contact at first, and the eye contact fades over the following months [[Jones2013Eyes]].
- Almost every autistic person experiences the senses differently. Sounds, lights, textures, smells or touch can feel too strong, or sometimes hardly noticeable [[Crane2009,Tomchek2007]].
- About 4 in 10 autistic people find everyday sounds too loud or painful right now, and about 6 in 10 do at some point in life [[Williams2021Hyperacusis]].
- A meltdown is a response to overload, not bad behaviour. A shutdown is a quieter version, where the person goes still or withdraws [[Lewis2023Meltdown,Phung2021]].
- Stimming, like rocking or hand-flapping, helps many autistic people calm down or show strong feelings [[Kapp2019]].
- Some autistic people find it hard to notice body signals like hunger, or to name their own feelings [[Kinnaird2019,Williams2023Interoception]].
- Small changes to a room, like softer light, less noise and a warning about what comes next, can lower stress. A sensory-adapted dentist's room did so in a large trial [[SteinDuker2023,MacLennan2023]].
- Infections in pregnancy are only weakly linked with autism, and most children whose mothers had a fever or infection are not autistic [[Jiang2016Infection]].
- Gut bacteria in autistic children often differ because of differences in diet. Current evidence does not show that gut bacteria cause autism [[Yap2021]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Early brain overgrowth: Courchesne reported head-size acceleration in most infants with autistic disorder [[Courchesne2003HC]]. Raznahan showed that much of it was a population-norm artefact [[Raznahan2013]]. MRI infant-sibling studies support brain-volume overgrowth at 12–24 months in a subset [[Hazlett2017,Sacco2015]]. Status: present in a subgroup, not a diagnostic marker.
- Imaging prediction in infancy: high accuracy in small, high-likelihood research cohorts [[Hazlett2017,Emerson2017]]. Large anatomical and connectivity data sets show small effects and poor reproducibility [[Haar2016,King2019FC,vanRooij2018]]. Status: not ready for clinical use.
- E/I balance: increased excitation as a cause [[Rubenstein2003,Yizhar2011]] versus a homeostatic compensation [[Antoine2019,Nelson2015]]. Status: an organising framework with mixed human evidence.
- Connectivity: "under-connectivity" versus "over-connectivity". Both are found, and depend on region, age and method [[DiMartino2014,Holiga2019]]. Status: unresolved.
- Predictive coding: hypo-priors [[Pellicano2012]] versus aberrant precision [[Lawson2014]] versus inflexible prediction-error weighting [[VandeCruys2014]]. Experimental support is early [[Lawson2017]]. Status: active debate.
- Maternal immune activation: strong causal mechanisms in mice [[Choi2016,Estes2016]] versus modest associations in humans, with confounding possible [[Jiang2016Infection,Atladottir2010]]. Status: a contributor for a subset at most.
- Gut microbiome: transfer of autism-like behaviour in mice [[Sharon2019]] and uncontrolled MTT benefits [[Kang2019]] versus diet-driven differences in large human data [[Yap2021]] and absence in sibling-matched cohorts [[Morton2023]]. Status: causal role not shown. Randomised trials are under way (pass F).
- Interoception: reduced heartbeat-counting accuracy [[Williams2023Interoception,Garfinkel2016]] versus no adult difference in a newer meta-analysis [[Klein2025]]. Alexithymia may explain much of the effect [[Mul2018,Kinnaird2019]]. Status: unresolved.
- Social theories: reduced social motivation [[Chevallier2012]] versus the double empathy problem [[Milton2012,Crompton2020]]. Status: contested. Autistic-led research supports a two-way account.
- Sensory supports: environmental adaptation has RCT support in dental care [[SteinDuker2023]], but popular sensory products such as weighted blankets did not improve measured sleep [[Gringras2014]]. Status: test each support. Autistic preferences matter alongside measured outcomes.
