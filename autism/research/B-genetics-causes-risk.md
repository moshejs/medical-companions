# B · Genetics, causes & risk factors (and what does not cause autism)

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: genes, risk.

Scope: heritability and twin studies, family recurrence, sex differences, common and rare genetic variation, copy-number variants, syndromic forms and X-linked inheritance, clinical genetic testing yields, prenatal and perinatal risk factors (relative and absolute risks), and the evidence on what does not cause autism (vaccines, thimerosal, acetaminophen, parenting). Numbers come from PubMed or Europe PMC abstracts. Two exceptions: Sandin 2017 is a research letter with no abstract, so its numbers were read from the PMC full text, and the Grove 2019 SNP heritability and genetic correlations were also read from the PMC full text. The valproate label wording comes from the current US Depakote label in openFDA (effective 2026-03-31).

Access notes:
- Language: many autistic adults prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"), and many families and clinicians use person-first language ("person with autism"). This file uses both. Nothing here is a reason for blame. A "risk factor" is a statistical association in a population. It is not a cause in any one family, and most children with any listed exposure are not autistic.
- The AAP 2020 clinical report (Hyman2020) and the ACMG 2013 genetics guideline full texts were paywalled. I cite the ACMG 2013 guideline only for what its abstract says. I did not quote the AAP's testing recommendations, and Hyman2020 is not listed.
- Correction to the brief: Christensen 2013 compares 4.42% (all 508 valproate-exposed children) with 1.53% (all 655,615 children). The figure 2.44% is the comparator **within mothers with epilepsy**, where it sits against 4.15% in the 432 exposed children. Both pairs are given below.
- Correction to the brief: Sandin 2014 itself estimated heritability at about 50%. The about 83% figure is the 2017 reanalysis of the same Swedish cohort (Sandin2017).
- I could not trace a validated figure for the share of **all** autistic people who have fragile X syndrome or tuberous sclerosis. Those directions are given only where a source states them (PTEN, 16p11.2). See Open questions.

## Findings

### genes · Heritability: twins and families
- Autism has a strong genetic component, and other causes contribute too (Established) [[Lord2018]].
- An early twin study of autism (Folstein and Rutter, 1977) examined 21 twin pairs [[Folstein1977]].
- In a meta-analysis of twin studies (13 identified, 7 meta-analysed), the monozygotic (MZ) twin correlation was 0.98 (95% CI 0.96–0.99). The dizygotic (DZ) correlation was 0.53 (0.44–0.60) at an assumed 5% prevalence and 0.67 (0.61–0.72) at 1% [[Tick2016]].
- The same meta-analysis put heritability at 64–91%. Shared environment became significant only as the assumed prevalence fell from 5% to 1% (7–35%). The authors judged earlier large shared-environment estimates "likely a statistical artefact" [[Tick2016]].
- In the UK population-based TEDS twin sample, MZ correlations across ASD measures were 0.77–0.99 and DZ correlations 0.22–0.65. Heritability was 56–95% [[Colvert2015]].
- A California twin study of 192 pairs found probandwise ASD concordance in male twins of 0.77 for MZ (95% CI 0.65–0.86) and 0.31 for DZ (0.16–0.46). In female twins it was 0.50 for MZ and 0.36 for DZ [[Hallmayer2011]].
- For strict autism in the same study, concordance in male twins was 0.58 for MZ (0.42–0.74) and 0.21 for DZ (0.09–0.43) [[Hallmayer2011]].
- That California study is the main outlier: it estimated moderate heritability (38% for ASD; 95% CI 14–67%) and a large shared-environment share (58%; 30–80%) [[Hallmayer2011]]. Later, larger studies attribute most variance to genes (see the next bullets and Open questions) [[Tick2016,Bai2019,Sandin2017]].
- In a Swedish cohort of 2,049,973 children, the 2014 analysis estimated ASD heritability at 0.50 (95% CI 0.45–0.56), with no shared-environment effect [[Sandin2014]].
- A 2017 reanalysis of the same Swedish data with standard twin-family models gave a heritability of 0.83 (95% CI 0.79–0.87) and nonshared environment of 0.17 (0.13–0.21) in the best-fitting model (additive genetic plus nonshared environment) [[Sandin2017]].
- In that reanalysis, the full model gave shared environment as 0.04 (0.00–0.14). Twins analysed alone gave a heritability of 0.87 (0.68–0.96) [[Sandin2017]].
- A five-country cohort (Denmark, Finland, Sweden, Israel and Western Australia; 2,001,631 people, 22,156 with ASD) found a median ASD heritability of 80.8% (95% CI 73.2–85.5%). Country estimates ranged from 50.9% (Finland) to 86.8% (Israel) [[Bai2019]].
- The same five-country study estimated the maternal effect at 0.4–1.6%. The authors found "no support" for a meaningful maternal contribution [[Bai2019]].
- Swedish twin cohorts born 1982–2008 showed no meaningful rise in environmental contribution over time. ASD heritability ranged from 0.88 to 0.97 across birth cohorts, so environmental change is "unlikely to explain" the rise in diagnoses (Established) [[Taylor2020Twins]].
- Heritability is a population statistic. It describes how much of the *variation* in a population tracks with genetic differences. It does not say what share of one person's autism is "genetic", and it does not mean the outcome is fixed [[Sandin2017,Bai2019]].

### genes · Family recurrence
- In the Swedish cohort, relative recurrence risk (RRR) fell as genetic relatedness fell [[Sandin2014]]:
  - MZ twin: 153.0 (95% CI 56.7–412.8)
  - DZ twin: 8.2 (3.7–18.1)
  - full sibling: 10.3 (9.4–11.3)
  - maternal half-sibling: 3.3 (2.6–4.2)
  - paternal half-sibling: 2.9 (2.2–3.7)
  - cousin: 2.0 (1.8–2.2)
- In the same Swedish cohort, ASD rates per 100,000 person-years were:
  - full sibling of an autistic child: 829, against 49 when no sibling was autistic
  - cousin of an autistic child: 155, against 49 [[Sandin2014]]
- In a six-site population study (California, Denmark, Finland, Israel, Sweden and Western Australia; 2,551,918 births for ASD), the risk of ASD rose 8.4-fold after an older autistic sibling and about 2-fold after an autistic cousin [[Hansen2019Recurrence]].
- Childhood autism (the narrower diagnosis) showed a 17.4-fold sibling recurrence in that study, and sibling recurrence differed by sex [[Hansen2019Recurrence]].
- In a prospective Baby Siblings Research Consortium study, 18.7% of 664 infants with an older autistic sibling were autistic by 36 months [[Ozonoff2011]].
- In that study, male infants had almost three times the risk of female infants. Having more than one older autistic sibling roughly doubled the risk again [[Ozonoff2011]].
- The 2024 consortium update (1605 infants, 18 sites) found 20.2% recurrence by age 3, which is not significantly different from the 2011 figure of 18.7% [[Ozonoff2024]].
- In the 2024 update, siblings of autistic girls were significantly more likely to be autistic than siblings of autistic boys. Male infant sex, more than one affected sibling, race and maternal education also predicted recurrence [[Ozonoff2024]].
- The 2024 update notes that population studies give about 10 times the ASD risk with an autistic sibling, while infant-sibling cohorts find higher rates. Prospective cohorts follow and assess every child closely, which helps explain why their rates are higher [[Ozonoff2024]].
- In a US claims cohort of 95,727 children with older siblings, 6.9% of children with an autistic older sibling were autistic, against 0.9% of those whose older siblings were not autistic [[Jain2015MMR]].

### genes · Sex differences and the female protective effect
- A meta-analysis of 54 prevalence studies gave a pooled male-to-female odds ratio of 4.20 (95% CI 3.84–4.60). In high-quality studies it was 3.32 (2.88–3.84), and the authors conclude the true ratio is "closer to 3:1" [[Loomes2017]].
- The same meta-analysis found a lower ratio in studies that screened the whole population (3.25) than in studies counting only existing diagnoses (4.56). This suggests autistic girls are under-diagnosed [[Loomes2017]].
- In two national twin samples (3,842 and 6,040 DZ pairs), siblings of girls with high autistic-trait scores had more autistic traits than siblings of boys with high scores. This is consistent with a female protective effect (Early evidence) [[Robinson2013]].
- In Danish iPSYCH data, siblings of autistic girls (n=1,707) had higher ASD rates than siblings of autistic boys (n=6,270). In the SSC and SPARK collections, mothers of autistic children carried more polygenic risk than fathers (0.08 SD) [[Wigdor2022]].
- In a large family sample, rare-variant and polygenic risk loads were inversely correlated and higher in autistic girls than boys. This fits a liability threshold that differs by sex (Early evidence) [[Antaki2022]].

### genes · Genetic architecture: common and rare variation
- Most genetic risk sits in common variants, each of tiny effect. In a Swedish sample, narrow-sense heritability was about 52.4%, mostly from common variation. Rare de novo mutations explained 2.6% of variance in liability [[Gaugler2014]].
- That 2.6% is small for the population, but de novo mutations "contribute substantially to individual liability" [[Gaugler2014]].
- The largest GWAS of its time (18,381 autistic people and 27,969 controls) found five genome-wide-significant loci, plus seven more shared with schizophrenia, depression and educational attainment [[Grove2019]].
- In that GWAS, SNP heritability on the liability scale was 0.118 (SE 0.010), assuming a population prevalence of 1.2% [[Grove2019]].
- Genetic correlations of autism in that GWAS were 0.412 with major depression, 0.211 with schizophrenia and 0.199 with educational attainment (all P<10⁻⁴) [[Grove2019]].
- In more than 2,500 simplex families, de novo missense and de novo likely gene-disrupting mutations contributed to about 12% and 9% of diagnoses respectively [[Iossifov2014]].
- Including copy-number variants, coding de novo mutations contribute to about 30% of all simplex diagnoses and 45% of diagnoses in girls [[Iossifov2014]].
- About one-third of de novo variants seen in families also appear as ordinary variation in 60,706 population adults (ExAC). Those variants do not add neurodevelopmental risk, so population reference data are essential when interpreting a "new" variant [[Kosmicki2017]].
- The Simons Simplex Collection CNV analysis (2,591 families) confirmed six de novo CNV risk loci: 1q21.1, 3q29, 7q11.23, 16p11.2, 15q11.2–13 and 22q11.2. Together with 65 genes, these made 71 risk loci [[Sanders2015Neuron]].
- An exome study of 35,584 samples (11,986 autistic) identified 102 risk genes at FDR ≤0.1 [[Satterstrom2020]].
- Of those 102 genes, 49 had more disruptive de novo variants in people with severe developmental delay and 53 in people ascertained for autism. Most act in gene-expression regulation or neuronal communication [[Satterstrom2020]].
- A joint analysis of 63,237 people found 72 ASD genes at FDR ≤0.001 (185 at FDR ≤0.05) [[Fu2022]].
- In that analysis, de novo protein-truncating variants made up 57.5% of the association evidence, damaging missense variants 21.1% and CNVs 8.44%. CNVs carried the greatest relative risk [[Fu2022]].
- Combined with developmental-delay cohorts (n=91,605), the same analysis found 373 genes at FDR ≤0.001 (664 at ≤0.05) [[Fu2022]].
- A study of 42,607 autism cases (35,130 recruited online through SPARK) found 60 exome-wide significant genes, five of them new (NAV3, ITSN1, MARK2, SCAF1, HNRNPUL2) [[Zhou2022]].
- That study described inherited, moderate-risk variants. Rare inherited loss-of-function variants in NAV3 carry an estimated relative risk of about 4 [[Zhou2022]].
- Autistic people with loss-of-function variants in moderate-risk genes had less cognitive impairment than those with variants in highly penetrant genes such as CHD8, SCN2A, ADNP, FOXP1 and SHANK3 (59% vs 88%) [[Zhou2022]].
- Parental age effects on autism reflect several genetic mechanisms. These include de novo mutations that accumulate in the father's germline and inherited risk carried by parents (Early evidence) [[Antaki2022]].
- In a given person, autism usually reflects many common variants, sometimes plus one rare variant of larger effect, and environmental contributions. Risk genes are expressed early in brain development and enriched in excitatory and inhibitory neuron lineages [[Satterstrom2020,Antaki2022]].

### genes · Copy-number variants (CNVs)
- A de novo 593-kb deletion at 16p11.2, and its reciprocal duplication, carry substantial susceptibility to autism. Together they appear to account for about 1% of cases [[Weiss2008NEJM]].
- In that study, the 16p11.2 deletion was found in:
  - 5 of 512 children referred for developmental delay or suspected autism
  - 3 of 299 autistic people in Iceland
  - 2 of 18,834 unscreened Icelandic controls [[Weiss2008NEJM]]
- Penetrance estimates for an abnormal phenotype (congenital anomaly, developmental delay or intellectual disability) ranged from 10.4% for 15q11.2 deletions to 62.4% for distal 16p11.2 deletions. That means many carriers are unaffected or mildly affected [[Rosenfeld2013]].
- Interstitial duplication of 15q11.2–q13 is among the CNVs most often linked with autism. In a 14-person series, every maternally derived duplication tested was on the autism spectrum, which implicates the maternally expressed gene UBE3A (Early evidence) [[Urraca2013]].
- In 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (1,402 people aged 6–68), males did not predominate among those with autism or psychotic disorders. ADHD affected 37.1% of children [[Schneider2014Del22q]].
- About 11% of people with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome show autism features (quality-weighted pooled prevalence) [[Richards2015]].

### genes · Syndromic forms and X-linked inheritance
- In every syndrome studied, a meta-analysis of 168 papers found autism features significantly more common than in the general population [[Richards2015]].
- Quality-weighted pooled prevalence of autism features in that meta-analysis [[Richards2015]]:

  | Syndrome | Autism features |
  |---|---|
  | Rett syndrome (females) | 61% |
  | Cohen syndrome | 54% |
  | Cornelia de Lange syndrome | 43% |
  | Tuberous sclerosis complex | 36% |
  | Angelman syndrome | 34% |
  | CHARGE syndrome | 30% |
  | Fragile X (males) | 30% |
  | Fragile X (mixed sex) | 22% |
  | Neurofibromatosis type 1 | 18% |
  | Down syndrome | 16% |
  | Noonan syndrome | 15% |
  | Williams syndrome | 12% |
  | 22q11.2 deletion | 11% |
- Fragile X syndrome is the leading inherited cause of intellectual disability and autism. It is a trinucleotide repeat disorder: more than 200 CGG repeats in FMR1 silence the gene and its protein, FMRP, is lost [[Hagerman2017]].
- FMR1, the fragile X gene at Xq27.3, was identified in 1991 together with its CGG repeat [[Verkerk1991]].
- In a large US clinic-based fragile X registry, half of males and nearly 20% of females met DSM-5 criteria for current ASD [[Kaufmann2017]].
- A meta-analysis of females with fragile X estimated ASD prevalence at 14% (95% CI 13–18%). It notes that up to 60% of males with fragile X meet ASD criteria [[Marlborough2021]].
- FMR1 lies on the X chromosome (Xq27.3) [[Verkerk1991]]. In line with X-linkage, autism is more common in males with fragile X (about half) than in females (14–20%) [[Kaufmann2017,Marlborough2021]].
- Pedigree teaching point: a full mutation passed from a mother to a son is not offset by a second X, whereas daughters have a second X that partly compensates (unverified: standard X-linked inheritance, not quoted from a cited abstract).
- Rett syndrome is caused by mutations in the X-linked gene MECP2. In the discovery paper, 5 of 21 sporadic patients carried de novo mutations [[Amir1999]].
- Rett syndrome affects about 1 in 10,000 live female births and is often caused by MECP2 mutations. It remains a clinical diagnosis based on distinct criteria, whatever the molecular result [[Neul2010]].
- Classic Rett syndrome has been proposed to be X-linked dominant with lethality in hemizygous males, which is why it occurs almost only in females. Children appear to develop typically until 6–18 months. They then lose speech and purposeful hand use and develop features that include autism [[Amir1999]].
- Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is autosomal dominant. It is caused by loss-of-function variants in TSC1 or TSC2, which overactivate mTOR signalling. Its neuropsychiatric features (TAND) can include autism and cognitive disability [[Henske2016]].
- In a cohort of 50 children with TSC assessed in depth, 28 (56%) were autistic. Autistic children were more likely to have intellectual disability (79% vs 32%) [[Mitchell2025TSC]].
- In 18 autistic children with extreme macrocephaly (+2.5 to +8.0 SD), 3 (17%) carried germline PTEN mutations [[Butler2005PTEN]].
- In a clinical cohort tested for PTEN, mutations were found in 8.3% of children with ASD and 12.2% of those with developmental delay or intellectual disability. Every carrier had macrocephaly greater than 2 SD [[Varga2009]].
- About 25% (95% CI 16–33%) of people with constitutional PTEN mutations meet criteria for, or show characteristics of, ASD (pooled prevalence, 14 studies) [[Cummings2022PTEN]].

### genes · Clinical genetic testing
- Chromosomal microarray (CMA) has a diagnostic yield of 15–20% in unexplained developmental delay or intellectual disability, ASD or multiple congenital anomalies. G-banded karyotyping yields about 3% [[Miller2010CMA]].
- The 2010 international consensus recommends CMA in place of karyotyping as the first-tier cytogenetic test for these groups. Karyotyping is kept for obvious chromosomal syndromes, family rearrangements or multiple miscarriages [[Miller2010CMA]].
- In 258 consecutively assessed autistic children in Newfoundland, CMA gave a molecular diagnosis in 9.3% (95% CI 6.1–13.5%) and whole-exome sequencing (WES) in 8.4% (3.7–15.9%; 8 of 95 trios) [[Tammimies2015]].
- In that cohort, 15.8% of children who had both tests (15 of 95) had an identifiable genetic cause. Yields were higher in children with more major and minor physical anomalies [[Tammimies2015]].
- A meta-analysis of 30 studies found exome sequencing yields of [[Srivastava2019]]:
  - 36% overall in neurodevelopmental disorders (global delay, intellectual disability and/or ASD)
  - 31% in isolated neurodevelopmental disorder
  - 53% when other conditions were present
- On that evidence, the authors' consensus places exome sequencing at the start of the evaluation of unexplained neurodevelopmental disorders, as a first-tier test [[Srivastava2019]].
- The ACMG 2013 guideline frames genetic evaluation in autism around confirming the diagnosis first and discussing yields and family investment before testing. It also emphasises recognising syndromic conditions and individualising the plan [[Schaefer2013]].
- PTEN testing has been suggested for autistic patients with extreme macrocephaly [[Butler2005PTEN,Varga2009]].
- Rett syndrome is diagnosed on clinical criteria. A MECP2 result supports the diagnosis but does not define it [[Neul2010]].
- A genetic finding can inform recurrence risk for relatives and medical management [[Butler2005PTEN]]. PTEN is the gene of Cowden syndrome, a cancer syndrome [[Butler2005PTEN]], and TSC has organ-specific management, including mTOR inhibitors [[Henske2016]].
- Fragile X screening is done by PCR for the FMR1 CGG repeat, and the full mutation is more than 200 repeats [[Hagerman2017,SalcedoArellano2020]].
- A 2020 review summarises the AAP recommendation as testing people with intellectual disability or global developmental delay, or with a family history of the mutation or premutation [[SalcedoArellano2020]].

### risk · Parental age
- In a five-country cohort of 5,766,794 children (30,902 with ASD), relative risks after mutual adjustment were [[Sandin2016]]:
  - fathers aged 50 or older vs 20–29: RR 1.66 (95% CI 1.49–1.85)
  - mothers aged 40–49 vs 20–29: RR 1.15 (1.06–1.24)
  - mothers younger than 20 vs 20–29: RR 1.18 (1.08–1.29)
- In the same cohort, risk also rose as the age gap between parents grew [[Sandin2016]].
- In a Swedish birth cohort of 1,075,588, children of fathers aged 50 or older were 2.2 times (95% CI 1.26–3.88) as likely to be autistic as children of fathers aged 29 or younger. The association held within discordant sibling pairs [[Hultman2011]].
- A meta-analysis of 27 studies gave adjusted ORs for the highest vs reference parental-age category of 1.41 (1.29–1.55) for mothers and 1.55 (1.39–1.73) for fathers. Each additional 10 years of maternal or paternal age raised risk by 18% and 21% respectively [[Wu2017ParentalAge]].
- An umbrella review graded maternal age of 35 or older as convincing evidence, with RR 1.31 (95% CI 1.18–1.45) [[Kim2019Umbrella]].
- Advanced parental age and preterm birth are the non-genetic risk factors that epidemiology has "established" [[Lyall2017]].

### risk · Preterm birth
- In 4,061,795 Swedish singletons, ASD prevalence by gestational age at birth was:

  | Gestational age at birth | ASD prevalence |
  |---|---|
  | Extremely preterm (22–27 weeks) | 6.1% |
  | Very to moderate preterm (28–33 weeks) | 2.6% |
  | Late preterm (34–36 weeks) | 1.9% |
  | Early term (37–38 weeks) | 1.6% |
  | Term (39–41 weeks) | 1.4% |

  [[Crump2021Preterm]]
- Adjusted prevalence ratios against term birth were [[Crump2021Preterm]]:

  | Birth timing vs term | Boys | Girls |
  |---|---|---|
  | Extremely preterm | 3.72 (95% CI 3.27–4.23) | 4.19 (3.45–5.09) |
  | All preterm | 1.35 (1.30–1.40) | 1.53 (1.45–1.62) |
  | Early term | 1.11 (1.08–1.13) | 1.16 (1.12–1.20) |
- These associations were "only slightly attenuated" in co-sibling analyses, consistent with a potential causal link (Early evidence) [[Crump2021Preterm]].
- A meta-analysis of 18 studies (3,366 preterm infants, median gestation 28 weeks) found an ASD prevalence of 7% (95% CI 4–9%) among children born preterm [[Agrawal2018Preterm]].

### risk · Maternal diabetes, weight and blood pressure
- In 322,323 Kaiser Permanente Southern California births, unadjusted annual ASD incidence per 1000 children was [[Xiang2015]]:
  - 3.26 with maternal pre-existing type 2 diabetes
  - 3.02 with gestational diabetes (GDM) diagnosed by 26 weeks
  - 1.77 with GDM diagnosed after 26 weeks
  - 1.77 with no diabetes
- After full adjustment, GDM diagnosed by 26 weeks remained associated with ASD (HR 1.42; 95% CI 1.15–1.74). Pre-existing type 2 diabetes did not (HR 1.21; 0.97–1.52) [[Xiang2015]].
- In the same cohort, antidiabetic medicine exposure was not independently associated with ASD [[Xiang2015]].
- A meta-analysis of 16 studies found a pooled RR of 1.48 for maternal diabetes, with high heterogeneity and publication bias [[Wan2018Diabetes]].
- The umbrella review graded several maternal factors as convincing [[Kim2019Umbrella]]:
  - chronic hypertension: OR 1.48 (1.29–1.70)
  - gestational hypertension: OR 1.37 (1.21–1.54)
  - pre-eclampsia: RR 1.32 (1.20–1.45)
  - overweight before or during pregnancy: RR 1.28 (1.19–1.36)
- An earlier meta-analysis listed pre-eclampsia and maternal hypertension among the factors with the strongest evidence *against* a role. Evidence on these factors has shifted with newer, larger studies [[Gardener2009,Kim2019Umbrella]].

### risk · Valproate and other medicines in pregnancy
- In 655,615 Danish children, the 14-year absolute risk of ASD was 1.53% (95% CI 1.47–1.58%) overall. It was 4.42% (2.59–7.46%) in the 508 children exposed to valproate in pregnancy (adjusted HR 2.9; 1.7–4.9) [[Christensen2013]].
- In the same cohort, childhood autism risk was 2.50% after valproate exposure against 0.48% overall (adjusted HR 5.2; 2.7–10.0) [[Christensen2013]].
- Restricting to the 6,584 children of mothers with epilepsy, ASD risk was 4.15% (2.20–7.81%) in 432 valproate-exposed children and 2.44% (1.88–3.16%) in 6,152 unexposed children. The adjusted HR was 1.7 (0.9–3.2) for ASD and 2.9 (1.4–6.0) for childhood autism [[Christensen2013]].
- The US Depakote label's boxed warning on Fetal Risk states that "valproate can cause decreased IQ scores and neurodevelopmental disorders following in utero exposure" [[FDA-Depakote]].
- Section 8.1 of the same label names ASD, intellectual disability and ADHD among these risks, compared with exposure to other antiseizure drugs or none [[FDA-Depakote]].
- The label states valproate should not be used in pregnant women with epilepsy or bipolar disorder unless other medicines have failed or are unacceptable, and that it is contraindicated for migraine prophylaxis in pregnancy. Quoted as published, not as advice. Anyone taking valproate should not stop it without their prescriber [[FDA-Depakote]].
- At population level, first-trimester antidepressant exposure was linked with ASD in 1,580,629 Swedish offspring (5.28% exposed vs 2.14% unexposed by age 15; HR 2.02) [[Sujan2017]].
- In sibling comparisons in the same study, the HR was 0.83 (95% CI 0.62–1.13), which points to familial confounding rather than a drug effect [[Sujan2017]].
- In Stockholm, 4.1% of children exposed to antidepressants in pregnancy were autistic, against 2.9% of unexposed children whose mothers had a psychiatric history (adjusted OR 1.45; 1.13–1.85). The sibling analysis pointed the same way with wide CIs [[Rai2017Antidepressants]].
- The Stockholm authors stress that the absolute risk was small, and that the number of cases preventable by avoiding antidepressants would be small [[Rai2017Antidepressants]].
- The umbrella review rated SSRI use in pregnancy as convincing (OR 1.84). It also found that antidepressant use *before* pregnancy carried similar risk (RR 1.48), which suggests confounding by the mother's underlying condition [[Kim2019Umbrella]].

### risk · Folic acid and nutrition
- In the Norwegian MoBa cohort (85,176 children), autistic disorder occurred in 0.10% of children whose mothers took folic acid from 4 weeks before to 8 weeks after the start of pregnancy, against 0.21% of unexposed children (adjusted OR 0.61; 95% CI 0.41–0.90) [[Suren2013]].
- MoBa found no association with Asperger syndrome or PDD-NOS, though power was limited. Prenatal fish-oil use, with a similar maternal profile, showed no association [[Suren2013]].
- In an Israeli case-control cohort of 45,300 children, maternal folic acid before pregnancy was associated with lower ASD risk (RR 0.56; 0.42–0.74), and so was folic acid during pregnancy (RR 0.32; 0.26–0.41) [[Levine2018]].
- These are observational associations. They "cannot establish causality" but support periconceptional folic acid, which is recommended anyway to prevent neural tube defects (Early evidence) [[Suren2013,Levine2018]].
- A review of reviews rated the evidence on folic acid and omega-3 deficiency as inconclusive [[Modabbernia2017]].

### risk · Maternal infection, fever and immune activation
- A meta-analysis of 15 studies (more than 40,000 ASD cases) found maternal infection in pregnancy associated with ASD (OR 1.13; 95% CI 1.03–1.23). The OR was 1.30 (1.14–1.50) for infections requiring hospitalisation [[Jiang2016Infection]].
- In all Danish births 1980–2005, any maternal infection in pregnancy was not significantly associated with ASD (adjusted HR 1.14; 0.96–1.34) [[Atladottir2010]].
- In the same Danish data, hospitalisation for a viral infection in the first trimester (HR 2.98; 1.29–7.15) and for a bacterial infection in the second trimester (HR 1.42; 1.08–1.87) was associated with ASD [[Atladottir2010]].
- In the Norwegian MoBa cohort, maternal fever in the second trimester was associated with ASD (adjusted OR 1.40; 1.09–1.79). With three or more fever episodes after 12 weeks the aOR was 3.12 (1.28–7.63) (Early evidence) [[Hornig2018]].
- Maternal immune activation is a proposed mechanism behind these associations (Hypothesis) [[Hornig2018,Jiang2016Infection]].

### risk · Interpregnancy interval
- In a systematic review of 7 studies (1,140,210 children), an interpregnancy interval under 12 months, against 36 months or more, was associated with ASD (pooled adjusted OR 1.90; 95% CI 1.16–3.09) and more strongly with autistic disorder (OR 2.62; 1.53–4.50) [[CondeAgudelo2016]].
- In a Finnish national study, against intervals of 24–59 months, ASD odds were higher at the extremes [[CheslackPostava2014]]:
  - under 12 months: OR 1.50 (1.28–1.74)
  - 60–119 months: OR 1.28 (1.08–1.52)
  - 120 months or more: OR 1.44 (1.12–1.85)
- Short interpregnancy interval is a "potential" risk factor (Early evidence) [[Lyall2017]].

### risk · What does not cause autism: vaccines
- In a nationwide Danish cohort of 657,461 children (5,025,754 person-years; 6,517 autism diagnoses), MMR-vaccinated children had a fully adjusted autism hazard ratio of 0.93 (95% CI 0.85–1.02) compared with unvaccinated children [[Hviid2019]].
- The same cohort found no increased risk in subgroups defined by sibling history of autism or other risk factors. It found no clustering of cases in the time after vaccination [[Hviid2019]].
- An earlier Danish cohort of 537,303 children (82.0% MMR-vaccinated) found adjusted relative risks of 0.92 (0.68–1.24) for autistic disorder and 0.83 (0.65–1.07) for other ASD. Neither varied with age at vaccination or time since vaccination [[Madsen2002]].
- Among US children with an autistic older sibling, MMR was not associated with higher ASD risk [[Jain2015MMR]]:
  - one dose vs none at age 2: RR 0.76 (0.49–1.18)
  - two doses vs none at age 5: RR 0.56 (0.31–1.01)
- A meta-analysis of five cohort studies (1,256,407 children) found no link between vaccination and autism (OR 0.99; 0.92–1.06). There was also no link for:
  - MMR: OR 0.84 (0.70–1.01)
  - thimerosal: OR 1.00 (0.77–1.31)
  - mercury: OR 1.00 (0.93–1.07) [[Taylor2014Vaccine]]
- In 467,450 Danish children, thimerosal-containing and thimerosal-free versions of the same vaccine carried similar autism risk (RR 0.85; 0.60–1.20). There was no dose-response (RR 0.98 per 25 µg ethylmercury) [[Hviid2003]].
- A US case-control study (256 ASD, 752 controls) found no increased ASD risk from prenatal or infant ethylmercury exposure from vaccines or immunoglobulins [[Price2010Thimerosal]].
- After thimerosal was removed from nearly all US childhood vaccines (1999–2001), autism counts in California's developmental services system kept rising through 2007. This does not support thimerosal as a primary cause [[Schechter2008]].
- Cumulative exposure to vaccine antigens by age 2 was not associated with ASD (aOR 0.999 per 25-unit increase), which counters the "too many vaccines too soon" idea [[DeStefano2013]].
- The 1998 Lancet paper by Wakefield and colleagues that proposed an MMR link was formally retracted by the Lancet in February 2010 [[Lancet2010Retraction]].
- In 2011 the BMJ published an investigation (Deer) and an editorial concluding that the Wakefield article was fraudulent [[Deer2011,Godlee2011]].
- A review of reviews lists vaccination and thimerosal exposure among factors unrelated to ASD risk (Established) [[Modabbernia2017]].

### risk · What does not cause autism: acetaminophen (paracetamol) in pregnancy
- In 2,480,797 Swedish children, crude 10-year absolute autism risk was 1.33% without and 1.53% with prenatal acetaminophen exposure. The population model gave HR 1.05 (1.02–1.08), with a risk difference of 0.09% [[Ahlqvist2024]].
- In sibling-control analyses of the same cohort, there was no association (HR 0.98; 0.93–1.04) and no dose-response. The authors attribute the population association to familial confounding [[Ahlqvist2024]].
- A Hong Kong sibling-matched cohort (124,333 children for ASD analysis) found no association with ASD (aHR 1.00; 0.91–1.11) [[Luo2026Paracetamol]].
- In that cohort, *pre-pregnancy* acetaminophen use was also "associated" with ASD (HR 1.12). This negative-control finding points to confounding, not a drug effect [[Luo2026Paracetamol]].
- A 2025 BMJ umbrella review (9 reviews, 40 studies) rated confidence in existing reviews as low to critically low. It concluded that the evidence "does not clearly link" maternal paracetamol with autism [[Sheikh2025]].
- A 2025 Navigation Guide review of 46 studies concluded the evidence is consistent with an association and urged limiting use. Its senior author disclosed serving as a plaintiffs' expert witness on this question [[Prada2025]].
- See Open questions for how the two positions differ. The strongest designs, which control for familial confounding, find no association [[Ahlqvist2024,Luo2026Paracetamol,Sheikh2025]].

### risk · What does not cause autism: parenting ("refrigerator mother")
- In the mid-20th century, Bruno Bettelheim promoted the "refrigerator mother" idea: that cold, unresponsive mothering caused autism. His remedy was to separate the child from the mother [[vanRosmalen2020]].
- Twin and family studies point to genetic influence, not parenting style. Heritability is about 80% [[Bai2019,Sandin2017]], with little or no shared-environment effect [[Tick2016,Sandin2014]].
- The five-country cohort estimated the maternal effect at only 0.4–1.6% [[Bai2019]].

### risk · Putting environmental risk in context
- An umbrella review of 67 environmental risk factors (544,212 cases) found convincing evidence only for maternal age 35 or older, chronic and gestational hypertension, overweight, pre-eclampsia, prepregnancy antidepressant use and SSRI use in pregnancy. Only overweight and SSRI use kept a high evidence grade in sensitivity analyses [[Kim2019Umbrella]].
- Most listed associations are modest (relative risks of about 1.1–2). Confounding by shared family genetics is a recurrent explanation, shown by sibling designs for antidepressants and acetaminophen [[Kim2019Umbrella,Sujan2017,Ahlqvist2024]].
- Birth complications linked with trauma, ischaemia or hypoxia show strong links with ASD. Maternal obesity, diabetes and caesarean section show weaker but significant associations [[Modabbernia2017]].
- Maternal smoking and assisted reproduction are "most likely" unrelated to ASD [[Modabbernia2017]].
- The rise in autism diagnoses over time is unlikely to be explained by environmental change, since genetic factors played a consistently larger role across birth cohorts [[Taylor2020Twins]].

### Exam hooks
- Twin and family heritability of ASD is about 80% (five-country median 80.8%; Swedish reanalysis 0.83) [[Bai2019,Sandin2017]].
- Twin meta-analysis: MZ correlation 0.98, and heritability 64–91% [[Tick2016]].
- Sibling recurrence in prospective infant-sibling cohorts is about 1 in 5: 18.7% (2011) and 20.2% (2024) [[Ozonoff2011,Ozonoff2024]].
- Recurrence is higher if the younger sibling is male, if more than one older sibling is autistic, or if the autistic older sibling is female [[Ozonoff2011,Ozonoff2024]].
- Relative recurrence risk falls with relatedness: MZ 153, full sibling 10.3, half-sibling about 3, cousin 2.0 [[Sandin2014]].
- Common variants carry most heritable liability; de novo mutations explain only 2.6% of population variance but matter greatly for individuals [[Gaugler2014]].
- Satterstrom 2020 identified 102 ASD risk genes; Fu 2022 identified 72 at FDR ≤0.001 [[Satterstrom2020,Fu2022]].
- The 16p11.2 deletion and duplication account for about 1% of autism [[Weiss2008NEJM]].
- Rett syndrome is caused by MECP2 (X-linked), occurs almost only in females, and shows autism features in about 61% [[Amir1999,Richards2015]].
- Fragile X: more than 200 CGG repeats in FMR1 silence the gene. About half of males and nearly 20% of females with fragile X meet ASD criteria [[Hagerman2017,Kaufmann2017]].
- PTEN: test in autism with extreme macrocephaly. About 25% of PTEN carriers have ASD features [[Butler2005PTEN,Cummings2022PTEN]].
- First-tier genetic tests: CMA yields 15–20% in unexplained DD/ID/ASD (karyotype about 3%); exome sequencing yields 36% in neurodevelopmental disorders overall [[Miller2010CMA,Srivastava2019]].
- Prenatal valproate: absolute ASD risk 4.42% vs 1.53% in all children (adjusted HR 2.9) [[Christensen2013]].
- Paternal age 50 or older vs 20–29: RR 1.66 for ASD [[Sandin2016]].
- Extremely preterm birth (22–27 weeks): 6.1% ASD prevalence vs 1.4% at term [[Crump2021Preterm]].
- MMR does not cause autism: HR 0.93 (0.85–1.02) in 657,461 Danish children [[Hviid2019]].
- The Wakefield 1998 paper was retracted (2010) and judged fraudulent (BMJ 2011) [[Lancet2010Retraction,Godlee2011]].
- Removing thimerosal from vaccines did not lower autism rates [[Schechter2008,Hviid2003]].
- Prenatal acetaminophen sibling-control HR 0.98 (0.93–1.04): the population association reflects familial confounding [[Ahlqvist2024]].
- Male-to-female ratio is closer to 3:1 than 4:1, and girls are under-diagnosed [[Loomes2017]].

### In plain words
- Autism runs in families mostly because of genes, which are passed down and shared in different amounts [[Bai2019,Sandin2014]].
- Parents do not cause autism by how they love or raise their child. The old "cold mother" idea was wrong [[vanRosmalen2020,Bai2019]].
- If one child in a family is autistic, a younger brother or sister has about a 1 in 5 chance of also being autistic. Most younger siblings are not autistic [[Ozonoff2024]].
- There is no single "autism gene". Usually many small genetic differences add up, and sometimes one bigger change plays a large part [[Gaugler2014,Satterstrom2020]].
- A genetic test (often a chromosome microarray or exome test) finds a specific cause in some autistic children, not most. It can help with family planning and health checks [[Tammimies2015,Srivastava2019]].
- Some genetic conditions, such as fragile X syndrome, Rett syndrome and tuberous sclerosis, make autism more likely [[Richards2015]].
- Vaccines, including MMR, do not cause autism. Very large studies of millions of children show this [[Hviid2019,Taylor2014Vaccine]].
- The study that started the vaccine scare was withdrawn and found to be fraudulent [[Lancet2010Retraction,Godlee2011]].
- Taking acetaminophen (paracetamol) in pregnancy has not been shown to cause autism. The best-designed studies, which compare brothers and sisters, find no link [[Ahlqvist2024,Luo2026Paracetamol]].
- Being born very early, or having older parents, slightly raises the chance of autism. Most children in these groups are not autistic [[Crump2021Preterm,Sandin2016]].
- A few medicines, such as valproate, can affect a baby's brain development in pregnancy. Anyone taking them should talk with their doctor before pregnancy and should not stop on their own [[Christensen2013,FDA-Depakote]].
- Taking folic acid before and early in pregnancy is already advised, and some studies link it with a lower chance of autism [[Suren2013,Levine2018]].
- These numbers describe large groups of people. They cannot tell you what caused autism in one particular person.

## Open questions and controversies
- **How large is the shared-environment share?** One position is moderate heritability (about 38%) with a large shared twin environment (about 58%) [[Hallmayer2011]]. The other is high heritability (64–91%, about 80%, 0.83) with little or no shared environment [[Tick2016,Bai2019,Sandin2017]]. Status: the larger, population-based and meta-analytic estimates dominate. Tick and colleagues attribute high shared-environment estimates to over-inclusion of concordant DZ twins.
- **Why did Sandin 2014 (50%) and Sandin 2017 (83%) differ on the same data?** The 2017 letter attributes the gap to how time-to-event data were modelled, and prefers the standard models [[Sandin2014,Sandin2017]]. Status: about 80% is the commonly quoted figure, and Bai 2019 independently gives 80.8% [[Bai2019]].
- **Female protective effect.** Several kinds of evidence point the same way: higher recurrence in siblings of autistic girls, more polygenic risk in mothers, and higher rare-variant load in autistic girls [[Robinson2013,Wigdor2022,Antaki2022,Ozonoff2024]]. Diagnostic bias against girls also inflates the observed ratio [[Loomes2017]]. Status: both mechanisms are likely (Early evidence).
- **Acetaminophen in pregnancy.** Position A: a Navigation Guide review of 46 studies (27 positive) supports an association and urges limiting use; its senior author disclosed plaintiff expert-witness work [[Prada2025]]. Position B: sibling-controlled cohorts in Sweden and Hong Kong find no association, a negative-control pre-pregnancy exposure also "associates", and a BMJ umbrella review rates the review evidence low to critically low [[Ahlqvist2024,Luo2026Paracetamol,Sheikh2025]]. Status: designs that control for familial confounding do not support a causal link. Untreated fever, the main reason for use, is itself associated with risk in some studies [[Hornig2018]]. The 2025 US federal statements and labelling actions on this question were not validated in this pass and are left to pass F or A.
- **Antidepressants and SSRIs in pregnancy.** Population associations are convincing (SSRI OR 1.84) [[Kim2019Umbrella]]. Sibling designs mostly attenuate them (HR 0.83 in Sweden) [[Sujan2017]], while one Stockholm study judged that the association "might not solely be" confounding [[Rai2017Antidepressants]]. Status: confounding by maternal mental health explains much or all of the association. Absolute risks are small.
- **Maternal hypertension and pre-eclampsia.** A 2009 meta-analysis listed them among factors with evidence against a role [[Gardener2009]]. A 2019 umbrella review rated them convincing (OR 1.37–1.48) [[Kim2019Umbrella]]. Status: unresolved as to causation. Shared genetics and other confounders are not excluded.
- **Folic acid.** Two large cohorts show lower risk with periconceptional folic acid [[Suren2013,Levine2018]], but a review of reviews calls the nutrient evidence inconclusive [[Modabbernia2017]]. Status: an association, not a proven causal protection.
- **Interpregnancy interval.** Both short and long intervals are associated with ASD [[CondeAgudelo2016,CheslackPostava2014]]. The mechanism is unknown, and Lyall and colleagues class short intervals only as a "potential" risk factor [[Lyall2017]].
- **Share of all autistic people with a single-gene syndrome.** This pass validated the share of people *with each syndrome* who are autistic [[Richards2015]], plus 16p11.2 at about 1% of autism [[Weiss2008NEJM]] and PTEN at 8.3% in a selected cohort with macrocephaly [[Varga2009]]. I did not find a validated abstract-level figure for the share of all autistic people with fragile X or tuberous sclerosis. Builders should not quote one from this file.
- **Exome-first testing in autism specifically.** Srivastava 2019 pools neurodevelopmental disorders including ASD (36% yield) [[Srivastava2019]]. In an unselected autism clinic sample, CMA (9.3%) and WES (8.4%) yields were similar and lower [[Tammimies2015]]. Status: yield depends strongly on the phenotype (intellectual disability, dysmorphology).
