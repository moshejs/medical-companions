# G · Co-occurring conditions, physical health, course & outcomes

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: cooccur, health, adult.

This pass covers the mental health and physical health conditions that often occur alongside autism, how and when they tend to appear across the lifespan, mortality and its main causes (including suicide, injury, drowning and wandering), and what long-term follow-up studies say about adult outcomes, quality of life, autistic burnout and ageing. Every number below comes from the abstract or full text of the cited source, with its population and comparator.

Language note: many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"), while many families and clinicians use person-first language ("person with autism"). This file uses both. Older studies use terms such as "Asperger's syndrome", "high-functioning" and "low-functioning" ASD; those terms appear here only when quoting how a study defined its groups (in Hirvikoski 2016 and Ghirardi 2018, "low-functioning" means ASD with a recorded intellectual disability).

Access notes:
- PubMed E-utilities was heavily rate-limited during this pass (shared with seven parallel agents), so searching was done through Europe PMC, and every record was then confirmed against PubMed esummary (see Provenance).
- Hirvikoski 2016 (BJPsych) is not in PubMed Central. Its mean ages at death and cause-specific odds ratios were read from the publisher's full-text HTML (Cambridge Core), Tables 3 and 4.
- Lai 2019 (Lancet Psychiatry) was read at abstract level only; the full text is paywalled. Its age-of-onset data are not given in the abstract, so the lifespan "emergence" anchors below come from cohort studies that report ages directly (Viscidi 2013, Bolton 2011, Rai 2018, Wing 2000, Anderson 2012, Meier 2015).
- Grove 2019 (Nat Genet) and the 2022 Lancet Commission were checked but dropped: the open text available to me did not state the autism–ADHD genetic correlation or the "profound autism" share in a form I could quote. The autism–ADHD genetic overlap is therefore cited from the Swedish family and twin register studies (Ghirardi 2018, 2021).
- Lai 2019 is the shared canonical key; Shaw2025, Lord2018 and Hyman2020 are also shared canonical keys and were each checked against PubMed.

## Findings

### cooccur · How common co-occurring mental health conditions are
- Lai 2019 pooled 96 studies of people with autism (diagnoses confirmed by DSM or ICD criteria) and estimated these prevalences of co-occurring conditions, with 95% CI:
  - ADHD 28% (25–32)
  - anxiety disorders 20% (17–23)
  - sleep-wake disorders 13% (9–17)
  - disruptive, impulse-control and conduct disorders 12% (10–15)
  - depressive disorders 11% (9–13)
  - obsessive-compulsive disorder 9% (7–10)
  - bipolar disorders 5% (3–6)
  - schizophrenia spectrum disorders 4% (3–5) [[Lai2019]]
- In Lai 2019, estimates from clinical samples were higher than those from population-based and registry-based studies, and remaining heterogeneity was very large (all I² >95%), so a single pooled figure hides wide variation by setting [[Lai2019]].
- Age, gender, intellectual functioning and country of study each explained some of the variation in Lai 2019 (Established) [[Lai2019]].
- Lai 2019 described three further categories narratively rather than pooling them: trauma and stressor-related disorders, substance-related and addictive disorders, and gender dysphoria [[Lai2019]].
- An umbrella review of 26 systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Hossain 2020) found a high burden of anxiety, depressive, bipolar and mood, schizophrenia spectrum, suicidal behaviour, ADHD and disruptive or conduct disorders in autistic people, mostly from studies of younger people in high-income countries [[Hossain2020]].
- Hossain 2020 noted limited evidence from low- and middle-income countries, so global estimates rest mainly on Western samples [[Hossain2020]].
- In a population-derived UK sample of 112 children aged 10–14 with ASD (Simonoff 2008), 70% had at least one co-occurring psychiatric disorder and 41% had two or more (3-month prevalence by parent interview) [[Simonoff2008]].
- The commonest diagnoses in Simonoff 2008 were social anxiety disorder 29.2% (95% CI 13.2–45.1), ADHD 28.2% (13.3–43.0) and oppositional defiant disorder 28.1% (13.9–42.2); 84% of those with ADHD had a further diagnosis [[Simonoff2008]].
- Clinical teams are advised to evaluate co-occurring psychiatric disorders routinely as part of assessment of autistic children (Established) [[Simonoff2008,Lai2019]].
- The AAP clinical report lists sleep and feeding disorders, gastrointestinal symptoms, obesity, seizures, ADHD, anxiety and wandering as co-occurring conditions primary care clinicians should know, because they affect function and quality of life [[Hyman2020]].

### cooccur · Anxiety
- In a meta-analysis of 31 studies (2,121 young people under 18 with ASD), 39.6% had at least one DSM-IV anxiety disorder [[vanSteensel2011]].
- The most frequent anxiety disorders in van Steensel 2011 were specific phobia (29.8%), OCD (17.4%) and social anxiety disorder (16.6%) [[vanSteensel2011]].
- In autistic adults (Hollocks 2019; 30 anxiety studies, n = 26,070), pooled current prevalence of any anxiety disorder was 27% and lifetime prevalence 42% [[Hollocks2019]].
- Hollocks 2019 found that questionnaire measures and the presence of intellectual disability significantly changed prevalence estimates, and that the literature over-relied on clinical samples [[Hollocks2019]].

### cooccur · Depression
- A meta-analysis of 66 studies (Hudson 2019) found pooled lifetime prevalence of depressive disorders of 14.4% (95% CI 10.3–19.8) and current prevalence of 12.3% (9.7–15.5) in autistic children, adolescents and adults [[Hudson2019]].
- In Hudson 2019, rates were higher when a standardised interview was used (lifetime 28.5%) and highest when participants reported their own symptoms (lifetime 48.6%, 95% CI 33.3–64.2) [[Hudson2019]].
- Hudson 2019 estimated that autistic people are about 4 times as likely as typically developing people to experience depression in their lifetime, with higher rates in studies of participants with higher intelligence [[Hudson2019]].
- In autistic adults, pooled current and lifetime depression prevalence were 23% and 37% (Hollocks 2019; 29 studies, n = 26,117) [[Hollocks2019]].
- In the Stockholm Youth Cohort, 19.8% of autistic people had a depression diagnosis by age 27, against 6.0% of the general population (adjusted RR 3.64; 95% CI 3.41–3.88) [[Rai2018Depression]].
- Depression risk in Rai 2018 was higher in autism without intellectual disability (adjusted RR 4.28; 4.00–4.58) than in autism with intellectual disability (adjusted RR 1.81; 1.51–2.17) [[Rai2018Depression]].
- Compared with their own non-autistic full siblings, autistic people in Rai 2018 still had more than twice the odds of a depression diagnosis (adjusted OR 2.50; 1.91–3.27), so shared family factors do not explain all of the excess [[Rai2018Depression]].

### cooccur · ADHD and the autism–ADHD overlap
- In 1,899,654 people born in Sweden 1987–2006, autistic people had far higher odds of an ADHD diagnosis than non-autistic people (OR 22.33; 95% CI 21.77–22.92) [[Ghirardi2018]].
- The autism–ADHD association in Ghirardi 2018 was stronger for autism without intellectual disability than for autism with intellectual disability [[Ghirardi2018]].
- Relatives of autistic people had higher ADHD risk, and the association was graded by genetic relatedness: monozygotic twins OR 17.77 (9.80–32.22), dizygotic twins 4.33 (3.21–5.85), full siblings 4.59 (4.39–4.80) (Established) [[Ghirardi2018]].
- In 2,398,608 Swedish individuals (Ghirardi 2021), the genetic correlation between autism and other neurodevelopmental disorders was rg = 0.73 (95% CI 0.66–0.79) [[Ghirardi2021]].
- Ghirardi 2021 estimated moderate genetic correlations of autism with anxiety (rg 0.47; 0.33–0.61), depression (rg 0.52; 0.37–0.66) and intentional self-harm (rg 0.54; 0.36–0.71) (Early evidence) [[Ghirardi2021]].
- Family history of autism with intellectual disability, epilepsy, chromosomal abnormalities or congenital malformations was less related to other psychiatric disorders, suggesting partly different causes in that subgroup (Hypothesis) [[Ghirardi2021]].

### cooccur · OCD, bipolar disorder and psychosis
- In Danish registers, people first diagnosed with autism had about twice the risk of a later OCD diagnosis (IRR 2.18; 95% CI 1.91–2.48) [[Meier2015]].
- People first diagnosed with OCD had nearly four times the risk of a later autism diagnosis (IRR 3.91; 3.46–4.40), and parental OCD was linked to autism in offspring [[Meier2015]].
- In autistic adults (Varcin 2022; 53 studies), the pooled prevalence of psychosis was 9.4% (N = 63,657; 95% CI 7.52–11.72) and of bipolar disorder 7.5% (N = 31,739; 5.79–9.53) [[Varcin2022]].
- Varcin 2022 found males more likely to have co-occurring psychosis and females more likely to have co-occurring bipolar disorder [[Varcin2022]].

### cooccur · Self-injury
- A meta-analysis of 37 papers (14,379 participants) estimated the prevalence of self-injurious behaviour in autism at 42% (95% CI 38–47%) [[SteenfeldtKristensen2020]].
- Hand-hitting was the most common form of self-injury (23%) and self-cutting the least common (3%); females had higher prevalence than males [[SteenfeldtKristensen2020]].
- Hair pulling and self-scratching were associated with intellectual disability; overall self-injury prevalence was not [[SteenfeldtKristensen2020]].

### cooccur · Eating disorders and ARFID
- In a prevalence meta-analysis of 21 studies (n = 7,442), 11.41% of autistic groups met criteria for avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID; 95% CI 2.89–35.76%), a very wide interval resting on only 3 autism studies [[Sader2025]].
- In the same meta-analysis, 16.27% of people with ARFID had an autism diagnosis (95% CI 8.64–28.53%) [[Sader2025]].
- People with anorexia nervosa had higher autistic traits than healthy controls (Hedges g = 0.88; 0.65–1.12), and 29% (19–38%) scored above the ADOS cut-off for autism (22 studies, 1,172 patients) [[InalKaleli2025]].
- Above-cut-off ADOS scores in anorexia do not equal an autism diagnosis; starvation itself can produce social and rigid features, so these numbers are a screening signal (Early evidence) [[InalKaleli2025,Westwood2017]].
- A review of autism in anorexia found consistent over-representation of autistic symptoms but could not settle the true prevalence because tools, methods and populations differed; co-occurring anorexia and autism may need tailored treatment [[Westwood2017]].

### cooccur · Diagnostic overshadowing, camouflaging and misdiagnosis
- Diagnostic overshadowing (a new symptom such as pain, low mood or catatonia being put down to autism itself and not assessed) is one proposed reason for unmet needs (Hypothesis). Autistic adults reported higher odds of unmet mental health needs (OR 2.2; 95% CI 1.3–3.7) and physical health needs (OR 1.9; 1.1–3.4), and more emergency department use (OR 2.1; 1.8–3.8), than non-autistic adults [[Nicolaidis2013]].
- In an international survey, 80% of autistic adults and 37% of non-autistic adults reported difficulty visiting a general practitioner [[Doherty2022]].
- The barriers autistic adults rated highest in Doherty 2022 were deciding whether symptoms warranted a visit (72%), booking by telephone (62%), not feeling understood (56%), communicating with the doctor (53%) and the waiting room environment (51%) [[Doherty2022]].
- Doherty 2022 linked these barriers to self-reported untreated conditions, missed screening and late presentations needing more extensive treatment [[Doherty2022]].
- A systematic review of camouflaging (masking autistic traits in social situations; 29 studies) found preliminary support that higher self-reported camouflaging is associated with worse mental health, and that sex and gender differences in camouflaging exist (Early evidence) [[Cook2021Camouflaging]].
- Late-diagnosed autistic women described "pretending to be normal", professionals missing their autism because of their gender, and misdiagnosis before the autism diagnosis (qualitative study, n = 14) [[Bargiela2016]].
- Misdiagnosis of autistic women as having borderline personality disorder (BPD) is widely discussed, but a meta-analysis found pooled BPD prevalence in autism of 4% (95% CI 0–9%) and autism in BPD of 3% (1–8%), within population ranges, and could not test misdiagnosis directly (Hypothesis) [[May2021BPD]].
- May 2021 noted that most included samples were small and clinical, with 11 of 19 studies at high risk of bias [[May2021BPD]].

### cooccur · Intellectual disability and language
- In the US ADDM Network 2022 surveillance, 39.6% of 8-year-olds with autism who had cognitive data (5,292 of 8,613) were classified as having intellectual disability [[Shaw2025]].
- The intellectual disability share differed by group in ADDM 2022: 52.8% of Black, 50.0% of American Indian or Alaska Native, 43.9% of Asian or Pacific Islander, 38.8% of Hispanic, 32.7% of White and 31.2% of multiracial autistic children [[Shaw2025]].
- About 30% of autistic children are estimated to remain minimally verbal even after years of intervention and education [[TagerFlusberg2013]].
- Minimally verbal autistic children are a highly variable group whose abilities are hard to assess reliably, and newer methods include eye-tracking, brain imaging and augmentative communication (Early evidence) [[TagerFlusberg2013]].
- In a cohort diagnosed at age 2 and followed to 19, verbal and nonverbal IQ at age 2 together predicted significant intellectual disability at 19 about 85% of the time [[Anderson2014Outcome]].

### health · Epilepsy
- A systematic review of 74 studies (283,549 patients) found a median period prevalence of epilepsy in autism of 12.1%, and of autism in epilepsy of 9.0% [[Lukmanji2019]].
- Excluding studies of syndromic epilepsy or developmental delay, these medians were 11.2% (epilepsy in autism) and 8.1% (autism in epilepsy) [[Lukmanji2019]].
- Lukmanji 2019 observed trends for epilepsy in autism to be higher in females and autism in epilepsy to be higher in males, with significant heterogeneity between studies [[Lukmanji2019]].
- In a meta-analysis by intellectual ability (Amiet 2008), pooled epilepsy prevalence was 21.5% in autistic people with intellectual disability against 8% in those without [[Amiet2008]].
- The male:female ratio was about 2:1 in autism with epilepsy against 3.5:1 in autism without epilepsy [[Amiet2008]].
- In 5,815 children with autism (Viscidi 2013), average epilepsy prevalence at ages 2–17 was 12.5%, rising to 26% among those aged 13 and older [[Viscidi2013]].
- Only older age and lower cognitive ability were independently associated with epilepsy in Viscidi 2013; language level and regression were not, after controlling for IQ [[Viscidi2013]].
- In 150 people diagnosed with autism in childhood and followed to age 21+, 22% developed epilepsy, and in most, seizures began after age 10 [[Bolton2011]].
- Generalised tonic-clonic seizures predominated (88%) in Bolton 2011, and most people (28 of 31) were controlled with one or two anticonvulsants [[Bolton2011]].
- In autistic Medicare enrollees aged 65 and over, epilepsy was far more common than in matched controls (OR 18.9; 95% CI 17.2–20.7) [[Hand2020Medicare]].
- In Hirvikoski 2016, epilepsy was the most common cause of death in the autism-with-intellectual-disability group, and death from nervous system diseases had an OR of 40.56 (26.82–61.33) in that group [[Hirvikoski2016]].

### health · Sleep
- Sleep problems, especially insomnia, affect children and adolescents with autism more than typically developing children, with reported rates of 40% to 80% [[Cortesi2010]].
- Cortesi 2010 noted that children with a history of developmental regression have more disturbed sleep, and that behavioural interventions help sleep onset and maintenance, with melatonin as a further option (Established) [[Cortesi2010]].
- In autistic adults (Morgan 2020; 8 datasets, 194 autistic and 277 controls), sleep was worse on most measures: lower subjective sleep efficiency (SMD −0.87), longer polysomnographic sleep onset latency (SMD 0.86) and more wake after sleep onset on actigraphy (SMD 0.57) [[Morgan2020Sleep]].
- Lai 2019 pooled 13% (9–17%) for diagnosed sleep-wake disorders, much lower than the 40–80% for reported sleep problems; a formal diagnosis is a stricter bar than a reported sleep problem, which likely explains much of the gap [[Lai2019,Cortesi2010]].

### health · Gastrointestinal symptoms
- In a meta-analysis of 15 controlled studies, autistic children had more general GI symptoms than comparison children (OR 4.42; 95% CI 1.90–10.28) [[McElhanon2014]].
- McElhanon 2014 found higher odds of diarrhoea (OR 3.63; 1.82–7.23), constipation (OR 3.86; 2.23–6.71) and abdominal pain (OR 2.45; 1.19–5.07) [[McElhanon2014]].
- McElhanon 2014 could not analyse underlying GI causes (for example reflux) because of high methodological variability; dietary restriction is a possible moderator (Hypothesis) [[McElhanon2014]].
- In autistic Medicare enrollees aged 65 and over, gastrointestinal conditions were more common than in controls (OR 5.2; 4.9–5.5) [[Hand2020Medicare]].

### health · Feeding selectivity and nutrition
- In a meta-analysis of 17 prospective controlled studies, autistic children had more feeding problems than peers (OR 5.11; 95% CI 3.74–6.97) [[Sharp2013Feeding]].
- Sharp 2013 found lower intake of calcium (OR 0.31; 0.11–0.85) and protein (OR 0.35; 0.14–0.56) in autistic children [[Sharp2013Feeding]].

### health · Obesity
- In 20 studies, 17% (95% CI 13–22%) of autistic children and adolescents had obesity, and the relative risk against controls was 1.58 (1.34–1.86) [[Sammels2022]].
- Almost all studies in Sammels 2022 were from the USA; the few European studies did not show a significant difference [[Sammels2022]].
- Another meta-analysis (Kahathuduwa 2019) found 22.2% obesity prevalence among autistic children and a 41.1% greater risk than neurotypical peers [[Kahathuduwa2019]].
- In Kahathuduwa 2019, non-Caucasian race, older age, female sex and living in the USA were associated with higher obesity rates [[Kahathuduwa2019]].
- Among 4,123 autistic Kaiser Permanente members aged 14–25, obesity affected 25% [[Davignon2018]].

### health · Dental health
- A meta-analysis of children and young adults with autism found pooled prevalence of dental caries of 60.6% (95% CI 44.0–75.1) and of periodontal disease of 69.4% (47.6–85.0, from only 3 studies) [[daSilva2017]].
- A later meta-analysis of 25 studies (da Motta 2026) found no significant difference in most caries indices (DMFT, dmft), but a higher DMFS (decayed, missing, filled surfaces) score in autistic children, suggesting more severe caries where it occurs [[daMotta2026]].

### health · Catatonia
- A meta-analysis of 7 studies (969 individuals, mean age 21 years) estimated that 10.4% (95% CI 5.8–18.0%) of autistic people have catatonia [[VaquerizoSerrano2021]].
- Treatments reported in Vaquerizo-Serrano 2021 included benzodiazepines, antipsychotics and electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), and the review suggested ECT might help (Early evidence) [[VaquerizoSerrano2021]].
- In 506 referrals to a UK specialist clinic, 17% of those aged 15 or older had severe exacerbation of catatonic features, usually emerging in adolescence or early adulthood [[Wing2000]].
- Those affected in Wing 2000 more often had impaired language and social passivity before the change [[Wing2000]].

### health · Physical health in autistic adults
- In 1,507 autistic adults in Kaiser Permanente Northern California and 15,070 matched controls (Croen 2015), all major psychiatric disorders, including depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, OCD, schizophrenia and suicide attempts, were significantly more common [[Croen2015Adults]].
- Nearly all medical conditions in Croen 2015 were more common in autistic adults, including immune, gastrointestinal and sleep disorders, seizures, obesity, dyslipidaemia, hypertension and diabetes [[Croen2015Adults]].
- Among 4,123 autistic people aged 14–25 (Davignon 2018), 34% had a co-occurring psychiatric condition, and the commonest medical conditions were infections (42%), obesity (25%), neurological (18%), allergy or immune (16%), musculoskeletal (15%) and gastrointestinal (11%) conditions [[Davignon2018]].
- Davignon 2018 concluded that autism should be approached as a chronic health condition requiring regular follow-up and routine screening [[Davignon2018]].
- An online survey found more heart conditions, lung conditions and diabetes in autistic than non-autistic adults, persisting after accounting for smoking, alcohol and body mass index [[Weir2021]].
- In 2,305 adults, autistic people had elevated rates of non-communicable conditions across all organ systems, including gastrointestinal, neurological, endocrine, visual, ear-nose-throat, skin, liver, kidney and haematological conditions (convenience sample; Early evidence) [[Ward2023PhysHealth]].

### health · Wandering, injury and drowning
- In a US survey of 1,218 autistic children, 49% had attempted to elope (wander away) at least once after age 4, and 26% were missing long enough to cause concern [[Anderson2012Elopement]].
- Of children who went missing in Anderson 2012, 24% were in danger of drowning and 65% in danger of traffic injury [[Anderson2012Elopement]].
- Elopement risk rose with autism severity: about 9% per 10-point increase in Social Responsiveness Scale T score (RR 1.09; 95% CI 1.02–1.16) [[Anderson2012Elopement]].
- Among 1,367 US deaths of people with an autism diagnosis (1999–2014), mean age at death was 36.2 years against 72.0 years in the general population [[Guan2017AJPH]].
- Injury caused 27.9% of these deaths (proportionate mortality ratio 2.93; 95% CI 2.64–3.24) [[Guan2017AJPH]].
- The leading injury causes in Guan 2017 were suffocation (PMR 31.93), asphyxiation (PMR 13.50) and drowning (PMR 39.89; 95% CI 31.34–50.06) [[Guan2017AJPH]].
- In 23 US news-reported fatal drownings of autistic children (2000–2017), wandering preceded 73.9% of incidents [[Guan2017Drowning]].
- In Guan 2017 (drowning), ponds were the most common site (52.2%), the water averaged 290.7 m from home, and 73.3% of incidents happened between 12:00 and 18:59 [[Guan2017Drowning]].
- In Australia (2002–2018), autistic children and adolescents had about 3 times the drowning risk of non-autistic peers (RR 2.85), but the confidence interval was wide and included 1 (0.61–13.24) [[Peden2020]].
- Autistic children aged 0–4 had the highest drowning rate in Peden 2020 (11.60 per 100,000 diagnosed) [[Peden2020]].

### adult · Mortality
- In a Swedish cohort of 27,122 autistic people and 2,672,185 matched controls (Hirvikoski 2016), 2.60% of autistic people died during follow-up against 0.91% of controls (OR 2.56; 95% CI 2.38–2.76) [[Hirvikoski2016]].
- Mean age at death in Hirvikoski 2016 was 53.87 years for autistic people against 70.20 years for controls [[Hirvikoski2016]].
- Mean age at death was 39.50 years in autism with intellectual disability and 58.39 years in autism without intellectual disability [[Hirvikoski2016]].
- All-cause mortality OR was 5.78 in autism with intellectual disability and 2.18 in autism without intellectual disability [[Hirvikoski2016]].
- Females with autism and intellectual disability had the highest relative mortality (OR 8.52) [[Hirvikoski2016]].
- Cause-specific mortality in the whole autism group was raised for nearly every cause, including nervous system diseases (OR 7.49; 5.78–9.72), suicide (OR 7.55; 6.04–9.44), endocrine diseases (OR 3.70), respiratory diseases (OR 2.68), circulatory diseases (OR 1.49) and cancers (OR 1.80) [[Hirvikoski2016]].
- A meta-analysis of 12 studies (154,238 autistic participants; Catalá-López 2022) found all-cause mortality RR 2.37 (95% CI 1.97–2.85; moderate confidence) [[CatalaLopez2022]].
- Catalá-López 2022 found deaths from natural causes RR 3.80 (2.06–7.01) and from unnatural causes RR 2.50 (1.49–4.18), both rated low confidence because of high heterogeneity (I² 95–96%) [[CatalaLopez2022]].
- In a US community cohort of 406 autistic adolescents and adults followed for 20 years, 6.4% died, at an average age of 39 [[SmithDaWalt2019]].
- Causes of death in Smith DaWalt 2019 included chronic conditions (such as cancer and heart disease), accidents (such as choking on food and accidental poisoning) and medication side effects [[SmithDaWalt2019]].
- Early childhood social reciprocity difficulties and higher functional impairment predicted mortality in Smith DaWalt 2019, after controlling for age and health [[SmithDaWalt2019]].
- In Medicare enrollees aged 65 and over, autistic older adults had a 2.87 times higher 5-year mortality rate (95% CI 2.61–3.07) than non-autistic older adults [[Krantz2023]].
- Median age of death among decedents in Krantz 2023 was 72 for autistic and 75 for non-autistic older adults, a much smaller gap than in younger cohorts [[Krantz2023]].
- In a Swedish cohort followed from age 16 to at most 46 (Taylor 2026), the mortality rate was 3.2 per 1,000 person-years in autistic people with psychiatric conditions, 0.96 in autistic people without, and 0.27 in non-autistic people without psychiatric conditions [[Taylor2026Mortality]].
- Autistic people with psychiatric conditions had a hazard ratio for premature death of 13.85 (12.86–14.91) against non-autistic people without such conditions, and 3.44 (2.94–4.02) against autistic people without them [[Taylor2026Mortality]].
- All these mortality figures are population statistics from specific cohorts and periods. They describe groups, not any one person's life expectancy.

### adult · Suicide and suicidal behaviour
- In Denmark (6,559,266 people aged 10+, 1995–2016), autistic people had higher rates of suicide attempt (adjusted IRR 3.19; 95% CI 2.93–3.46) and suicide (adjusted IRR 3.75; 2.85–4.92) [[Kolves2021]].
- Among autistic people in Kõlves 2021, females had 4.41 times (3.74–5.19) the suicide-attempt rate of males; in non-autistic people the female:male IRR was 1.41 [[Kolves2021]].
- Autistic people with no other psychiatric diagnosis had an IRR for suicide attempt of 1.33 (0.99–1.78), while those with co-occurring conditions had 9.27 (8.51–10.10) [[Kolves2021]].
- In Kõlves 2021, 92.3% of autistic people who attempted suicide and 90.6% of those who died by suicide had at least one other diagnosis [[Kolves2021]].
- In Sweden (54,168 autistic people, 270,840 controls), suicide risk was highest in autism without intellectual disability but with ADHD (suicide OR 13.09; 8.54–20.08) [[Hirvikoski2020]].
- Suicide risk was also raised in autism with intellectual disability (suicide OR 2.31; 1.16–4.57); adjusting for psychiatric comorbidity attenuated the estimates [[Hirvikoski2020]].
- Relatives of autistic people also had increased risk of suicidal behaviour in Hirvikoski 2020 [[Hirvikoski2020]].
- In Hirvikoski 2016, the suicide OR was 9.40 (7.43–11.90) in autism without intellectual disability and 2.41 (1.14–5.11) in autism with intellectual disability [[Hirvikoski2016]].
- The mean time from registered autism diagnosis to suicide in Hirvikoski 2016 was 2.53–2.86 years, under 3 years in both groups [[Hirvikoski2016]].
- A meta-analysis of 10 studies (10.4 million people) found a pooled suicide RR of 2.85 (95% UI 2.05–4.03) for autistic people, significantly higher for autistic females than males [[Santomauro2024Suicide]].
- Santomauro 2024 estimated 13,400 excess suicide deaths among autistic people worldwide in 2021, 1.8% of all suicide deaths [[Santomauro2024Suicide]].
- In 374 adults newly diagnosed with Asperger's syndrome at a UK clinic (Cassidy 2014), 66% self-reported lifetime suicidal ideation and 35% self-reported suicide plans or attempts [[Cassidy2014]].
- Lifetime suicidal ideation in Cassidy 2014 was far more common than in a UK general population sample (OR 9.6; 95% CI 7.6–11.9), and depression was an important risk factor [[Cassidy2014]].

### adult · Course and long-term outcomes
- A meta-analysis of 15 follow-up studies (N = 828; Steinhausen 2016) rated overall adult outcome as good in 19.7% (95% CI 14.2–26.6), fair in 31.1% (23.2–40.4) and poor in 47.7% (36.6–59.0) [[Steinhausen2016]].
- These "good/fair/poor" categories are researcher-defined global adjustment ratings in children with autistic disorders followed into adolescence and adulthood; in Howlin 2004 the ratings drew on independence, friendships and employment [[Steinhausen2016,Howlin2004]].
- Childhood autism subtype moderated poor outcome, and age at follow-up showed inconsistent associations, with strong heterogeneity in Steinhausen 2016 [[Steinhausen2016]].
- In 68 autistic adults with childhood performance IQ 50 or above, followed to a mean age of 29 (Howlin 2004), outcomes were rated "Very Good" 12%, "Good" 10%, "Fair" 19%, "Poor" 46% and "Very Poor" 12% [[Howlin2004]].
- Childhood performance IQ of at least 70 predicted better outcome in Howlin 2004, but within the normal IQ range neither verbal nor performance IQ predicted outcome reliably for individuals [[Howlin2004]].
- Ten of 68 participants in Howlin 2004 had developed epilepsy by adulthood [[Howlin2004]].
- A systematic review of 25 longitudinal studies (Magiati 2014) found adaptive functioning tended to improve and autism-related behaviour severity often improved, while the diagnosis was generally stable [[Magiati2014]].
- Childhood IQ and early language ability were the strongest predictors of adult outcome in Magiati 2014 (Established) [[Magiati2014]].
- Lord 2018 notes that the outlook is brighter than 50 years ago, with more autistic people able to speak, read and live in the community, but most will not work full-time or live independently [[Lord2018]].

### adult · "Optimal outcome" and loss of diagnosis
- Fein 2013 documented 34 young people with an early autism diagnosis who no longer met criteria and scored in the typical range on socialisation, communication and most language tests ("optimal outcome") [[Fein2013]].
- Early in development, the optimal-outcome group had milder social symptoms than the autistic comparison group but equally severe communication and repetitive-behaviour difficulties [[Fein2013]].
- In a cohort diagnosed at age 2 and followed to 19, 9% no longer met criteria for autism and had largely overcome core difficulties ("very positive outcome") [[Anderson2014Outcome]].
- Young people with a very positive outcome in Anderson 2014 were more likely to have received treatment and had a larger drop in repetitive behaviours between ages 2 and 3 [[Anderson2014Outcome]].
- Young people with an optimal outcome still had elevated current ADHD and specific phobias compared with typically developing peers, though their psychiatric symptoms decreased over time [[Orinstein2015]].
- Eigsti, Fein and Larson now propose replacing "optimal outcome" with "loss of autism diagnosis" (LAD), to reflect that many excellent outcomes (autonomy, daily living skills, communication, relationships, work) occur whether or not autistic features remain [[Eigsti2023]].
- Loss of diagnosis is a minority outcome (9% by age 19 in one prospective cohort), and Fein 2013 describes its existence, frequency and interpretation as still controversial [[Anderson2014Outcome,Fein2013]].

### adult · Quality of life
- A meta-analysis of 10 studies (486 autistic people, 17,776 controls) found quality of life lower in autism, with a large effect (Cohen's d −0.96), and no effect of age [[vanHeijst2015]].
- In van Heijst 2015, a study of adults aged 53–83 found the same quality-of-life gap in older autistic adults [[vanHeijst2015]].
- A systematic review of 14 studies found adult autistic quality of life lower than that of typically developing adults on general-population tools, and no validated autism-specific adult quality-of-life measure [[Ayres2018]].
- In 370 UK autistic adults (WHOQoL-BREF), quality of life was lower than population norms in every domain [[Mason2018QoL]].
- Employment, receiving support and being in a relationship predicted higher quality of life, and having a mental health condition predicted lower quality of life in all four domains [[Mason2018QoL]].

### adult · Autistic burnout
- In a community-based participatory study (19 interviews, 19 public online sources), autistic adults described burnout as chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to stimuli [[Raymaker2020]].
- Burnout in Raymaker 2020 arose from life stressors adding to a cumulative load, plus barriers to relief, so expectations outweighed abilities [[Raymaker2020]].
- Autistic adults linked burnout to harm to health, independent living and quality of life, including suicidal behaviour (Early evidence) [[Raymaker2020]].
- Recovery factors described in Raymaker 2020 were acceptance and social support, time off or reduced expectations, and doing things in an autistic way or unmasking [[Raymaker2020]].
- Raymaker 2020 concluded that autistic burnout appears distinct from occupational burnout and clinical depression (Hypothesis) [[Raymaker2020]].
- A Delphi study with autistic adults defined autistic burnout as exhaustion, withdrawal, problems with thinking, reduced daily living skills and increased autistic traits, and warned that depression treatments might worsen it (Hypothesis) [[Higgins2021]].
- In 141 autistic adults, burnout was associated with masking and depression, and the AASPIRE Autistic Burnout Measure did not reliably separate current from past burnout, so measurement is still early (Early evidence) [[Arnold2023Burnout]].

### adult · Ageing autistic adults
- In 4,685 autistic Medicare enrollees aged 65+ and 46,850 matched controls (Hand 2020), autistic older adults had higher odds of nearly all physical health conditions, including Parkinson's disease (OR 6.1; 5.3–7.0) [[Hand2020Medicare]].
- Mental health conditions were also more common in Hand 2020: schizophrenia and psychotic disorders OR 25.3, attention deficit disorders OR 24.4, personality disorders OR 24.1 and suicidality or self-inflicted injury OR 11.1 [[Hand2020Medicare]].
- Age-associated conditions such as osteoporosis, cognitive disorders, heart disease, cancer, cerebrovascular disease and osteoarthritis were also more common in autistic older adults [[Hand2020Medicare]].
- In US Medicaid adults aged 30–64, the 5-year prevalence of dementia was 4.04% with autism only and 5.22% with autism plus intellectual disability, against 0.97% with neither and 7.10% with intellectual disability only [[Vivanti2021]].
- After adjustment, dementia was more frequent in autism only (aHR 1.96; 95% CI 1.69–2.28) and autism with intellectual disability (aHR 2.89; 2.62–3.17) than in the general population (claims data; Early evidence) [[Vivanti2021]].
- In 236 adults aged 20–79 (IQ >80), autistic adults scored higher on visual memory, lower on generativity and theory of mind, and similar on verbal memory [[Lever2016]].
- Age-related cognitive differences in autism were parallel or reduced, not increased, and theory-of-mind differences were no longer present in adults aged 50+ (cross-sectional; Hypothesis that autism may partly protect against age-related cognitive decline) [[Lever2016]].
- Autistic adults in Lever 2016 still reported many everyday cognitive failures that were not tied to test scores [[Lever2016]].

### Lifespan anchors: when co-occurring conditions typically emerge
- Feeding problems are documented in autistic children against comparison children (OR 5.11) [[Sharp2013Feeding]].
- Sleep problems are reported in 40–80% of autistic children and adolescents [[Cortesi2010]].
- Elopement or wandering was reported in 49% of autistic children after age 4 [[Anderson2012Elopement]].
- Drowning risk in Peden 2020 was highest at ages 0–4 (rate) and 5–9 (share of cases) [[Peden2020]].
- ADHD and anxiety are already common in middle childhood: in 10–14-year-olds, social anxiety 29.2% and ADHD 28.2% [[Simonoff2008]].
- Epilepsy prevalence was 12.5% across ages 2–17 and 26% at 13+, and seizures most often began after age 10 [[Viscidi2013,Bolton2011]].
- Catatonic deterioration usually appears in adolescence or early adult life; it affected 17% of clinic referrals aged 15+ [[Wing2000]].
- Depression accumulates through adolescence into young adulthood: 19.8% diagnosed by age 27 [[Rai2018Depression]].
- OCD often follows an earlier autism diagnosis (IRR 2.18 for later OCD) [[Meier2015]].
- Psychosis and bipolar disorder peak in onset in adulthood; their prevalence in autistic adults is 9.4% and 7.5% [[Varcin2022]].
- Autistic burnout is described by autistic adults as arising from accumulated life stressors and load [[Raymaker2020]].
- Dementia diagnoses are more frequent before 65: 4.04% 5-year prevalence at ages 30–64 with autism only, against 0.97% in the general population [[Vivanti2021]].

### Exam hooks
- ADHD is the most common co-occurring condition in Lai 2019's pooled estimates (28%), followed by anxiety (20%) [[Lai2019]].
- Lai 2019 pooled prevalence: depression 11%, OCD 9%, bipolar 5%, schizophrenia spectrum 4% [[Lai2019]].
- Clinical samples give higher co-occurrence estimates than population or registry samples [[Lai2019]].
- 70% of autistic 10–14-year-olds had at least one psychiatric disorder in a population-derived sample [[Simonoff2008]].
- Epilepsy in autism: median 12.1% overall; about 21.5% with intellectual disability against 8% without [[Lukmanji2019,Amiet2008]].
- Epilepsy in autism is associated with female sex and intellectual disability, and seizures often start after age 10 [[Amiet2008,Bolton2011]].
- GI symptoms in autistic children: OR 4.42 overall; constipation OR 3.86 [[McElhanon2014]].
- Feeding problems in autistic children: OR 5.11 [[Sharp2013Feeding]].
- Catatonia affects about 1 in 10 autistic people (10.4%) and usually emerges in adolescence or early adulthood [[VaquerizoSerrano2021,Wing2000]].
- Autistic people have about 2.4 to 2.6 times the all-cause mortality of the general population [[CatalaLopez2022,Hirvikoski2016]].
- Most common cause of death in autism with intellectual disability: epilepsy; in autism without intellectual disability, suicide risk is especially raised (OR 9.40) [[Hirvikoski2016]].
- Of the leading injury causes of death in autism, drowning had the highest proportionate mortality ratio (PMR 39.89) [[Guan2017AJPH]].
- More than 90% of autistic people who attempted or died by suicide in Denmark had another psychiatric diagnosis [[Kolves2021]].
- Autistic females have a higher relative suicide risk than autistic males [[Kolves2021,Santomauro2024Suicide]].
- Childhood IQ and early language are the strongest predictors of adult outcome [[Magiati2014]].
- About 30% of autistic children remain minimally verbal [[TagerFlusberg2013]].
- In ADDM 2022, 39.6% of 8-year-old autistic children with cognitive data had intellectual disability [[Shaw2025]].
- Diagnosed sleep-wake disorder (13%) is much rarer than reported sleep problems (40–80%) because the definitions differ [[Lai2019,Cortesi2010]].
- Autistic burnout: chronic exhaustion, loss of skills, reduced stimulus tolerance [[Raymaker2020]].

### In plain words
- Many autistic people also have another condition, such as ADHD, anxiety or depression. These can be treated, and treating them can make daily life much easier [[Lai2019]].
- Anxiety and low mood are not "just part of autism". If they change or get worse, they deserve their own check-up [[Hollocks2019,Nicolaidis2013]].
- About 1 in 8 autistic people have epilepsy. It is more common when there is also an intellectual disability, and seizures can start in the teenage years or later [[Lukmanji2019,Bolton2011]].
- Sleep problems are very common in autistic children. Simple bedtime routines often help, and a doctor can discuss other options [[Cortesi2010]].
- Tummy problems like constipation are more common in autistic children. Pain can show up as a change in behaviour, so it is worth checking [[McElhanon2014]].
- Some autistic children wander away. Water nearby is a real danger, so close supervision, fences or barriers around water, and personal alarms are worth thinking about [[Anderson2012Elopement,Guan2017Drowning,Peden2020]].
- Autistic people, on average, die younger than other people. This is a group statistic, not a prediction for any one person, and many of the causes, like epilepsy, mental health problems and accidents, can be found and treated [[Hirvikoski2016,Taylor2026Mortality]].
- Thoughts of suicide are more common in autistic people, especially autistic women and people who also have depression or ADHD. If you are worried, get help early [[Kolves2021,Cassidy2014]].
- Visiting the doctor can be hard: phone booking, noisy waiting rooms and not feeling understood. Asking for written or online contact and a quiet place to wait is reasonable [[Doherty2022]].
- Studies that rated adult "outcomes" mostly counted jobs and living alone. They did not ask autistic people what a good life means to them [[Steinhausen2016,Ayres2018]].
- Autistic burnout is deep exhaustion with loss of skills, often after long periods of masking and stress. Rest, acceptance and fewer demands help people recover [[Raymaker2020]].
- Autistic adults can and do grow old. Older autistic adults need the same health checks as everyone else, and some extra ones [[Hand2020Medicare]].

## Open questions and controversies
- How common are co-occurring conditions really? Clinical samples give higher estimates than population or registry samples, and heterogeneity is very large (I² >95%) [[Lai2019]]; self-report and interview methods give depression rates up to 48.6% lifetime against 14.4% overall [[Hudson2019]]. Status: unresolved; population cohorts with standard tools are needed.
- Are autistic women misdiagnosed with BPD? Lived-experience accounts describe missed autism and misdiagnosis in women [[Bargiela2016]]; the only meta-analysis found overlap within population ranges (BPD in autism 4%) and could not test misdiagnosis [[May2021BPD]]. Status: hypothesis; small, high-risk-of-bias samples.
- Autism in anorexia nervosa: true co-occurrence or starvation effects? 29% of people with anorexia score above the ADOS cut-off [[InalKaleli2025]], but tools and populations differ and prevalence cannot be fixed [[Westwood2017]]. Status: open.
- Why do autistic people die younger? Mortality is raised for nearly every cause [[Hirvikoski2016]]; co-occurring psychiatric conditions explain much of the excess [[Taylor2026Mortality]]; healthcare access barriers are linked to late presentations [[Doherty2022]]. Hirvikoski 2016 could not separate shortcomings in care from biological vulnerability. Status: open.
- Is the life expectancy gap as big as headlines say? Guan 2017 found a mean age at death of 36.2 years, but it used US death certificates that list autism (a biased sample of deaths) [[Guan2017AJPH]]; among people who reach 65, the median age-at-death gap was only 3 years [[Krantz2023]]. Status: mean age at death is not life expectancy; cohort-specific.
- "Outcome" ratings: Steinhausen 2016 rated 47.7% as "poor" [[Steinhausen2016]], but these ratings track independence and employment in older cohorts, and quality-of-life tools were not built for autistic adults [[Ayres2018]]. Status: autistic-led outcome measures are still being developed.
- "Optimal outcome" and loss of diagnosis: Fein 2013 documented typical-range functioning after loss of diagnosis [[Fein2013]]; critics and the authors themselves now prefer "loss of autism diagnosis" and stress that many good lives include autistic features [[Eigsti2023]]; residual ADHD and anxiety remain in some [[Orinstein2015]]. Status: language and framing shifting.
- Is autistic burnout distinct from depression? Qualitative and Delphi work says yes [[Raymaker2020,Higgins2021]]; measurement studies find strong links with depression and weak separation of current and past burnout [[Arnold2023Burnout]]. Status: hypothesis; validation ongoing.
- Does autism protect against cognitive ageing or raise dementia risk? Cross-sectional data suggest parallel or reduced age-related cognitive differences [[Lever2016]]; claims data suggest more early-onset dementia diagnoses [[Vivanti2021]]. Status: both need longitudinal confirmation (Hypothesis).
- Dental caries: one meta-analysis found 60.6% prevalence [[daSilva2017]]; a larger later one found no difference in most caries indices but more severe surface-level decay [[daMotta2026]]. Status: possibly more severe rather than more frequent decay (Hypothesis); needs better-controlled studies.

## Provenance
- Searching: Europe PMC REST search (PubMed E-utilities esearch was returning HTTP 429 rate-limit errors because seven other passes were using it at the same time).
- Validation: every one of the 71 sources was confirmed against its PubMed esummary record (authors, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, PMID, PMCID and DOI). The Sources lines were generated from those records. DOIs were also cross-checked against Europe PMC records, and none differed. No source is unverified.
- Reading: at least the abstract of every source was read, through Europe PMC (resultType=core) or PubMed efetch. Amiet 2008 was read through PubMed efetch because its Europe PMC abstract is cut off at a "<" sign. Hirvikoski 2016 was also read in full text on the publisher site (Tables 3 and 4: mean ages at death and cause-specific ORs). The Lancet Commission 2022 has no abstract and was not cited. Grove 2019 was not cited because no open text gave a quotable autism–ADHD genetic correlation.
- Shared canonical keys used: Lai2019 (PMID 31447415), Shaw2025 (40232988), Lord2018 (30078460) and Hyman2020 (31843864), each checked against PubMed.
- Key choices that avoid collisions with other passes: Santomauro2024Suicide (not the canonical Santomauro2025 GBD paper), Anderson2012Elopement, Anderson2014Outcome, Rai2018Depression, May2021BPD, Mason2018QoL, Morgan2020Sleep, Sharp2013Feeding, Cook2021Camouflaging, Croen2015Adults, Hand2020Medicare, Ward2023PhysHealth, Taylor2026Mortality, Arnold2023Burnout, Guan2017AJPH and Guan2017Drowning.
- Dates: da Motta's caries meta-analysis is filed under 2026 (PubMed issue date J Autism Dev Disord 2026;56(7)), and Taylor 2026 (BJPsych) is an early-view record without volume or issue.
- Limits: Lai 2019, Hossain 2020, Steinhausen 2016, Howlin 2004 and the obesity, dental, GI and feeding meta-analyses were read at abstract level only. Numbers from them are quoted as the abstracts give them. Age-of-onset anchors come from individual cohorts, not from a pooled source, and are labelled as stage markers rather than precise ages.
- Not covered here (other passes): prevalence trends and identity language (A), genetics and causes, including vaccines (B), sensory neuroscience (C), screening and diagnostic criteria (D), interventions and the ABA debate (E), medicines (F), and school, work and family (H).
