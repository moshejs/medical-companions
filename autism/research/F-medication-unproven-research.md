# F · Medication, unproven treatments & research

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: meds, unproven, frontier.

This pass covers medicines used by autistic people, mostly for co-occurring problems rather than autism itself. It also covers treatments that are unproven or harmful, and the research pipeline. Trial numbers come from PubMed or Europe PMC abstracts. Label statements come from current US labels in openFDA. Approval dates come from Drugs@FDA and the openFDA device databases. Trial status comes from ClinicalTrials.gov (API v2, read 2026-10-25).

Access notes:
- Language. Many autistic adults prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"). Many families and clinicians use person-first language ("person with autism"). This file uses both. Older trials use DSM-IV terms such as "autistic disorder" and "pervasive developmental disorder (PDD)". These are kept only when quoting a trial or label.
- The FDA indications are worded "irritability associated with autistic disorder". In this file, "irritability" means the label's target behaviours: aggression, self-injury, severe tantrums and quickly changing moods.
- Doses are quoted as the labels publish them. They are not instructions.
- NICE CG170 and CG142 wording was read on nice.org.uk on 2026-10-25. The PubMed-indexed BMJ summaries (Kendall2013, Pilling2012) are cited beside them.
- Drugs@FDA lists the Risperdal and Abilify efficacy supplements by date but does not name the indication. That the 2006 and 2009 supplements were the autism approvals is matched from the label text and the trial dates.
- I found no record in the listed services for these, so they are not stated as facts:
  - the FDA's 2019 consumer warning on chlorine dioxide ("MMS")
  - FTC or FDA warning letters on camel milk and other autism "cures"
  - leuprolide ("Lupron protocol") for autism
  - the Autistica / James Lind Alliance "Top 10" (Cusack 2016)
  - The section "Open questions and controversies" and the Provenance note say this where relevant.

## Findings

### meds · No medicine treats the core features of autism
- No medicine is approved for the core features of autism (social communication differences and restricted, repetitive behaviours). The 2026 drug-development review states: "There are no approved drugs for autism core features" [[Pandina2026]].
- The AAP clinical report covers medication for co-occurring conditions, not core features. It lists sleep, feeding, gastrointestinal symptoms, obesity, seizures, ADHD, anxiety and wandering as conditions that affect function and quality of life [[Hyman2020]].
- NICE CG170 (under 19s): "Do not use the following interventions for the management of core features of autism in children and young people: antipsychotics, antidepressants, anticonvulsants, exclusion diets (such as gluten- or casein-free diets)" [[NICE-CG170,Kendall2013]].
- NICE CG142 (adults) says do not use these for the core features of autism in adults:
  - anticonvulsants
  - chelation
  - exclusion diets
  - vitamins, minerals and dietary supplements
  - cholinesterase inhibitors
  - oxytocin
  - secretin
  - testosterone regulation
  - hyperbaric oxygen
  - antipsychotics
  - antidepressants ("for the routine management") [[NICE-CG142,Pilling2012]]
- The 2018 Lancet seminar says considerable work is still needed to understand how and when behavioural and medical treatments can be effective, and for which children [[Lord2018]].
- Prescribing is common even so. In 33,565 commercially insured US children with autism (claims data, 2001–2009):
  - 64% filled at least one psychotropic prescription
  - 35% had polypharmacy across at least 2 classes
  - 15% used at least 3 classes at once
  - median length of polypharmacy: 346 days [[Spencer2013]]
- Spencer and colleagues note "minimal evidence" for the effectiveness or appropriateness of multidrug treatment in autism and call for standards of care [[Spencer2013]].
- Placebo response is large in autism drug trials. A meta-analysis pooled 86 RCTs with 2,360 participants on placebo:
  - the standardised mean change on placebo was −0.32 for social communication and −0.23 for repetitive behaviours
  - 19% (95% CI 16–22%) of participants on placebo were rated at least "much improved"
  - caregiver ratings, flexible dosing and larger multisite trials predicted a larger placebo response [[Siafis2020]]

### meds · Antipsychotics for irritability: risperidone
- The RUPP Autism Network trial randomised 101 children aged 5–17 with autistic disorder and severe tantrums, aggression or self-injury to risperidone (49) or placebo (52) for 8 weeks [[McCracken2002]].
  - Dose: 0.5–3.5 mg/day.
  - The ABC-Irritability score fell 56.9% on risperidone and 14.1% on placebo (P<0.001).
  - Positive response (≥25% fall in irritability and CGI-I "much" or "very much improved"): 69% (34/49) vs 12% (6/52).
- Weight gain in that trial was 2.7 ± 2.9 kg on risperidone vs 0.8 ± 2.2 kg on placebo over 8 weeks (P<0.001). Increased appetite, fatigue, drowsiness, dizziness and drooling were more common on risperidone. In two thirds of responders (23/34), benefit was maintained at 6 months [[McCracken2002]].
- RUPP longer-term extension [[RUPP2005Risperidone]]:
  - Part I: 63 responders took open-label risperidone for 16 more weeks (mean dose 1.96 mg/day at entry) and gained a mean of 5.1 kg.
  - Part II: blinded discontinuation. Relapse was 62.5% with gradual switch to placebo vs 12.5% on continued risperidone.
- In a Dutch placebo-discontinuation study, relapse occurred in 3/12 children who stayed on risperidone vs 8/12 who switched to placebo (p=.049). Two children stopped treatment because of weight gain [[Troost2005]].
- Parent training added to medication helps. In a 24-week trial of 124 children aged 4–13 [[Aman2009]]:
  - Risperidone plus parent training beat risperidone alone on the Home Situations Questionnaire (p=.006; d=0.34) and on ABC-Irritability (d=0.48).
  - The final risperidone dose was lower with parent training: 1.98 vs 2.26 mg/day.
- Risperdal US label, indication: "treatment of irritability associated with autistic disorder, including symptoms of aggression towards others, deliberate self-injuriousness, temper tantrums, and quickly changing moods". The indication text says efficacy came from 3 short-term trials in ages 5–17. The clinical-studies section describes the two 8-week pivotal trials in ages 5–16 [[FDA-Risperdal]].
- Risperdal label dosing for autism irritability:
  - start 0.25 mg/day if under 20 kg, 0.5 mg/day if 20 kg or more
  - after at least 4 days, raise to 0.5 or 1.0 mg/day
  - later increases at intervals of 2 weeks or more
  - "The effective dose range is 0.5 mg to 3 mg per day"
  - "No dosing data are available for children who weigh less than 15 kg" [[FDA-Risperdal]]
- Drugs@FDA lists two Risperdal (NDA020272) efficacy supplements approved on 6 October 2006 (S-036, S-041) [[FDA-DrugsFDA-Risperdal]].
- Risperdal label, adverse reactions in pediatric autism trials (≥5% and more than placebo; risperidone n=107, placebo n=115):
  - sedation 63% vs 15%
  - increased appetite 44% vs 15%
  - fatigue 31% vs 9%
  - drooling 12% vs 4%
  - parkinsonism 8% vs 1% [[FDA-Risperdal]]
- Risperdal label, prolactin: in placebo-controlled studies of up to 8 weeks in children aged 5–17 with autistic disorder or other non-psychotic disorders, prolactin was elevated in 49% on risperidone vs 2% on placebo [[FDA-Risperdal]].
- Risperdal label, weight in pediatric trials:
  - short-term trials (3–8 weeks): mean gain 2 kg vs 0.6 kg on placebo
  - gain of ≥7% body weight: about 33% vs 7%
  - longer open-label studies: mean gain 5.5 kg at week 24 and 8 kg at week 48 [[FDA-Risperdal]]
- The Risperdal label carries a boxed warning on increased mortality in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis. It also lists metabolic changes (hyperglycaemia, dyslipidaemia, weight gain), hyperprolactinaemia and tardive dyskinesia under warnings [[FDA-Risperdal]].

### meds · Antipsychotics for irritability: aripiprazole
- Flexible-dose aripiprazole, 8 weeks, ages 6–17 (n=98: 47 aripiprazole, 51 placebo) [[Owen2009]]:
  - ABC-Irritability improved more on aripiprazole from week 1 through week 8.
  - Discontinuation for adverse events: 10.6% vs 5.9%.
  - Extrapyramidal adverse events: 14.9% vs 8.0%.
  - Mean weight gain: 2.0 kg vs 0.8 kg.
- Fixed-dose aripiprazole, 8 weeks, n=218 [[Marcus2009]]:
  - ABC-Irritability change: −12.4 (5 mg/day), −13.2 (10 mg/day), −14.4 (15 mg/day) vs −8.4 on placebo (all p<.05).
  - Weight change: +1.3, +1.3 and +1.5 kg vs +0.3 kg.
  - Sedation was the most common adverse event leading to discontinuation.
- Cochrane review: two RCTs (316 children and adolescents) gave mean differences vs placebo of −6.17 on ABC-Irritability (95% CI −9.07 to −3.26), −7.93 on ABC-Hyperactivity and −2.66 on ABC-Stereotypy, all moderate-quality evidence [[Hirsch2016]].
- Cochrane harms vs placebo: weight +1.13 kg (95% CI 0.71 to 1.54); sedation RR 4.28; tremor RR 10.26. All three included trials were industry-supported [[Hirsch2016]].
- Maintenance trial: 85 stabilised children were randomised to stay on aripiprazole or switch to placebo for 16 weeks [[Findling2014]].
  - Relapse was 35% vs 52% (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.28 to 1.12; P=.097, not significant; NNT 6).
  - The authors advise periodic reassessment of the need to continue.
- In a 52-week open-label safety study (n=330), 60.3% completed one year and 10.6% stopped because of adverse events [[Marcus2011]].
  - Mean BMI z-score rose by 0.31 after more than 9 months.
  - At more than 9 months, low HDL cholesterol was the most frequent fasting metabolic abnormality (30%).
- Abilify label dosing for autism irritability (ages 6–17): start 2 mg/day, then 5 mg/day, with later increases to 10 or 15 mg/day if needed. Adjustments of up to 5 mg/day happen at intervals of at least one week. Recommended range 5–15 mg/day [[FDA-Abilify]].
- The Abilify label states that "the efficacy of ABILIFY for the maintenance treatment of irritability associated with autistic disorder was not established" [[FDA-Abilify]].
- Abilify label weight data: in two short-term pediatric autism trials (median exposure 56 days), mean weight change was +1.6 kg (n=209) vs +0.4 kg on placebo (n=98) [[FDA-Abilify]].
- Drugs@FDA lists an Abilify (NDA021436) efficacy supplement (S-027) approved on 19 November 2009 [[FDA-DrugsFDA-Abilify]].

### meds · How the antipsychotics compare, and how they are monitored
- Meta-analysis of 45 placebo-controlled RCTs (2,856 participants) on irritability and emotional dysregulation [[SalazarDePablo2023]]:
  - Antipsychotics as a class: SMD 1.028 (95% CI 0.824–1.232).
  - Aripiprazole: 1.179. Risperidone: 1.074.
  - ADHD medicines: SMD 0.471.
  - No other drug class beat placebo.
- A 2016 meta-analysis of 46 RCTs in youth aged 2–17 also found risperidone and aripiprazole had the strongest evidence and the largest effect sizes on ABC-Irritability [[Fung2016]].
- NICE CG170 says to consider antipsychotics for behaviour that challenges only when psychosocial or other interventions are insufficient or cannot be delivered because of the severity of the behaviour [[NICE-CG170]]. NICE also says the prescriber should:
  - review effectiveness and side effects after 3–4 weeks
  - stop treatment if there is no clinically important response at 6 weeks
  - start low, use the minimum effective dose and review regularly
- Both US labels tell prescribers to monitor weight and to monitor glucose regularly in people with, or at risk of, diabetes. Both describe dyslipidaemia as a class effect [[FDA-Risperdal,FDA-Abilify]].
- The Risperdal label states that prolactin elevations "occur and persist during chronic administration" [[FDA-Risperdal]].
- The label data point the opposite way on prolactin for aripiprazole: "blood prolactin decreased" is listed as a frequent investigation finding, and "blood prolactin increased" as rare [[FDA-Abilify]].

### meds · ADHD symptoms in autistic children
- RUPP methylphenidate crossover trial (72 children aged 5–14 with PDD and hyperactivity) [[RUPP2005Methylphenidate]]:
  - 49% (35/72) were responders.
  - Effect sizes were 0.20–0.54 depending on dose and rater.
  - Adverse effects ended the study drug in 18% (13/72).
  - Response was smaller and adverse effects more frequent than in typically developing children with ADHD.
- Cochrane review (4 crossover trials, 113 children) [[Sturman2017]]:
  - High-dose methylphenidate improved teacher-rated hyperactivity (SMD −0.78, 95% CI −1.13 to −0.43; low-quality evidence).
  - There was no evidence that it worsens or improves the core features of autism.
  - Reduced appetite was more likely (RR 8.28).
- Atomoxetine, 8 weeks, 97 children aged 6–17 with autism and ADHD [[Harfterkamp2012]]:
  - ADHD-RS fell from 40.7 to 31.6 vs 38.6 to 38.3 on placebo (difference −6.7, 95% CI −10.0 to −3.4; p<.001).
  - CGI-I "much improved" was not significantly different: 20.9% vs 8.7%.
  - Adverse events: 81.3% vs 65.3%.
- 2×2 trial of atomoxetine and parent training, 10 weeks, 128 children aged 5–14 [[Handen2015]]:
  - Atomoxetine, atomoxetine plus parent training, and placebo plus parent training each beat placebo on ADHD symptoms (effect sizes 0.57–0.98).
  - Atomoxetine, with or without parent training, also reduced noncompliance.
- Extended-release guanfacine, 8 weeks, 62 children (mean age 8.5) [[Scahill2015]]:
  - ABC-Hyperactivity fell 43.6% (34.2 to 19.3) vs 13.2% on placebo (34.2 to 29.7); effect size 1.67.
  - CGI-I responders: 50% (15/30) vs 9.4% (3/32).
  - Common adverse events: drowsiness, fatigue and decreased appetite.
  - Blood pressure fell in the first 4 weeks and returned nearly to baseline by week 8.
- In the 2023 meta-analysis, ADHD medicines also reduced irritability and emotional dysregulation vs placebo, though less than antipsychotics (SMD 0.471 vs 1.028) [[SalazarDePablo2023]].

### meds · Sleep: behavioural first, then melatonin
- AAN 2020 guideline [[WilliamsBuckley2020]]:
  - First, assess for medicines and co-existing conditions that could cause the sleep problem.
  - Counsel parents on behavioural strategies "as a first-line treatment approach".
  - Offer melatonin if behavioural strategies have not helped, starting with a low dose.
  - Recommend pharmaceutical-grade melatonin, and counsel about the lack of long-term safety data.
- NICE CG170 says do not use a medicine for sleep unless problems persist despite a sleep plan. If one is needed, consider melatonin with specialist input, alongside non-drug measures and with regular review [[NICE-CG170]].
- Parent-based sleep education (80 children aged 2–10 with delayed sleep onset) improved sleep latency on actigraphy and parent-rated insomnia within a few sessions. Group and individual formats worked equally well [[Malow2014]].
- Paediatric prolonged-release melatonin trial: 125 children aged 2–17.5 whose sleep had not improved with behavioural intervention alone; 13 weeks [[Gringras2017]].
  - Total sleep time rose 57.5 min vs 9.14 min on placebo (adjusted difference 32.43 min; p=.034).
  - Sleep latency fell 39.6 vs 12.5 min.
  - Clinically meaningful response: 68.9% vs 39.3% (NNT 3.38).
  - Somnolence was more common on melatonin.
- Open-label follow-up to 52 weeks [[Maras2018]]:
  - Children randomised to melatonin slept 62.08 min longer and fell asleep 48.6 min faster than at baseline.
  - 76% of completers (55/72) gained at least 1 hour in total sleep time, sleep latency or both.
- Over 104 weeks, benefits were maintained. Weight, height, BMI and pubertal (Tanner) development stayed within normal ranges for age [[Malow2021]].
- A 2011 meta-analysis of 5 crossover RCTs found that melatonin, compared with placebo, increased sleep duration by 44 min and shortened sleep-onset latency by 39 min, with minimal side effects. It did not reduce night-time awakenings [[Rossignol2011]].

### meds · SSRIs and repetitive behaviour
- STAART citalopram trial: 149 children aged 5–17 with high levels of repetitive behaviour, 12 weeks, mean maximum dose 16.5 mg/day [[King2009F]].
  - Response: 32.9% vs 34.2% on placebo (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.61–1.51).
  - Change in CYBOCS-PDD: −2.0 vs −1.9.
  - Adverse events were more likely on citalopram, including increased energy, impulsiveness, hyperactivity, stereotypy, diarrhoea and insomnia.
- Cochrane review (9 RCTs, 320 participants) [[Williams2013SSRI]]:
  - "There is no evidence of effect of SSRIs in children and emerging evidence of harm."
  - In adults, the evidence is limited to small studies with unclear risk of bias.
- NICE CG142 says antidepressants should not be used "for the routine management of core features of autism in adults". Anxiety and depression in autistic people are treated as co-occurring conditions (covered in pass G) [[NICE-CG142]].

### unproven · Why families try unproven treatments
- A systematic review (20 studies, 9,540 participants) found complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) use in autism ranged from 28% to 95% (median 54%). Special diets and supplements ranked first in 75% of studies [[Hofer2017]].
- In the Autism Treatment Network registry (3,173 children) [[Perrin2012]]:
  - 28% reported any CAM use: 17% special diets and 20% other CAM.
  - Higher use was linked to gastrointestinal symptoms (OR 1.88), seizures (OR 1.58) and behaviour problems (OR 1.29).
- The secretin story shows how publicity drives demand. One anecdote led to "thousands" of children receiving injections before trials were done. After hearing a negative trial result, 69% of parents in that trial still wanted secretin [[Sandler1999]].
- Large placebo responses (19% "much improved" on placebo) and caregiver-rated outcomes make an ineffective product look effective in uncontrolled use [[Siafis2020]].
- Crowdfunding campaigns for autism "stem cell" treatment at an international clinic made strong efficacy claims and relied on testimonials. Early academic trials and press releases were used to lend these clinics credibility [[Snyder2021]].
- The AAP advises shared decision-making with families when choosing interventions [[Hyman2020]]. Doctors are advised to ask about and discuss CAM use, because some products are potentially harmful [[Hofer2017,Perrin2012]].

### unproven · Secretin
- Single-dose IV synthetic human secretin vs saline in 60 children (56 analysed): no outcome improved [[Sandler1999]]. The Autism Behavior Checklist fell 8.9 points with secretin vs 17.8 with placebo (difference −8.9, 95% CI −19.4 to 1.6).
- Cochrane review: 16 RCTs and more than 900 children. The authors concluded secretin "should not be recommended or administered as a treatment for ASD" [[Williams2012Secretin]].
- NICE says do not use secretin to manage autism in any context in under-19s, or for core features in adults [[NICE-CG170,NICE-CG142]].

### unproven · Chelation
- Chelation was promoted on the idea that heavy metals (including the mercury-containing vaccine preservative thimerosal) cause autism. Large studies do not support the vaccine link:
  - Danish cohort of 657,461 children: MMR vaccine adjusted HR for autism 0.93 (95% CI 0.85–1.02) [[Hviid2019]]
  - meta-analysis of 1,256,407 children in cohort studies: no association with vaccination (OR 0.99), thimerosal (OR 1.00) or mercury (OR 1.00) [[Taylor2014Vaccine]]
- Cochrane found only one eligible RCT (oral DMSA) and "no clinical trial evidence" that chelation works for autism. Given reported hypocalcaemia, renal impairment and death, the authors judged that "the risks of using chelation for ASD currently outweigh proven benefits" [[James2015]].
- Between 2003 and 2005, CDC received reports of 3 deaths from cardiac arrest caused by hypocalcaemia during chelation. Two were children, both given edetate disodium [[Brown2006]].
- The NIMH-sponsored "Mercury Chelation to Treat Autism" trial is listed on ClinicalTrials.gov as withdrawn, with 0 participants enrolled [[NCT00376194]].
- NICE says do not use chelation to manage autism in any context in children and young people, or for core features in adults [[NICE-CG170,NICE-CG142]].

### unproven · Hyperbaric oxygen
- One multicentre RCT (62 children aged 2–7) reported better physician CGI scores after 40 sessions at 1.3 atm and 24% oxygen than with slightly pressurised room air: 30% vs 8% "much" or "very much improved" [[Rossignol2009]].
- A controlled within-subject study with 16 children, at the same 1.3 atm and 24% oxygen, found "no consistent effects" on 11 directly observed behaviours [[Jepson2011]].
- NICE says do not use hyperbaric oxygen for autism in children and young people, or for core features in adults [[NICE-CG170,NICE-CG142]].

### unproven · Gluten-free, casein-free (GFCF) diets
- Cochrane 2008 found two small RCTs (n=35) and called the evidence "poor" [[Millward2008]]. The review was later withdrawn from the Cochrane Library (2019), not replaced with a positive update.
- A 2018 systematic review of 6 RCTs (214 children) found "little evidence" that a GFCF diet helps autism symptoms. With few exceptions, core-symptom scores did not differ between groups [[Piwowarczyk2018]].
- NICE lists exclusion diets (gluten- or casein-free, and ketogenic for adults) among the interventions not to use for core features [[NICE-CG170,NICE-CG142]].

### unproven · Stem cells and cord blood
- A phase I open-label trial of one autologous cord-blood infusion in 25 children found it safe and feasible. Its uncontrolled behavioural gains were used to choose endpoints for a controlled trial [[Dawson2017]].
- That phase II RCT (180 children aged 2–7) found one cord-blood infusion was not associated with better socialisation or fewer autism symptoms at 6 months [[Dawson2020]]. Subgroup signals in children without intellectual disability were exploratory.

### unproven · Camel milk and other marketed products
- A 2022 meta-analysis claiming camel milk lowered CARS scores was retracted by the journal in 2023 [[Kandeel2022]].
- NICE lists vitamins, minerals and dietary supplements (for example vitamin B6 or iron) among the interventions not to use for core features of autism in adults [[NICE-CG142]].

### frontier · Leucovorin (folinic acid) and cerebral folate deficiency
- A 12-week RCT of high-dose folinic acid (2 mg/kg/day, max 50 mg) in 48 children with autism and language impairment found better verbal communication (effect 5.7 standardised points; d=0.70). The effect was larger in children positive for folate receptor-α autoantibodies (d=0.91) [[Frye2018]]. (Early evidence)
- A 2021 meta-analysis by the same group estimated a 38% pooled prevalence of cerebral folate deficiency in autism and 71% for folate receptor-α autoantibodies. It drew on 21 leucovorin studies, of which 4 were placebo-controlled [[Rossignol2021]]. (Early evidence)
- The current US leucovorin label (indication added 3/2026) is for "cerebral folate transport deficiency in adult and pediatric patients who have a confirmed variant in the folate receptor 1 gene (FOLR1-CFTD)", not for autism [[FDA-Wellcovorin]].
- The label states that "no clinical trials have been conducted" in FOLR1-CFTD. The evidence came from 46 patients in 26 published case reports and reviews [[FDA-Wellcovorin]].
- On 22 September 2025, US federal leaders publicly promoted leucovorin for speech-related difficulties in autism [[Rothman2026]].
- Leucovorin prescribing to US children with autism (Epic Cosmos; 838,801 patients) [[Rothman2026]]:
  - 34.1 per 100,000 encounters per month (2023 to January 2025)
  - 335.2 in August 2025
  - 835.4 in November 2025, more than 24-fold the earlier mean

### frontier · Oxytocin and vasopressin
- SOARS-B (NCT01944046): 290 children aged 3–17 were randomised to intranasal oxytocin (target 48 IU/day) or placebo for 24 weeks [[Sikich2021,NCT01944046]].
  - The primary outcome, ABC-modified Social Withdrawal, changed −3.7 vs −3.5 (difference −0.2, 95% CI −1.5 to 1.0; P=0.61).
  - Secondary outcomes did not differ. (Established: negative)
- In an Australian trial of 87 children aged 3–12 given 32 IU/day for 12 weeks, oxytocin had no overall effect on SRS-2 (p=0.686). An age interaction hinted at benefit in 3–5-year-olds [[Guastella2023]]. (Early evidence)
- Balovaptan is a vasopressin V1a receptor antagonist.
  - Adults (V1aduct, NCT03504917, phase 3, 322 adults): stopped for futility. Vineland-II 2DC change at week 24 was +4.56 vs +6.83 on placebo [[Jacob2022,NCT03504917]].
  - Children (aV1ation, NCT02901431, 5–17 years): difference vs placebo −0.16 (90% CI −2.56 to 2.23; P=.91). No secondary endpoint improved [[Hollander2022,NCT02901431]]. (Established: negative)

### frontier · Bumetanide (NKCC1 chloride importer)
- A phase 2b study (88 participants aged 2–18, 3 months) reported better CARS scores in completers, and hypokalaemia that depended on dose [[Lemonnier2017]]. (Early evidence, superseded)
- BAMBI (92 children aged 7–15, 91 days) found no benefit on SRS-2 (difference −3.16, 95% CI −9.68 to 3.37) [[Sprengers2021]].
  - Hypokalaemia: 51% vs 0%.
  - Orthostatic hypotension: 36% vs 11%.
- The two phase 3 SIGN trials (211 patients each; ages 7–17 and 2–6) were stopped early for lack of efficacy [[Fuentes2023,NCT03715166,NCT03715153]]:
  - no significant difference on CARS2 at week 26
  - more thirst, polyuria, hypokalaemia and dry mouth on bumetanide
  - the sponsor stopped development for autism (Established: negative)

### frontier · Other mechanism-based and repurposed drugs
- Arbaclofen (GABA-B agonist): a phase 2 RCT of 150 participants aged 5–21 missed its primary outcome, the ABC Social Withdrawal/Lethargy subscale [[VeenstraVanderWeele2017,NCT01288716]]. A secondary clinician rating improved. A European phase 2 trial in 124 children completed in January 2023; no results are posted on the registry [[NCT03682978]]. (Early evidence)
- Suramin (purinergic antagonist):
  - pilot of 10 boys, one infusion: ADOS-2 comparison score −1.6 in the 5 treated boys [[Naviaux2017]]
  - 14-week RCT, 52 boys: no significant benefit on the primary ABC-Core outcome. The 20 mg/kg arm did not beat placebo [[Hough2023]] (Hypothesis / early evidence)
- Sulforaphane (broccoli-derived), 15-week RCT in 57 children: no significant effect on the primary OACIS outcome. Caregiver-rated ABC improved [[Zimmerman2021]]. (Early evidence)
- Cannabinoids:
  - A trial in 150 participants aged 5–21 failed its primary HSQ-ASD outcome. The co-primary CGI-I for disruptive behaviour was "much" or "very much improved" in 49% on whole-plant extract vs 21% on placebo [[Aran2021,NCT02956226]].
  - Somnolence (28%) and decreased appetite (25%) were common on extract.
  - The sponsor's cannabidiol oral solution trial (103 participants, NCT04745026) posted results with no statistically significant difference vs placebo on ABC subscales or Vineland-3 at week 12 [[NCT04745026]].
  - Academic CBD trials are still listed as ongoing or of unknown status [[NCT04517799]]. (Early evidence; mixed)

### frontier · Microbiota transfer therapy (MTT)
- In an open-label study of 18 children (NCT02504554), MTT combined 2 weeks of antibiotics, a bowel cleanse and 7–8 weeks of faecal microbiota transplant [[Kang2017,NCT02504554]].
  - Gastrointestinal symptom scores fell about 80%.
  - Parent- and clinician-rated autism scores improved.
  - There was no placebo arm. (Early evidence)
- At 2-year follow-up of the same 18 children, most GI gains persisted and autism-related scores improved further. The authors call for a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial [[Kang2019]].
- Phase 2 placebo-controlled MTT trials in children (n=50, NCT04182633) and adults (n=55, NCT03408886) are listed as active, not recruiting, with no posted results [[NCT04182633,NCT03408886]].

### frontier · Genetic therapies in syndromic autism (Angelman syndrome)
- Angelman syndrome is a single-gene neurodevelopmental disorder (loss of maternal UBE3A). Many affected children meet autism criteria. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) aim to switch on the silent paternal UBE3A copy. This differs from treating autism in general (Hypothesis for wider autism) [[Hipp2025]].
- The TANGELO phase 1 trial of rugonersen (61 children aged 1–12) met its primary safety and tolerability endpoint [[Hipp2025]].
  - EEG δ-power partly normalised, depending on dose.
  - Exploratory developmental gains exceeded natural-history expectations. (Early evidence)
- Phase 3 ASO trials registered in Angelman syndrome:
  - GTX-102: NCT06617429 (n=129, active, not recruiting)
  - obudanersen (ION582): REVEAL, NCT06914609 (n=156)
  - rugonersen: BEACON, NCT07605429 (n=165, recruiting since June 2026) [[NCT06617429,NCT06914609,NCT07605429]]

### frontier · Diagnostic aids cleared by the FDA
- FDA created a new device type, the "Pediatric Autism Spectrum Disorder Diagnosis Aid" (product code QPF, class II, 21 CFR 882.1491), via the De Novo grant to Cognoa's ASD Diagnosis Aid (DEN200069) on 2 June 2021 [[FDA-DEN200069]].
- Canvas Dx is Cognoa's AI device combining a caregiver questionnaire, two home videos and a clinician questionnaire. It was tested in 425 children aged 18–72 months with developmental concerns, 29% of whom had autism [[Megerian2022]].
  - It gave a determinate result in 31.8%.
  - In those children: sensitivity 98.4%, specificity 78.9%.
  - Overall PPV 80.8%, NPV 98.3%.
- Canvas Dx was cleared again as a 510(k) on 11 April 2025 (K243558) [[FDA-K243558]].
- The EarliPoint eye-tracking system was cleared on 8 June 2022 (K213882) [[FDA-K213882]].
- In the EarliPoint pivotal study (NCT03469986), eye-tracking of social visual engagement in 475 children aged 16–30 months gave sensitivity 71.0% and specificity 80.7% vs expert diagnosis [[Jones2023,NCT03469986]]. In the 335 children whose diagnosis was certain, the figures were 78.0% and 85.4%.
- These devices are aids for clinicians. Screening and diagnostic tools are covered in pass D. No device or questionnaire here is for self-diagnosis.

### frontier · What autistic people and families want from research
- UK community consultation found a "clear disparity" between how autism research was funded and what the community prioritised. People wanted research that makes a difference to day-to-day lives [[Pellicano2014]].
- A review of 7 priority-setting studies found the most common priorities were research with real-world impact on daily life and lifelong skill-building [[Roche2021]].
- Spectrum 10K, a UK genetic study of 10,000 autistic people, was postponed in 2021 after backlash from the autism community. A later interview study of 22 autistic adults recorded concerns about trust, data reuse and autistic representation [[Ellis2023]].
- A 2026 industry–academic review lists lessons for future drug programmes [[Pandina2026]]:
  - "measure what matters to patients and caregivers"
  - enrich trial populations by mechanism
  - do not use diagnostic tools as efficacy endpoints
  - design trials that reduce placebo response

### Exam hooks
- No medicine is approved for the core features of autism. Drugs target co-occurring problems such as irritability, ADHD symptoms and insomnia [[Pandina2026,Hyman2020]].
- The only two FDA-labelled autism indications are risperidone (ages 5–16/17) and aripiprazole (ages 6–17), both for "irritability associated with autistic disorder" [[FDA-Risperdal,FDA-Abilify]].
- RUPP 2002: 101 children, 8 weeks, responders 69% on risperidone vs 12% on placebo, weight +2.7 vs +0.8 kg [[McCracken2002]].
- Stopping risperidone after 6 months: relapse 62.5% on placebo vs 12.5% on continued drug [[RUPP2005Risperidone]].
- Risperidone raises prolactin (elevated in 49% vs 2% in pediatric trials). Aripiprazole tends to lower it [[FDA-Risperdal,FDA-Abilify]].
- Risperidone's most common pediatric side effect is sedation (63% vs 15%), followed by increased appetite (44% vs 15%) [[FDA-Risperdal]].
- Adding parent training to risperidone improved behaviour more and allowed a lower dose [[Aman2009]].
- NICE: review an antipsychotic at 3–4 weeks and stop it if there is no meaningful response by 6 weeks [[NICE-CG170]].
- Methylphenidate helps hyperactivity in autism, but response (49%) is lower and side effects more frequent than in non-autistic ADHD [[RUPP2005Methylphenidate]].
- Guanfacine ER: 50% vs 9.4% responders for hyperactivity [[Scahill2015]].
- Citalopram did not reduce repetitive behaviour (32.9% vs 34.2% response) and caused more activation side effects [[King2009F]].
- Sleep: behavioural strategies come first. Prolonged-release melatonin added about 32 min of sleep vs placebo at 13 weeks [[WilliamsBuckley2020,Gringras2017]].
- Secretin: 16 RCTs and more than 900 children, no benefit. Do not use [[Williams2012Secretin,NICE-CG170]].
- Chelation has caused deaths from hypocalcaemia (edetate disodium) and has no trial evidence of benefit [[Brown2006,James2015]].
- MMR vaccination does not increase autism risk (HR 0.93, 657,461 children) [[Hviid2019]].
- Oxytocin (SOARS-B), balovaptan (V1aduct, aV1ation) and bumetanide (SIGN 1 and 2) were all negative in large trials [[Sikich2021,Jacob2022,Hollander2022,Fuentes2023]].
- About 1 in 5 participants on placebo in autism drug trials is rated "much improved" [[Siafis2020]].
- The 2026 leucovorin label indication is FOLR1 cerebral folate transport deficiency, not autism [[FDA-Wellcovorin]].

### In plain words
- No pill changes autism itself. Some medicines can help with things that make life harder, like very aggressive outbursts, trouble focusing or not sleeping [[Hyman2020]].
- Risperidone and aripiprazole can calm severe aggression and self-injury. They often cause weight gain and sleepiness, so doctors check weight and blood tests [[McCracken2002,FDA-Risperdal]].
- If a medicine for aggression does not help within about 6 weeks, it should usually be stopped [[NICE-CG170]].
- Parent training works alongside medicine and can mean a lower dose is needed [[Aman2009]].
- For sleep, start with a calm bedtime routine. If that is not enough, doctors may try melatonin [[WilliamsBuckley2020]].
- Many families try special diets or supplements. Ask your doctor about any product, because some can cause harm [[Hofer2017]].
- Chelation, secretin and hyperbaric oxygen do not help autism, and chelation has killed children [[James2015,Brown2006,Williams2012Secretin]].
- Vaccines do not cause autism. Very large studies have checked this [[Hviid2019,Taylor2014Vaccine]].
- When a new treatment is in the news, look for a large, placebo-controlled trial before trusting it. In autism trials, many children seem to improve on a dummy treatment too [[Siafis2020]].
- Several new medicines that looked promising in small studies (oxytocin, bumetanide, balovaptan) did not work in big trials [[Sikich2021,Fuentes2023,Jacob2022]].
- Leucovorin is approved for a rare genetic folate disorder, not for autism. Research in autism is still early [[FDA-Wellcovorin,Frye2018]].
- Many autistic people want research to focus on everyday life: health, school, work and wellbeing [[Pellicano2014,Roche2021]].

## Open questions and controversies
- How long should antipsychotics continue?
  - For continuing: relapse is common after stopping risperidone (62.5% vs 12.5%) [[RUPP2005Risperidone]].
  - Against assuming it is needed: aripiprazole maintenance did not significantly delay relapse (35% vs 52%, P=.097), and its label says maintenance efficacy was not established [[Findling2014,FDA-Abilify]]. Weight gain builds over time [[FDA-Risperdal]].
  - Status: periodic reassessment and the lowest effective dose are advised [[NICE-CG170,Findling2014]].
- Psychotropic polypharmacy: it is common (35% on 2 or more classes) [[Spencer2013]], but no trials support multidrug regimens.
- Leucovorin [[Frye2018,Rossignol2021,FDA-Wellcovorin,Rothman2026]]:
  - Supporters cite small RCTs and a meta-analysis by the trialists' group.
  - Critics note that no large independent trial exists, that the US label is for a rare genetic folate disorder and rests on 46 case reports, and that prescribing rose more than 24-fold after political promotion.
  - Status: unresolved (Early evidence).
- Hyperbaric oxygen: one RCT was positive [[Rossignol2009]] and a controlled within-subject study was negative [[Jepson2011]]. NICE advises against it [[NICE-CG170]]. Status: not recommended.
- Why drug trials fail [[Siafis2020,Pandina2026]]:
  - Large placebo responses and caregiver-rated endpoints blur drug–placebo differences.
  - Authors call for outcomes that matter to autistic people, mechanism-based enrichment, and endpoints other than diagnostic tools.
- Microbiota transfer: promising open-label results [[Kang2017,Kang2019]], but the placebo-controlled phase 2 trials have no posted results yet [[NCT04182633,NCT03408886]].
- Cannabinoids: mixed. A co-primary outcome was positive and the other primary failed [[Aran2021]]. The industry CBD trial was negative on its registry results [[NCT04745026]].
- Genetic research and consent: Spectrum 10K was postponed after community objections [[Ellis2023]]. Autistic stakeholders want research priorities and trust built in from the start [[Pellicano2014,Roche2021]].
- Diagnostic aids [[Megerian2022,Jones2023]]:
  - These AI and eye-tracking devices may shorten waits.
  - Canvas Dx gives no result in about two thirds of children.
  - Eye-tracking sensitivity was 71% in all children tested.
  - Status: cleared as aids to clinicians, not stand-alone tests.
- Treatments that could not be verified here: chlorine dioxide ("MMS"), the "Lupron protocol", and FTC actions against autism "cure" marketing. No PubMed, Europe PMC, openFDA or ClinicalTrials.gov record was found, so the companion should not state specifics about them from this pass.
