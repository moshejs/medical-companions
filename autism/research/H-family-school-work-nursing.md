# H · Family, school, work, adulthood & nursing

Pass date: 2026-11-02. Modules served: school, adult, nursing, health.

This pass covers family life (parenting stress, caregiver mental health, siblings, respite, money, parent training and post-diagnostic support), school (settings and inclusion, structured teaching, visual schedules and Social Stories, bullying, absence and exclusion, and the legal frameworks in outline), adulthood (the transition "services cliff", employment, workplace adjustments, higher education, independent living, relationships and parenthood, late diagnosis, victimisation and police contact) and nursing and healthcare (barriers to care, toolkits and hospital passports, sensory-adapted environments, preparing for procedures, pain assessment, sedation, emergency and crisis care, restraint, staff training and documentation). Every number comes from the abstract (or, where stated, the full text) of the cited source, with its population and comparator.

Language note: many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"), while many families and clinicians use person-first language ("person with autism"). A UK survey of 3,470 people found no single term that everyone accepts; "autistic" was endorsed by many autistic adults and parents but fewer professionals [[Kenny2016]]. This file uses both. Older studies say "high-functioning" or "Asperger syndrome"; those words appear only when describing how a study defined its sample.

## Findings

### health · Parenting stress and caregiver mental health
- A meta-analysis of comparison-group studies found a large effect size for higher parenting stress in parents of autistic children than in parents of typically developing children; the large effect versus other disabilities should be read with caution because it may be driven by comparisons with Down syndrome [[Hayes2013]].
- Across 133 studies of clinical groups, parenting stress was more strongly linked to child externalising problems (weighted r = 0.57, d = 1.39) than to internalising problems (r = 0.37, d = 0.79), and stress was higher in parents of children with autism or developmental delay than in other clinical groups [[Barroso2018]].
- A meta-analysis of 31 studies (9,208 parents of autistic children) estimated that 31% (95% CI 24–38%) had a depressive disorder and 33% (95% CI 20–48%) an anxiety disorder; the pooled estimates were 10% for OCD, 4% for personality disorders, 2% for alcohol and substance use disorders and 1% for schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, with significant heterogeneity [[Schnabel2020]].
- In a US sample of 391 parents of autistic children and a matched comparison group, divorce was more common (23.5% vs 13.8%); the rate stayed high through childhood, adolescence and early adulthood, while in the comparison group it fell after the child was about 8 years old [[Hartley2010]].
- The authors of the parenting-stress meta-analysis recommend assessing parenting stress routinely and throughout behavioural intervention programmes (Established) [[Barroso2018]].

### health · Siblings, respite and family finances
- Across 69 independent samples, typically developing siblings of autistic people had slightly more negative outcomes overall than comparison siblings (Hedges g = −0.26), with differences for anxiety (g = −0.25), depression (g = −0.36) and beliefs about disability (g = −0.56); there was no significant difference in adjustment, externalising problems, coping or family functioning [[Shivers2019]].
- The sibling meta-analysis authors recommend including siblings in family interventions and support [[Shivers2019]].
- In 101 mother–father couples raising an autistic child, each extra hour per week of respite care was associated with a half standard deviation higher marital quality, an association mediated by lower daily stress and more daily uplifts (cross-sectional) [[Harper2013]].
- US data (Medical Expenditure Panel Survey 2002–2008): mothers of autistic children earned on average 35% ($7,189) less than mothers of children with another health limitation and 56% ($14,755) less than mothers of children with no health limitation, were 6% less likely to be employed and worked 7 hours less per week [[Cidav2012]].
- In the same data, fathers' labour-market outcomes did not differ significantly, children with ASD were 9% less likely to have both parents working, and family earnings were 28% ($17,763) lower than for children with no health limitation [[Cidav2012]].
- Estimated lifetime cost of supporting an autistic person with intellectual disability: $2.4 million in the US and £1.5 million (US $2.2 million) in the UK; without intellectual disability: $1.4 million (US) and £0.92 million (UK) [[Buescher2014]].
- For children, the largest cost components were special education and parental productivity loss; in adulthood, residential care or supported living and the person's own lost productivity dominated [[Buescher2014]].

### health · Post-diagnostic support and parent training
- In a UK survey of 1,047 parents, families usually waited about a year after first concerns before seeking help, then waited on average about 3.5 years from first approaching a professional to a confirmed diagnosis; just over half were dissatisfied with the diagnostic process, and post-diagnostic support was rated unsatisfactory [[Crane2016Diagnosis]].
- NICE CG128 recommends discussing the findings in person and without delay, giving parents a written report, sharing it with the GP, and offering a follow-up appointment with the autism team within 6 weeks of the end of the assessment [[NICE-CG128]].
- NICE CG128 also recommends discussing the chance of autism in siblings and future children, and giving information on local and national support organisations, welfare-benefit advice, educational support and preparing for the future [[NICE-CG128]].
- NICE CG170 recommends that every autistic child or young person has a case manager or key worker, and that all families (including siblings) and carers are told about their right to short breaks and other respite care and to a formal carer's assessment of their own needs [[NICE-CG170]].
- NICE CG170 recommends extra support at times of increased need, such as puberty, starting or changing schools, or the birth of a sibling [[NICE-CG170]].
- The AAP clinical report notes that autistic children have service needs in behavioural, educational, health, leisure and family-support areas, and calls for shared decision-making with families when choosing interventions [[Hyman2020]].
- Empower-Autism RCT (379 caregivers of children diagnosed within 12 months, North-West England): an online group programme combining psychoeducation with Acceptance and Commitment Therapy improved caregiver mental health at 52 weeks versus usual post-diagnostic psychoeducation (GHQ-30 mean difference −4.95, 95% CI −8.21 to −1.68) (Early evidence: one trial) [[Leadbitter2025]].
- RUBI parent training RCT (NCT01233414): 180 children aged 3–7 with autism and disruptive behaviour at 6 US centres were randomised to 24 weeks of parent training (11 core sessions, up to 2 optional, 2 phone boosters, 2 home visits) or parent education (information about autism, no behaviour-management strategies) [[Bearss2015,NCT01233414]].
- At week 24, the parent-rated Aberrant Behavior Checklist–Irritability score fell 47.7% (23.7 to 12.4) with parent training vs 31.8% (23.9 to 16.3) with parent education (treatment effect −3.9, 95% CI −6.2 to −1.7; effect size 0.62) [[Bearss2015]].
- Home Situations Questionnaire–ASD scores fell 55% vs 34.2% (effect size 0.45); neither parent-rated measure met the prespecified minimal clinically important difference [[Bearss2015]].
- A blinded clinician rated 68.5% of children as positive responders after parent training vs 39.6% after parent education [[Bearss2015]].
- A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (653 participants) of parent training for disruptive behaviour in autistic children found a standardised mean difference of −0.59 (95% CI −0.88 to −0.30), with significant heterogeneity (Established) [[Postorino2017]].

### school · Settings, inclusion and structured teaching
- A systematic review of 233 experimental studies of inclusive-education interventions for autistic students found that most targeted the student's social skills, mostly with cognitive-behavioural approaches; there was no consensus on how to measure inclusion, and about half of studies did not measure implementation [[Perrelet2025]].
- Lived-experience consultants in that review argued that much of the research reflects "integration" (the student adapts to the school) rather than inclusion (the environment adapts to the student) [[Perrelet2025]].
- US special-education data for 2019: 71% of autistic students exiting high school graduated with a diploma, compared with 48% of students with intellectual disability and 72.5% of students with other disabilities; autistic students had lower dropout rates (6–8% vs 15–18% for other disabilities) [[McLean2026]].
- A meta-analysis of 13 TEACCH (structured teaching) studies with 172 people found small effects on perceptual, motor, verbal and cognitive skills and moderate-to-large gains in social and maladaptive behaviour; the authors called the results exploratory [[ViruesOrtega2013]].
- The third-generation US evidence review synthesised 972 articles and identified 28 focused intervention practices that met its criteria for evidence-based practice [[Hume2021]].
- In a US population-based cohort of 1,787 autistic adolescents (ADDM Network, born 2004), school-based IEP services varied widely across sites, students with intellectual disability were less likely to receive school-based mental health services, and 37% did not take part in standardised testing [[Hughes2024IEP]].
- In the same cohort, ADHD (47%) and anxiety (39%) were the most common co-occurring conditions recorded by age 16 [[Hughes2024IEP]].

### school · Visual schedules, Social Stories and transitions
- A review of 31 visual activity schedule studies (16 of acceptable quality) concluded that visual activity schedules meet criteria as an evidence-based practice for autistic people from preschool through adulthood, especially combined with systematic instruction (Established) [[Knight2015]].
- A meta-analysis of single-subject studies found Social Stories had "low to questionable" overall effectiveness; they worked better for reducing behaviours that cause problems than for teaching social skills, and better in general-education settings (Early evidence) [[Kokina2010]].
- NICE CG170 recommends visual supports (words, pictures or symbols meaningful to the child) and adapting the environment for sensory sensitivities to lighting, noise and wall colours [[NICE-CG170]].

### school · Bullying
- A meta-analysis of 17 studies estimated that 44% of autistic school-age youth had been bullied (victimisation), 10% had bullied others, and 16% both; by type, victimisation was 33% physical, 50% verbal and 31% relational [[Maiano2016]].
- Autistic youth had a higher risk of general and verbal victimisation than typically developing peers [[Maiano2016]].
- In nationally representative US data, 46.3% of autistic adolescents had been bullied in the past school year, 14.8% had bullied others and 8.9% both; victimisation was more likely with more classes in general education, ADHD, lower social skills and some conversational ability [[Sterzing2012]].

### school · Attendance, exclusion and school refusal
- In a UK online survey of parents of 486 autistic students (mean age 11), students missed on average 5 of 23 possible school days in one month, and 43% met the threshold for persistent absence (missing more than 10%) [[Totsika2020]].
- Of absences in that study, 43% were school refusal, 9% exclusion, 9% school withdrawal and 32% non-problematic (illness or appointments); truancy was almost non-existent [[Totsika2020]].
- Students in mainstream schools, older students, and those from single-parent or unemployed households were more likely to miss school [[Totsika2020]].
- Among 62 autistic students aged 9–16 in inclusive schools (Norway), the 33 with school refusal behaviour were less socially motivated, had more difficulty initiating tasks and generating ideas, and had more withdrawn and depressive symptoms [[Munkhaugen2019]].
- A multi-informant English study (12 autistic pupils with avoidance or exclusion, plus parents, teachers and local authority professionals) described parents "fighting" a complex system for support, and found pupils could attend and enjoy school when they received appropriate support [[Gray2023School]].

### school · Legal frameworks (general principles, not legal advice)
- US: the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) provides special education services to eligible students with disabilities, including autistic students, up to age 21; the mean exit age for autistic students was about 18 years (graduation age 17.9), so many leave before eligibility ends [[McLean2026]].
- US: Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act gives accommodations to students who may not qualify for an IEP; over 1.38 million students are served by Section 504 plans, and most of 23 families of autistic children interviewed reported negative experiences, often schools unwilling to provide accommodations [[Lindsay2026]].
- England: Education, Health and Care Plans (EHCPs) are the legal framework for accessing additional support for school-age children; six mothers of autistic children in mainstream school described the process as intimidating and overwhelmingly difficult [[Keville2025]].
- NICE CG128 recommends that, with consent, the diagnostic profile is shared with education professionals (for example through a school visit) so it can inform the child's individual education plan [[NICE-CG128]].

### adult · Transition and the "services cliff"
- In the US NLTS2 cohort, 39.1% of autistic young adults aged 19–23 received no services in the years after leaving high school; use ranged from 9.1% for speech therapy to 41.9% for case management [[Shattuck2011Services]].
- The odds of receiving no services were higher for African American young adults and those from low-income families [[Shattuck2011Services]].
- A 14-year longitudinal study of 204 autistic people (59% with intellectual disability) found services declined during high school, dropped at school exit (sharply for those with intellectual disability) and unmet needs rose right after exit: the "services cliff" begins before school ends [[Laxman2019]].
- NICE CG170 recommends reassessing autistic young people in child services at around age 14, completing transition usually by age 18, and telling them about their right to a social care assessment at 18 [[NICE-CG170]].
- Among 66 US young adults who had recently left school, 56% spent time in sheltered workshops or day activity centres, and those without intellectual disability were three times more likely to have no daytime activities than those with intellectual disability [[Taylor2011Employment]].

### adult · Employment
- In NLTS2, 55.1% of autistic young adults had held paid employment and 34.7% had attended college during the first 6 years after high school; more than 50% of those who had left school in the past 2 years had no employment or education [[Shattuck2012]].
- Autistic youth had the lowest employment and highest non-participation rates of the disability groups compared (speech/language impairment, learning disability, intellectual disability) [[Shattuck2012]].
- At ages 21–25, 53.4% of autistic young adults had ever worked for pay since high school (lowest of the disability groups), earning on average $8.10 per hour [[Roux2013]].
- UK (ONS, Annual Population Survey, 6 months to June 2020): 21.7% of disabled people whose main impairment was autism were employed, the lowest of any impairment group, against 52.1% of all disabled people and 81.3% of non-disabled people (July–September 2020) [[ONS2021Outcomes]].
- UK (ONS, year ending June 2021): employment for disabled people whose main impairment was autism was 29.0%, against 53.5% for all disabled people and 81.6% for non-disabled people (July–September 2021); autism was first added as an answer option in January 2020, so these are early, self-reported estimates [[ONS2022Outcomes]].
- In an online survey of 254 US autistic adults, 61.4% were employed; those who had disclosed their diagnosis to their employer were more than three times as likely to be employed (cross-sectional association, not cause) [[Ohl2017]].
- A systematic review of 50 empirical employment studies (58,134 autistic adults) found most were limited by small samples, poor participant description and lack of randomisation or controls [[Hedley2017]].
- Project SEARCH plus ASD Supports RCT (49 students aged 18–21): at 3 months after graduation, 90% of the intervention group had competitive part-time jobs (US $9.53–10.66 per hour) vs 6% of controls; 87% kept their jobs at 12 months, while 12% of controls had found jobs by 12 months (Early evidence: small trial) [[Wehman2017]].
- A systematic review found no strong evidence for or against a workplace "autism advantage" (e.g., attention to detail) and warned against stereotypes that may not fit individuals [[Bury2020]].
- NICE CG142 recommends considering an individual supported employment programme for autistic adults without or with mild learning disability, including CV and interview help, job matching, advice to employers on reasonable adjustments and ongoing support after starting work [[NICE-CG142]].

### adult · Workplace adjustments and the law
- A scoping review of 25 studies found job coaching was the most common workplace support; successful strategies included minimising distractions, reducing noise and predictable job duties [[Khalifa2020]].
- In Great Britain, autism may count as a disability under the Equality Act 2010 if it has a substantial and long-term negative effect on normal daily activities, which brings an entitlement to reasonable adjustments; 82 autistic clinicians described unmet adjustments and recommended that employer and employee discuss and revisit adjustments individually [[Smith2025Adjustments]].
- In the US, Title I of the Americans with Disabilities Act covers employment discrimination; an analysis of EEOC charges found autistic charging parties were more often younger and male and more often filed against retail employers, and charges against larger and service-industry employers were more likely to be upheld [[VanWieren2008]].

### adult · Higher education
- A systematic review found only 20 articles describing 69 autistic college students, just two of them experimental, showing how thin the evidence for university support is [[Gelbar2014]].
- In interviews with 14 autistic people who had dropped out of university, themes included late or difficult diagnosis, lack of autism understanding, mental health challenges, culture shock and lack of proactive support; leaving was often felt as traumatic and shameful [[Cage2020]].

### adult · Independent living
- In NLTS2 (full text), since leaving high school 87.1% of autistic young adults had ever lived with a parent or guardian, 16.6% had ever lived independently and 11.8% had lived in a supervised setting; 79.1% had never lived anywhere else [[Anderson2014Residential]].
- UK (ONS, year ending June 2020 data): 74.5% of disabled people whose main impairment was autism lived with their parents; in the 2021 data the figure was 76.0%, the highest of any impairment group [[ONS2021Outcomes,ONS2022Outcomes]].
- Living independently was more likely for those who were White, had better conversation and functional skills, and had higher household income [[Anderson2014Residential]].

### adult · Relationships, sexuality and parenthood
- In 229 autistic adults without intellectual disability (online and clinic sample), 73% had romantic relationship experience and only 7% had no wish for a relationship; those with an autistic partner were more satisfied than those with a non-autistic partner [[Strunz2017]].
- A qualitative study (31 autistic, 26 non-autistic adults) found both groups described intimacy in similar terms; autistic people more often named uncertainty about relationships and communication as barriers [[Sala2020]].
- NICE CG142 asks staff to be sensitive to sexuality (including asexuality) and to be aware that communication differences can lead to misunderstanding others or being exploited [[NICE-CG142]].
- Autistic adults (n = 95) had less sexual knowledge, got less of it from social sources and reported more sexual victimisation than 117 non-autistic adults; actual knowledge partly mediated the increased risk [[BrownLavoie2014]].
- In a survey of 355 autistic and 132 non-autistic mothers (all with an autistic child), autistic mothers more often reported pre- or postnatal depression, feeling misunderstood by professionals, anxiety and selective mutism in parenting contexts, and isolation; they still reported putting their child's needs first [[Pohl2020]].
- Nine autistic mothers described motherhood as joyful with intense connection to their children, and also sensory needs in pregnancy and being misunderstood by professionals [[Dugdale2021]].

### adult · Late diagnosis, identity and wellbeing
- A scoping review of adult diagnosis found adults often have strong emotions after diagnosis, the route to diagnosis is unclear and variable, and few post-diagnostic services exist [[Huang2020AdultDx]].
- Nine adults diagnosed after age 50 had previously been treated for anxiety and depression, had felt isolated and "alien" growing up, and saw the diagnosis as positive, allowing a "reconfiguration of self" [[Stagg2019]].
- Fourteen women diagnosed in late adolescence or adulthood described "pretending to be normal" and professionals missing their autism because of their gender [[Bargiela2016]].
- In 78 autistic university students, those who learned they were autistic at a younger age reported higher quality of life; participants advised against waiting until adulthood to tell children (Early evidence) [[Oredipe2023]].
- NICE CG142 recommends that professionals discuss with autistic adults whether and how they want family, partners or carers involved, and give carers information even if the person does not want them involved [[NICE-CG142]].

### adult · Victimisation, police contact and criminal justice
- A systematic review concluded that autistic people do not appear to be over-represented in the criminal justice system, and found little evidence for the often-claimed over-representation of particular crime types [[King2014CJS]].
- In nationally representative US data, about 20% of autistic youth had been stopped and questioned by police by age 21 and nearly 5% had been arrested; females were less likely and youth with externalising behaviours more likely to be involved [[Rava2017]].
- Of 394 police officers in England and Wales, only 42% were satisfied with how they had worked with autistic people and 37% had received autism training; autistic adults and parents (n = 80) were largely dissatisfied with police contact [[Crane2016Police]].
- Autistic adults (n = 45) reported more childhood victimisation (including maltreatment, teasing and sexual assault by peers) and more adult sexual victimisation than matched non-autistic adults, with no difference in perpetration [[Weiss2018Victimization]].
- In 272 Australian users of an autism alert card, about half used it, mostly on transport (17%), in healthcare (16%) and in retail (16%); 76.2% would recommend it, though some met disbelief or patronising responses [[Edwards2025AlertCard]].

### nursing · Barriers to healthcare
- In an international survey, 80% of 507 autistic adults vs 37% of 157 non-autistic adults reported difficulty visiting a GP [[Doherty2022]].
- Top barriers for autistic adults: deciding whether symptoms warrant a visit (72%), booking appointments by telephone (62%), not feeling understood (56%), difficulty communicating with the doctor (53%) and the waiting room environment (51%) [[Doherty2022]].
- Barriers were linked to self-reported harm, including untreated conditions, missed screening and late presentation; formally diagnosed and self-identified autistic respondents did not differ [[Doherty2022]].
- Compared with 228 non-autistic adults, 209 autistic adults had higher odds of unmet physical (OR 1.9), mental health (OR 2.2) and prescription (OR 2.8) needs, lower odds of tetanus vaccination (OR 0.5) and Pap smears (OR 0.5), and higher odds of using the emergency department (OR 2.1) [[Nicolaidis2013]].
- Interviews with 39 autistic adults and 16 supporters (AASPIRE) identified patient factors (sensory sensitivities, slow processing, body awareness, verbal communication), provider factors (wrong assumptions, unwillingness to allow written communication) and system factors (complexity, stigma) [[Nicolaidis2015]].
- A systematic review of 6 studies identified patient–provider communication, sensory sensitivities and executive functioning (planning) as key barriers to physical healthcare for autistic adults [[Mason2019Barriers]].
- In a survey of 304 UK GPs, 39.5% had never received formal autism training; GPs knew the key features but had limited confidence in identifying and managing autistic patients [[Unigwe2017]].

### nursing · Toolkits, hospital passports and alert cards
- The AASPIRE Healthcare Toolkit, including the Autism Healthcare Accommodations Tool (a personalised accommodations report for the primary care provider), was tested with 259 autistic adults and 51 primary care providers [[Nicolaidis2016]].
- After using the toolkit, the mean number of healthcare barriers fell from 4.07 to 2.82, healthcare self-efficacy rose from 37.9 to 39.4, and satisfaction with provider communication rose from 30.9 to 32.6; 97% of providers read the report and 87% would recommend it (single-arm pre/post) [[Nicolaidis2016]].
- A review of 60 UK hospital passports (for people with intellectual disabilities) found wide variation in format and content; some omitted allergies, risk assessment or the need for reasonable adjustments [[Northway2017]].
- Health passports tailored for emergency departments in three Ontario regions were rated helpful, user-friendly and good for communication by staff, agencies and families [[Heifetz2018]].
- Autistic adults asked for online or text booking, sending the reason for the visit in advance, the first or last appointment of the day and a quiet place to wait [[Doherty2022]].

### nursing · Sensory-adapted environments and reasonable adjustments
- NICE CG142 lists environmental adjustments for autistic adults: personal space of at least an arm's length, visual supports, low-arousal wall colours such as cream, reduced fluorescent lighting and reduced noise (or earplugs); if the setting can't be changed, vary the length of the assessment and allow breaks [[NICE-CG142]].
- NICE CG170 suggests appointments at the start or end of the day to cut waiting, and single rooms for children who need a general anaesthetic, for example for dental treatment [[NICE-CG170]].
- SADE-2 randomised crossover trial (NCT02430051; 162 autistic children aged 6–12): a sensory-adapted dental environment (changed lighting, sound and touch) lowered physiological stress during cleaning (skin conductance level −1.22 μS, 95% CI −2.17 to −0.27) vs a regular environment [[SteinDuker2023,NCT02430051]].
- Video-coded behavioural distress was also lower in the sensory-adapted setting (Cohen d −0.84 to −1.19), and no child withdrew because of adverse effects [[SteinDuker2023]].
- An earlier pilot crossover (22 autistic and 22 typically developing children) found lower physiological anxiety, pain and sensory discomfort in the sensory-adapted dental environment for both groups [[Cermak2015]].
- Autistic adults described accommodations as essential; the Doherty authors wrote that adjustments for autism are "as necessary as ramps for wheelchair users" [[Doherty2022]].

### nursing · Preparing for procedures
- A Blood Draw Intervention Program (offered to 58 of 210 families with concerns) raised blood-draw compliance from 85.4% to 96.6% (OR 4.80, 95% CI 1.12–20.59) in a research setting (quasi-randomised) [[Davit2011]].
- Gradual exposure (stimulus fading) with differential reinforcement enabled daily blood glucose sampling in an autistic adolescent with diabetes whose needle phobia had blocked monitoring for over 2 years (single case) [[Shabani2006]].
- Low-cost virtual-reality exposure therapy reduced extreme blood-draw phobia in an autistic adult, with gains that generalised and were maintained (single case) [[Meindl2019]].
- A five-session TEACCH-based programme increased compliance with a 10-step dental assessment in 38 autistic children (4–9 years) and 34 autistic adults (19–41 years) [[Orellana2014]].
- In an RCT of 80 autistic children aged 7–17 with dental fear, watching a favourite film through video goggles during the visit lowered anxiety and behaviour scores by 0.8 points between visits; video peer modelling alone did not [[Isong2014]].
- In a feasibility RCT (32 parent–child pairs) an iPad social-script app before imaging was linked to fewer externalised behaviours in children (effect size 0.56) and lower parental anxiety (effect size 0.33) [[Johnson2014iPad]].
- Parents and staff of autistic children having day procedures valued clear explanations, flexibility (skipping unnecessary blood pressure checks, not changing into gowns), social stories, comfort objects and premedication; long waits, no private space and poor staff training were barriers [[Taghizadeh2019]].

### nursing · Pain assessment
- Children with autism do feel and show pain: during venepuncture, 21 autistic children aged 3–7 showed a strong facial pain reaction, larger than that of 22 non-autistic children, contradicting the belief that autistic children are insensitive to pain [[Nader2004]].
- Parents' global reports of their autistic child's pain matched observed pain behaviour less well than in the comparison group, so global parent report alone may miss pain [[Nader2004]].
- Observers judging videos of autistic children's venepuncture based their pain ratings on the children's facial activity, not on information they were given about pain sensitivity [[Messmer2008]].
- The Non-communicating Children's Pain Checklist–Revised (NCCPC-R), tested in 71 children aged 3–18 with severe cognitive impairment, suggested that a score of 7 or more indicates pain (sensitivity 84%, specificity up to 77%) [[Breau2002]].
- The FLACC scale scores five behaviours (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability); in 89 children aged 2 months to 7 years after surgery it had high inter-rater reliability and fell after analgesia [[Merkel1997]].
- The revised FLACC (r-FLACC) lets parents add the child's own pain behaviours; in 52 children aged 4–19 with cognitive impairment, inter-rater ICCs were 0.76–0.90, and scores fell from 6.1 to 1.9 after analgesia [[Malviya2006]].
- NICE CG142 asks professionals to be aware of under-reporting and under-recognition of physical disorders in autistic people [[NICE-CG142]].

### nursing · Anaesthesia, sedation and premedication
- A review of perioperative care covered premedication with alpha-2 agonists (clonidine, dexmedetomidine) and with midazolam and ketamine, and simple strategies: social stories, tablets, comfort items, disguising bitter medicine in a favourite drink, and talking with parents before the day ("the parents are their child's expert") [[Taghizadeh2015]].
- In 121 autistic and 881 non-autistic children having general anaesthesia for dental treatment (2006–2011), autistic children more often had non-standard premedication types and routes, but complications, pain, recovery-room time and time to discharge were similar [[Arnold2015]].
- The anaesthesia literature on autism consists mostly of case reports; knowing each patient's behaviour and reducing stress are the main recommendations [[Vlassakova2016]].
- The same review stresses staff training and having a clinical practice guideline at every institution [[Taghizadeh2015]].

### nursing · Emergency department, crisis and meltdowns
- In the 2008 US Nationwide Emergency Department Sample, 13% of ED visits by autistic children aged 3–17 were for a psychiatric problem vs 2% of visits by other children (multivariate OR 9.13, 95% CI 8.61–9.70) [[Kalb2012ED]].
- Autistic adolescents aged 12–21 used the emergency department four times as often as adolescents without autism (between 2005 and 2013) [[Liu2017ED]].
- For autistic adults aged 22–64, 15% of ED visits were for a primary psychiatric disorder vs 4.2% for matched adults without autism; mean ED charges were 2.3 times higher, and ASD-associated visits rose from 2,549 to 6,087 per 100,000 between 2006 and 2011 [[Vohra2016]].
- Thirty-two autistic adults described meltdowns as being overwhelmed by sensory, information and social-emotional load, with intense emotions and trouble thinking; many tried to get away or be alone to avoid harm [[Lewis2023Meltdowns]].
- NICE NG10 de-escalation for children and young people includes calming techniques and distraction, offering a move to a quiet room or area, and keeping a therapeutic relationship [[NICE-NG10]].
- NICE CG142 recommends a 24-hour crisis management plan listing likely triggers, how autism may affect behaviour before and during a crisis, the roles of services, and advice to families on their role [[NICE-CG142]].
- In 111 users of the US START crisis-prevention model for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities and mental health needs, psychiatric hospitalisations and ED visits fell over one year, and caregiver service experiences improved (pre/post, no control) [[Kalb2019START]].

### nursing · Restraint and restrictive practice
- In a US paediatric ED (3,469 patients aged 5–17 needing psychiatric consultation, 2013–2025), physical restraint was used for 14.1% of autistic patients vs 6.0% of non-autistic psychiatric patients (adjusted OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.68–3.08); pharmacological restraint was 43.2% vs 31.9% (aOR 1.67) [[McGaughey2026]].
- In another children's hospital ED (6,288 behavioural-health encounters, 2021–2023), restraint was used in 1.97% overall; autism (aOR 7.25, 95% CI 3.61–14.55), arrival by police or EMS (aOR 3.07) and child protective services involvement (aOR 1.91) were independently linked to restraint [[Rolison2026]].
- NICE NG10: use a restrictive intervention only if de-escalation and other preventive strategies (including p.r.n. medication) have failed and there is potential for harm; keep trying de-escalation throughout [[NICE-NG10]].
- NICE NG10: restriction must be proportionate, the least restrictive option, used for no longer than necessary, and must take account of the person's preferences, physical health and developmental age; never to punish or establish dominance [[NICE-NG10]].
- NICE NG10: avoid taking a person to the floor; if necessary use the supine position, or prone only as briefly as possible; never restrict airway, breathing, circulation or the person's ability to communicate [[NICE-NG10]].
- NICE NG10: after any restrictive intervention, hold an immediate post-incident debrief covering physical harm, ongoing risks and the emotional impact on the patient, staff and witnesses [[NICE-NG10]].

### nursing · Staff training
- England and Wales: section 181 of the Health and Care Act 2022 requires registered health and social care providers to ensure each person working for them receives training on learning disability and autism appropriate to their role [[HCA2022]].
- The Oliver McGowan Mandatory Training is NHS England's standardised programme for this duty; a Tier 1 pilot (90-minute e-learning plus a 1-hour webinar with experts by experience) for health students improved confidence in communicating with autistic people (p < 0.0001) [[Bashir2026]].
- Only 37% of surveyed police officers in England and Wales had autism training, and both officers and the autism community asked for role-specific training [[Crane2016Police]].

### nursing · Documentation and family as experts
- Hospital passports and accommodation reports should record communication needs, sensory needs, allergies, risk assessment and needed reasonable adjustments; reviews show these items are sometimes missing [[Northway2017,Nicolaidis2016]].
- Parents are their child's experts on what agitates and settles them; perioperative reviews recommend talking with them before the day of a procedure [[Taghizadeh2015]].
- For the r-FLACC, parents add the child's own pain behaviours, which improves reliability [[Malviya2006]].
- NICE CG142 recommends agreeing with the autistic adult how information is shared with family, partners or carers, and not withdrawing services because family are involved unless agreed [[NICE-CG142]].
- The AAP report frames management as shared decision-making with families [[Hyman2020]].

### Exam hooks
- Parent training (RUBI) beat parent education for disruptive behaviour in autistic children aged 3–7: ABC-Irritability fell 47.7% vs 31.8%; clinician-rated response 68.5% vs 39.6% [[Bearss2015]].
- About 31% of parents of autistic children meet criteria for a depressive disorder and 33% for an anxiety disorder [[Schnabel2020]].
- Divorce: 23.5% in parents of autistic children vs 13.8% in comparison parents [[Hartley2010]].
- NICE CG128: offer a follow-up appointment within 6 weeks of the end of the diagnostic assessment and a written report [[NICE-CG128]].
- NICE CG170: every autistic child should have a key worker; families have a right to respite and a carer's assessment [[NICE-CG170]].
- Bullying victimisation in autistic youth: pooled 44% [[Maiano2016]]; 46.3% in US national data [[Sterzing2012]].
- 43% of autistic students in a UK survey had persistent absence; school refusal was the commonest reason [[Totsika2020]].
- Visual activity schedules meet criteria as an evidence-based practice [[Knight2015]]; Social Stories have low to questionable overall effect [[Kokina2010]].
- Services cliff: 39.1% of US autistic young adults received no services after high school [[Shattuck2011Services]].
- UK ONS: 21.7% employment for people whose main impairment was autism (2020), 29.0% (2021) [[ONS2021Outcomes,ONS2022Outcomes]].
- Project SEARCH plus ASD Supports: 90% vs 6% employed 3 months after graduation [[Wehman2017]].
- Autistic people are not shown to be over-represented in the criminal justice system [[King2014CJS]].
- 80% of autistic adults vs 37% of non-autistic adults report difficulty visiting a GP [[Doherty2022]].
- Autistic children show pain during venepuncture; facial activity is a better guide than global parent report [[Nader2004,Messmer2008]].
- NCCPC-R cut-off: 7 or more suggests pain (sensitivity 84%) [[Breau2002]]; FLACC = Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability [[Merkel1997]].
- Restrictive intervention only when de-escalation has failed, least restrictive, shortest time, followed by debrief [[NICE-NG10]].
- Autistic children in a paediatric ED were restrained more often (14.1% vs 6.0%) [[McGaughey2026]].
- Health and Care Act 2022 s181: registered health and social care providers must give staff role-appropriate training on learning disability and autism [[HCA2022]].

### In plain words
- Caring for an autistic child can be very tiring. Many parents feel stressed, anxious or low, and it is fine to ask for help for yourself too [[Schnabel2020,NICE-CG170]].
- In England, families can ask for a carer's assessment and about short breaks (respite) [[NICE-CG170]].
- Parent training teaches practical ways to handle meltdowns and difficult behaviour at home, and it helps many families [[Bearss2015]].
- Brothers and sisters can need support too, and good services include them [[Shivers2019]].
- Picture timetables help many autistic people know what is coming next [[Knight2015]].
- Bullying happens to many autistic pupils. Tell the school, and ask what they will do to keep your child safe [[Maiano2016]].
- Leaving school can mean losing services suddenly. Start planning for adult life early, from about age 14 [[Laxman2019,NICE-CG170]].
- Many autistic adults want to work and can work well with the right job match and support [[Wehman2017,Khalifa2020]].
- You can ask an employer or a hospital for reasonable adjustments, such as a quiet place to wait, written information or the first appointment of the day [[Smith2025Adjustments,Doherty2022]].
- Getting diagnosed as an adult can bring relief and a new way of understanding yourself [[Stagg2019]].
- A hospital passport tells staff how you communicate, what upsets you and what helps [[Northway2017]].
- Autistic people feel pain. If someone can't say they hurt, watch their face and body, and ask the people who know them best [[Nader2004,Malviya2006]].
- A meltdown is not bad behaviour. It is being overwhelmed. A quiet space and calm, few words help most [[Lewis2023Meltdowns,NICE-NG10]].
- Screening questionnaires and checklists are not a diagnosis and should not be used to rule autism in or out. Only a trained team can diagnose autism [[NICE-CG128]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Is the parent-training effect clinically meaningful? Parent training beat parent education on both parent-rated outcomes and blinded clinician ratings [[Bearss2015]], and a meta-analysis supports efficacy [[Postorino2017]]; but neither RUBI parent measure reached its prespecified minimal clinically important difference, and parent raters were not blind [[Bearss2015]]. Status: efficacious; size of real-life benefit uncertain.
- Inclusion or specialist settings? Research on "inclusive" interventions mostly asks autistic students to adapt (integration), and there is no agreed measure of inclusion [[Perrelet2025]]; general-education classes were linked to more bullying victimisation [[Sterzing2012]] and mainstream students missed more school [[Totsika2020]], yet pupils could thrive in mainstream with the right support [[Gray2023School]]. Status: no head-to-head evidence; individual choice.
- TEACCH and Social Stories are widely used in schools but their pooled evidence is limited (TEACCH exploratory; Social Stories low to questionable) [[ViruesOrtega2013,Kokina2010]], while visual schedules have stronger single-case support [[Knight2015]]. Status: use as part of individual plans, monitor effect.
- Does restraint disparity grow with age? One ED study found no autism–restraint difference at 5–8 years but higher odds at 9–12 and over 12 [[McGaughey2026]]; a published commentary noted that no age-by-autism interaction was tested and that the age pattern should be treated as hypothesis-generating [[Hira2026]]. Status: overall disparity reported in two EDs [[McGaughey2026,Rolison2026]]; age trend unproven (Hypothesis).
- Should people disclose autism at work? Disclosure was associated with being employed more than three times as often [[Ohl2017]], but this cross-sectional link can't show cause, and autistic clinicians described adjustments happening "under the radar" [[Smith2025Adjustments]]. Status: personal decision; no trial evidence.
- Is there an "autism advantage" at work? Popular claims of superior attention to detail are not backed by strong evidence [[Bury2020]]. Status: unresolved; avoid stereotypes.
- When to tell a child they are autistic: students told younger reported better quality of life [[Oredipe2023]], but evidence is a single survey of university students. Status: Early evidence.
- Alert cards and passports: users value them [[Edwards2025AlertCard,Heifetz2018]], but formats vary and no trial shows better outcomes [[Northway2017]]. Status: low-cost, plausible, unproven on hard outcomes.

- Unverified (no listed API indexes them; read directly on 2026-11-02): NICE CG128, CG170, CG142 and NG10 (nice.org.uk recommendation pages), ONS "Outcomes for disabled people in the UK" 2020 and 2021 (ons.gov.uk), and the Health and Care Act 2022 section 181 (legislation.gov.uk).
- Not used or dropped: Hayes & Watson 2013 gives no numerical effect size in its abstract and the full text is paywalled, so it is cited only for "large effect size". The brief's Roux 2015 National Autism Indicators Report, US federal statutes (IDEA, ADA, Section 504) and the Equality Act 2010 text are not in the validating services, so they are described only through peer-reviewed papers. UK Department for Education exclusion statistics were not used, and school-level exclusion data come from Totsika 2020. No PubMed paper with data on hospital-passport outcomes in autism specifically was found; the passport studies cover intellectual and developmental disabilities. The AQ and M-CHAT-R/F canonical works are not cited here; the plain-words line on screening tools cites NICE CG128, which says screening tools should not be used to make or rule out a diagnosis.
