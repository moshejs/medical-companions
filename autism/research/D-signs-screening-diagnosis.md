# D · Early signs, screening, DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 criteria and assessment

Pass date: 2026-11-02. Modules served: milestones, screening, criteria, assessment.

This pass covers how autism is noticed and confirmed. It includes developmental milestones and early signs, regression, how autism can look different in girls and in bilingual children, screening tools and their real-world accuracy, the DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 criteria, the DSM-IV to DSM-5 transition, the multidisciplinary assessment, diagnostic instruments, age at diagnosis, adult and late diagnosis, differential diagnosis and the medical workup.

## Findings

### milestones · What surveillance milestones are and how they changed in 2022
- In 2022, the CDC "Learn the Signs. Act Early." checklists were revised by an expert group convened by the AAP. The group placed each milestone at the visit age by which most children (≥75%) would be expected to reach it. The aim was to discourage a "wait and see" approach [[Zubler2022]].
- The revision covers health supervision visits at 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 24 and 30 months and at 3, 4 and 5 years. The 15- and 30-month checklists were new [[Zubler2022]].
- CDC previously had 216 milestones across 10 checklists. After the revision there were 159 milestones across 12 checklists, a 26.4% reduction. The average number per checklist fell from 22 to 13 [[Zubler2022]].
- Of the 159 final milestones, 94 (59.1%) were original CDC milestones and 65 (40.9%) were new. About one third of retained milestones moved to a different age, and 21 of those 31 moved to an older age [[Zubler2022]].
- 127 of 159 milestones (79.9%) were supported by normative data. Social-emotional and cognitive milestones had the least normative data: 59.5% of social-emotional milestones had 0–1 normed references [[Zubler2022]].
- The panel chose the 75th percentile deliberately. At the 50th percentile, half of children would "miss" a milestone. At 85–90%, too few at-risk children would be flagged [[Zubler2022]].
- The separate "warning signs" list was dropped because most warning signs became milestones. 59 of the 77 old warning signs (76.6%) had a corresponding new milestone [[Zubler2022]].
- Missing a milestone is a reason for developmental screening. It is not a diagnosis. The checklists are not meant to replace validated screening tools [[Zubler2022]].
- Examples of social-communication milestones in the revised checklists (paraphrased):
  - 2 months: looks at the parent's face; smiles back when talked to.
  - 9 months: looks when their name is called; plays peek-a-boo.
  - 12 months: waves bye-bye; plays games such as pat-a-cake.
  - 15 months: shows the parent an object they like; points to ask for something.
  - 18 months: points to show something interesting; tries to say three or more words besides "mama" or "dada".
  - 24 months: notices when others are upset; says two-word phrases.
  - 30 months: says about 50 words; plays next to and sometimes with other children [[Zubler2022]].
- The AAP recommends developmental surveillance at every health supervision visit and standardized general developmental screening at 9, 18 and 30 months [[Lipkin2020]].

### milestones · Red flags and early signs
- A 2026 systematic review of warning signs lists two of the most consistent language red flags for autism: no single words by 16 months and no spontaneous two-word phrases by 24 months. These are more predictive when combined with social differences [[Araujo2026]].
- The same review lists other early signs: no "goodbye" gesture at 12 months, no declarative pointing at 12–18 months, a poor range of gestures at 16 months, and repeated failure to respond to name at 9–12 months despite normal hearing [[Araujo2026]].
- NICE CG128 lists features suggesting possible autism in preschool children, including:
  - language delay, for example fewer than ten words by age 2
  - regression in or loss of speech
  - absent or delayed response to name despite normal hearing
  - reduced social smiling
  - reduced imitation
  - reduced joint attention, such as not following a point or not pointing or showing to share interest
  - repetitive movements
  - over- or under-reaction to sensory input [[NICE-CG128]].
- NICE warns against ruling out autism because of good eye contact, smiling, affection to family, reported pretend play, normal language milestones, or a previous assessment that found no autism [[NICE-CG128]].
- The 2021 ESCAP guidance repeats CDC "red flags": no response to name by 12 months, no pointing to show interest by 14 months, and no pretend play by 18 months. It notes that their absence in very young children should not rule out autism [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- A 2023 JAMA review names three common early signs in the first 2 years: no response to name, little or no use of gestures, and lack of imaginative play [[Hirota2023]].
- In a prospective study (25 infants later diagnosed with autism vs 25 typically developing infants), gaze to faces, shared smiles and directed vocalizations were similar at 6 months. They then declined in the autism group, with significant differences by 12 months on most measures (Established) [[Ozonoff2010]].
- Eye-tracking showed that attention to eyes is present at 2 months in infants later diagnosed with autism and then declines from 2 to 6 months. It starts at normal levels rather than being absent from birth (Early evidence) [[Jones2013Nature]].
- In a study of 150 infant siblings of autistic children, those later diagnosed could be distinguished by 12 months. Signs included atypical eye contact, visual tracking and attention disengagement, orienting to name, imitation, social smiling and social interest (Early evidence) [[Zwaigenbaum2005]].
- A 2015 multidisciplinary panel reviewed the earliest signs of autism in children aged ≤24 months. It stressed that early identification shortens families' "diagnostic odyssey" [[Zwaigenbaum2015]].
- Parents often notice differences early. In US national survey data (1420 autistic children vs 2098 with intellectual disability or developmental delay), parents of autistic children had concerns at younger ages. They were more likely to get "reassuring or passive" responses from providers, and passive responses were linked to longer diagnostic delays [[Zuckerman2015]].
- NICE says to always take parents' or carers' concerns seriously, even if others do not share them [[NICE-CG128]].

### milestones · Regression (loss of skills)
- A 2013 meta-analysis (85 articles, 29,035 autistic participants) found a regression prevalence of 32.1% (95% CI 29.5–34.8). The mean onset was 1.78 years (95% CI 1.67–1.89) [[Barger2013]].
- Regression rates by type in that meta-analysis: language 24.9%, language plus social 38.1%, mixed 32.5%, unspecified 39.1% [[Barger2013]].
- Regression rates by sampling method: population-based 21.8%, clinic-based 33.6%, parent survey 40.8%. Risk was equal in males and females [[Barger2013]].
- A 2021 update (75 studies, 33,014 participants, studies from 2010 onward) found a pooled regression rate of 30% (95% CI 27–32%) with very high heterogeneity (I² 96.91). The weighted average onset was 19.8 months [[Tan2021]].
- In that update, rates depended on the definition used: language 20%, language plus social 40%, mixed 30%, unspecified 27%. Studies at low risk of bias found 27% [[Tan2021]].
- Measurement method matters. In a prospective infant-sibling study (147 with and 83 without a family history), a regressive onset was found in 88% by examiner and 69% by parent prospective dimensional ratings, but in only 29–47% by retrospective or categorical measures (Early evidence) [[Ozonoff2018Onset]].
- In Ozonoff 2010, repeated evaluation documented loss of skills in most infants later diagnosed with autism, but most parents did not report a regression [[Ozonoff2010]].
- NICE says to refer a child younger than 3 years with regression in language or social skills directly to the autism team for assessment [[NICE-CG128]].
- NICE says to refer first to a paediatrician or paediatric neurologist any child older than 3 with language regression, and any child at any age with regression in motor skills [[NICE-CG128]].
- Severe, sudden regression after typical development in many areas is rare. If it occurs, Landau–Kleffner syndrome (an acquired epileptic aphasia identified by clinical and EEG findings) should be considered [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Rett syndrome and epileptic encephalopathy are listed by NICE as differential diagnoses involving developmental regression [[NICE-CG128]].

### milestones · Girls and women
- A meta-analysis of 54 prevalence studies (53,712 autistic participants) found a pooled male-to-female odds ratio of 4.20 (95% CI 3.84–4.60) [[Loomes2017]].
- The ratio was lower in high-quality studies (3.32) and in studies that actively screened the population (3.25), compared with studies counting only existing diagnoses (4.56). The authors conclude the true ratio is closer to 3:1 and that girls who meet criteria are at disproportionate risk of not being diagnosed [[Loomes2017]].
- In the 2022 ADDM data, autism was 3.4 times as common in 8-year-old boys (49.2 per 1,000) as in girls (14.3 per 1,000) [[Shaw2025]].
- In a population sample of 10–12-year-olds with high autistic-trait scores, girls who met diagnostic criteria had more additional problems (lower intellectual level, behavioural difficulties) than girls with equally high traits who did not. This pattern was not seen in boys. It suggests that girls without extra problems are less likely to be diagnosed [[Dworzynski2012]].
- In a Dutch survey of 2,275 autistic people, girls with an Asperger's diagnosis were identified later than boys, and adult women with autistic disorder were identified later than men [[Begeer2013]].
- In 228 age- and IQ-matched autistic youth, girls with higher IQ were less likely to meet ADI-R criteria, even though parents reported more autistic traits and lower adaptive skills in girls [[Ratto2018]].
- The ESCAP guidance notes that girls' social and communication differences are often more subtle. Girls may be more adept at "masking" or "camouflaging", more likely to seek friendships (often one or two friends), and show more imaginative play, more emotional language and fewer repetitive behaviours [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- A qualitative study of 92 autistic adults described camouflaging as masking plus compensation, motivated by fitting in. Short- and long-term costs included exhaustion and threats to self-perception [[Hull2017]].
- A systematic review of 29 studies found sex and gender differences in camouflaging, and that more self-reported camouflaging was linked to worse mental health (Early evidence) [[Cook2021Camouflaging]].
- Fourteen late-diagnosed women (aged 22–30) described "pretending to be normal" and professionals missing their autism because of their gender [[Bargiela2016]].
- A systematic review of barriers to diagnosis in girls and young women under 21 identified compensatory behaviours, others' perceptions, lack of information and clinician bias [[LockwoodEstrin2021]].
- NICE CG128 states that autism may be under-recognised in girls, and that features may have been masked by coping mechanisms or a supportive environment [[NICE-CG128]].

### milestones · Bilingual children and cultural context
- In 75 autistic children (mean age 56 months), 45 from bilingual homes and 30 from monolingual homes, language levels did not differ. Bilingual exposure did not cause additional language delay [[Hambly2012]].
- In school-age autistic children without intellectual disability, the current amount of exposure to a language was the strongest predictor of vocabulary (62% of variance) and morphology (49%) in that language. The same was true for typically developing peers. Many autistic children can learn two languages when exposure is adequate [[GonzalezBarrero2018]].
- NICE says to take account of cultural variation, but not to assume that language delay is explained by English not being the family's first language, or by early hearing problems. Clinicians should ask about the child's use and understanding of their first language [[NICE-CG128]].
- In 2,568 8-year-olds meeting ADDM surveillance criteria, Black (OR 0.79), Hispanic (OR 0.76) and other-race (OR 0.65) children were less likely than White children to have a documented autism diagnosis after adjustment [[Mandell2009]].

### screening · Recommendations
- The AAP 2020 clinical report recommends standardized autism screening at 18 and 24 months together with ongoing developmental surveillance. It notes that autism can be diagnosed as young as 18 months and that more accurate and culturally sensitive screening approaches are needed [[Hyman2020]].
- The 2000 AAN and Child Neurology Society practice parameter (now retired) proposed two levels: routine developmental surveillance and autism-specific screening for all children, followed by diagnostic evaluation for those at risk [[Filipek2000]].
- In 2016, the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) issued an "I statement". It found the evidence insufficient to judge the balance of benefits and harms of screening for autism in children aged 18–30 months when no concerns have been raised by parents or clinicians [[Siu2016]].
- The USPSTF statement covers only children without concerns and without a diagnosis. It reviewed the accuracy of screening tools and the benefits of early treatment for children found through screening [[Siu2016]].
- The ESCAP guidance contrasts the AAP's universal screening with the USPSTF's "insufficient evidence" position. It stresses that screening tools only alert clinicians to the possibility of autism and cannot confirm a diagnosis [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- A negative screen does not exclude autism. If other information points to developmental differences, a full assessment is still warranted [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- NICE CG128 does not recommend a universal screening tool. It relies on recognizing features and referring children to a local multidisciplinary autism team [[NICE-CG128]].

### screening · M-CHAT family (toddlers)
- The original M-CHAT (2001) had 23 yes/no parent items and was tested in 1,293 children. 39 of 58 children evaluated were diagnosed with an autism spectrum disorder [[Robins2001]].
- With the original M-CHAT plus a follow-up interview, in 18,989 toddlers, 54% of children who screened positive on both steps had autism and 98% had clinically significant developmental concerns [[Chlebowski2013]].
- That study recommended a total score of ≥3 as the screen-positive cutoff. A score of ≥7 justified direct referral without the follow-up interview [[Chlebowski2013]].
- The revised M-CHAT-R/F was validated in 16,071 toddlers screened at 18- and 24-month visits in Atlanta and Connecticut [[Robins2014]].
- The M-CHAT-R has 20 yes/no items, so scores run from 0 to 20. A score of 0–2 is low likelihood. A follow-up interview is recommended for scores of 3–7. A score of 8 or more is screen-positive without needing the interview [[Robins2014,Sturner2022]].
- Children who scored ≥3 initially and still scored ≥2 after the follow-up interview had a 47.5% risk of an autism diagnosis (95% CI 41–54%) and a 94.6% risk of any developmental delay or concern (95% CI 92–98%) [[Robins2014]].
- Children in the M-CHAT-R/F validation were diagnosed about 2 years younger than the US national median age at the time [[Robins2014]].
- A screen-positive child who does not have autism usually still needs help. In the validation, almost 95% of children positive after follow-up had a developmental delay or concern [[Robins2014]].
- A 2023 meta-analysis (50 studies, 51 samples) found pooled M-CHAT(-R/F) sensitivity of 0.83 (95% CI 0.77–0.88) and specificity of 0.94 (95% CI 0.89–0.97). Accuracy was higher with the follow-up interview, in low-likelihood (general population) samples and in larger studies [[Wieckowski2023]].
- Disclosure: the senior author of that meta-analysis co-owns the M-CHAT copyright and receives licensing royalties [[Wieckowski2023]].

### screening · Real-world performance and disparities
- In a truly universal primary-care screening program (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, n = 25,999 toddlers, follow-up to age 4–8 years), screening reached 91% of children and autism prevalence was 2.2%. The M-CHAT/F sensitivity was 38.8% and PPV 14.6% [[Guthrie2019]].
- In that cohort, sensitivity was higher in older toddlers and with repeated screening. PPV was lower in girls. Specificity and PPV were lower in children of color and in lower-income households [[Guthrie2019]].
- In 36,233 toddlers in a Utah health system, 73% were screened and 1.4% were later diagnosed with autism. M-CHAT sensitivity was 33.1% and PPV 17.8% [[Carbone2020]].
- Screen-positive children in that system were diagnosed earlier than unscreened children (38.5 vs 48.5 months). Providers often skipped the follow-up interview [[Carbone2020]].
- Hispanic children were slightly less likely to be screened (adjusted prevalence ratio 0.95), and family physicians screened far less often than pediatricians (APR 0.12) [[Carbone2020]].
- At 18 months, in a community sample enriched for screen-positive children (n ≈ 400), M-CHAT-R alone had sensitivity 0.73 and specificity 0.66. The two-stage M-CHAT-R/F had sensitivity 0.36 and specificity 0.89, and Q-CHAT-10 had sensitivity 0.34 and specificity 0.95 [[Sturner2022]].
- The authors conclude that at 18 months the follow-up interview lowers sensitivity with little gain in PPV, and that screening needs to be repeated after this age [[Sturner2022]].
- Why the published numbers differ: validation studies that follow only screen-positive children cannot count missed cases. Studies that follow everyone (Guthrie, Carbone) find far lower sensitivity [[Guthrie2019,Wieckowski2023]].

### screening · Other tools: Q-CHAT, SCQ, AQ, AQ-10
- The Q-CHAT has 25 parent-rated items scored 0–4 and gives a continuous trait score. Autistic children scored a mean of 51.8 (SD 14.3) vs 26.7 (SD 7.8) in 779 unselected toddlers. Test-retest reliability was 0.82 [[Allison2008]].
- The 10-item Q-CHAT-10 at a cut-point of 3 had sensitivity 0.91, specificity 0.89 and PPV 0.58 in a case-control sample (126 autistic toddlers vs 754 controls) [[Allison2012]].
- In that case-control sample, the AQ-10 at a cut-point of 6 had:
  - adult version: sensitivity 0.88, specificity 0.91, PPV 0.85
  - adolescent version: sensitivity 0.93, specificity 0.95
  - child version: sensitivity 0.95, specificity 0.97 [[Allison2012]].
- Case-control accuracy is usually higher than accuracy in real clinics, because the comparison groups are very clearly separated [[Allison2012,Ashwood2016]].
- The full Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ) is a 50-item self-report scale scored 0–50. Adults with an Asperger or "HFA" diagnosis (n = 58) scored a mean of 35.8 vs 16.4 in 174 controls. 80% of the diagnosed group scored 32 or more, compared with 2% of controls [[BaronCohen2001]].
- In 100 consecutive referrals to an adult diagnostic clinic, the AQ showed good screening properties at a threshold of 26 [[WoodburySmith2005]].
- In 476 adults referred to a national UK diagnostic service, 73% received an autism diagnosis. AQ score did not significantly predict diagnosis: sensitivity 0.77, PPV 0.76, specificity 0.29, NPV 0.36. 64% of those below the cutoff were in fact autistic [[Ashwood2016]].
- In that study, generalized anxiety disorder appeared to "mimic" autism and raise AQ scores, causing false positives [[Ashwood2016]].
- NICE CG142 (amended 2021) says to consider the AQ-10 for adults with possible autism who do not have a moderate or severe learning disability. A score of 6 or above, or clinical suspicion, should lead to an offer of a comprehensive assessment [[NICE-CG142]].
- The Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ) is a parent questionnaire derived from the ADI. In a population cohort of 9–10-year-olds, it had sensitivity 0.88 and specificity 0.72 for any autism diagnosis vs none [[Chandler2007]].
- In the general population, 4–5% of children scored above the SCQ autism-spectrum cutoff. About 90% of high scorers had some diagnosed neurodevelopmental condition [[Chandler2007]].
- In 590 clinic-referred children aged 2–16, the standard SCQ cutoff of ≥15 gave lower sensitivity in younger children and low specificity overall. Accuracy improved when the SCQ was combined with the ADOS [[Corsello2007]].
- Why this matters for patients: online versions of the AQ and similar questionnaires measure traits. They do not make a diagnosis, and anxiety, depression and other conditions can raise scores [[Ashwood2016,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].

### criteria · DSM-5-TR autism spectrum disorder (2022)
- DSM-5 (2013) merged autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, childhood disintegrative disorder and PDD-NOS into one diagnosis, autism spectrum disorder, with two core domains [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- DSM-5 places autism among the neurodevelopmental disorders and makes clear it is not a psychotic disorder [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Criterion A requires persistent differences in social communication and social interaction across contexts, with all three of the following, currently or by history:
  - A1: social-emotional reciprocity (back-and-forth conversation, sharing interests or emotions, starting or responding to interaction)
  - A2: nonverbal communication (eye contact, gestures, facial expression, body language, and understanding them)
  - A3: developing, maintaining and understanding relationships [[APA2022,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Criterion B requires at least two of four restricted or repetitive patterns:
  - B1: stereotyped or repetitive movements, use of objects or speech
  - B2: insistence on sameness, inflexible routines or ritualized behaviour
  - B3: highly restricted, fixated interests that are unusual in intensity or focus
  - B4: hyper- or hyporeactivity to sensory input, or unusual interest in sensory aspects of the environment [[APA2022,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Criterion C: symptoms are present in the early developmental period. They may not become fully apparent until social demands exceed capacities, or may be masked later by learned strategies [[APA2022,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Criterion D: symptoms cause clinically significant impairment in social, occupational or other important areas of current functioning [[APA2022,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Criterion E: the differences are not better explained by intellectual developmental disorder or global developmental delay. When both are present, social communication should be below what is expected for general developmental level [[APA2022]].
- The 2022 wording change: the DSM-5-TR text of criterion A was clarified to say "all of the following". The 2013 phrase "as manifested by the following" had been read by some as needing fewer than all three A symptoms. The intended rule (all three A, at least two B) did not change [[APA2022,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Specifiers record whether the person has accompanying intellectual impairment, accompanying language impairment, an association with a known genetic or other medical condition or environmental factor, an association with another neurodevelopmental, mental or behavioural problem, or catatonia [[APA2022,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Severity is rated separately for social communication and for restricted, repetitive behaviours: Level 1 "requiring support", Level 2 "requiring substantial support", Level 3 "requiring very substantial support" [[APA2022]].
- The ESCAP guidance criticizes the severity levels because DSM-5 does not define what "some", "substantial" or "very substantial" support means [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- DSM-5 states that people with a well-established DSM-IV diagnosis of autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder or PDD-NOS should be given the diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder. This is often called the "grandfather clause" [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- DSM-5 has no category for people whose autism features improve so that they no longer meet full criteria but still have needs. ESCAP notes that ICD-11 does account for them [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].

### criteria · ICD-11 6A02 autism spectrum disorder
- ICD-11 was released by WHO in June 2018 and came into effect on 1 January 2022 [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- As in DSM-5, ICD-11 groups autism into two core domains:
  - persistent deficits in starting and sustaining reciprocal social interaction and social communication
  - restricted, repetitive and inflexible patterns of behaviour, interests or activities.
  The differences must cause impairment and are usually seen across settings, although severity can vary with context [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Instead of DSM severity levels, ICD-11 subdivides autism by two axes: intellectual development and functional language:
  - 6A02.0: no disorder of intellectual development; mild or no impairment of functional language
  - 6A02.1: with disorder of intellectual development; mild or no impairment of functional language
  - 6A02.2: no disorder of intellectual development; impaired functional language
  - 6A02.3: with disorder of intellectual development; impaired functional language
  - 6A02.4: no disorder of intellectual development; absence of functional language
  - 6A02.5: with disorder of intellectual development; absence of functional language [[Fuentes2021ESCAP,Chahboun2022]].
- ICD-11 also has 6A02.Y ("other specified") and 6A02.Z ("unspecified"), which have no criteria. The ESCAP authors note that the validity and stability of the six subtype combinations had not yet been shown [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- ICD-11's developmental language disorder section includes a category of mainly pragmatic language impairment. This parallels DSM-5's social (pragmatic) communication disorder [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Like DSM-5, ICD-11 no longer separates Asperger syndrome. A Norwegian review discusses how some people for whom "Asperger" was part of their identity found the change confusing or unwelcome [[Chahboun2022]].

### criteria · The DSM-IV to DSM-5 transition
- In 933 participants from the DSM-IV field trial, only 60.6% (95% CI 57–64%) of those with a clinical autism-spectrum diagnosis met the proposed DSM-5 criteria. Specificity was 94.9% [[McPartland2012]].
- Sensitivity in that study varied by subgroup: autistic disorder 0.76, Asperger's disorder 0.25, PDD-NOS 0.28 [[McPartland2012]].
- In contrast, in 4,453 children with DSM-IV PDD diagnoses, the proposed DSM-5 criteria identified 91% using parent-report data alone. Sensitivity stayed high in girls and children under 4 [[Huerta2012]].
- In that study, the specificity of DSM-5 was 0.53 overall, rising to 0.63 when both parent report and clinical observation were required [[Huerta2012]].
- A 2014 meta-analysis (14 studies) found a pooled 31% decrease in autism-spectrum diagnoses under DSM-5. Decreases were 22% for autistic disorder and 70% for PDD-NOS. The 70% decrease for Asperger's was not statistically significant [[Kulage2014]].
- A 2015 systematic review of 25 studies found that most studies estimated 50–75% of people would keep their diagnosis. The largest losses were in people with IQ over 70 and prior PDD-NOS or Asperger's diagnoses [[Smith2015JADD]].
- A 2020 five-year follow-up meta-analysis (33 studies) found smaller decreases: autism spectrum 20.8% (95% CI 16.0–26.7), autistic disorder 10.1%, Asperger's 23.3%. The PDD-NOS change was not significant [[Kulage2020]].
- In that follow-up, fewer than one third (28.8%) of people with a DSM-IV diagnosis but not a DSM-5 diagnosis qualified for social (pragmatic) communication disorder [[Kulage2020]].
- Disagreement: early field-trial reanalyses predicted large losses (about 40% not meeting criteria) [[McPartland2012]], but studies using parent and clinician data predicted about 9% [[Huerta2012]]. The later meta-analysis puts the decrease at about 21% [[Kulage2020]].

### criteria · Social (pragmatic) communication disorder (SCD)
- DSM-5 introduced SCD for persistent difficulties using verbal and nonverbal communication socially, without restricted and repetitive behaviours or interests [[Norbury2014]].
- If the second domain (restricted, repetitive behaviours) is absent, the condition is classified as social communication disorder rather than autism [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- A practitioner review found that SCD lacks well-validated assessment measures. It overlaps with ADHD, conduct disorder and genetic syndromes, and may be better seen as a dimensional profile across many conditions (Hypothesis) [[Norbury2014]].
- A research review describes the long debate over how pragmatic impairments relate to language disorder and autism, and the uncertain impact of the new SCD category [[Swineford2014]].
- Distinguishing point for students: SCD requires that restricted or repetitive behaviours are absent now and in the past. If they were present at any time in development, autism must be considered first [[Norbury2014,APA2022]].

### assessment · Pathway and team
- NICE CG128 recommends a local multidisciplinary autism team. Its core includes a paediatrician and/or child and adolescent psychiatrist, a speech and language therapist, and a psychologist. Access to a paediatric neurologist, occupational therapist and others is recommended [[NICE-CG128]].
- NICE says to start the autism diagnostic assessment within 3 months of referral to the autism team [[NICE-CG128]].
- A case coordinator should be the single point of contact for each family during assessment [[NICE-CG128]].
- NICE lists what every assessment should include:
  - the parents' and child's concerns
  - home, education and social care experience
  - a developmental history focused on ICD-11 or DSM-5 features
  - direct observation of social and communication skills
  - medical, prenatal, perinatal and family history
  - a physical examination
  - differential diagnosis
  - systematic assessment for co-occurring conditions
  - a profile of strengths and needs [[NICE-CG128]].
- The physical examination should look for skin signs of neurofibromatosis or tuberous sclerosis (Wood's light), signs of injury including self-harm or maltreatment, congenital anomalies and dysmorphic features, including macro- or microcephaly [[NICE-CG128]].
- NICE says to gather hearing and vision results before or during the assessment [[NICE-CG128]].
- A follow-up appointment should be offered within 6 weeks of the end of the assessment [[NICE-CG128]].
- A second opinion is advised when the diagnosis remains uncertain, the team disagrees, the family disagrees, or local skills are lacking for complex co-occurring conditions [[NICE-CG128]].
- The ESCAP minimum assessment includes clinical history, assessment of autism features, developmental level and language, physical and sensory examination (hearing, vision, neurological screen), emotional and behavioural difficulties, and socio-environmental factors [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- No supporting instrument should be used on its own to make the diagnosis. Diagnosis requires expert clinical judgement using information from all relevant sources [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- A 2023 JAMA review describes the reference standard as a comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation combining semistructured direct observation and a semistructured caregiver interview [[Hirota2023]].

### assessment · Diagnostic instruments
- The ADOS (2000) is a semistructured, standardized observation of social interaction, communication, play and imaginative use of materials. It has four modules of about 30 minutes, chosen by expressive language level [[Lord2000]].
- A Toddler Module (Module T) extends the ADOS to children under 30 months. It uses a "range of concern" approach instead of a single cutoff [[Luyster2009]].
- ADOS calibrated severity scores standardize raw totals across modules and ages (derived from 1,807 assessments of 1,118 autistic people aged 2–16), so severity can be compared over time [[Gotham2009]].
- The ADI-R is a semistructured caregiver interview linked to ICD-10 and DSM-IV criteria. It is suitable from a mental age of about 18 months into adulthood [[Lord1994]].
- A Cochrane review (preschool children, 13 publications) found summary accuracy:
  - ADOS: sensitivity 0.94 (95% CI 0.89–0.97), specificity 0.80 (0.68–0.88)
  - CARS: sensitivity 0.80, specificity 0.88
  - ADI-R: sensitivity 0.52 (0.32–0.71), specificity 0.84 (0.61–0.95) [[Randall2018]].
- In that review, autism prevalence across included samples was 74%. Many studies were at high risk of bias, and performance fell when those studies were excluded. In lower-prevalence settings, false positives would be more common [[Randall2018]].
- A 2021 meta-analysis (22 articles) found ADOS-2 sensitivity of 0.89–0.92 and specificity of 0.81–0.85. ADI-R sensitivity was 0.75 and specificity 0.82, with specificity lower in clinical than research samples (0.72 vs 0.85) [[Lebersfeld2021]].
- The JAMA review gives ADOS-2 sensitivity of 91% and specificity of 76%, and ADI sensitivity of 80% and specificity of 72% [[Hirota2023]].
- In 595 children aged 12–47 months, using both the ADI-R and the ADOS significantly improved specificity compared with either alone, with balanced sensitivity and specificity above 80% [[Kim2012Lord]].
- In the Cochrane review, by contrast, one study found that adding the ADI-R to the ADOS did not improve accuracy over the ADOS alone [[Randall2018]].
- A systematic review of 68 articles found that the ADI-R and ADOS had the largest evidence base. Combined, they reached a correct classification rate of 80.8% against the gold standard [[Falkmer2013]].
- In the 2022 ADDM data, 66.5% of autistic 8-year-olds had a documented autism test in their records (range 24.7–93.5% by site). The most common were the ADOS, Autism Spectrum Rating Scales, CARS, Gilliam Autism Rating Scale and Social Responsiveness Scale [[Shaw2025]].

### assessment · How stable is an early diagnosis?
- In 1,269 toddlers from the general population first evaluated between 12 and 36 months, the overall diagnostic stability of autism was 0.84 (95% CI 0.80–0.87). This was higher than any other diagnostic group [[Pierce2019]].
- Stability in that study was 0.50 at 12–13 months, 0.79 at 14 months and 0.83 at 16 months. Only 7 toddlers (1.8%) first thought to be autistic had typical development at the final visit [[Pierce2019]].
- In the same study, 105 toddlers (23.8%) not identified as autistic at the first visit were identified at a later visit [[Pierce2019]].
- In 418 infant siblings of autistic children, a diagnosis at 18 months was stable in 93% and at 24 months in 82% [[Ozonoff2015]].
- In that sibling study, many children diagnosed at 36 months had not yet been diagnosed at 18 months (63%) or 24 months (41%). Screening may need to be repeated [[Ozonoff2015]].
- In a prospective cohort followed from age 2 to 9, best-estimate diagnoses agreed 67% of the time (weighted kappa 0.72). Most change was movement from PDD-NOS to autism. Experienced clinical judgement at age 2 was the strongest predictor (OR 12.8) [[Lord2006]].

### assessment · Age at diagnosis and delays
- In the 2022 ADDM data, the median age at earliest known autism diagnosis among 8-year-olds was 47 months. It ranged from 36 months (California) to 69.5 months (Texas, Laredo) [[Shaw2025]].
- Cumulative incidence of diagnosis or eligibility by 48 months was 1.7 times as high in children born in 2018 as in those born in 2014. This indicates earlier identification over time [[Shaw2025]].
- Among 4-year-olds in ADDM 2022, for every 10 children meeting the autism case definition, one more met the definition of "suspected" autism [[Shaw2025]].
- A meta-analysis of 56 studies from 40 countries (120,540 autistic people) found a mean age at diagnosis of 60.48 months (range 30.90–234.57). For studies including only children aged 10 or younger, the mean was 43.18 months [[vantHof2021]].
- A critical review of 42 studies (1990–2012) found mean ages at diagnosis from 38 to 120 months, decreasing over time. Earlier diagnosis was linked to greater symptom severity, higher socioeconomic status and greater parental concern [[Daniels2014]].
- In a UK survey of 1,047 parents, parents typically waited about 1 year after first concerns before seeking help. There was then a delay of about 3.5 years from first approaching a professional to diagnosis. Just over half were dissatisfied with the process [[Crane2016Diagnosis]].

### assessment · Diagnosis in adults and late diagnosis
- In UK primary-care records (CPRD), recorded autism incidence rose by 787% between 1998 and 2018. The rise was steeper in females and steepest in adults, which suggests better recognition rather than a true rise alone [[Russell2022]].
- In English primary-care data (2018), 2.94% of 10–14-year-olds had an autism diagnosis (1 in 34), compared with 0.02% of people aged 70 or older (1 in 6,000) [[ONions2023]].
- The same study estimated that 59–72% of autistic people in England (about 435,700–1,197,300 people) may be undiagnosed [[ONions2023]].
- New diagnoses in 2018 were about 1 in 250 for 5–9-year-olds, about 1 in 4,000 for 20–49-year-olds and about 1 in 18,000 for people aged 50 or older [[ONions2023]].
- Diagnosing autism for the first time in adults is harder because:
  - there may be no one to give a developmental history
  - the person may have learned camouflaging strategies
  - co-occurring conditions are common
  Possible misdiagnosis, especially in women, should be explored [[Lai2015LancetPsych]].
- In 161 adults diagnosed with autism in two Italian centres, the median age at diagnosis was 23 years. The first mental-health evaluation had been at a median of 13 years, an 11-year gap. Two thirds had previously received other diagnoses, most often intellectual disability, psychoses, personality disorders and depression [[FusarPoli2022]].
- In 1,211 Dutch autistic adults, 24.6% reported at least one earlier psychiatric diagnosis they saw as a misdiagnosis. This was more common in women (31.7%) than men (16.7%). Personality disorder was the most frequent [[Kentrou2024]].
- A scoping review found that adults often feel strong emotions after diagnosis, that adult pathways are unclear and inconsistent, and that post-diagnostic support is scarce [[Huang2020]].
- NICE CG142 says a comprehensive adult assessment should be team-based, done by trained professionals, and involve an informant or documents such as school reports where possible [[NICE-CG142]].
- The adult assessment covers:
  - core features present in childhood and continuing into adulthood
  - early developmental history
  - behavioural problems
  - functioning at home, in education and at work
  - physical and mental health
  - other neurodevelopmental conditions
  - sensory sensitivities [[NICE-CG142]].
- For complex adult assessments, NICE suggests tools such as the ADOS-G, ADI-R, RAADS-R, ASDI or Adult Asperger Assessment. For adults with a learning disability, it particularly suggests the ADOS-G and ADI-R [[NICE-CG142]].

### assessment · Differential diagnosis and look-alikes
- NICE CG128 lists the main differential diagnoses:
  - neurodevelopmental: specific language delay or disorder, intellectual disability or global delay, developmental coordination disorder
  - mental and behavioural: ADHD, mood disorder, anxiety, attachment disorders, ODD, conduct disorder, OCD, psychosis
  - regression conditions: Rett syndrome, epileptic encephalopathy
  - other: severe hearing impairment, severe visual impairment, maltreatment, selective mutism [[NICE-CG128]].
- In adults, NICE CG142 lists differentials and co-occurring conditions: other neurodevelopmental conditions, schizophrenia, depression and other mood disorders, anxiety (especially social anxiety disorder and OCD), epilepsy, and speech and language problems including selective mutism [[NICE-CG142]].
- Hearing loss: absent response to name despite normal hearing is an autism feature, so hearing should be checked first [[NICE-CG128]].
- Intellectual disability: many children with profound intellectual disability develop in ways that resemble autism. A developmental assessment is crucial for differential diagnosis [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Under DSM-5-TR, when autism and intellectual disability co-occur, social communication should be below the person's general developmental level [[APA2022]].
- Language disorder: in Huerta 2012, the DSM-5 criteria were tested against 690 children with non-autism diagnoses such as language disorder. Specificity improved when both parent report and clinician observation were required [[Huerta2012]].
- Anxiety: in adult referrals, generalized anxiety disorder raised AQ scores and produced false positives [[Ashwood2016]].
- Anxiety, depression, OCD, psychosis and personality disorders can overlap with autism in adults. They may be misdiagnoses, true co-occurring conditions, or both [[Lai2015LancetPsych,Kentrou2024]].
- Social (pragmatic) communication disorder is distinguished by the absence of restricted, repetitive behaviours, current or past [[Norbury2014,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Landau–Kleffner syndrome presents with sudden language loss and EEG abnormalities after typical development [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Rett syndrome (MECP2) should be considered when there is regression, particularly in girls. ESCAP lists MECP2 testing in the basic genetic assessment [[Fuentes2021ESCAP,NICE-CG128]].
- Autism is more common with severe sensory impairments (profound deafness, congenital blindness), profound intellectual disability, and many genetic syndromes, including fragile X, Down, Rett, Cornelia de Lange, tuberous sclerosis complex, Angelman, NF1, Noonan, Williams and 22q11.2 deletion. In these groups, diagnosis is harder because social and repetitive features can be part of the syndrome [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].

### assessment · Recommended medical workup
- NICE says not to perform medical investigations routinely as part of the autism assessment. It says to consider:
  - genetic tests, as advised by the regional genetics centre, if there are dysmorphic features, congenital anomalies or intellectual disability
  - an EEG only if epilepsy is suspected [[NICE-CG128]].
- NICE CG142 says not to use biological tests, genetic tests or neuroimaging routinely to diagnose autism in adults [[NICE-CG142]].
- ESCAP: if a physical, sensory and neurological examination finds no specific problem, EEG and neuroimaging are usually not needed. More detailed neurological testing is warranted for epilepsy or unexplained loss of skills [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- ESCAP's basic genetic assessment includes:
  - a family history across generations
  - a physical examination
  - MECP2 testing
  - fragile X testing in males, and in females if family history or phenotype suggests it
  - PTEN analysis when other features suggest it [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Genetic causes can now be identified in 25–35% of autistic people, according to ESCAP [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- A consensus of 33 studies (21,698 patients) made chromosomal microarray (CMA) the first-tier cytogenetic test for unexplained developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism or multiple congenital anomalies. Its yield is 15–20%, compared with about 3% for a karyotype [[Miller2010CMA]].
- A 2019 meta-analysis (30 articles) found exome sequencing yield of 36% overall for neurodevelopmental disorders: 31% for isolated cases and 53% with associated conditions. The authors proposed exome sequencing as a first-tier test [[Srivastava2019]].
- In a population-based autism clinic sample (258 children), CMA gave a molecular diagnosis in 9.3% and exome sequencing in 8.4%. The combined yield was 15.8%, and higher in children with more physical anomalies [[Tammimies2015]].
- The ACMG 2013 guidance asks geneticists to confirm the autism diagnosis first, discuss testing options and yields with the family, coordinate with the medical home, and individualize the evaluation [[Schaefer2013]].
- No biomarker specific to autism diagnosis has been identified [[Hirota2023]].
- Why the workup matters: a genetic finding can inform family counselling, recurrence risk and screening for associated medical problems such as heart or brain conditions [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- The AAP report says primary-care clinicians should know the diagnostic criteria, the appropriate etiologic evaluation, and common co-occurring conditions, for example sleep, feeding, gastrointestinal symptoms, seizures, ADHD, anxiety and wandering [[Hyman2020]].

### Exam hooks
- The CDC 2022 milestones are set at the age by which ≥75% of children reach them. Missing one means screen, not diagnose [[Zubler2022]].
- The AAP recommends autism-specific screening at 18 and 24 months, plus surveillance at every visit [[Hyman2020]].
- The USPSTF 2016 gives an "I statement" (insufficient evidence) for screening children aged 18–30 months with no concerns raised. It is not a recommendation against screening [[Siu2016]].
- M-CHAT-R scoring: 0–2 low, 3–7 needs the follow-up interview, ≥8 refer directly [[Robins2014,Sturner2022]].
- The M-CHAT-R/F PPV for autism is 47.5%, and for any developmental concern 94.6% [[Robins2014]].
- In universal real-world screening, M-CHAT/F sensitivity was only 38.8% and PPV 14.6%. A negative screen does not rule out autism [[Guthrie2019]].
- About one third of autistic children lose skills (32.1%), at a mean of about 1.8 years [[Barger2013]].
- NICE: a child under 3 with language or social regression goes straight to the autism team. Motor regression at any age goes to a paediatrician or neurologist first [[NICE-CG128]].
- DSM-5-TR requires all 3 social-communication criteria (A1–A3) and at least 2 of 4 restricted or repetitive criteria (B1–B4) [[APA2022,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- DSM-5 severity levels 1–3 are rated separately for the two domains and describe the support needed [[APA2022]].
- ICD-11 code 6A02 is subdivided by intellectual development and functional language (6A02.0–6A02.5, plus .Y and .Z) [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Social (pragmatic) communication disorder = social communication difficulties without restricted or repetitive behaviours [[Norbury2014]].
- The ADOS is an observation and the ADI-R is a caregiver interview. Neither alone makes the diagnosis [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Cochrane summary accuracy: ADOS sensitivity 0.94 and specificity 0.80; ADI-R sensitivity 0.52 and specificity 0.84 [[Randall2018]].
- NICE targets the start of assessment within 3 months of referral [[NICE-CG128]].
- An EEG is indicated only when seizures or epilepsy are suspected [[NICE-CG128]].
- Chromosomal microarray yield is 15–20%, versus about 3% for a karyotype [[Miller2010CMA]].
- The NICE adult AQ-10 cutoff is 6 or more [[NICE-CG142]].
- The ADDM 2022 median age at diagnosis was 47 months [[Shaw2025]].
- The male-to-female ratio is about 3:1 in high-quality, actively screened studies, not 4:1 [[Loomes2017]].

### In plain words
- Babies grow skills step by step. Doctors use milestone lists that most children (3 in 4) reach by a given age [[Zubler2022]].
- Missing a milestone does not mean a child is autistic. It means it is a good time for a closer check [[Zubler2022]].
- Early signs can include not answering to their name, not pointing to show you things, few gestures, and slow speech [[Araujo2026,Hirota2023]].
- If your child loses words or social skills they used to have, tell a health professional right away [[NICE-CG128]].
- Learning two languages does not cause autism or extra language delay [[Hambly2012]].
- Screening questionnaires are a first step, not a diagnosis. A "positive" screen often means the child needs some kind of support, even if it is not autism [[Robins2014]].
- A "negative" screen does not rule autism out. If you are still worried, keep asking [[Guthrie2019,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Online autism quizzes measure traits. They cannot tell you if you are autistic, and stress or anxiety can raise scores [[Ashwood2016]].
- A diagnosis is made by a team who talk with the family, watch and play with the child, and look at the whole story [[NICE-CG128]].
- Autism can look quieter in many girls and women, who may hide differences to fit in. This can delay diagnosis [[Fuentes2021ESCAP,Hull2017]].
- Many adults are diagnosed later in life. It is never too late to ask for an assessment [[ONions2023,Russell2022]].
- Blood tests or brain scans cannot diagnose autism. Genetic tests can sometimes find a cause and help the family [[Hirota2023,Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Hearing should be checked, because a child who cannot hear well may not answer to their name [[NICE-CG128]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Universal toddler screening: the AAP recommends universal autism screening at 18 and 24 months [[Hyman2020]]; the USPSTF found insufficient evidence for children with no concerns [[Siu2016]]. Status: unresolved. Real-world sensitivity of 33–39% [[Guthrie2019,Carbone2020]] supports both sides: screening finds children earlier, but misses many.
- How accurate is the M-CHAT? A meta-analysis finds pooled sensitivity of 0.83 [[Wieckowski2023]]; universal cohorts with full follow-up find 0.33–0.39 [[Guthrie2019,Carbone2020]]. The difference comes mostly from how missed cases are counted. Status: real-world figures are the better guide for primary care.
- Should the follow-up interview be used at 18 months? Robins 2014 supports the two-stage M-CHAT-R/F [[Robins2014]]; Sturner 2022 finds it lowers sensitivity at 18 months with little PPV benefit, and suggests ordinal Q-CHAT-10 scoring [[Sturner2022]]. Status: Early evidence.
- How common is regression? Retrospective parent report gives about 30% [[Barger2013,Tan2021]]; prospective dimensional tracking suggests declining trajectories may be the rule (69–88%) [[Ozonoff2018Onset]]. Status: definitions differ; no standard measure.
- DSM-5 and people who lost or would lose a diagnosis: early estimates of about 40% not meeting criteria [[McPartland2012]] vs about 9% [[Huerta2012]]; later meta-analysis about 21% [[Kulage2020]]. Few of those lost qualify for SCD [[Kulage2020]]. Status: settled for counts; service access remains a concern [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]].
- Is SCD a valid separate disorder? Norbury suggests it may be a dimensional profile across conditions [[Norbury2014]]; Swineford describes an open research agenda [[Swineford2014]]. Status: Hypothesis.
- Do girls present differently, or are tools biased? Girls with high traits are less often diagnosed unless they have extra problems [[Dworzynski2012]], and girls with higher IQ are less likely to meet ADI-R criteria [[Ratto2018]]. Camouflaging research is still limited by measurement and sampling [[Cook2021Camouflaging]]. Status: Early evidence for both bias and real differences.
- AQ in adult referrals: NICE recommends the AQ-10 (cutoff 6) [[NICE-CG142]]; in a specialist clinic, the AQ did not predict diagnosis and missed 64% of autistic adults below the cutoff [[Ashwood2016]]. Status: use with caution; never as a gate to assessment on its own.
- Does adding the ADI-R to the ADOS help? Kim and Lord found better specificity with both [[Kim2012Lord]]; Cochrane found no gain in one study [[Randall2018]]. Status: depends on age and setting.
- ICD-11 and sensory features: the ESCAP guidance states that ICD-11, unlike DSM-5, does not include sensory hyper- or hyporeactivity [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]]. This could not be checked against the WHO text in this pass. Builders should not present it as settled.
- Genetic testing as first tier: ESCAP lists MECP2, fragile X and PTEN as basic tests [[Fuentes2021ESCAP]]; Srivastava proposes exome sequencing first [[Srivastava2019]]; NICE advises genetic testing only with dysmorphic features, anomalies or intellectual disability [[NICE-CG128]]. Status: practice varies by country and payer.
