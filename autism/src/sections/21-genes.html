Genes & heritability

Autism runs in families mostly through genes: many common variants of tiny effect, sometimes one rare variant of large effect. Follow the evidence from twins to DNA to one family tree, then see what a sibling's chance looks like and which genetic tests are offered.

Many autistic adults prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"); many families and clinicians use person-first language ("person with autism"). This guide uses both, with respect. A genetic finding explains a difference in development; it is not a fault and not anyone's blame.

Heritability is not inheritance

Heritability is a population statistic. It describes how much of the variation in autism across a population tracks with genetic differences. A heritability of about 80% does not mean that 80% of one person's autism is "genetic", and it does not mean the outcome is fixed. [[Sandin2017,Bai2019]]

Inheritance is about one family: which variant passed from which parent, or arose new. A variant can be strongly linked with autism and still not be inherited: de novo variants appear for the first time in the child. Rare de novo mutations explain only 2.6% of the population variance in liability, yet "contribute substantially to individual liability". [[Gaugler2014]]

Genetic tests and what they find

The ACMG 2013 guideline frames the genetic evaluation around confirming the autism diagnosis first, then discussing the options, yields and family investment before testing, recognising syndromic conditions and individualising the plan. [[Schaefer2013]] The European ESCAP guidance states that genetic causes can now be identified in 25–35% of autistic people. Why a result can matter: it can inform recurrence risk for relatives and medical care. PTEN is the gene of Cowden syndrome, a cancer syndrome, and tuberous sclerosis has organ-specific management including mTOR inhibitors. Where the evidence is still moving

Established

About 80%, and stable over time

Five countries, about 2 million people: median heritability 80.8% (95% CI 73.2–85.5%), maternal effect 0.4–1.6%. In Swedish twins born 1982–2008, heritability stayed at 0.88–0.97, so environmental change is "unlikely to explain" the rise in diagnoses. [[Bai2019,Taylor2020Twins]]

Outlier

The California twin study

Hallmayer 2011 (192 pairs) estimated 38% heritability and 58% shared environment. A twin meta-analysis judged large shared-environment estimates "likely a statistical artefact"; larger studies attribute most variance to genes. The same Swedish data gave 50% in 2014 and 83% in a 2017 reanalysis with standard models. [[Hallmayer2011,Tick2016,Sandin2014,Sandin2017]]

Early evidence

A female protective effect?

Siblings of autistic girls have higher autism rates than siblings of autistic boys; mothers of autistic children carry slightly more polygenic risk than fathers; autistic girls carry more rare-variant and polygenic load. Under-diagnosis of girls also inflates the male-to-female ratio, which is "closer to 3:1" than 4:1. [[Robinson2013,Wigdor2022,Antaki2022,Loomes2017]]

Hypothesis A 2026 study in human neural stem cells proposed one mechanism: ZFX, a gene on the X chromosome that is active on both X chromosomes in females, switches on many autism genes and may "buffer damaging mutations". It has not yet been tested in people. [[Andersen2026]]

Established

No single "autism gene"

Most heritable liability sits in common variants of tiny effect; in one person, autism usually reflects many such variants, sometimes with one rare variant of larger effect, plus environmental contributions. Risk genes are expressed early in brain development, in excitatory and inhibitory neuron lineages. See The developing brain. [[Gaugler2014,Satterstrom2020,Antaki2022]]

Early evidence

Moderate-risk inherited genes

In 42,607 autism cases, rare inherited loss-of-function variants in NAV3 carried a relative risk of about 4. People with variants in moderate-risk genes had less cognitive impairment than those with variants in highly penetrant genes such as CHD8, SCN2A, ADNP, FOXP1 and SHANK3 (59% vs 88%). [[Zhou2022]]

Established

Syndromes, one by one

Tuberous sclerosis is autosomal dominant (TSC1 or TSC2, overactive mTOR); 28 of 50 children assessed in depth were autistic. About 25% of people with PTEN mutations show autism characteristics. In a 14-person series, every maternally derived 15q11.2–q13 duplication tested was on the autism spectrum (early evidence). [[Henske2016,Mitchell2025TSC,Cummings2022PTEN,Urraca2013]]

Prenatal and other non-genetic factors, and what does not cause autism, are in Risk factors & myths.
