Life beyond the numbers

Identity

What a late diagnosis can mean

Nine adults diagnosed after 50 had been treated for anxiety and depression, had felt isolated and "alien" growing up, and saw the diagnosis as positive: a "reconfiguration of self". [[Stagg2019]] Fourteen late-diagnosed women described "pretending to be normal" and professionals missing their autism because of their gender. [[Bargiela2016]] A scoping review found strong emotions after diagnosis, unclear routes to it and few services afterwards. [[Huang2020]]

Work

Strengths at work, without stereotypes

A systematic review found no strong evidence for or against a workplace "autism advantage" such as attention to detail, and warned against stereotypes that may not fit the person. [[Bury2020]] What does help: job matching and support. In the Project SEARCH plus ASD Supports trial (49 students aged 18–21), 90% had competitive jobs 3 months after graduation against 6% of controls, and 87% kept them at 12 months. Early evidence [[Wehman2017]] Employment predicted higher quality of life in 370 UK autistic adults. [[Mason2018QoL]]

Study

University

Evidence on university support is thin: a review found 20 articles describing just 69 autistic students, only two of them experimental. [[Gelbar2014]] Fourteen people who had left university named late or difficult diagnosis, lack of understanding, mental health, culture shock and no proactive support; leaving often felt traumatic and shameful. [[Cage2020]]

Relationships

Love, sex and parenthood

In 229 autistic adults, 73% had relationship experience and only 7% had no wish for one; those with an autistic partner were more satisfied. [[Strunz2017]] Autistic adults had less sexual knowledge and more sexual victimisation than non-autistic adults. [[BrownLavoie2014]] Autistic mothers more often felt misunderstood by professionals and isolated, while putting their child first, and described motherhood as joyful and intensely connected. [[Pohl2020,Dugdale2021]]

Safety

Victims more often than offenders

A systematic review found autistic people do not appear over-represented in the criminal justice system. [[King2014CJS]] About 20% of US autistic youth had been stopped and questioned by police by 21 and nearly 5% arrested. [[Rava2017]] Autistic adults reported more childhood and adult victimisation than matched non-autistic adults, with no difference in perpetration. [[Weiss2018Victimization]] Only 37% of 394 police officers in England and Wales had autism training. [[Crane2016Police]]

Ageing

Growing older

In 4,685 autistic Medicare enrollees aged 65 and over, nearly all physical health conditions were more common than in matched controls, including Parkinson's disease (OR 6.1). [[Hand2020Medicare]] In adults aged 30–64, 5-year dementia prevalence was 4.04% with autism only against 0.97% in the general population (claims data). Early evidence [[Vivanti2021]] Cross-sectional tests found age-related cognitive differences parallel or smaller, not larger. Hypothesis [[Lever2016]] These are group statistics, not forecasts for any one person.

Mental health and suicide risk

Autistic adults have higher rates of anxiety, depression and suicidal thoughts, and most of the extra risk travels with co-occurring mental health conditions that can be recognised and treated. In Denmark, autistic people had a higher suicide rate (adjusted IRR 3.75), and 90.6% of those who died by suicide had at least one other diagnosis. [[Kolves2021]] In a Swedish cohort, the suicide odds ratio was 9.40 in autism without intellectual disability. [[Hirvikoski2016]] Of 374 adults newly diagnosed at a UK clinic, 66% reported lifetime suicidal thoughts, and depression was an important risk factor. [[Cassidy2014]] These are population statistics. Camouflaging and burnout are being studied as possible pathways. Early evidence [[Cassidy2023Camouflaging,Raymaker2020]]

If you are strugglingIf you or someone you support is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number now. If thoughts of suicide are building, tell someone you trust and contact a GP or doctor, or a crisis line in your country. Many crisis lines offer text or online chat, which some autistic people find easier than a phone call.
