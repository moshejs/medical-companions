Part 1 · Understanding autism

Words, identity & neurodiversity

How we talk about autism shapes how autistic people are seen and supported. This module covers identity-first and person-first language, the double empathy problem, masking, and the models behind the words.

This page says

Many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"); many families and clinicians use person-first language ("person with autism"). Neither is wrong, and the respectful thing is to ask what each person prefers. Switch the wording here and every highlighted phrase on this page, including the animations, rewrites itself. [[Kenny2016,Keating2023,Schuck2025]]

What do people prefer?

In the UK, a survey of 3,470 people found that "autism" and "on the autism spectrum" were the most widely endorsed terms. "Autistic" was endorsed by many autistic adults, family members and parents but by fewer professionals; "person with autism" by almost half of professionals but fewer autistic adults and parents. Even so, about 40% of autistic respondents did not endorse "autistic", and over 50% did not endorse "autistic person". [[Kenny2016,Keating2023]]

In the US, autistic adults (n = 299) "overwhelmingly" preferred identity-first terms, while professionals (n = 207) were more likely to use person-first terms. A global survey of 654 autistic adults consistently favoured "autism", "autistic person", "is autistic" and "neurotypical people". In Australia, people with a stronger autistic identity preferred identity-first terms, while people who had experienced and internalised more stigma found them less preferable. [[Taboas2023,Keating2023,Bury2023]]

Ranges are across studies, not one survey. 10 of 14 studies found more participants preferred identity-first language; the 2 studies favouring person-first were in Dutch; people with intellectual disability were under-represented. The authors found "no consensus" and advise respecting individual preference. [[Schuck2025,BottemaBeutel2021Ableist,Keating2023]]

Person-first language is used more often in scholarly writing for children with disabilities than for children without, and most for the most stigmatised conditions, so it "may actually accentuate stigma". Early evidence [[Gernsbacher2017]]

Masking: the energy cost of a day

Camouflaging, often called masking, means hiding or compensating for autistic traits to fit in. In interviews with 92 autistic adults, its motivations were fitting in and connecting with others, its techniques were masking and compensation, and its consequences included exhaustion, challenging stereotypes and threats to self-perception. Early evidence

What the research shows

A review of 29 studies found three preliminary patterns: adults with more autistic traits camouflage more, there are sex and gender differences, and more self-reported camouflaging goes with worse mental health. Samples were not representative of the whole autistic community. [[Cook2021Camouflaging]]

Gender

Women and girls

Autistic women reported more camouflaging than autistic men, with no gender difference among non-autistic adults; measured as the gap between outward presentation and inner traits, the difference was large (Cohen's d 0.98), with wide variation in both groups. This is one proposed reason girls are recognised later. [[Hull2020Gender,Lai2017Camouflaging]]

Debate

Is "camouflaging" one thing?

Fombonne questions the construct's validity and its use to explain late diagnosis and a "female autism phenotype". Lai and colleagues reply that better measurement is warranted, that subjective experience matters for adult diagnosis, and that camouflaging "is not necessarily a feature of autism in female individuals". [[Fombonne2020Camouflage,Lai2021Commentary]]

Research tool

The CAT-Q

The Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire has 25 items in three factors (compensation, masking, assimilation), with internal consistency α = 0.94. It is a research measure, not a diagnostic test. [[Hull2019CATQ]]

Three ways of seeing autism

Medical model

A disorder in the person

Describes autism as a set of deficits to diagnose and treat, as in DSM-5-TR's "autism spectrum disorder". The neurodiversity paradigm criticises it for an overfocus on deficits, for locating difficulties in the individual rather than their context, and for a narrow perspective. [[APA2022,Pellicano2022]]

Social model

Barriers in the surroundings

Looks at the context: noise, rules, attitudes and services that disable people. Some behaviour analysts now call for their field to adopt the social model of disability and the neurodiversity paradigm, and to change practice to match. [[Mathur2024,Pellicano2022]]

Neurodiversity

A natural variation in minds

Treats autism as one form of variation in a diversity of minds, while still supporting people with their disabilities. Identifying as autistic and knowing about neurodiversity went with seeing autism as an identity "that needs no cure", yet groups overlapped in recognising its difficulties, a "deficit-as-difference" view. Early evidence [[Pellicano2022,denHouting2019,Kapp2013]]

The idea of neurodiversity is often credited to the Australian sociologist Judy Singer in the late 1990s. Botha and colleagues show that "neurological diversity" was in use earlier and conclude the concept was "collectively developed by neurodivergent people". [[Botha2024]] The 2014 Lancet seminar on autism ends: "creation of a supportive environment that accepts and respects that the individual is different is crucial". [[Lai2014]]

Symbols

The puzzle piece

In 400 members of the public, puzzle pieces, including autism puzzle-piece logos, evoked negative implicit and explicit associations: "incompleteness, imperfection, and oddity". [[Gernsbacher2018Puzzle]] This guide's own logo is a gold infinity loop, not a puzzle piece.

Debate

"Profound autism"

The 2021 Lancet Commission, written with autistic people and parents, proposed "profound autism" as an administrative term for autistic people who need 24-hour access to an adult to stay safe. Applying a working definition, 26.7% of autistic 8-year-olds in ADDM (2000–2016) met it. A 2026 Delphi study reached 76% agreement on a research definition; a preprint (not peer reviewed) found no evidence it is a biologically distinct group. [[Lord2022Lancet,Yu2022,Hughes2023,Siegel2026,Eicher2026]]

In practice

Ask, and follow the person

Surveys from the UK, US, Australia and across the English-speaking world agree on one thing: preferences differ, so respect the person's own choice. That includes children and adults with intellectual disability, who are under-represented in the surveys.
