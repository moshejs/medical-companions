The sensory world

Four patterns, two questions: Dunn's model

Dunn's model crosses two questions. How much input does it take before the nervous system notices (a high or low threshold)? And does the person act on it or let it happen (an active or passive strategy)? The answers give four patterns. Established model [[Dunn2001]]

Every person has some amount of each pattern. The model describes preferences across the general population from birth to 90 years; it is not a disorder in itself. [[Dunn2001]] One autistic person can be over-responsive to sound and under-responsive to pain on the same day.

Words: many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"), while many families and clinicians use person-first language ("person with autism"). A UK survey of 3,470 community members found no single term that everyone accepted, so this guide uses both. [[Kenny2016]]

What people often get wrong

Early evidence

A meltdown is not a tantrum

Autistic informants describe meltdowns as responses to overload that feel out of their control, not as chosen behaviour. [[Phung2021,Lewis2023Meltdown]] Adults described extreme emotion, trouble thinking and remembering, a sense of "not being themselves", and then a release of pent-up emotion. [[Lewis2023Meltdown]]

What helped, in their words: compassion and collaboration from a trusted adult, fewer demands, and space. Demands such as eye contact during distress made things worse. [[Phung2021]] Many adults try to get away from triggers or be alone when a meltdown is coming. [[Lewis2023Meltdown]]

Early evidence

Shutdown is the quiet version

Autistic youth described shutdown or inertia as "feeling frozen". Shutdowns are more inward, with withdrawal from the surroundings, and range from mild to severe. [[Phung2021]] Because a shutdown is quiet, it is easy to miss or to read as defiance or "not trying".

Early evidence

Stimming: leave harmless stims alone

Stimming means repetitive movements or sounds, such as rocking, hand-flapping or humming. In interviews with 32 autistic adults, stimming served as self-regulation, to soothe or to express intense emotion, and participants objected to treatment that aims to eliminate it. [[Kapp2019]] A stim that causes injury is a different matter for the person and their clinician: see Co-occurring conditions.

Established

How common are sensory differences?

  • 95% of 281 autistic children aged 3–6 showed some difference on the Short Sensory Profile. [[Tomchek2007]]
  • Over 90% of autistic children had differences across several senses on the DISCO interview, persisting across age and ability. [[Leekam2007]]
  • 94.4% of autistic adults had an extreme score in at least one sensory quadrant, with striking variability between people. [[Crane2009]]
  • 92% of autistic 10–14-year-olds, but also 67% of non-autistic children with special educational needs: common, not unique to autism. [[Green2016DSM5]]

These are group statistics from research samples.

Established

Ben-Sasson's meta-analyses

The 2009 meta-analysis (14 studies) found the largest autistic–typical difference in under-responsivity, then over-responsivity, then sensation seeking, with the biggest differences at ages 6–9. [[BenSasson2009]]

The 2019 update (55 questionnaire studies, 4,606 autistic people) found large effects against typical controls for all three. Only over-responsivity also exceeded every other clinical comparison group. [[BenSasson2019]]

Early evidence

Interoception and pain

Interoception is sensing the body's inner state: heartbeat, hunger, needing the toilet. A Bayesian meta-analysis (15 studies) found lower heartbeat-counting performance (g = −0.333) with higher confidence (g = 0.430) in autistic people, and no difference on other measures. [[Williams2023Interoception]] A 2025 meta-analysis of five adult studies found no significant difference, so the findings conflict. [[Klein2025]]

Alexithymia (difficulty identifying and describing one's own feelings) was found in 49.93% of autistic participants against 4.89% of non-autistic participants [[Kinnaird2019]], and it, rather than autism itself, mediated the link between interoception and empathy in one study. [[Mul2018]] Seeming indifferent to pain or temperature is one DSM-5-TR example of criterion B4 [[APA2022]], and pain can show up as a change in behaviour, so it is worth checking. [[McElhanon2014]]

Early evidence

What happens in the brain?

Sensory differences have been measured with EEG, MEG and fMRI in hearing, touch and vision, including differences in combining the senses. Established that differences exist; the mechanisms are open. [[Marco2011]] Over-responsive youth showed stronger primary-sensory and amygdala responses that did not habituate. [[Green2015SOR]] A cortex-wide gene-expression study found its largest changes in primary visual cortex, a sensory area. [[Gandal2022]] More in The developing brain.
