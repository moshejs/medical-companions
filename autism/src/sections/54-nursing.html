Pain when words are few: an observational walkthrough

Autistic children do feel and show pain: during venepuncture, 21 autistic children aged 3–7 showed a strong facial pain reaction, larger than non-autistic children's, and parents' global reports matched observed pain less well. [[Nader2004]] Observers judged pain from facial activity. [[Messmer2008]] Observational tools help. Below, the five categories of the FLACC scale (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability), each scored 0–2, paraphrased from the published tool. [[Merkel1997]] The revised r-FLACC lets parents add the person's own pain behaviours; in 52 children with cognitive impairment, inter-rater ICCs were 0.76–0.90 and scores fell from 6.1 to 1.9 after analgesia. [[Malviya2006]]

Teaching toolPractise the logic here; in practice use your organisation's approved tool, observe against the person's usual behaviour, and act on clinical judgement and local policy, not on a score alone.

Parent adds (r-FLACC)

Another tool, the Non-communicating Children's Pain Checklist–Revised, was tested in 71 children aged 3–18 with severe cognitive impairment; a score of 7 or more suggested pain (sensitivity 84%, specificity up to 77%). [[Breau2002]]

On the ward: a checklist

AreaWhat to doWhy (evidence)
Assessment & documentationRead and update the hospital passport or accommodations report: communication, sensory needs, allergies, risks, needed adjustments, what upsets and what settles. Ask the family what the person's usual behaviour looks like, and record pain with an observational tool.A review of 60 UK passports found wide variation; some omitted allergies, risk assessment or reasonable adjustments. [[Northway2017]] The AASPIRE toolkit's accommodations report was read by 97% of providers. [[Nicolaidis2016]] Global parent report alone may miss pain. [[Nader2004]]
MedicinesAsk how the person usually takes medicines: liquid or tablet, which drink, what time, from whom. Keep routines where safe. Plan premedication for procedures with the prescriber and family.Perioperative reviews describe disguising bitter medicine in a favourite drink, and premedication with alpha-2 agonists (clonidine, dexmedetomidine), midazolam or ketamine. [[Taghizadeh2015]] Autistic children more often had non-standard premedication types and routes, with similar complications, pain and recovery times. [[Arnold2015]]
Restrictive practicePrevention first: the adaptations in the animation aim to make restraint unnecessary. If any restrictive intervention is considered, follow your organisation's policy and national guidance, involve the family, and debrief afterwards.In one US paediatric ED, physical restraint was used for 14.1% of autistic patients needing psychiatric consultation against 6.0% of non-autistic ones (adjusted OR 2.28). [[McGaughey2026]] In another, autism was independently linked to restraint (adjusted OR 7.25). [[Rolison2026]] Whether the gap grows with age is untested. Hypothesis [[Hira2026]]
TrainingComplete role-appropriate autism training; in England this is the Oliver McGowan Mandatory Training. Know where your organisation's autism guidance is.The Oliver McGowan programme is NHS England's standardised training on learning disability and autism; a Tier 1 pilot improved health students' confidence in communicating with autistic people. [[Bashir2026]] 39.5% of 304 UK GPs had never had formal autism training. [[Unigwe2017]] Reviews call for staff training and a guideline at every institution. [[Taghizadeh2015]]

Crisis

Meltdown: de-escalation steps

  1. Treat it as overload, not defiance: autistic adults describe meltdowns as being overwhelmed by sensory, information and social-emotional load. [[Lewis2023Meltdown]]
  2. Lower the load: fewer people, less noise and light, offer a quieter space. [[MacLennan2023]]
  3. Fewer words, calm voice, no demands such as eye contact. [[Phung2021]]
  4. Give room and time; many people try to get away or be alone to avoid harm. [[Lewis2023Meltdown]]
  5. Ask the family what helps; then plan to prevent the next one. [[Taghizadeh2015]]
Communication

Adapting how you talk

"Respect the way I need to communicate with you": autistic adults asked for written communication, time to process and providers who do not make wrong assumptions. [[Nicolaidis2015]] In an international survey, 72% of autistic adults found it hard to decide whether symptoms warranted a visit and 53% found it hard to communicate with the doctor. [[Doherty2022]] For people who speak little, offer their own AAC: see Communication, AAC & therapies.

Going home

Discharge teaching

Give the plan in writing, with pictures if helpful: what happened, what to watch for, medicines, who to call and when. Update the passport with what worked today, so the next visit starts there. [[Northway2017,Nicolaidis2016]] Emergency-department passports were rated helpful and good for communication by staff and families. [[Heifetz2018]]

Emergency

The emergency department

13% of ED visits by autistic children were for a psychiatric problem against 2% in other children (OR 9.13). [[Kalb2012ED]] Autistic adolescents used the ED four times as often. [[Liu2017ED]] In autistic adults, 15% of ED visits were psychiatric against 4.2%. [[Vohra2016]] A crisis-prevention model (START) was followed by fewer hospitalisations and ED visits (pre/post, no control). [[Kalb2019START]]

Theatre

Anaesthesia and sedation

The anaesthesia literature on autism is mostly case reports; knowing each patient's behaviour and reducing stress are the main recommendations. [[Vlassakova2016]] A five-session TEACCH-based programme increased compliance with a dental assessment in children and adults. [[Orellana2014]] A social-script iPad app before imaging was linked to fewer externalised behaviours (effect size 0.56). [[Johnson2014iPad]]

Families

Families as experts

Parents are their child's experts on what agitates and settles them; perioperative reviews recommend talking with them before the day. [[Taghizadeh2015]] With adults, agree how information is shared with family, and remember that adults make their own decisions. The AAP frames care as shared decision-making with families.
