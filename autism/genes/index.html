Genes & heritability

Autism runs in families mostly through genes: many common variants of tiny effect, sometimes one rare variant of large effect. Follow the evidence from twins to DNA to one family tree, then see what a sibling's chance looks like and which genetic tests are offered.

Many autistic adults prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"); many families and clinicians use person-first language ("person with autism"). This guide uses both, with respect. A genetic finding explains a difference in development; it is not a fault and not anyone's blame.

Panel 1 · twins

Twins who share all or half their genes

Identical (monozygotic, MZ) twins share essentially all their DNA; non-identical (dizygotic, DZ) twins share about half, like other siblings. In a California study of 192 twin pairs, if one male twin was autistic the other was too in 77% of MZ pairs (95% CI 65–86%) against 31% of DZ pairs (16–46%); in female twins the figures were 50% and 36%. A meta-analysis of twin studies found an MZ correlation of 0.98 (0.96–0.99) against 0.53 (0.44–0.60) for DZ twins at an assumed 5% prevalence. The first twin study of autism, in 1977, examined 21 pairs. 150,197,589

In plain wordsWhen one identical twin is autistic, the other usually is too. For non-identical twins it happens much less often. That gap points to genes.
Exam hookMZ concordance much higher than DZ = genetic influence. Twin meta-analysis: MZ correlation 0.98, heritability 64–91%. 589

Heritability is not inheritance

Heritability is a population statistic. It describes how much of the variation in autism across a population tracks with genetic differences. A heritability of about 80% does not mean that 80% of one person's autism is "genetic", and it does not mean the outcome is fixed. 34,514

Inheritance is about one family: which variant passed from which parent, or arose new. A variant can be strongly linked with autism and still not be inherited: de novo variants appear for the first time in the child. Rare de novo mutations explain only 2.6% of the population variance in liability, yet "contribute substantially to individual liability". 167

Where the evidence is still moving

Established

About 80%, and stable over time

Five countries, about 2 million people: median heritability 80.8% (95% CI 73.2–85.5%), maternal effect 0.4–1.6%. In Swedish twins born 1982–2008, heritability stayed at 0.88–0.97, so environmental change is "unlikely to explain" the rise in diagnoses. 34,587

Outlier

The California twin study

Hallmayer 2011 (192 pairs) estimated 38% heritability and 58% shared environment. A twin meta-analysis judged large shared-environment estimates "likely a statistical artefact"; larger studies attribute most variance to genes. The same Swedish data gave 50% in 2014 and 83% in a 2017 reanalysis with standard models. 197,512,514,589

Early evidence

A female protective effect?

Siblings of autistic girls have higher autism rates than siblings of autistic boys; mothers of autistic children carry slightly more polygenic risk than fathers; autistic girls carry more rare-variant and polygenic load. Under-diagnosis of girls also inflates the male-to-female ratio, which is "closer to 3:1" than 4:1. 20,332,484,630

Established

No single "autism gene"

Most heritable liability sits in common variants of tiny effect; in one person, autism usually reflects many such variants, sometimes with one rare variant of larger effect, plus environmental contributions. Risk genes are expressed early in brain development, in excitatory and inhibitory neuron lineages. See The developing brain. 20,167,520

Early evidence

Moderate-risk inherited genes

In 42,607 autism cases, rare inherited loss-of-function variants in NAV3 carried a relative risk of about 4. People with variants in moderate-risk genes had less cognitive impairment than those with variants in highly penetrant genes such as CHD8, SCN2A, ADNP, FOXP1 and SHANK3 (59% vs 88%). 651

Established

Syndromes, one by one

Tuberous sclerosis is autosomal dominant (TSC1 or TSC2, overactive mTOR); 28 of 50 children assessed in depth were autistic. About 25% of people with PTEN mutations show autism characteristics. In a 14-person series, every maternally derived 15q11.2–q13 duplication tested was on the autism spectrum (early evidence). 104,218,385,596
