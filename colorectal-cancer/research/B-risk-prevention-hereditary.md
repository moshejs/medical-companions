## Findings

### risk · Age and population context
- Colorectum was the third most common cancer site worldwide in 2022: 9.6% of all new cancers. It was the second leading cause of cancer death: 9.3% of cancer deaths [[Bray2024]].
- In the US, CRC is most often diagnosed at ages 65–74. An estimated 10.5% of new cases occur in people younger than 50 [[USPSTF2021]].
- About 153,020 US diagnoses and 52,550 deaths were projected for 2023. Of these, 19,550 cases and 3,750 deaths were in people younger than 50 [[Siegel2023CRC]].
- The share of US cases diagnosed before age 55 rose from 11% in 1995 to 20% in 2019 (Established) [[Siegel2023CRC]].
- Incidence in people younger than 55 has risen 1–2% per year since the mid-1990s, while overall incidence fell 1% per year in 2011–2019 [[Siegel2023CRC]].
- Ageing, high-income diets, obesity, physical inactivity and smoking are named as the main drivers of CRC burden [[Dekker2019]].

### risk · Family history (no known syndrome)
- Pooled RR with at least one affected first-degree relative (FDR) was 2.24 (95% CI 2.06–2.43; 47 studies). With at least two affected relatives it was 3.97 (2.60–6.06) [[Butterworth2006]].
- Butterworth's life-table conversion: a population lifetime risk of 1.8% for a 50-year-old rises to 3.4% (2.8–4.0) with one or more affected relatives and to 6.9% (4.5–10.4) with two or more [[Butterworth2006]].
- Johns and Houlston pooled RRs (27 studies):
  - any FDR with CRC: 2.25 (2.00–2.53)
  - affected parent: 2.26 (1.87–2.72)
  - affected sibling: 2.57 (2.19–3.02)
  - more than one affected relative: 4.25 (3.01–6.08)
  - relative diagnosed before 45: 3.87 (2.40–6.22)
  - FDR with an adenoma: 1.99 (1.55–2.55) [[Johns2001]]
- The familial RR is higher for colon cancer in the relative (2.42; 2.20–2.65) than for rectal cancer (1.89; 1.62–2.21) [[Johns2001]].
- A 2019 meta-analysis found lower RRs, especially in cohort studies [[Roos2019]]:
  - one FDR: 1.92 (1.53–2.41) in case-control studies; 1.37 (0.76–2.46) in cohorts
  - two or more FDRs: 2.81 (1.73–4.55) case-control; 2.40 (1.76–3.28) cohort
  - FDR diagnosed before 50: 3.26 (2.82–3.77) in cohorts
- Roos 2019 cumulative absolute risk to age 85 in Western Europe: 4.8% (2.7–8.3) with one FDR, 8.2% (6.1–10.9) with two or more FDRs, and 11% (9.5–12.4) with an FDR diagnosed before 50 [[Roos2019]].
- The family-history RR falls as the person at risk gets older (9.28 million people, 63 studies) [[Wong2018]]:
  - overall: 1.76 (1.57–1.97)
  - age <40: 3.29 (1.67–6.49); age ≥40: 1.42 (1.24–1.62)
  - age <50: 2.81 (1.94–4.07); age ≥50: 1.47 (1.28–1.69)
- After age 55 (PLCO trial, 144,768 participants), family history carried a smaller excess [[Schoen2015]]:
  - one FDR: HR 1.23 (1.07–1.42)
  - two or more FDRs: HR 2.04 (1.44–2.86)
  - the age of the affected FDR made no difference (p trend 0.59)
- In PLCO, 10.3% of participants aged 55–74 reported a family history of CRC [[Schoen2015]].
- Utah population genealogy (2.3 million people): familial RR 2.05 (1.96–2.14) with one or more affected FDRs. Second- and third-degree relatives alone added little: the only significant constellation was 1 SDR plus 2 TDRs, FRR 1.33 [[Taylor2010]].
- In the same data, 1 FDR plus 1 SDR plus 3 or more TDRs gave FRR 3.28 (2.44–4.31) [[Taylor2010]].
- One affected FDR roughly doubles risk. Screening adherence among people with a family history is below 50% [[Lowery2016]].
- Nordic twin registries (44,788 twin pairs) put the heritable share of CRC at 35% (95% CI 10–48%) (Established) [[Lichtenstein2000]].
- About 35% of CRC risk is attributed to heritable factors, and about 29% of the UK population has a family history of CRC in an FDR or SDR [[Monahan2020]].

### risk · Inflammatory bowel disease
- Ulcerative colitis, Denmark and Sweden 1969–2017 (96,447 patients vs 949,207 matched references) [[Olen2020UC]]:
  - incident CRC 1.29 vs 0.82 per 1000 person-years: HR 1.66 (1.57–1.76)
  - CRC death: HR 1.59 (1.46–1.72)
- The UC excess has fallen over time. In 2013–2017 (Sweden), HR for incident CRC was 1.38 (1.20–1.60), or 1 extra case per 1058 patients per 5 years. HR for CRC death was 1.25 (1.03–1.51), or 1 extra death per 3041 patients per 5 years [[Olen2020UC]].
- People with UC were diagnosed at a less advanced stage, but after adjusting for stage they still had a higher risk of CRC death (HR 1.54; 1.33–1.78) [[Olen2020UC]].
- Crohn's disease (47,035 patients) [[Olen2020CD]]:
  - incident CRC 0.82 vs 0.64 per 1000 person-years: adjusted HR 1.40 (1.27–1.53)
  - CRC death: HR 1.74 (1.54–1.96)
  - among those eligible for surveillance, excess risk was confined to Crohn's diagnosed before 40, colon-only disease, or primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)
- Older meta-analysis of UC (116 studies), as context for the decline [[Eaden2001]]:
  - cumulative probability 2% at 10 years, 8% at 20 years and 18% at 30 years of colitis
  - incidence 2, 7 and 12 per 1000 patient-years in decades 1, 2 and 3

### risk · Red and processed meat
- IARC (Monograph 114) classified processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) and red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A) (Established) [[Bouvard2015]].
- Chan 2011, prospective studies [[Chan2011]]:
  - red and processed meat, highest vs lowest intake: RR 1.22 (1.11–1.34)
  - per 100 g/day: RR 1.14 (1.04–1.24)
  - the curve is roughly linear up to about 140 g/day, then flattens
- Separately: fresh red meat RR 1.17 (1.05–1.31) per 100 g/day, and processed meat RR 1.18 (1.10–1.28) per 50 g/day [[Chan2011]].
- WCRF/AICR Continuous Update Project (111 cohorts): CRC risk rose 12% per 100 g/day of red and processed meat (95% CI 4–21%) [[Vieira2017]].

### risk · Alcohol and smoking
- Alcohol, 61 studies (moderate = 2–3 drinks/day; heavy = 4 or more) [[Fedirko2011]]:
  - moderate vs non-drinkers: RR 1.21 (1.13–1.28)
  - heavy: RR 1.52 (1.27–1.81)
  - dose-response: RR 1.07 at 10 g/day, 1.38 at 50 g/day and 1.82 at 100 g/day of ethanol
- WCRF/AICR: CRC risk rose 7% per 10 g/day of ethanol (5–9%) [[Vieira2017]].
- Smoking, ever vs never [[Botteri2008]]:
  - incidence RR 1.18 (1.11–1.25), or 10.8 extra cases per 100,000 person-years
  - mortality RR 1.25 (1.14–1.37)
  - significant only after 30 years of smoking, and stronger for rectal cancer
- In prospective studies, CRC incidence rose with pack-years: RR 1.51 for an increase of 60 pack-years [[Liang2009]].

### risk · Body fatness, diabetes and physical activity
- Obese vs normal BMI: RR 1.33 (1.25–1.42; 41 studies). Highest vs lowest waist circumference: RR 1.46 (1.33–1.60; 13 studies) [[Ma2013]].
- WCRF/AICR dose-response (7.4 million participants) [[Abar2018]]:
  - per 5 kg/m² BMI: RR 1.06 (1.04–1.07)
  - per 10 cm waist: RR 1.02 (1.02–1.03)
  - per 5 cm height: RR 1.04 (1.02–1.05)
  - the BMI association is stronger in men
- Diabetes, 30 cohorts: incidence SRR 1.27 (1.21–1.34); mortality SRR 1.20 (1.03–1.40) [[Jiang2011]].
- An earlier diabetes meta-analysis gave a similar RR of 1.30 (1.20–1.40), alike in women (1.33) and men (1.29) [[Larsson2005]].
- Sweden: diabetes diagnosed before 50 was linked to a 1.9-fold risk of CRC before 50. Combined with a family history, the risk was 6.9-fold (4.1–12) [[AliKhan2020]].
- Most vs least physically active, colon cancer: RR 0.76 (0.72–0.81; 52 studies). The cohort-only estimate was 0.83 (0.78–0.88) [[Wolin2009]].

### risk · Fibre, whole grains, dairy and calcium
- Fibre and whole grains [[Aune2011]]:
  - per 10 g/day total fibre: RR 0.90 (0.86–0.94)
  - per 10 g/day cereal fibre: RR 0.90 (0.83–0.97)
  - per 3 servings/day of whole grains: RR 0.83 (0.78–0.89)
- WCRF/AICR: per 90 g/day whole grains, RR 0.83 (95% CI 11–21% lower). Per 400 g/day dairy, RR 0.87 (10–17% lower). Fruit, coffee, tea, cheese, poultry and legumes showed no association [[Vieira2017]].
- Dairy: per 400 g/day total dairy, RR 0.83 (0.78–0.88); per 200 g/day milk, RR 0.91; per 50 g/day cheese, RR 0.96 (0.83–1.12, not significant) [[Aune2012]].
- Calcium: per 300 mg/day total calcium, RR 0.92 (0.89–0.95). Per 300 mg/day supplemental calcium, RR 0.91 (0.86–0.98) [[Keum2014]].

### risk · Population attributable fractions
- US 2014: 42.0% of all cancers and 45.1% of cancer deaths in adults 30 and older were attributable to evaluated modifiable factors. Colorectal cancer had the second-highest number of attributable cases (76,910) and deaths (28,290) [[Islami2018]].
- US 2019 update: 40.0% of cancers were attributable overall. For CRC, 78,440 attributable cases and 25,800 attributable deaths. CRC had the largest number of attributable deaths after lung cancer [[Islami2024]].

### risk · Prevention trials and chemoprevention
- Aspirin, 20-year follow-up of four trials (14,033 patients, mean 6 years of treatment) [[Rothwell2010]]:
  - colon cancer incidence HR 0.76 (0.60–0.96); mortality HR 0.65 (0.48–0.88)
  - no effect on rectal cancer overall
  - 5 years or more of aspirin cut proximal colon cancer by about 70% (incidence HR 0.35)
- Doses above 75 mg/day gave no extra benefit. Five years of 75–300 mg/day lowered the 20-year risk of fatal CRC by 1.76 percentage points (0.61–2.91) [[Rothwell2010]].
- Daily aspirin reduced cancer death (OR 0.79; 0.68–0.92; 8 trials). The benefit appeared only after 5 years (all cancers HR 0.66; GI cancers HR 0.46) [[Rothwell2011]].
- In six primary-prevention trials of low-dose aspirin, cancer incidence was lower from year 3 onward (OR 0.76; 0.66–0.88). The higher bleeding risk early on faded over time [[Rothwell2012]].
- Nurses' Health Study (82,911 women) [[Chan2005]]:
  - regular aspirin: RR 0.77 (0.67–0.88), significant only after more than 10 years of use
  - more than 14 tablets per week for over 10 years: RR 0.47 (0.31–0.71)
  - major GI bleeding rose with dose, from 0.77 to 1.57 per 1000 person-years
- Adenoma-prevention RCT (1121 people with prior adenomas) [[Baron2003]]:
  - any adenoma: 47% with placebo, 38% with 81 mg aspirin, 45% with 325 mg
  - RR 0.81 (0.69–0.96) for 81 mg
  - advanced neoplasms: RR 0.59 (0.38–0.92) for 81 mg
- Modelled for average-risk people aged 50–65 taking aspirin for 10 years: 7% (women) to 9% (men) fewer cancer, MI or stroke events over 15 years, and 4% fewer deaths over 20 years. Bleeding is the main harm [[Cuzick2015]].
- ASPREE (19,114 people aged 70 and older, 100 mg/day, median 4.7 years) [[McNeil2018]]:
  - all-cause death: HR 1.14 (1.01–1.29)
  - cancer death: 3.1% vs 2.3%, HR 1.31 (1.10–1.56)
- In ASPREE, incident cancer did not differ (HR 1.04; 0.95–1.14). But aspirin was linked to more metastatic cancer (HR 1.19) and stage 4 cancer at diagnosis (HR 1.22), suggesting caution in older people (Early evidence) [[McNeil2021]].
- USPSTF 2016 gave a B recommendation for low-dose aspirin to prevent CVD and CRC in adults 50–59 with 10-year CVD risk of 10% or more, low bleeding risk, and willingness to take it for 10 or more years [[BibbinsDomingo2016]].
- USPSTF 2022 recast aspirin as a CVD-prevention decision only: an individual decision at 40–59 (C) and against starting at 60 or older (D) [[USPSTF2022Aspirin]].
- Vitamin D3 2000 IU/day (VITAL, 25,871 participants, median 5.3 years): no reduction in invasive cancer (HR 0.96; 0.88–1.06) or colorectal cancer (HR 1.09; 0.73–1.62) [[Manson2019]].
- Calcium 1200 mg/day (930 people with prior adenomas): adenoma recurrence 31% vs 38%, adjusted RR 0.81 (0.67–0.99) [[Baron1999]].
- A later trial (2259 participants) found no significant effect on adenoma recurrence over 3–5 years [[Baron2015]]:
  - vitamin D3 1000 IU: RR 0.99 (0.89–1.09)
  - calcium 1200 mg: RR 0.95 (0.85–1.06)
  - both: RR 0.93 (0.80–1.08)

### genes · How much CRC is hereditary
- 2–5% of colon cancers arise in well-defined inherited syndromes. Up to one-third show familial clustering without a known syndrome [[Jasperson2010]].
- Autosomal dominant or recessive high-risk syndromes account for 3–5% of CRC, and about 30% of CRC patients have at least one affected relative [[Zaffaroni2024]].
- Lynch syndrome, FAP and other polyposis syndromes account for only 5–10% of all CRC diagnoses [[Monahan2020]].
- In 1058 unselected CRC patients tested for 25 genes, 9.9% (8.2–11.9%) carried a pathogenic variant. 3.1% had Lynch syndrome, and 7.0% had non-Lynch variants (including 1.0% BRCA1/2) [[Yurgelun2017]].
- In 450 patients diagnosed before 50, 16% had a germline mutation. A third of these (33.3%) did not meet the testing criteria for their gene [[Pearlman2017]].

### genes · Lynch syndrome: genes, prevalence and testing
- Lynch syndrome is autosomal dominant. It is caused by germline variants in a mismatch repair gene (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) or in EPCAM [[Syngal2015]].
- Deletions of the 3′ end of EPCAM (TACSTD1) cause read-through transcription that methylates and silences the neighbouring MSH2 [[Ligtenberg2009]].
- Estimated population carrier frequency (Established) [[Win2017]]:
  - any MMR gene: 1 in 279
  - MLH1: 1 in 1,946
  - MSH2: 1 in 2,841
  - MSH6: 1 in 758
  - PMS2: 1 in 714
- In the same analysis, 1 in 45 people carry a MUTYH mutation (mostly monoallelic) [[Win2017]].
- Lynch syndrome was found in 2.8% (95% CI 2.1–3.8%) of 1,566 unselected CRC patients, about 1 in 35. Each proband had on average three relatives who also carried the mutation [[Hampel2008]].
- In that cohort, 28% of Lynch cases did not meet the revised Bethesda guidelines [[Hampel2008]].
- Earlier Ohio cohort (1,066 patients): 19.5% of tumours were MSI-high, and 2.2% of patients had Lynch syndrome. Five of the 23 probands met neither Amsterdam nor Bethesda criteria [[Hampel2005]].
- In 10,206 CRC probands, 3.1% carried an MMR mutation [[Moreira2012]]:
  - universal tumour MMR testing: sensitivity 100%, specificity 93.0%
  - Bethesda guidelines: sensitivity 87.8%
- About 15% of CRCs show microsatellite instability (MSI), many of them sporadic [[Umar2004]].
- Amsterdam II criteria: at least three relatives with a Lynch-associated cancer (CRC, endometrium, small bowel, ureter or renal pelvis). All of these must hold [[Vasen1999,Syngal2015]]:
  - one relative is an FDR of the other two
  - two successive generations are affected
  - at least one diagnosed before 50
  - FAP excluded
  - tumours verified where possible
- Revised Bethesda criteria for MSI testing include [[Umar2004,Syngal2015]]:
  - CRC before age 50
  - synchronous or metachronous Lynch-related tumours at any age
  - MSI-H histology before 60
  - CRC with one or more FDRs with a Lynch-related cancer, one diagnosed before 50
- ACG 2015: "All newly diagnosed colorectal cancers (CRCs) should be evaluated for mismatch repair deficiency" by IHC and/or MSI. Tumours with MLH1 loss should have BRAF or MLH1 promoter-methylation testing [[Syngal2015]].
- EGAPP 2009 found sufficient evidence to offer Lynch syndrome testing to everyone with newly diagnosed CRC, to benefit their relatives. Relatives who inherit the mutation have about a 45% risk of CRC by age 70 [[EGAPP2009]].
- European 2021 guideline: all colorectal and endometrial cancers should be tested for MMR deficiency [[Seppala2021]].
- UK guidance points to universal dMMR testing as set out in NICE DG27 [[Monahan2020]].

### genes · Lynch syndrome: cancer risk by gene (PLSD and other cohorts)
- PLSD 2020: 6,350 carriers followed for 51,646 person-years. 1,808 cancers were observed prospectively under surveillance [[DominguezValentin2020]].
- Cumulative CRC incidence to age 75, PLSD 2020 [[DominguezValentin2020]]:

  | Gene | Females | Males |
  |---|---|---|
  | MLH1 | 48.3% | 57.1% |
  | MSH2 | 46.6% | 51.4% |
  | MSH6 | 20.3% | 18.2% |
  | PMS2 (both sexes) | 10.4% | 10.4% |

- Cumulative CRC incidence to age 70, PLSD 2020 [[DominguezValentin2020]]:

  | Gene | Females | Males |
  |---|---|---|
  | MLH1 | 44.1% | 52.8% |
  | MSH2 | 41.9% | 46.3% |
  | MSH6 | 20.3% | 11.7% |
  | PMS2 (both sexes) | 3.4% | 3.4% |

- Endometrial cancer to age 75: 37.0% (MLH1), 48.9% (MSH2), 41.1% (MSH6) and 12.8% (PMS2) [[DominguezValentin2020]].
- Ovarian cancer to age 75: 11.0% (MLH1), 17.4% (MSH2), 10.8% (MSH6) and 3.0% (PMS2) [[DominguezValentin2020]].
- Upper GI cancers (stomach, small bowel, bile duct, gallbladder, pancreas) to 75 in MSH2 carriers: 12.8% in females and 19.5% in males [[DominguezValentin2020]].
- Prostate cancer to age 75: 23.8% in MSH2 carriers and 13.8% in MLH1 carriers [[DominguezValentin2020]].
- PMS2 carriers had no increased cancer risk before 50. Ten-year crude survival after colon, endometrial or ovarian cancer was over 80% [[DominguezValentin2020]].
- The earlier PLSD report (3,119 carriers) gave CRC risk to 75 of 46% (MLH1), 43% (MSH2) and 15% (MSH6). Endometrial risk was 43%, 57% and 46% [[Moller2018]].
- French ERISCAM families, CRC risk to age 70: 41% (MLH1), 48% (MSH2) and 12% (MSH6) [[Bonadona2011]].
- PMS2 families, cumulative risk to 70: CRC 19% in males and 11% in females; endometrial 12% [[tenBroeke2015]].
- Risk-reducing hysterectomy at 25 would prevent endometrial cancer before 50 in 15% (MLH1), 18% (MSH2), 13% (MSH6) and 0% (PMS2) of carriers. Little is gained by surgery before 40 [[DominguezValentin2021]].

### genes · Lynch syndrome: surveillance and aspirin
- Controlled 15-year Finnish trial in Lynch families [[Jarvinen2000]]:
  - CRC: 6% with colonoscopy every 3 years vs 16% without (a 62% reduction)
  - deaths: 10 vs 26
  - CRC deaths: none in the screened group vs 9 in controls
- ACG 2015: colonoscopy "at least every 2 years, beginning between ages 20 and 25 years". Annual colonoscopy should be considered in confirmed carriers [[Syngal2015]].
- ACG 2015 table: start at 20–25 (MLH1/MSH2) or 25–30 (MSH6/PMS2), every 1–2 years [[Syngal2015]].
- Europe (EHTG/ESCP) 2021 [[Seppala2021]]:
  - colonoscopy every 2 or 3 years for MLH1, MSH2 and MSH6 carriers (biennial after a previous CRC)
  - PMS2 carriers may be considered for 5-yearly colonoscopy
  - start at 25 for MLH1/MSH2 and at 35 for MSH6/PMS2
- UK (BSG/ACPGBI/UKCGG) 2020: colonoscopy every 2 years until 75, from age 25 for MLH1/MSH2 and from 35 for MSH6/PMS2 [[Monahan2020]].
- CAPP2, 600 mg aspirin vs placebo for about 2 years (861 carriers, mean follow-up 55.7 months) [[Burn2011]]:
  - time to first CRC (ITT): HR 0.63 (0.35–1.13), not significant
  - per-protocol (2 or more years of treatment): HR 0.41 (0.19–0.86)
- CAPP2 at 10 years (mean follow-up about 10 years) [[Burn2020]]:
  - CRC in 9% (40/427) on aspirin vs 13% (58/434) on placebo
  - ITT HR 0.65 (0.43–0.97)
  - per-protocol HR 0.56 (0.34–0.91)
  - no effect on non-colorectal Lynch cancers
- In CAPP2, obese carriers had 2.41-fold CRC risk (1.22–4.85). This excess was seen only in the placebo arm (Early evidence) [[Movahedi2015]].
- European 2021: carriers should be told there is a "high probability that daily aspirin will reduce their cancer risk". The guideline gives a minimum dose of 75–100 mg daily [[Seppala2021]].
- UK 2020: recommends advising people with Lynch syndrome that daily aspirin reduces CRC risk. The optimal dose is uncertain, and CAPP3 is comparing doses [[Monahan2020]].
- ACG 2015 judged the aspirin evidence "not sufficiently robust or mature" to recommend standard use. That predates CAPP2's 2020 report [[Syngal2015]].

### genes · Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)
- FAP is autosomal dominant, caused by germline APC variants on chromosome 5q21. People develop hundreds of adenomas, and cancer almost inevitably follows at an average age of 35–40 without treatment [[Galiatsatos2006]].
- FAP occurs in about 1 in 8,300 births and accounts for less than 1% of CRC. About 70% of patients have a family history [[Half2009]].
- UK guidance gives a prevalence of about 1 in 8,500 [[Monahan2020]].
- FAP accounts for about 1% of CRCs. 15–30% of APC variants arise de novo [[Zaffaroni2024]].
- Without prophylactic treatment, CRC risk reaches "virtually 100% by 60 years" [[Zaffaroni2024]].
- St Mark's series: average age at cancer 39 years; 87% had cancer by 45 and 93% by 50 [[Syngal2015]].
- Attenuated FAP usually has 10 to 100 adenomas, appears later and carries a lower cancer risk [[Half2009]].
- Extracolonic features [[Bulow2004,Half2009,Zaffaroni2024]]:
  - duodenal adenomas in most patients: cumulative 90% by age 70, Spigelman stage IV in 52%
  - duodenal cancer: cumulative 4.5% by age 57
  - desmoid tumours in about 8% of men and 13% of women over a lifetime
  - thyroid cancer: 1.5–12% lifetime
  - also CHRPE, osteomas and hepatoblastoma
- After colectomy, duodenal cancer and desmoids are the two main causes of death in FAP [[Half2009]].
- Registry data: CRC incidence was 50–70% in people who presented with symptoms vs 3–10% in those found by registry surveillance [[Monahan2020]].
- When colonoscopy should start:
  - Europe 2024: at age 12 for APC carriers with classic FAP; no later than 18–20 for attenuated FAP [[Zaffaroni2024]]
  - UK 2020: at 12–14, then every 1–3 years depending on phenotype; upper GI surveillance from 25 [[Monahan2020]]
- Prophylactic colectomy is usually advised by the late teens or early twenties [[Half2009]].
- Europe 2024 links surgical timing to phenotype. In 303 surgical cases, age thresholds predicting CRC were 46 (attenuated), 31 (sparse) and 27 years (profuse polyposis) [[Zaffaroni2024]].
- ACG 2015 absolute indications for immediate surgery: documented or suspected cancer, or significant symptoms [[Syngal2015]].

### genes · MUTYH-associated polyposis and other polyposis syndromes
- MUTYH-associated polyposis (MAP) is autosomal recessive (biallelic MUTYH variants). It causes about 7% of adenomatous polyposis and 0.7% of CRCs, and 77.4% of patients have fewer than 100 adenomas [[Zaffaroni2024]].
- Lifetime CRC risk in MAP is about 50.5%, with mean age at CRC of 47.85 years [[Zaffaroni2024]].
- ACG 2015 gives a MAP CRC risk of 19% by 50 and 43% by 60 [[Syngal2015]].
- Monoallelic MUTYH carriers, CRC risk to 70 [[Win2014]]:
  - 7.2% for men and 5.6% for women
  - 12.5% and 10% with an FDR diagnosed with CRC by 50
  - general population for comparison: 2.9% (men) and 2.1% (women)
- UK 2020 does not recommend routine colonoscopy for monoallelic MUTYH carriers. For MAP it advises colonoscopy from 18–20, then yearly [[Monahan2020]].
- Peutz–Jeghers syndrome is autosomal dominant, usually from STK11 variants (19p13.3), with hamartomatous polyps and mucocutaneous pigmentation [[Beggs2010]].
- Peutz–Jeghers incidence is 1 in 50,000 to 1 in 200,000 births. Estimated lifetime colorectal cancer risk is 39% [[Syngal2015]].
- Juvenile polyposis is autosomal dominant [[Brosens2007]]:
  - CRC RR 34.0 (14.4–65.7)
  - cumulative lifetime CRC risk 38.7%
  - mean age at CRC 43.9 years
- Juvenile polyposis is diagnosed with five or more juvenile polyps in the colorectum. Incidence is 1 in 100,000 to 1 in 160,000 [[Syngal2015]].
- WHO 2019 serrated polyposis syndrome (SPS) criteria [[Monahan2020]]:
  - criterion 1: at least 5 serrated lesions proximal to the rectum, all ≥5 mm, with 2 or more ≥10 mm
  - criterion 2: more than 20 serrated lesions anywhere, with at least 5 proximal to the rectum
  - the count is cumulative across colonoscopies
- SPS is the most common polyposis syndrome but often goes unrecognised. No common germline cause is known. FDRs should have colonoscopic screening [[Rex2026SPS]].

### genes · Screening of relatives with a family history
- USMSTF 2017 [[Rex2017]]:
  - CRC or advanced adenoma in an FDR before 60, or in two FDRs at any age: colonoscopy every 5 years, from age 40 or 10 years before the youngest affected relative's diagnosis, whichever is earlier
  - a single FDR diagnosed at 60 or older: average-risk options from age 40
- Average-risk US adults begin screening at 45 [[USPSTF2021,Wolf2018]]. ACS reaffirmed age 45 in its 2026 update [[Wolf2026]].
- UK 2020 risk categories and plans [[Monahan2020]]:
  - moderate risk (one FDR diagnosed before 50, or two FDRs at any age): one-off colonoscopy at 55
  - high risk (three or more FDRs across two or more generations): colonoscopy every 5 years from 40 to 75
- If no APC variant has been found in an at-risk FDR of someone with FAP, UK guidance advises colonoscopy from 12–14, then every 5 years [[Monahan2020]].

### genes · Inheritance arithmetic for pedigrees
- Autosomal dominant syndromes (Lynch, FAP, Peutz–Jeghers, juvenile polyposis): each child of a carrier has a 50% chance of inheriting the variant [[Syngal2015,Galiatsatos2006]].
- MAP is autosomal recessive: each child of two carrier parents has a 25% chance of being affected (biallelic) and 50% of being a carrier (standard Mendelian arithmetic) [[Galiatsatos2006,Zaffaroni2024]].
- A typical Lynch proband has at least three relatives who carry the mutation and could benefit from surveillance [[Hampel2008]].

### Exam hooks
- One affected FDR roughly doubles CRC risk (RR about 2.2); two or more roughly quadruple it [[Johns2001,Butterworth2006]].
- The family-history RR is highest when the person at risk is young: about 3 under 40, about 1.4 at 40 and older [[Wong2018]].
- Processed meat is IARC Group 1 and red meat is Group 2A [[Bouvard2015]].
- Processed meat raises CRC risk by about 18% per 50 g/day; red meat by about 17% per 100 g/day [[Chan2011]].
- Obesity: RR 1.33. Diabetes: RR about 1.27. Physical activity: RR 0.76 for colon cancer [[Ma2013,Jiang2011,Wolin2009]].
- Aspirin's effect on CRC appears only after years: most benefit is seen with 5 or more years of use, and in the proximal colon [[Rothwell2010]].
- ASPREE (age 70 and older): aspirin did not reduce cancer and was linked to higher cancer mortality [[McNeil2018,McNeil2021]].
- VITAL: vitamin D3 2000 IU/day did not reduce cancer incidence [[Manson2019]].
- The Lynch syndrome carrier frequency is about 1 in 279 [[Win2017]].
- About 3% of CRC patients have Lynch syndrome [[Hampel2008,Moreira2012]].
- Universal MMR tumour testing catches Lynch cases that Bethesda criteria miss (sensitivity 100% vs 87.8%) [[Moreira2012]].
- Lynch CRC risk by gene: MLH1 and MSH2 are high (about 45–57% to age 75), MSH6 is moderate and PMS2 is low [[DominguezValentin2020]].
- MSH6 confers a high endometrial cancer risk but only a modest CRC risk [[DominguezValentin2020]].
- Amsterdam II "3-2-1-0": 3 relatives, 2 generations, 1 diagnosed before 50, 0 FAP [[Vasen1999,Syngal2015]].
- Colonoscopy every 3 years cut CRC by 62% in Lynch families [[Jarvinen2000]].
- CAPP2: aspirin 600 mg reduced CRC in Lynch syndrome, ITT HR 0.65 at 10 years [[Burn2020]].
- FAP: APC, autosomal dominant, hundreds to thousands of adenomas, CRC risk virtually 100% by 60 [[Zaffaroni2024]].
- After colectomy, duodenal cancer and desmoids are the leading causes of death in FAP [[Half2009]].
- MAP: biallelic MUTYH, autosomal recessive, often fewer than 100 adenomas [[Zaffaroni2024]].
- USMSTF: an FDR diagnosed before 60 means colonoscopy from age 40, or 10 years before the relative's diagnosis, every 5 years [[Rex2017]].

### In plain words
- Having a parent, brother, sister or child with bowel cancer about doubles your own risk. Having two close relatives raises it more [[Butterworth2006,Johns2001]].
- Even with a family history, most people will never get bowel cancer. The doubling starts from a small number [[Butterworth2006,Roos2019]].
- Eating less processed meat, such as bacon, ham and sausages, and less red meat lowers risk a little [[Chan2011,Vieira2017]].
- Drinking less alcohol, not smoking, staying active and keeping a healthy weight all lower risk [[Fedirko2011,Botteri2008,Wolin2009,Ma2013]].
- Whole grains and fibre are linked to lower risk [[Aune2011]].
- Aspirin can lower bowel cancer risk, but it takes years, and it can cause bleeding. In people over 70 it did not help. Talk to a doctor before starting it [[Rothwell2010,McNeil2021]].
- Vitamin D pills did not prevent cancer in a large trial [[Manson2019]].
- About 1 in 300 people carry a Lynch syndrome gene change, and most do not know it [[Win2017,DominguezValentin2020]].
- If you have Lynch syndrome, regular colonoscopy greatly lowers your chance of dying from bowel cancer [[Jarvinen2000]].
- Each child of a parent with Lynch syndrome or FAP has a 1 in 2 chance of inheriting it [[Syngal2015]].
- FAP causes hundreds of polyps. Without surgery to remove the large bowel, cancer is almost certain [[Zaffaroni2024]].
- Many hospitals now test every bowel cancer for signs of Lynch syndrome, so relatives can be warned [[Syngal2015,Seppala2021]].
- Long-standing colitis raises bowel cancer risk, but much less than it used to [[Olen2020UC]].

## Open questions and controversies
- How big is the familial RR? Older meta-analyses give about 2.2–2.3 [[Johns2001,Butterworth2006]]. Cohort-only pooling gives 1.37 for one FDR (CI includes 1) [[Roos2019]], and PLCO after age 55 gives 1.23 [[Schoen2015]]. Recall bias in case-control studies may inflate older estimates. Status: unresolved, and guidelines differ (USMSTF colonoscopy from 40 vs a UK one-off colonoscopy at 55) [[Rex2017,Monahan2020]].
- Aspirin for primary prevention: long-term trial follow-up shows CRC benefit after 5 or more years [[Rothwell2010]]. ASPREE in people 70 and older showed no benefit and more advanced-stage cancer [[McNeil2021]]. USPSTF dropped CRC prevention from its 2022 aspirin statement [[USPSTF2022Aspirin]]. Status: age-dependent and individual.
- Aspirin in Lynch syndrome: European and UK guidelines endorse it [[Seppala2021,Monahan2020]], while ACG 2015 did not [[Syngal2015]]. The best dose is unknown (CAPP3 is ongoing; results were not found in PubMed as of this pass).
- Lynch colonoscopy interval: ACG says 1–2 years [[Syngal2015]], Europe 2–3 years with consensus split [[Seppala2021]], UK 2 years [[Monahan2020]]. For PMS2, Europe allows 5-yearly. CRC still occurs in about half of MLH1/MSH2 carriers by 75 despite surveillance, which challenges the assumption that adenomas are the precursor [[DominguezValentin2020]].
- Penetrance estimates depend on how people were found. Clinic-based PLSD cohorts and family-based segregation studies (Bonadona, ten Broeke) give different numbers, especially for MSH6 and PMS2 [[DominguezValentin2020,Bonadona2011,tenBroeke2015]].
- Vitamin D and calcium: observational data favour calcium [[Keum2014]], but RCTs of vitamin D and calcium on adenomas or CRC were null [[Baron2015,Manson2019]]. An earlier calcium trial was modestly positive [[Baron1999]].
- Monoallelic MUTYH: risk is about 2–3 times the population [[Win2014]], yet UK guidance does not recommend extra screening [[Monahan2020]].
- IBD risk is falling over calendar time [[Olen2020UC]]. In Crohn's, excess risk is concentrated in colonic disease, diagnosis before 40, or PSC [[Olen2020CD]].
