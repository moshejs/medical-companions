# A · Epidemiology, symptoms, clinical presentation and history

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: home, what, symptoms, history.

This pass covers how common colorectal cancer (CRC) is worldwide and in the United States, where in the bowel it arises, the rise of early-onset CRC, projected burden and survival trends; how CRC presents (symptoms, predictive values in primary care, FIT in people with symptoms, emergency presentation, diagnostic intervals); and a cited timeline of the history of the disease from Miles (1908) to the 2026 ACS screening update.

## Findings

### home · Global burden at a glance
- GLOBOCAN 2022: colorectal cancer was the third most diagnosed cancer worldwide (9.6% of all new cancers, after lung 12.4% and female breast 11.6%) and the second leading cause of cancer death (9.3% of cancer deaths, after lung 18.7%) [[Bray2024]].
- GLOBOCAN 2024 (newest estimates, published 2026): 2,041,007 new CRC cases (9.9% of all cancers; rank 3) and 917,895 CRC deaths (9.4% of cancer deaths; rank 2) worldwide [[Sung2026]].
- GLOBOCAN 2024 by sex: 1,138,656 cases in men (age-standardised rate, world, 22.4 per 100,000; cumulative risk birth to 74 years 2.58%) and 902,351 in women (ASR 15.3; cumulative risk 1.72%) [[Sung2026]].
- GLOBOCAN 2024 deaths by sex: 504,807 men (ASR 9.6; cumulative risk 1.00%) and 413,088 women (ASR 6.3; cumulative risk 0.64%) [[Sung2026]].
- Regional variation is five-fold to six-fold. The highest incidence rates are in Europe, Australia/New Zealand and Northern America (30–40 per 100,000 in men; 24–32 in women). The lowest are in Eastern, Western and Middle Africa and South-Central Asia (5–8 per 100,000) [[Sung2026]].
- Incidence rates are up to four times higher in higher- than in lower-Human Development Index countries. Mortality varies less because case fatality is higher in lower-HDI countries [[Sung2026]].
- GLOBOCAN 2020: over 1.9 million new CRC cases and 930,000 deaths. The highest incidence was in Australia/New Zealand and European regions (40.6 per 100,000, males) and the lowest in several African regions and Southern Asia (4.4 per 100,000, females). Mortality was highest in Eastern Europe (20.2 per 100,000, males) and lowest in Southern Asia (2.5 per 100,000, females) [[Morgan2023]].
- About 10% of new CRC cases worldwide are attributable to modifiable risk factors (in men: smoking 50% of that share, alcohol 32%, high BMI 10%, insufficient physical activity 8%). A US study with a broader set of risk factors estimated up to 55% could be prevented [[Sung2026]].
- Screening has been estimated to account for 79% of averted CRC deaths in the United States, with the rest attributed to treatment advances [[Sung2026]].
- Japan's CRC incidence quadrupled between 1970 and 1990, paralleling post-war economic development. Similar rises are now seen in many transitioning countries (Established) [[Sung2026]].
- Long-standing declines in CRC incidence and mortality in historically high-risk countries in Europe, Oceania and Northern America reflect lifestyle change (for example less smoking) and screening. Screening has sped up progress since the early 2000s (Established) [[Sung2026]].

### home · Projected burden
- GLOBOCAN-based projection: CRC burden will rise to 3.2 million new cases and 1.6 million deaths a year by 2040, mostly in high or very high HDI countries [[Morgan2023]].
- All cancers combined are predicted to reach 34.4 million new cases by 2050, up 67% from 2024 [[Sung2026]].
- A US projection (SEER rates plus Census population growth) estimated that in 2040 CRC will remain the fourth most common cancer (147,000 cases). Its deaths (34,000) would rank behind pancreatic (46,000) and liver (41,000) cancer (Early evidence: modelled projection) [[Rahib2021]].

### home · United States: cases, deaths, risk
- ACS projection for 2026: 158,850 new CRC cases (108,860 colon, 49,990 rectum) and 55,230 CRC deaths in the United States [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- 2026 projection by age: 24,640 cases (16%) and 3,890 deaths (7%) in people under 50; 47,600 cases (30%) and 13,480 deaths (24%) at 50–64; 86,610 cases (55%) and 37,860 deaths (69%) at 65 and older [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- 45% of new diagnoses are now in people younger than 65, up from 27% in 1995. Nearly one third of 2026 CRC deaths will be in people younger than 65 [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- ACS projection for 2025: 154,270 new CRC cases and 52,900 deaths [[Siegel2025]].
- ACS projection for 2023: about 153,020 diagnoses and 52,550 deaths, including 19,550 cases and 3,750 deaths in people younger than 50 [[Siegel2023CRC]].
- CRC is the second most common cause of cancer death in the United States and ranks first in adults younger than 50 [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- In the late 1990s CRC was the fourth leading cause of cancer death in men and in women younger than 50. By 2024 it was first in men and second in women in that age group [[Siegel2024]].
- Lifetime probability (birth to death) of developing invasive CRC, 2018–2021: 4.1% (1 in 24) for males and 3.8% (1 in 26) for females [[Siegel2025]].
- Probability by age interval (males / females): birth–49, 0.4% (1 in 238) / 0.4% (1 in 255); 50–64, 1.2% (1 in 85) / 0.9% (1 in 117); 65–84, 2.6% (1 in 39) / 2.1% (1 in 47); 85 and older, 1.7% / 1.6% [[Siegel2025]].
- Incidence (2018–2022) is 32% higher in men than in women (40.5 vs 30.7 per 100,000). The gap is 19% under age 50 and 44% at ages 50–64 [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Death rate 2019–2023: 42% higher in men than in women (15.3 vs 10.8 per 100,000) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Incidence by race and ethnicity ranges from 28.5 per 100,000 in Asian American, Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander people to 80.9 in Alaska Native people. Alaska Native people have more than twice the incidence (80.9 vs 35.2) and about 2.5 times the mortality (31.5 vs 12.9) of White people [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Black individuals are the most likely to be diagnosed with metastatic CRC (26% vs 22% of White individuals) and have the lowest 5-year relative survival (59% vs 65%) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- By US state, incidence ranges from 45 per 100,000 (Mississippi, Kentucky) to 28 per 100,000 (Utah). Mortality ranges from 18 (Mississippi) to 10 per 100,000 (Massachusetts, Connecticut) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- More than 1.4 million people were living after a CRC diagnosis in the United States as of 1 January 2025 [[Siegel2026CRC]].

### home · United States: trends
- Overall CRC incidence fell 45% from its 1985 peak to 2022. The decline slowed from 1.3% a year (2011–2017) to 0.6% a year (2017–2022) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- From 2002 to 2012, incidence in adults aged 65 and older fell 4%–5% a year. This is credited to widespread colonoscopy after Medicare expanded coverage in 2001 [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- 2013–2022: incidence fell 0.9% a year overall and 2.5% a year in people 65 and older, but rose 3% a year in ages 20–49 and 0.4% a year in ages 50–64 [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Overall CRC mortality fell 56%, from 29.1 per 100,000 in 1970 to 12.7 in 2023. It declined about 2% a year from 2005 to 2020 and was stable from 2020 to 2023 [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Mortality in adults younger than 50 has risen 1% a year since 2004. In ages 50–64 it rose 1.2% a year from 2019 to 2023 (2.3% a year in White individuals). It fell 2.3% a year from 2012 in people 65 and older [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- An earlier report, covering 2011–2020, found CRC mortality falling 2% a year overall but rising 0.5%–3% a year in people under 50 [[Siegel2023CRC]].
- 60% of all new US cases were advanced stage (regional or distant) in 2019, compared with 52% in the mid-2000s and 57% in 1995. This reverses the earlier shift to earlier-stage diagnosis [[Siegel2023CRC]].
- In ages 50–64, the rise in incidence is confined to regional and distant stage disease (about 1% a year, 2013–2022), even though two in three people in this age group are up to date with screening [[Siegel2026CRC]].

### what · Anatomical subsite distribution
- In 2026, rectal cancer accounts for 49,990 of 158,850 projected US cases (about 31%) and colon cancer for 108,860 (about 69%; the colon count includes appendix) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Rectal cancer made up 32% of all CRC in 2022, up from 27% in 2004 (Siegel2026CRC) and from 27% in 1995 to 31% in 2019 (Siegel2023CRC). Its incidence rose 1% a year from 2018 to 2022 after decades of decline [[Siegel2026CRC,Siegel2023CRC]].
- Incidence by subsite, 2018–2022, all ages (per 100,000): colorectum 35.3; proximal colon 13.3; distal colon 8.7; rectum 11.1; large intestine not otherwise specified 2.2 [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Under 50 the distribution is reversed toward the left side: rectum 5.7, distal colon 4.1, proximal colon 2.9 per 100,000. At 65 and older, proximal colon dominates (73.1, vs distal 31.5 and rectum 36.6) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Calculated from those subsite rates (all ages): proximal colon ≈38%, distal colon ≈25%, rectum ≈31%, not otherwise specified ≈6% of CRC. These shares are derived here, not stated by the source [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Male excess differs by subsite: incidence is 9% higher in men for proximal colon, 41% higher for distal colon and 57% higher for rectal tumours [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Even in people 65 and older, rectal cancer is now 21% more common than distal colon cancer, reversing the historical pattern [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Right-sided (proximal) and left-sided (distal) CRC differ in incidence, pathogenesis, molecular pathways and outcome (Established) [[Lee2015]].
- Right-sided tumours more often show mismatch-repair defects and flat histology. Left-sided tumours more often follow the chromosomal-instability pathway (KRAS, APC, PIK3CA, TP53) with polypoid morphology. The review reports differing therapy responses by side (Established) [[Baran2018]].
- Several decades ago CRC was infrequently diagnosed. Ageing, Western diets, obesity, inactivity and smoking now make it the world's fourth most deadly cancer, with almost 900,000 deaths a year (2019 review) [[Dekker2019]].
- New treatment options have doubled overall survival for advanced disease to about 3 years. CRC tends to cause symptoms only at an advanced stage, which is the rationale for organised screening [[Dekker2019]].

### what · Early-onset colorectal cancer (under 50)
- In the United States, early-onset CRC incidence (ages 20–49) has risen 1%–2% a year since the mid-1990s, mostly through advanced-stage and left-sided tumours. It rose 3% a year from 2013 to 2022, helped by a post-2019 jump in localized disease as screening began at 45 (Established) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Regional-stage early-onset CRC has risen about 3.4% a year since 2011 and distant-stage about 2.7% a year since at least 1998 [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Early-onset incidence 1998–2022, ages 20–49: sigmoid colon nearly doubled (2.1 to 3.7 per 100,000) and rectum nearly doubled (3.6 to 6.6 per 100,000), compared with a 26% increase in proximal tumours [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Early-onset incidence is rising in every US racial and ethnic group (2013–2022): about 2% a year in Black people, 3% in AANHPI, AIAN and White people, and 4% in Hispanic people [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- 22% of US CRCs in 2022 were diagnosed before age 55, twice the 1995 share (11%) [[Siegel2026CRC]]. An earlier report gave 11% in 1995 rising to 20% in 2019 [[Siegel2023CRC]].
- Birth-cohort effect: in the US, risk is rising in every generation born after about 1950, and after about 1960 in Canada, Australia and England. Risk tracks when you were born more than when you are diagnosed (Established) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- US SEER 1974–2013 (n = 490,305): compared with people born around 1950, those born around 1990 have double the risk of colon cancer (IRR 2.40, 95% CI 1.11–5.19) and quadruple the risk of rectal cancer (IRR 4.32, 95% CI 2.19–8.51) [[Siegel2017]].
- Rectal cancer in adults aged 20–29 rose 3.2% a year from 1974 to 2013. The share of rectal cancer diagnosed before 55 doubled from 14.6% to 29.2% (1989–90 to 2012–13) [[Siegel2017]].
- Age-specific CRC risk for recent birth cohorts has escalated back to the level of those born around 1890 [[Siegel2017]].
- 50 countries (CI5plus, to 2017): early-onset CRC (ages 25–49) rose in 27 of 50 countries. In 20 of those 27, the rise was confined to, or faster than, the trend in older adults [[Sung2025]].
- Highest early-onset ASRs (2013–17): Australia 16.5, Puerto Rico 15.2, New Zealand 14.8, USA 14.8 and South Korea 14.3 per 100,000; lowest in Uganda (4.4) and India (3.5) [[Sung2025]].
- Fastest early-onset increases (AAPC, latest 10 years): New Zealand 3.97%, Chile 3.96%, Puerto Rico 3.81%, England 3.59% a year [[Sung2025]].
- Earlier global analysis (36 countries, to 2012): early-onset incidence rose in 19 countries. In nine (Australia, Canada, Denmark, Germany, New Zealand, Slovenia, Sweden, UK, USA) it rose exclusively in young adults. The steepest rises were in Korea (AAPC 4.2) and New Zealand (4.0) [[Siegel2019]].
- Europe, 20 countries (1990–2016, 143.7 million people aged 20–49): CRC incidence rose 7.9% a year at ages 20–29 (2004–2016), 4.9% at 30–39 (2005–2016) and 1.6% at 40–49 (2004–2016), consistent with an age-cohort phenomenon [[Vuik2019]].
- Part of the apparent rise in young people reflects a 2013 coding change that made some appendiceal carcinoid tumours reportable. Studies excluding appendiceal cancer still show a genuine rise that pre-dates the change and is accompanied by rising mortality (Established) [[Sung2026]].
- 16%–20% of CRC diagnosed before 50 is due to germline pathogenic variants (for example Lynch syndrome), but most early-onset cases are sporadic [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Three in four patients younger than 50 are diagnosed with advanced disease, including 27% with distant metastases vs 21%–23% of older patients [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Despite later stage, people under 50 have similar or higher 5-year relative survival. The gap is largest for distant-stage disease: 23% vs 17% (ages 50–64) and 10% (65 and older) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Proposed drivers include Western-style diet, obesity, physical inactivity and antibiotic use, especially in early life, acting through the microbiome and host immunity. The cause remains unknown (Hypothesis) [[Akimoto2021,Patel2022EOCRC]].
- Established risk factors from older cohorts do not fully explain the pattern. For example, obesity and inactivity relate more strongly to colon than rectal cancer, yet the rise is driven by rectal cancer. Research is examining ultra-processed foods, microplastics and antibiotics (Hypothesis) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Imaging-related incidental detection is unlikely to explain the early-onset rise. Such detection mostly finds early-stage disease, the opposite of the observed pattern (Early evidence) [[Siegel2026CRC]].

### what · Survival trends
- US 5-year relative survival for CRC rose from 50% in the mid-1970s to 65% for diagnoses in 2015–2021. These are population statistics, not individual predictions [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- 5-year relative survival by era (ACS Table 6), colon and rectum: 50% (1975–77), 61% (1995–97), 64% (2014–20). Colon: 51%, 61%, 63%. Rectum: 48%, 62%, 67% [[Siegel2025]].
- 5-year relative survival by stage (2015–2021): 91% localized vs 15% distant. For regional disease, the most common stage at diagnosis, it ranges from 69% (65 and older) to 81% (under 50) [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Distant-stage rectal cancer had the largest survival gain: 5-year survival rose from 8% in the mid-1990s to 18% [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- CONCORD-3 (2010–14): the highest 5-year net survival for gastrointestinal cancers was in South Korea, including 71.8% for colon and 71.1% for rectum [[Allemani2018]].
- ICBP SURVMARK-2 (seven high-income countries, 1995–2014): 5-year rectal cancer survival rose by more than 13 percentage points in Denmark, Ireland and the UK. Colon cancer showed progress across incidence, mortality and survival [[Arnold2019]].
- Men and women have similar overall 5-year relative survival (64% vs 65%). Rectal cancer survival is 67% vs 63% for colon, and women do better than men for rectal cancer (69% vs 65%) [[Siegel2026CRC]].

### symptoms · Common presenting features and their predictive value
- Most CRCs are diagnosed after symptoms begin, and most present symptomatically in primary care (Established) [[Hamilton2005,Astin2011]].
- Exeter case-control study (349 cases aged 40 and over; 1,744 controls), positive predictive values (PPV) in primary care: rectal bleeding 2.4%; weight loss 1.2%; abdominal pain 1.1%; diarrhoea 0.94%; constipation 0.42% [[Hamilton2005]].
- In the same study, PPVs for signs and tests: abnormal rectal examination 4.0%, abdominal tenderness 1.1%, haemoglobin <10.0 g/dL 2.3%, positive faecal occult blood 7.1%, blood glucose >10 mmol/L 0.78% [[Hamilton2005]].
- UK GPRD case-control study (5,477 cases; 38,314 controls): in men aged 80 and over, PPV was 4.5% for rectal bleeding and 3.9% for change in bowel habit. PPVs were lower in women and younger patients [[Hamilton2009]].
- Only 27% of patients in that study had reported either of the two higher-risk symptoms. Most symptomatic CRCs present with only a low-risk symptom [[Hamilton2009]].
- Systematic review (23 primary-care studies): pooled PPV of rectal bleeding was 8.1% (95% CI 6.0%–11%) in people aged 50 and over. Anaemia's pooled PPV was 9.7% (3.5%–27%) and abdominal pain's 3.3% (0.7%–16%) [[Astin2011]].
- Rectal bleeding with weight loss or change in bowel habit raises risk: pooled positive likelihood ratios were 1.9 and 1.8. With abdominal pain, diarrhoea or constipation the ratio was 1 or less [[Astin2011]].
- GPRD cohort (762,325 patients): 3-year PPV of a first episode of rectal bleeding for CRC was 2.4% in men and 2.0% in women. Predictive values rose with age [[Jones2007]].
- Anaemia (THIN, 6,442 cases): haemoglobin <9.0 g/dL had a likelihood ratio for CRC of 27 in men and 41 in women [[Hamilton2008]].
- In men over 60 with Hb <11 g/dL and iron deficiency, PPV was 13.3%. In women with Hb <10 g/dL and iron deficiency, PPV was 7.7% [[Hamilton2008]].
- Rectal bleeding as the first symptom was associated with less advanced stage and lower mortality (HR 0.56). Mild anaemia (Hb 10.0–12.9 g/dL) was associated with more advanced stage and higher mortality (adjusted HR 1.7) [[Stapley2006]].
- In younger people, symptoms overlap with common benign conditions. Change in bowel habit or abdominal pain may be due to irritable bowel syndrome, and rectal bleeding is frequently caused by haemorrhoids [[Monahan2022]].

### symptoms · Early-onset CRC: red-flag symptoms
- Meta-analysis (81 studies, people under 50): the most common presenting features were haematochezia (pooled prevalence 45%), abdominal pain (40%) and altered bowel habit (27%) [[Demb2024]].
- Haematochezia was associated with at least a 5-fold increased risk of early-onset CRC (estimates 5.2–54.0) [[Demb2024]].
- Time from symptom to diagnosis averaged 6.4 months (range 1.8–13.7; 23 studies), median 4 months (2.0–8.7; 16 studies) [[Demb2024]].
- US case-control study (5,075 early-onset cases): four red flags 3 months to 2 years before diagnosis were associated with early-onset CRC, with odds ratios 1.34–5.13. These were abdominal pain, rectal bleeding, diarrhoea and iron-deficiency anaemia [[Fritz2023]].
- In the same study, risk rose with the number of red flags: 1 (OR 1.94), 2 (3.59), 3 or more (6.52). About 19.3% of patients had their first sign 3 months to 2 years before diagnosis (median diagnostic interval 8.7 months) [[Fritz2023]].
- Younger patients are more likely than older ones to present with haematochezia (for example 41% vs 26%), iron-deficiency anaemia and abdominal pain [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Time to diagnosis is about 40% longer in people under 50 than in older people, partly because of misdiagnosis as more common conditions [[Siegel2026CRC]].

### symptoms · FIT in people with symptoms
- A systematic review informing NICE DG30 assessed FIT at a cut-off of 10 µg Hb/g faeces. Pooled sensitivity was 92.1% for OC-Sensor (specificity 85.8%), and 100% in one HM-JACKarc study (specificity 76.6%) [[Westwood2017]].
- Triage with FIT at about 10 µg Hb/g could correctly rule out CRC and avoid colonoscopy in 75%–80% of symptomatic patients [[Westwood2017]].
- Meta-analysis in primary care (23 studies, 69,536 participants): at 10 µg/g, sensitivity was 87.2% and specificity 84.4%. At ≥150 µg/g, sensitivity was 64.1% and specificity 95.0% [[PinVieito2022]].
- Moving from a 10 to a 20 µg/g threshold would miss fewer than one additional CRC per 1,000 patients investigated (at 2% CRC prevalence) [[PinVieito2022]].
- NICE FIT study (9,822 patients referred on the 2-week-wait pathway; CRC prevalence 3.3%): at cut-offs of 2, 10 and 150 µg/g, sensitivity was 97.0%, 90.9% and 70.8%. PPV was 8.7%, 16.1% and 31.1%, and NPV 99.8%, 99.6% and 98.9% [[DSouza2021Gut]].
- In the NICE FIT study, FIT positivity fell from 37.2% to 19.0% to 7.6% across the same cut-offs. The area under the ROC curve was 0.93. FIT predicted CRC better than symptoms [[DSouza2021Gut]].
- In patients under 50 in the NICE FIT study, 1.5% (16/1,103) had CRC. FIT sensitivity was 81.3% at 10 µg/g, and NPV stayed above 99.5% at all cut-offs [[DSouza2021CD]].
- ACPGBI/BSG guideline (2022): a FIT threshold of fHb ≥10 µg Hb/g should be used in primary care to select patients with lower GI symptoms for urgent referral (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence) [[Monahan2022]].
- The same guideline recommends that patients not be excluded from referral on FIT alone. Patients with fHb <10 µg Hb/g but persistent, unexplained symptoms and ongoing GP concern should still be referred [[Monahan2022]].
- The guideline suggests FIT for people with iron-deficiency anaemia to inform urgency, and flexible sigmoidoscopy for persistent anorectal bleeding even if fHb <10 µg Hb/g (weak recommendations) [[Monahan2022]].
- In the NICE FIT study, CRC risk was about 0.2% with undetectable fHb and 0.4% with fHb <10 µg Hb/g [[Monahan2022]].
- FIT-negative cancers in one cohort were right-sided (caecal), and most were in patients with iron-deficiency anaemia. FIT sensitivity was 80.0% in IDA vs 89.0% with other symptoms (Early evidence) [[Monahan2022]].
- Under NICE DG30 (2017), FIT was recommended for "low-risk" symptoms: people without rectal bleeding who have unexplained symptoms but do not meet suspected-cancer referral criteria under NG12 [[Monahan2022]].
- UK service evaluation (13,361 FITs): CRC detection was 0.1% at fHb <4, 0.6% at 4–9.9, 3.3% at 10–99.9 and 20.7% at ≥100 µg Hb/g. Below 20 µg/g, the CRC rate was under 0.3% [[Bailey2021]].
- In that evaluation, CRC detection at 10–19.9 µg/g (1.4%) fell below NICE's 3% PPV threshold for urgent referral [[Bailey2021]].

### symptoms · Emergency presentation and diagnostic intervals
- England, 2006–2008 (91,416 colorectal cancers): 26% were diagnosed as an emergency presentation, 27% via the 2-week-wait route, 20% via routine GP referral and 2% by screening [[EllissBrookes2012]].
- 1-year relative survival for colorectal cancer was 50% after emergency presentation, compared with 82% via 2-week wait and 98% if screen-detected (all routes 74%) [[EllissBrookes2012]].
- Six high-income countries, 2012–2017: rectal cancer had the lowest share of emergency presentations of the cancers studied (12.1% on average; 9.1%–19.8% by jurisdiction) [[McPhail2022]].
- Across all sites in that study, emergency presenters had substantially higher 12-month mortality (OR >1.9 in every jurisdiction and cancer stratum). Emergency presentation was more likely at older age and advanced stage [[McPhail2022]].
- England, 1997–2012: 24.3% of 286,591 CRC resections were emergency operations. 30-day mortality was 13.3% after emergency vs 3.4% after elective surgery [[Askari2017]].
- After the English bowel screening programme began (2006), the share of eligible patients needing emergency surgery fell from 23.4% to 14.9% [[Askari2017]].
- Predictors of emergency CRC surgery were female sex (OR 1.23), age over 79 (1.55), most-deprived quintile (1.64) and Black African/Caribbean ethnicity (1.36) [[Askari2017]].
- Danish primary-care cohort (268 patients): for patients whose symptoms suggested serious illness, 3-year mortality fell with diagnostic intervals up to 5 weeks and then rose [[Torring2011]].
- A systematic review across cancers (209 studies) found more reports linking shorter time to diagnosis with better outcomes for colorectal cancer than for many other sites (Early evidence: heterogeneous studies) [[Neal2015]].
- Turkish multicentre cohort (1,795 patients): symptom duration was not associated with tumour site or stage. Anaemia, change in bowel habit, weight loss and a right-colon tumour were associated with longer symptom time [[Ozturk2019]].

### symptoms · Right-sided versus left-sided presentation
- The rise in CRC among younger people is dominated by left-sided (distal colon and rectal) tumours. ACS links this left-sided predominance to differences in presentation, including more rectal bleeding in younger patients [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- In a 1,795-patient cohort, the principal presenting symptoms were significantly associated with left colon cancer, while anaemia and a right-colon tumour were each associated with longer symptom time (Early evidence) [[Ozturk2019]].
- FIT-negative cancers in one UK cohort were right-sided (caecal) and mostly in patients with iron-deficiency anaemia, so a low FIT result is less reassuring when there is iron-deficiency anaemia (Early evidence) [[Monahan2022]].
- Rectal bleeding as a first symptom tends to mean earlier stage, while anaemia tends to mean later stage [[Stapley2006]].

### history · Surgery and pathology
- 1908: W. Ernest Miles described abdominoperineal excision for carcinoma of the rectum and terminal pelvic colon in The Lancet [[Miles1908]].
- A historical review dates Miles's first radical operation to 1907; abdominoperineal resection became a gold standard for many years [[Lirici2016]].
- 1932: Cuthbert Dukes published his classification of cancer of the rectum, the basis of "Dukes stage", still used in later surgical series [[Dukes1932,Heald1986]].
- 1982: Heald, Husband and Ryall found tumour foci in the mesorectum several centimetres beyond the visible tumour edge, and proposed total excision of the mesorectum (TME) [[Heald1982]].
- In that 1982 series, 50 curative TME resections followed for over 2 years had no pelvic or staple-line recurrence [[Heald1982]].
- 1986: Heald's TME series reported 5-year survival of 87% and tumour-free survival by Dukes stage of A 94%, B 87% and C 58%. Permanent colostomy was limited to 11% [[Heald1986]].
- A historical review identifies TME, developed by Heald in 1982, as the current gold standard for rectal cancer surgery [[Lirici2016]].

### history · Heredity and genes
- 1913: Aldred Scott Warthin published "Heredity with reference to carcinoma", describing his "Family G" from cases seen from 1895 [[Warthin1913,Douglas2005]].
- Warthin's interest began in 1895 with the family history of a seamstress, Pauline Gross. Family G is now recognised as the first reported Lynch syndrome family [[Campos2024]].
- By 2005, Family G had 929 known descendants. CRC (SIR 3.20) and endometrial cancer (SIR 3.51) predominated, and an MSH2 splice-site mutation was confirmed [[Douglas2005]].
- 1925: Lockhart-Mummery published "Cancer and heredity" in The Lancet [[LockhartMummery1925]].
- 1939: Lockhart-Mummery and Dukes reported on familial adenomatosis of the colon and rectum [[LockhartMummery1939]].
- 1951: Eldon Gardner published a genetic and clinical study of intestinal polyposis as a predisposing factor for colorectal carcinoma [[Gardner1951]].
- 1953: Gardner and Richards described cutaneous and subcutaneous lesions together with hereditary polyposis and osteomatosis, later known as Gardner syndrome [[Gardner1953,Nishisho1991]].
- 1966: Henry Lynch and colleagues described two large Midwestern kindreds with hereditary cancer [[Lynch1966]].
- 1984: Boland and Troncale described "familial colonic cancer without antecedent polyposis" [[Boland1984]].
- The condition first called "cancer family syndrome" was later renamed Lynch syndrome in recognition of Henry Lynch's work [[Campos2024]].
- 1975: Muto, Bussey and Morson presented evidence that most colorectal cancers evolve through a polyp-to-cancer sequence. Malignant potential rises with adenoma size, villous histology and degree of atypia, but most adenomas never become cancer [[Muto1975]].
- 1988: Vogelstein and colleagues showed four genetic alterations accumulating along with clinical tumour progression: ras mutations in 9% of adenomas under 1 cm vs 58% over 1 cm, and chromosome 18 loss in 73% of carcinomas [[Vogelstein1988]].
- 1990: Fearon and Vogelstein proposed their genetic model of multistep colorectal tumorigenesis [[Fearon1990]].
- 1991: the APC gene on chromosome 5q21 was identified [[Kinzler1991,Groden1991]]. Germline APC mutations were shown to cause both FAP and Gardner syndrome [[Nishisho1991]].
- 1993: a predisposition locus was mapped to chromosome 2 [[Peltomaki1993]]. Familial tumours showed widespread microsatellite alterations, also found in 13% of sporadic cancers [[Aaltonen1993]].
- 1993: the first mismatch-repair gene, MSH2, was identified as an HNPCC gene [[Fishel1993,Leach1993]]. MLH1 followed in 1994 [[Bronner1994]].

### history · Screening and endoscopy
- 1967: Greegor published "Diagnosis of large-bowel cancer in the asymptomatic patient", an early report on finding colorectal cancer before symptoms appear [[Greegor1967]].
- 1973: Wolff and Shinya reported colonoscopic polypectomy through the fibreoptic colonoscope, removing neoplasms beyond the reach of the sigmoidoscope [[Wolff1973NEJM,Wolff1973AnnSurg]].
- 1993, Minnesota trial (46,551 participants aged 50–80): annual guaiac FOBT with rehydration cut 13-year CRC mortality by 33% (5.88 vs 8.83 per 1,000). Biennial screening (8.33 per 1,000) was not significantly different from control [[Mandel1993]].
- 2000, Minnesota at 18 years: CRC incidence ratios were 0.80 (annual) and 0.83 (biennial) vs control [[Mandel2000]].
- 2013, Minnesota at 30 years: CRC mortality relative risk was 0.68 (annual) and 0.78 (biennial). All-cause mortality did not change [[Shaukat2013]].
- 1996, Nottingham trial (about 150,000 people aged 45–74): biennial Haemoccult cut cumulative CRC mortality by 15% (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.74–0.98) at a median 7.8 years [[Hardcastle1996]].
- At 19.5 years of follow-up in Nottingham, the CRC mortality reduction was 13%, with no significant reduction in incidence [[Scholefield2012]].
- 1996, Funen (Denmark) trial (30,967 screened vs 30,966 controls): biennial Hemoccult-II over 10 years gave a CRC mortality ratio of 0.82 (95% CI 0.68–0.99) [[Kronborg1996]].
- 1993, National Polyp Study (1,418 patients after adenoma removal): CRC incidence was 76%–90% lower than in three reference groups (5 cancers observed vs 20.7–48.3 expected) [[Winawer1993]].
- 2012, National Polyp Study: after adenoma removal there were 12 CRC deaths vs 25.4 expected (standardised incidence-based mortality ratio 0.47), suggesting a 53% reduction in CRC mortality [[Zauber2012]].
- 2018: the ACS lowered its recommended starting age for average-risk screening to 45 [[Wolf2018]].
- 2021: the USPSTF recommended screening at 50–75 (A) and added 45–49 (B) [[USPSTF2021]].
- 2026: the ACS reaffirmed starting at 45 and continuing through 75. It added the next-generation multitarget stool DNA and multitarget stool RNA tests as preferred stool options every 3 years. Blood-based tests are recommended only for people who decline preferred tests [[Wolf2026]].

### history · Drug treatment
- 1957: Heidelberger and colleagues reported the fluorinated pyrimidines (5-fluorouracil) as a new class of tumour-inhibitory compounds [[Heidelberger1957]].
- 1990: adjuvant fluorouracil plus levamisole for resected stage C (node-positive) colon cancer cut recurrence by 41% and overall deaths by 33%. The authors concluded it should be standard treatment for stage C colon cancer [[Moertel1990]].
- 1992: a meta-analysis of nine trials showed leucovorin doubled tumour response to 5-FU (23% vs 11%) in advanced CRC, with no detectable overall survival difference [[ACCMAP1992]].
- 2004, MOSAIC: adding oxaliplatin to 5-FU/leucovorin after resection of stage II–III colon cancer improved 3-year disease-free survival (78.2% vs 72.9%; HR for recurrence 0.77) [[Andre2004]].
- 2004: bevacizumab plus IFL in untreated metastatic CRC raised median survival from 15.6 to 20.3 months (HR 0.66) [[Hurwitz2004]].
- 2004, BOND: cetuximab plus irinotecan in irinotecan-refractory metastatic CRC gave a 22.9% response rate vs 10.8% with cetuximab alone [[Cunningham2004]].
- 2015: PD-1 blockade with pembrolizumab achieved a 40% immune-related response rate in mismatch-repair-deficient CRC vs 0% in mismatch-repair-proficient CRC [[Le2015]].
- 2020, KEYNOTE-177: first-line pembrolizumab vs chemotherapy in MSI-H/dMMR metastatic CRC doubled median progression-free survival (16.5 vs 8.2 months; HR 0.60). Grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events were 22% vs 66% [[Andre2020]].
- 2022: dostarlimab alone in 12 patients with dMMR locally advanced rectal cancer gave a 100% clinical complete response. None needed chemoradiotherapy or surgery during 6–25 months of follow-up (Early evidence: 12 patients) [[Cercek2022]].

### Exam hooks
- CRC is the third most common cancer and the second leading cause of cancer death worldwide (GLOBOCAN 2022 and 2024) [[Bray2024,Sung2026]].
- Worldwide in 2024: about 2.0 million new CRC cases and 918,000 deaths [[Sung2026]].
- US lifetime risk of CRC is about 1 in 24 for men and 1 in 26 for women [[Siegel2025]].
- CRC is now the leading cause of cancer death in US adults under 50 [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Early-onset CRC rises are driven by the distal colon and rectum, a birth-cohort effect starting with generations born after about 1950 [[Siegel2026CRC,Siegel2017]].
- People born around 1990 have about 4 times the rectal cancer risk of those born around 1950 (IRR 4.32) [[Siegel2017]].
- Stage is the strongest predictor of survival: 5-year relative survival is 91% localized vs 15% distant [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Haematochezia is the commonest presenting feature of early-onset CRC (about 45%) and carries at least 5-fold risk [[Demb2024]].
- The PPV of rectal bleeding in primary care is about 2%–8%, depending on age and setting [[Hamilton2005,Astin2011,Jones2007]].
- The UK FIT threshold for urgent referral of symptomatic patients is fHb ≥10 µg Hb/g [[Monahan2022]].
- In the NICE FIT study, FIT NPV at 10 µg/g was 99.6% [[DSouza2021Gut]].
- About a quarter of English CRCs were diagnosed as emergencies (26%), with 1-year survival of 50% [[EllissBrookes2012]].
- Muto, Bussey and Morson (1975) established the adenoma-carcinoma (polyp-cancer) sequence [[Muto1975]].
- Fearon and Vogelstein proposed the multistep genetic model in 1990 [[Fearon1990]]. APC was cloned in 1991 [[Kinzler1991,Groden1991]].
- MSH2 was the first Lynch syndrome (HNPCC) mismatch-repair gene, identified in 1993 [[Fishel1993,Leach1993]].
- The Minnesota trial: annual guaiac FOBT cut CRC mortality by 33% at 13 years [[Mandel1993]].
- The National Polyp Study: polypectomy reduced CRC incidence by 76%–90% and CRC mortality by about 53% [[Winawer1993,Zauber2012]].
- Heald's TME (1982) removes the mesorectum intact to prevent pelvic recurrence [[Heald1982]].
- KEYNOTE-177 (2020) made pembrolizumab a first-line option for MSI-H/dMMR metastatic CRC [[Andre2020]].
- The USPSTF lowered the screening starting age to 45 in 2021 (B recommendation) [[USPSTF2021]].

### In plain words
- Bowel cancer is one of the most common cancers in the world. About 2 million people learn they have it each year [[Sung2026]].
- In the United States, about 1 in 24 men and 1 in 26 women will get bowel cancer during their life [[Siegel2025]].
- Most bowel cancer happens in people over 50, but it is becoming more common in younger adults. Doctors do not yet know why [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- In younger people, it most often starts in the lower part of the bowel, near the bottom [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- Blood from your bottom is the most common warning sign in younger people. Tummy pain and changes in your bowel habit are next [[Demb2024]].
- Most of the time, these symptoms are caused by something less serious, like piles (haemorrhoids) or irritable bowel. That is why a doctor needs to check them [[Monahan2022]].
- Feeling tired from low iron (anaemia) can be a sign of bowel cancer, especially in older adults [[Hamilton2008]].
- A simple poo test called FIT looks for tiny amounts of blood. It helps doctors decide who needs a camera test (colonoscopy) quickly [[Monahan2022]].
- Cancer found early is much easier to treat. In the US, about 9 in 10 people with cancer that has not spread are alive 5 years later. These are averages across many people, not a promise for any one person [[Siegel2026CRC]].
- About 1 in 4 bowel cancers in England were found only after an emergency hospital visit. People found this way did less well [[EllissBrookes2012]].
- Most bowel cancers grow slowly from small growths called polyps. Removing polyps during a colonoscopy can stop cancer before it starts [[Muto1975,Winawer1993]].
- Some families carry genes that raise bowel cancer risk, such as Lynch syndrome. The first such family was studied more than 100 years ago [[Douglas2005,Campos2024]].
- In the US, adults at average risk are advised to start screening at age 45 [[USPSTF2021,Wolf2026]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Why is early-onset CRC rising? Candidates include obesity, inactivity, diet quality, early-life antibiotics and the microbiome (Hypothesis) [[Akimoto2021,Patel2022EOCRC]]. ACS notes that established risk factors do not fully explain the rectal-predominant rise and lists ultra-processed foods and microplastics as active research areas [[Siegel2026CRC]]. Status: unresolved.
- How much of the young-adult rise is a coding artefact? GLOBOCAN 2024 notes that the 2013 ICD-O-3 reclassification of appendiceal carcinoids inflates young-onset counts. Studies excluding appendix still show a real increase with rising mortality [[Sung2026]]. Status: a real increase is the consensus; the size of the artefact varies by dataset.
- Right versus left colon: should these be treated as different diseases? Reviews describe different molecular pathways and therapy responses [[Baran2018,Lee2015]]. Whether right-sided tumours present later or with longer symptoms is contested: a 1,795-patient cohort found symptom duration did not differ by site [[Ozturk2019]], while FIT-negative cancers cluster in the right colon [[Monahan2022]].
- Which FIT threshold should trigger urgent referral in people with symptoms? UK guidance uses ≥10 µg Hb/g [[Monahan2022]]. Moving to 20 µg/g would miss fewer than one extra CRC per 1,000 tested at 2% prevalence [[PinVieito2022]]. A service evaluation found 10–19.9 µg/g carried a 1.4% CRC rate, below NICE's 3% PPV threshold [[Bailey2021]]. Lower thresholds (2 µg/g) increase sensitivity to 97% at the cost of more referrals [[DSouza2021Gut]].
- Does a longer diagnostic interval worsen survival? Evidence suggests a U-shaped relationship. The sickest patients are diagnosed fastest, which confounds simple analyses [[Torring2011]]. A cross-cancer review found more evidence of harm from delay for CRC than for many other cancers, but the studies were heterogeneous [[Neal2015]]. One cohort found no link between symptom duration and stage [[Stapley2006]].
- Should guaiac FOBT be judged to reduce incidence? Minnesota (with rehydration and annual testing) reduced incidence [[Mandel2000]]. Nottingham's 20-year follow-up did not, despite removal of 615 large adenomas [[Scholefield2012]].
- Is early-onset CRC overtreated? ACS raises overtreatment of young patients as an increasing concern under study in trials [[Siegel2026CRC]].
