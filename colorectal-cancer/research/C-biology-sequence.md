# C · Biology: the crypt, the adenoma–carcinoma sequence, the serrated pathway, CIN/MSI/CIMP, consensus molecular subtypes and metastasis

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: colon, sequence, genes (mechanism of mismatch repair), biomarkers (biology of MSI/RAS/BRAF), home.

This pass covers the normal colon and its crypts, the Wnt/APC "destruction complex", the stepwise adenoma–carcinoma sequence and how long it takes, the serrated pathway, the three instability pathways (chromosomal instability, microsatellite instability, CpG island methylator phenotype), the mismatch-repair machinery and why mismatch-repair-deficient tumours respond to PD-1 blockade, the consensus molecular subtypes, tumour sidedness, metastatic routes and frequencies, field effects, and the microbiome (Fusobacterium nucleatum; pks+ E. coli and colibactin).

## Findings

### colon · Anatomy and wall
- The large intestine runs from the caecum (at the terminal ileum) through the appendix, colon, rectum and anal canal. It is shorter than the small intestine but has a wider lumen, and it is marked by teniae coli (3 longitudinal smooth-muscle bands), haustra (sacculations) and omental appendices (fat-filled peritoneal pouches) [[Omole2025]].
- Main job: absorbing water, vitamins and electrolytes from indigestible material. The large intestine is roughly 5 feet long, about one-fifth of the length of the GI tract [[Azzouz2023,Omole2025]].
- Measured colon length on CT colonography in 295 adults (FIT-positive) was 150.3 ± 18.5 cm (range 109.7–195.9). Women had longer colons than men (154.3 vs 147.1 cm; p = 0.022) [[Utano2022]].
- The rectum is the most distal part of the large intestine, between the sigmoid colon and the anal canal, and is the most posterior pelvic viscus [[Wang2023]].
- The superior mesenteric artery (SMA; arising at L1) supplies the midgut colon: caecum, ascending colon and proximal transverse colon [[Omole2026b]].
- The inferior mesenteric artery (IMA; arising at L3) supplies the hindgut: the distal third of the transverse colon, the descending and sigmoid colon, and the upper rectum. It ends in the left colic, sigmoid and superior rectal arteries [[Omole2026a]].
- The marginal artery of Drummond links the SMA and IMA territories. Fewer than 50% of people have a fully continuous collateral around the splenic flexure (Griffith point), a watershed for ischaemia [[Omole2026a]].
- The sigmoid colon is a hindgut structure and shares the hindgut's blood supply, innervation and lymphatic drainage [[Harkins2023]].
- Lymphatic drainage follows the supplying vessels. Standard cancer surgery (complete mesocolic excision with central vascular ligation) removes the mesocolon as an intact envelope, with the lymph nodes, and ties the supplying arteries and draining veins at their roots [[Hohenberger2009]].
- In one surgical series of 1,329 R0 colon resections, consistent complete mesocolic excision went with a fall in 5-year local recurrence from 6.5% (1978–84) to 3.6% (1995–2002). Cancer-related 5-year survival rose from 82.1% to 89.1% (historical comparison, single centre) [[Hohenberger2009]].
- Embryology underlies "sidedness". Proximal (right, midgut) and distal (left, hindgut) colon differ in developmental origin, patterning-gene exposure, mutagen exposure and gut flora [[Missiaglia2014,Iacopetta2002]].

### colon · The crypt and its stem cells
- The intestinal epithelium is the most rapidly self-renewing tissue in adult mammals [[Barker2007]]. The single epithelial layer is renewed every 4–5 days [[VanDerFlier2009]].
- Lgr5, a Wnt target gene, marks cycling columnar cells at the crypt base in the small intestine and colon. In lineage tracing in adult mice, a single Lgr5+ cell generated all epithelial lineages over 60 days, which identifies it as the stem cell (Established; mouse evidence) [[Barker2007]].
- In the small intestine, Paneth cells sit between the Lgr5+ stem cells and supply Wnt3, EGF, TGF-α and the Notch ligand Dll4. Removing Paneth cells removes the stem cells [[Sato2011]].
- The colon has no Paneth cells. Reg4+ deep crypt secretory (DCS) cells sit among the Lgr5+ stem cells and act as "Paneth cell equivalents". Ablating them in mice causes loss of colonic stem cells [[Sasaki2016,Sato2011]].
- Human colonic crypts each hold between 5 and 10 active stem cells (mean 7). Stem cells replace one another 0.65–2.7 times per crypt per year (mean 1.3) [[Nicholson2018]].
- Mitochondrial-DNA lineage tracing gives a "functional" stem-cell number of 5–6 per human colonic crypt. The stem cells follow neutral drift [[Baker2014]].
- A new mutation becomes "fixed" in a crypt in two steps. First, one of about 7 stem cells is mutated. Second, its progeny take over the whole crypt, which takes several years [[Nicholson2018]].
- Normal human colonic crypts divide (crypt fission) about once every 30–40 years. In adenomas the fission rate rises by at least an order of magnitude [[Baker2014]]. About 0.7% of crypts undergo fission each year [[Nicholson2018]].
- Crypts also fuse, which balances fission. Fission is estimated at about 0.011 divisions per crypt per year (Early evidence) [[Baker2019]].
- Goblet cells (mucin producers) counted per crypt in tissue sections: 72 (ascending) and 75 (descending colon) in adults up to 60 years, versus 51 and 54 in adults aged 67 or older [[Baidoo2024]].
- Driver mutations are already present in about 1% of normal-looking colorectal crypts in middle-aged people. Adenomas and carcinomas are rare outcomes of widespread neoplastic change in normal epithelium [[LeeSix2019]].
- Crypt organisation explains how mutations in stem cells become fixed and spread, and so underlies theories of how adenomas begin [[Humphries2008]].

### sequence · Wnt signalling and the APC destruction complex
- Without a Wnt signal, a "destruction complex" breaks down β-catenin. The complex contains APC and Axin (tumour suppressors), the kinases GSK-3 and CK1, PP2A and the E3 ubiquitin ligase β-TrCP [[Stamos2013]].
- The steps are: Axin scaffolds the kinases, which phosphorylate a Ser/Thr-rich motif near β-catenin's N-terminus. β-TrCP recognises the phosphorylated motif and ubiquitinates β-catenin, which the proteasome then degrades [[Stamos2013]].
- Wnts drive most adult tissue stem cells, and mutated Wnt pathway components cause cancer [[Nusse2017,Clevers2012]].
- β-catenin activates transcription only when bound to Tcf-4 (TCF7L2). Nuclei of APC-/- colon cancer cells contain a constitutively active β-catenin–Tcf-4 complex, and putting APC back removes β-catenin from Tcf-4 [[Korinek1997]].
- Colorectal tumours with intact APC carried activating β-catenin (CTNNB1) mutations at its phosphorylation sites. So the pathway can be switched on by mutating either APC or β-catenin [[Morin1997]].
- TCGA: 93% of colorectal cancers had a deregulated Wnt pathway (93% of non-hypermutated and 97% of hypermutated tumours) [[TCGA2012]].
- In about 80% of cases this came from biallelic APC inactivation or activating CTNNB1 mutation [[TCGA2012]].

### sequence · The adenoma–carcinoma sequence (Fearon–Vogelstein)
- Fearon and Vogelstein's 1990 model: colorectal cancer results from the mutational activation of oncogenes plus inactivation of several tumour-suppressor genes. The accumulation of changes matters more than their exact order (Established) [[Fearon1990]].
- Evidence base for the model (172 tumours at different stages) [[Vogelstein1988]]:
  - ras mutations: 9% of adenomas under 1 cm, 58% of adenomas over 1 cm, 47% of carcinomas.
  - Chromosome 18q deletion: 11–13% of early adenomas, 47% of advanced adenomas, 73% of carcinomas.
  - Chromosome 17p (TP53) loss: 75% of carcinomas, and usually only in carcinomas.
- APC mutations occur early. They were found in 63% of adenomas and 60% of carcinomas (41 tumours), including adenomas as small as 0.5 cm, and the frequency stayed constant from benign to malignant stages [[Powell1992]].
- In Vogelstein's "gatekeeper" summary:
  - An APC mutation in the colon gives a microscopic clone that grows into a slow-growing small adenoma.
  - A second mutation (such as KRAS) unleashes a second clonal expansion.
  - Further mutations (PIK3CA, SMAD4, TP53) produce a malignant, invasive tumour that can metastasise [[Vogelstein2013]].
- A typical tumour carries 2–8 "driver" mutations. The rest are passengers. About 140 driver genes are known across cancers [[Vogelstein2013]].
- Because colonic stem cells divide throughout life, a colorectal tumour in a 90-year-old has nearly twice as many (passenger) mutations as an identical tumour in a 45-year-old [[Vogelstein2013]].
- TCGA (276 tumours) [[TCGA2012]]:
  - 16% of colorectal carcinomas were hypermutated.
  - Among non-hypermutated tumours, the eight most frequently mutated genes were APC, TP53, KRAS, PIK3CA, FBXW7, SMAD4, TCF7L2 and NRAS.
- Hypermutated tumours carried APC mutations less often than non-hypermutated tumours (51% vs 81%, P = 0.0023), and TP53 mutations less often (20% vs 60%, P < 0.0001) [[TCGA2012]].
- Further TCGA frequencies in non-hypermutated tumours [[TCGA2012]]:
  - KRAS, NRAS or BRAF altered in 55% (mutually exclusive).
  - PIK3CA mutated in 15%.
  - TP53 pathway altered in 59%.
- Chromosome-arm deletions in TCGA tumours: 18p/q (which includes SMAD4) in 66%, and 17p/q (which includes TP53) in 56% [[TCGA2012]].
- The TGF-β/SMAD pathway (TGFBR1/2, ACVR2A, ACVR1B, SMAD2/3/4) was altered in 27% of non-hypermutated and 87% of hypermutated tumours [[TCGA2012]].
- Excluding hypermutated cancers, colon and rectal cancers had very similar patterns of genomic alteration [[TCGA2012]].
- BRAF-mutant tumours were 11% (57/524) of metastatic CRC in a two-centre cohort. They were linked to right-sided primaries, MSI and shorter median survival (10.4 vs 34.7 months) [[Tran2011]].
- Population evidence that adenomas are precursors: in the National Polyp Study, removing adenomas lowered colorectal cancer incidence by 76–90% versus three reference groups (1,418 patients, mean 5.9 years) [[Winawer1993]].
- Long-term follow-up of the same study found colorectal cancer mortality of 0.47 (95% CI 0.26–0.80) versus that expected from SEER, a 53% reduction [[Zauber2012]].
- Reviews class the evidence for the adenoma–carcinoma sequence as epidemiological, clinicopathological and genetic [[Leslie2002]].
- Cancer is described by hallmark capabilities, in this order of listing [[Hanahan2011]]:
  - The six original capabilities: sustained proliferative signalling, evading growth suppressors, resisting cell death, replicative immortality, angiogenesis, and invasion/metastasis.
  - Enabling characteristics: genome instability and inflammation.
  - Emerging hallmarks: energy-metabolism reprogramming and immune evasion.
- The 2022 update proposes these provisional additions [[Hanahan2022]]:
  - Phenotypic plasticity as a new hallmark.
  - Non-mutational epigenetic reprogramming and "polymorphic microbiomes" as new enabling characteristics.

### sequence · How long it takes (dwell time and progression rates)
- Jones 2008 used comparative lesion sequencing: shared mutations give the time between benign, invasive and metastatic clones [[Jones2008]]:
  - About 17 years for a large benign tumour to evolve into an advanced cancer.
  - Under 2 years for cells within that cancer to gain the ability to metastasise.
  - Few, if any, selective events are needed for that step.
- Preclinical (screen-detectable) colorectal cancer lasts a mean of 4.5 years (95% CI 4.1–4.8) to 5.8 years (5.3–6.3) before symptoms. Estimates were similar for both sexes and all age groups from 55 to ≥80 years (German screening colonoscopy registry, 1.88 million records) [[Brenner2011]].
- Annual transition from advanced adenoma to colorectal cancer, from 840,149 German screening colonoscopies [[Brenner2007]]:
  - Women: 2.6% per year at ages 55–59, rising to 5.6% per year at ≥80.
  - Men: 2.6% to 5.1% per year.
- Projected 10-year cumulative risk of cancer in a person carrying an advanced adenoma [[Brenner2007]]:
  - Women: 25.4% at age 55, 42.9% at age 80.
  - Men: 25.2% at age 55, 39.7% at age 80.
- Untreated polyps ≥10 mm followed by barium enema (226 patients, Mayo Clinic, pre-colonoscopy era) [[Stryker1987]]:
  - Cumulative risk of cancer at the polyp site: 2.5% at 5 years, 8% at 10 years, 24% at 20 years.
  - 37% of polyps enlarged.
  - Invasive cancers appeared at a mean of 108 months.
- Small 6–9 mm polyps watched by CT colonography over a mean of 4.7 years (382 patients) [[Pooler2023]]:
  - 24.9% grew, 62.0% stayed stable and 13.1% regressed.
  - Growing polyps more often had advanced histology (23.2% vs 7.0%; OR 4.0).
- Pooled prevalence in average-risk North Americans (18 studies) [[Heitman2009]]:
  - Any adenoma 30.2%.
  - Non-advanced adenoma 17.7%.
  - Advanced adenoma 5.7%.
  - Colorectal cancer 0.3%.
  - Prevalence was higher in cohorts with mean age ≥65.
- Metastatic seeding may happen early. In 17 of 21 evaluable patients (81%), modelling of 118 biopsies from 23 patients suggested that disseminated cells seeded metastases while the primary was clinically undetectable, typically under 0.01 cm³ (Early evidence) [[Hu2019]].
- The "Big Bang" model (349 glands from 15 tumours) proposes that a colorectal tumour grows mainly as one expansion. Most detectable subclonal alterations arise early, and some tumours are "born to be bad" (Early evidence) [[Sottoriva2015]].

### sequence · Serrated pathway
- WHO 2019 groups serrated polyps into hyperplastic polyps, traditional serrated adenomas (TSA) and sessile serrated lesions (SSL). TSAs and SSLs are cancer precursors [[Crockett2019]].
- The serrated pathway is characterised by RAS and RAF mutations, disrupted Wnt signalling and widespread CpG island methylation [[Crockett2019]].
- Serrated-class polyps are found in 20–40% of average-risk people, mostly hyperplastic. SSLs, the commonest premalignant serrated type, are found in up to 15% by high-detecting endoscopists [[Crockett2019]].
- Risk factors for SSLs include white race, a family history of CRC, smoking and alcohol [[Crockett2019]].
- Share of colorectal cancers arising by the serrated route; estimates disagree [[IJspeert2015,Bettington2017,Snover2011]]:
  - At least 15% (IJspeert 2015; Bettington 2017).
  - About 35% (Snover 2011, whose estimate splits CRC into roughly 60% conventional-adenoma, 35% serrated and 5% Lynch).
- Post-colonoscopy (interval) cancers have been suggested to arise even more often from serrated polyps [[IJspeert2015]].
- Sessile serrated adenomas with dysplasia or carcinoma (137 lesions) [[Bettington2017]]:
  - Median size 9 mm; 86.5% proximal.
  - 92.7% BRAF-mutated; 94.0% CIMP-positive.
  - 74.5% had lost MLH1 (mismatch-repair deficient).
- These "caught in the act" lesions are rare and occur at a similar age to SSLs with carcinoma. This suggests a long dwell time as a non-dysplastic SSL, then a rapid transition to cancer once dysplasia appears (Early evidence) [[Bettington2017]].
- CIMP-positive tumours include almost all BRAF-mutant colorectal cancers (odds ratio 203). Sporadic mismatch-repair deficiency arises almost only from CIMP-associated MLH1 methylation [[Weisenberger2006]].
- Toyota 1999 defined CIMP. Of 30 differentially methylated loci, 19 (63%) became methylated with age in normal colon and 7 (23%) were methylated only in cancers. The cancer-specific set defined a CIMP+ subset that includes most sporadic MSI cancers with MLH1 methylation [[Toyota1999]].
- Hypermethylation of the MLH1 promoter is found in most sporadic MSI colorectal cancers and silences MLH1 protein. Demethylation with 5-aza-2'-deoxycytidine restored MLH1 expression and mismatch-repair capacity in cell lines [[Herman1998]].
- Cancers from serrated lesions are not prevented by removing conventional adenomas and may be missed in routine screening [[Snover2011]].

### genes · Genomic instability: CIN, MSI, CIMP
- There are at least 3 pathways of genomic instability in CRC: chromosomal instability (CIN), microsatellite instability (MSI) and the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP). Most CRCs arise through CIN [[Pino2010]].
- CIN means widespread aneuploidy and loss of heterozygosity. It can come from defects in chromosome segregation, telomere stability and the DNA damage response [[Pino2010]].
- Colorectal cancer cells without MSI gain or lose chromosomes at rates above 10⁻² per chromosome per generation. This persistent CIN behaves as a dominant trait, whereas MSI is recessive [[Lengauer1997]].
- MSI is found in about 15% of colorectal cancers [[Boland2010,Vilar2010]]:
  - About 3% are Lynch syndrome (germline mismatch-repair mutation).
  - About 12% are sporadic, from MLH1 promoter hypermethylation in CIMP tumours.
- TCGA [[TCGA2012]]:
  - 16% of CRCs were hypermutated, defined as more than 12 mutations per 10⁶ bases (median 728 total mutations) versus a median of 58 non-silent mutations in non-hypermutated tumours.
  - Three-quarters of hypermutated tumours were MSI-high, usually with MLH1 silencing.
  - One-quarter had somatic mismatch-repair or POLE mutations.
- Hypermutated tumours are near-diploid, with far fewer copy-number changes, and enriched for hypermethylation, CIMP and BRAF V600E [[TCGA2012]].
- MSI discovery, 1993:
  - About 12% of colorectal carcinomas carried somatic deletions in poly(dA·dT) repeats, an estimated 100,000+ such mutations per cell. The authors proposed a "mutator mutation" [[Ionov1993]].
  - MSI at (CA)n repeats was found in 28% (25/90) of colorectal tumours. It went with proximal location, longer survival and no LOH at 5q, 17p and 18q [[Thibodeau1993]].
- MSI tumours tend to arise in the proximal colon. They have a lymphocytic infiltrate and a poorly differentiated, mucinous or signet-ring appearance. Prognosis is slightly better than for tumours without MSI [[Boland2010]].
- Lynch prevalence among people with CRC [[Hampel2005,Hampel2008]]:
  - Columbus, Ohio (1,066 patients): 19.5% of tumours were MSI and 2.2% of patients had Lynch syndrome (Hampel 2005).
  - Pooled cohort of 1,566 patients: 2.8% (95% CI 2.1–3.8%), about 1 in 35 patients (Hampel 2008).

### genes · How mismatch repair works and why dMMR tumours carry neoantigens
- Mismatch repair (MMR) corrects base–base mismatches and insertion/deletion mispairs that escape the polymerase's own proofreading. It improves replication fidelity by several orders of magnitude, and losing it creates a mutator phenotype [[Jiricny2006,Kunkel2005,Li2008]].
- Microsatellites are short repeats such as (A)n or (CA)n. They are especially error-prone because the polymerase slips: the new strand dissociates and re-anneals out of register, leaving extrahelical loops (insertion–deletion loops) [[Vilar2010]].
- The repair steps, in order [[Vilar2010,Boland2010,Li2008]]:
  - Recognition: MSH2 pairs with MSH6 (MutSα, best at single-base mismatches) or with MSH3 (MutSβ, larger insertion–deletion loops).
  - Recruitment: MLH1 pairs with PMS2 (MutLα), and the MutS–MutL sliding clamp interacts with replication factor C/PCNA.
  - Excision: exonuclease 1 removes the error-containing strand.
  - Resynthesis and ligation: DNA polymerase δ and DNA ligase.
- Insertions or deletions in microsatellites inside coding regions cause frameshifts that truncate proteins [[Vilar2010]].
- Genes with coding repeats hit by MSI include TGFβR2, ACVR2, BAX and caspase-5. Every human MMR gene except MLH1 contains a mononucleotide repeat of at least A7, so repair can decay further as components are lost [[Boland2010]].
- In TCGA's hypermutated tumours, ACVR2A, APC, TGFBR2, MSH3 and MSH6 were frequent targets, along with mostly BRAF V600E [[TCGA2012]].
- Mutation load by mismatch-repair status (whole-exome sequencing) [[Le2015]]:
  - dMMR tumours: mean 1,782 somatic mutations per tumour.
  - pMMR tumours: mean 73 (P = 0.007).
  - High mutation load was associated with longer progression-free survival (P = 0.02).
- Pembrolizumab in metastatic colorectal cancer, by MMR status [[Le2015]]:
  - dMMR: immune-related objective response 40% (4/10); 20-week progression-free survival 78% (7/9).
  - pMMR: 0% (0/18) and 11% (2/18).
- Across 12 tumour types with dMMR, PD-1 blockade gave objective responses in 53% and complete responses in 21% [[Le2017]].
- In a responder, neoantigen-specific T-cell clones that recognised mutant tumour peptides expanded rapidly. This supports the idea that the large neoantigen load makes dMMR cancers sensitive to checkpoint blockade, regardless of tissue of origin [[Le2017]].

### biomarkers · Consensus molecular subtypes (CMS)
- Six expression-based classification systems converged on four consensus molecular subtypes (CMS1–CMS4) [[Guinney2015]]:

  | Subtype | Share | Main features |
  |---|---|---|
  | CMS1, MSI immune | 14% | Hypermutated, MSI, strong immune activation |
  | CMS2, canonical | 37% | Epithelial, marked WNT and MYC activation |
  | CMS3, metabolic | 13% | Epithelial, metabolic dysregulation |
  | CMS4, mesenchymal | 23% | TGF-β activation, stromal invasion, angiogenesis |
  | Mixed | 13% | Possibly transitional or intratumoural heterogeneity |

- CMS1 had few copy-number alterations and widespread hypermethylation and held the majority of MSI tumours. It was enriched for BRAF mutations, more common in women and right-sided, and of higher grade [[Guinney2015]].
- CMS2–4 showed higher CIN. CMS2 had more oncogene gains and tumour-suppressor losses and was mainly left-sided [[Guinney2015]].
- CMS3 had fewer copy-number alterations, nearly 30% hypermutated, more CIMP, and over-represented KRAS mutations [[Guinney2015]].
- No CMS is defined by a single mutation, and no genetic aberration is limited to one subtype [[Guinney2015]].
- Prognosis by CMS [[Guinney2015]]:
  - CMS4 had worse overall and relapse-free survival after adjusting for MSI, BRAF and KRAS.
  - CMS2 had better survival after relapse.
  - CMS1 had very poor survival after relapse.
- Combining genomic and transcriptomic subtypes (tumour, stroma and immune) points towards "multi-gene, multi-drug" treatment models (Hypothesis) [[Dienstmann2017]].

### biomarkers · Tumour sidedness
- Proximal carcinomas (PETACC-3, 3,045 patients) were more often mucinous, MSI-high, BRAF-like and serrated-signature. Distal carcinomas were more often CIN with EGFR or HER2 amplification and epiregulin over-expression [[Missiaglia2014]].
- In the same cohort, survival after relapse in stage III was worse for proximal tumours (HR 1.95, 95% CI 1.6–2.4) [[Missiaglia2014]].
- MSI and CIMP are linked to right-sided tumours, and CIN to left-sided tumours. Lynch syndrome and FAP tumours occur predominantly in the right and left colon, respectively [[Iacopetta2002]].
- Meta-analysis of 66 studies (1,437,846 patients): left-sided primary location was associated with lower risk of death (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.79–0.84) [[Petrelli2017]].
- Pooled analysis of six RAS wild-type metastatic CRC trials (2,159 patients). Right-sided versus left-sided overall survival HR was 2.03 (1.69–2.42) in control arms and 1.38 (1.17–1.63) with anti-EGFR therapy [[Arnold2017]].
- Chemotherapy plus EGFR antibody helped left-sided tumours (OS HR 0.75, 0.67–0.84) but not right-sided tumours (HR 1.12, 0.87–1.45) [[Arnold2017]].
- Left-sided tumours (splenic flexure and distal) made up 63–74% of patients in three metastatic cohorts. In PROVETTA they had better overall survival (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.28–0.70) [[Loupakis2015]].
- US data show a shift towards left-sided tumours: rectal cancer rose from 27% of CRC in 1995 to 31% in 2019 [[Siegel2023CRC]].

### sequence · Metastasis: routes and frequencies
- Synchronous liver metastases: 14.5% of patients in the Burgundy population registry. The 5-year cumulative metachronous liver metastasis rate was also 14.5%, by stage [[Manfredi2006]]:
  - Stage I: 3.7%.
  - Stage II: 13.3%.
  - Stage III: 30.4%.
- German registry (5,772 patients, 2002–07): 24.7% developed liver metastases. 71% of these were synchronous (about 17.7% of all patients) and 29% metachronous [[Hackl2014]].
- Stockholm 2008 cohort (1,026 patients): liver metastases in 26.5% within 5 years [[Engstrand2018]].
- In that cohort, liver and lung metastases were more frequent in left-sided than right-sided colon cancer (28.4% vs 22.1% liver; 19.7% vs 13.2% lung) [[Engstrand2018]].
- The Stockholm authors' background states that liver metastases develop in 25–30% of people with CRC [[Engstrand2018]].
- Lung metastases, Burgundy registry 1976–2005 [[Mitry2010]]:
  - Synchronous lung metastases in 11.0%.
  - 5-year cumulative risk of metachronous lung metastases 5.8%.
  - Rectal versus colon cancer: OR 2.80 for synchronous and 2.63 for metachronous lung metastases.
- Swedish registry (49,096 patients), rectal versus colon cancer [[Riihimaki2016]]:
  - More often spread to thoracic organs (OR 2.4) and the nervous system (OR 1.5).
  - Less often within the peritoneum (OR 0.3).
- In rectal cancer with low stage at diagnosis, thoracic metastases were almost as common as liver metastases [[Riihimaki2016]].
- Anatomical rationale (unverified): the portal venous drainage of the colon and upper rectum carries tumour cells to the liver first, while lower-rectal veins drain to the systemic (caval) circulation and reach the lungs. This is textbook anatomy and is not stated in the cited abstracts.
- Mucinous and signet-ring adenocarcinomas spread to the peritoneum more often (OR 3.8 colon, 3.2 rectum) and to the liver less often (OR 0.5/0.6) [[Riihimaki2016]].
- Peritoneal carcinomatosis in 11,124 Stockholm patients [[Segelman2012]]:
  - 8.3% overall: synchronous 4.3%, metachronous cumulative incidence 4.2%.
  - Peritoneum was the only metastatic site in 4.8%.
  - Right-sided colon cancer predicted metachronous peritoneal spread (HR 1.77).
- BRAF-mutant metastatic CRC has a distinct pattern of metastatic spread [[Tran2011]].
- In paired biopsies, the primary tumour and its metastases differ little genetically, and canonical drivers were acquired early (Early evidence) [[Hu2019]].

### home · Field effects
- "Field effect" (field cancerisation) means a field of cellular and molecular change that predisposes to neoplasia across a territory [[Lochhead2015]].
- The broader "etiologic field effect" adds diet, lifestyle, microbes, hormones and genes acting on the whole tissue. It can explain shared molecular features between synchronous colorectal cancers even when intervening normal colon lacks those changes (Hypothesis) [[Lochhead2015]].
- Normal-appearing colorectal crypts accumulate mutations from several processes. Some processes are ubiquitous, while others occur only in some people, some crypts or some periods of life [[LeeSix2019]].
- The age-related CpG island methylation seen in normal colon overlaps with most loci methylated in colon cancer, a form of epigenetic field change [[Toyota1999]].

### home · Microbiome contributors (labelled by evidence)
- Fusobacterium nucleatum [[Kostic2012,Castellarin2012]]:
  - Sequences were enriched in colorectal carcinomas versus matched normal tissue (Kostic: 9 genome pairs, then 95 qPCR pairs; Castellarin: 99 subjects, p = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶).
  - Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes were depleted in tumours (Kostic).
  - Tumour Fusobacterium was associated with lymph-node metastasis (Castellarin) (Established association).
- A high tumour load of F. nucleatum DNA was associated with higher CRC-specific mortality (multivariable HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.04–2.39) and with MSI-high (OR 5.22) (Early evidence; cohort of 1,069) [[Mima2016]].
- Fusobacterium and its associated microbiome persist in distant metastases and through xenograft passages. Metronidazole reduced Fusobacterium load and tumour growth in mice (Early evidence; preclinical) [[Bullman2017]].
- Commensal E. coli carrying the pks genomic island make colibactin, a genotoxin that causes DNA double-strand breaks and cell-cycle arrest in cultured cells [[Nougayrede2006]].
- In colitis-prone mice, pks+ E. coli promoted invasive carcinoma, and deleting pks reduced tumours. Mucosa-associated pks+ E. coli were common in people with IBD and CRC (Early evidence) [[Arthur2012]].
- People with FAP had patchy colonic biofilms of E. coli and Bacteroides fragilis, with enriched colibactin (clbB) and B. fragilis toxin (bft) genes. Co-colonised mice developed tumours faster (Early evidence) [[Dejea2018]].
- Five months of exposure to pks+ E. coli produced a distinct mutational signature (SBS-pks/ID-pks) in human intestinal organoids. The same signature appeared in a subset of 5,876 human cancer genomes, mainly CRC (Established mechanism; signature) [[PleguezuelosManzano2020]].
- In the 100,000 Genomes cohort (2,208 CRCs), SBS-pks and ID-pks were enriched in 5.0% and 4.4% of patients. 5.3% of APC driver mutations matched the colibactin motif [[PleguezuelosManzano2020]].
- Colibactin-induced double-strand breaks sit in an AT-rich hexameric motif. That motif is enriched among CRC mutations and matches mutational hotspots in cancer genomes [[DziubanskaKusibab2020]].
- Díaz-Gay 2025 (981 CRC genomes from 11 countries; Early evidence for the early-onset link) [[DiazGay2025]]:
  - Colibactin signatures SBS88 and ID18 were 3.3 times more common in people diagnosed before 40 than after 70.
  - The signatures were imprinted early in tumour development.
  - Mutation loads were higher in countries with higher CRC incidence.
  - ID18 accounted for about 25% of APC driver indels in colibactin-positive cases.
  - The authors suggest that early-life exposure to colibactin may contribute to rising early-onset CRC.
- Reviews also implicate certain E. coli and B. fragilis strains, and propose the microbiota as screening, prognostic or predictive biomarkers (Hypothesis for clinical use) [[Wong2019]].
- "Polymorphic microbiomes" are proposed as an enabling characteristic of cancer (Hypothesis) [[Hanahan2022]].

### home · Burden and "bad luck"
- Colorectal cancer is the world's fourth most deadly cancer, with almost 900,000 deaths a year [[Dekker2019]]. In the US in 2023, an estimated 153,020 people were diagnosed and 52,550 died [[Siegel2023CRC]].
- Lifetime cancer risk across tissues correlated (r = 0.81) with the total number of normal stem-cell divisions. FAP carries about 30 times the colorectal than duodenal cancer risk, matching about 150 times as many stem-cell divisions in the colon (Hypothesis; contested) [[Tomasetti2015]].
- A follow-up estimated that replication errors account for about two-thirds of mutations in human cancers. The authors argue that this is compatible with the fraction preventable by environmental change (Hypothesis; contested) [[Tomasetti2017]].

### Exam hooks
- APC is the "gatekeeper". It is mutated early (63% of adenomas, including ones of 0.5 cm), and its frequency does not rise from adenoma to carcinoma [[Powell1992]].
- Loss of APC stabilises β-catenin, which binds TCF4 and switches on Wnt target genes constitutively [[Korinek1997,Morin1997]].
- In the Fearon–Vogelstein model, the accumulation of oncogene and tumour-suppressor hits matters more than their order [[Fearon1990]].
- KRAS mutations appear as adenomas grow (9% under 1 cm vs 58% over 1 cm). 18q loss marks advanced adenomas, and 17p/TP53 loss marks the transition to carcinoma [[Vogelstein1988]].
- MSI is found in about 15% of CRC: about 3% Lynch and about 12% sporadic MLH1 promoter methylation [[Boland2010]].
- Sporadic MSI with BRAF V600E points to MLH1 methylation within CIMP, not Lynch syndrome [[Weisenberger2006,Herman1998]].
- MutSα = MSH2+MSH6 (mismatch recognition). MutLα = MLH1+PMS2 (recruits the excision machinery) [[Vilar2010,Boland2010]].
- dMMR tumours carry about 25 times more somatic mutations than pMMR tumours (mean 1,782 vs 73) and respond to PD-1 blockade [[Le2015]].
- CMS: CMS1 14% MSI-immune; CMS2 37% canonical WNT/MYC; CMS3 13% metabolic/KRAS; CMS4 23% mesenchymal, worst prognosis [[Guinney2015]].
- Sessile serrated lesions are mostly proximal, BRAF-mutant and CIMP-positive. Once dysplasia appears they can progress quickly [[Bettington2017]].
- About 17 years pass from large adenoma to advanced cancer, but under 2 years to metastatic capability [[Jones2008]].
- An advanced adenoma becomes cancer at about 2.6–5.6% per year, rising with age [[Brenner2007]].
- Rectal cancer metastasises to the lung more often than colon cancer (synchronous OR 2.80) [[Mitry2010]].
- Right-sided tumours: MSI/CIMP/BRAF, more often CMS1, worse prognosis in metastatic disease, and little benefit from anti-EGFR therapy [[Missiaglia2014,Arnold2017]].
- The colon has no Paneth cells. Reg4+ deep crypt secretory cells form the niche [[Sasaki2016]].
- Hypermutated tumours (16% in TCGA) are near-diploid. Non-hypermutated tumours show CIN, with 18q loss in 66% and 17p loss in 56% [[TCGA2012]].
- Colibactin from pks+ E. coli leaves a specific mutational signature (SBS88/ID18) in CRC genomes [[PleguezuelosManzano2020]].

### In plain words
- The inside of your bowel is lined with millions of tiny pits called crypts. Each has a few stem cells at the bottom that keep making new lining cells [[Nicholson2018,Barker2007]].
- The bowel lining replaces itself every few days, which is why mistakes in copying DNA can add up over a lifetime [[VanDerFlier2009,Vogelstein2013]].
- Most bowel cancers start as a small, harmless growth called a polyp (an adenoma). A few polyps slowly pick up more gene changes and turn into cancer [[Fearon1990,Vogelstein2013]].
- This change is usually slow: often 10 years or more. That is why finding and removing polyps can prevent cancer [[Jones2008,Winawer1993]].
- About 3 in 10 adults of screening age have at least one adenoma, but only about 3 in 1,000 have cancer when screened [[Heitman2009]].
- Removing adenomas during colonoscopy roughly halved deaths from bowel cancer in one long study [[Zauber2012]].
- Some cancers grow from flat, hard-to-see "sessile serrated" lesions, often on the right side of the colon [[Crockett2019,Bettington2017]].
- In about 15 in 100 bowel cancers, the cell's spell-checker for DNA (mismatch repair) is broken [[Boland2010]].
- A broken spell-checker means many more mutations. That makes the cancer easier for the immune system to spot, which is why immunotherapy can work so well for these tumours [[Le2015,Le2017]].
- About 3 in 100 people with bowel cancer have Lynch syndrome, an inherited spell-checker fault that can run in families [[Hampel2008]].
- Bowel cancer most often spreads to the liver, because blood from the bowel goes to the liver first. Cancers low in the rectum spread to the lungs more often [[Hackl2014,Mitry2010]].
- Survival figures here describe large groups of people, not any one person [[Petrelli2017]].
- Some gut bacteria make a chemical that can damage DNA. Scientists are studying whether this helps explain bowel cancer in younger people, but it is not yet proven [[PleguezuelosManzano2020,DiazGay2025]].

Scrubber design note (for builders; a composite of the anchors above, not a single-study timeline). Suggested order for a 10–15+ year adenoma–carcinoma animation:
1. Normal crypt: about 7 stem cells, Wnt high at the base, renewal every 4–5 days [[Nicholson2018,VanDerFlier2009]].
2. An APC hit in one stem cell. It takes over the crypt (fixation over several years), then crypt fission speeds up [[Nicholson2018,Baker2014,Powell1992]].
3. Small adenoma under 1 cm; ras mutation present in only 9% [[Vogelstein1988]].
4. KRAS hit leads to a second clonal expansion, an adenoma over 1 cm (ras in 58%) [[Vogelstein1988,Vogelstein2013]].
5. 18q/SMAD4 loss gives an advanced adenoma (18q loss in 47%) [[Vogelstein1988]].
6. TP53/17p loss gives invasive carcinoma (17p loss in 75%) [[Vogelstein1988]]. The total from large adenoma to advanced cancer is about 17 years [[Jones2008]].
7. The preclinical, screen-detectable cancer lasts about 4.5–5.8 years [[Brenner2011]].
8. The metastatic clone arises within under 2 years [[Jones2008]], possibly earlier [[Hu2019]].
9. Spread goes to the liver via portal blood and to the lungs (more often from rectal tumours) [[Manfredi2006,Mitry2010]].

## Open questions and controversies
- **How long does the sequence take, and when does metastasis start?** Position A: a long benign phase, about 17 years from large adenoma to advanced cancer, with metastatic capacity late (under 2 years) [[Jones2008]]. Position B: dissemination is often early, while the primary is under 0.01 cm³ [[Hu2019]], and tumours grow as a "Big Bang" with early heterogeneity [[Sottoriva2015]]. Status: both are model-based inferences (Early evidence), and they may reflect different tumours.
- **What share of CRC is serrated?** "≥15%" [[IJspeert2015,Bettington2017]] versus "~35%" [[Snover2011]]. Status: there are no longitudinal studies of individual serrated lesions, so the estimates are indirect [[IJspeert2015]].
- **How common are liver metastases?** Population registries give 24.7–26.5% overall and 14.5–17.5% synchronous [[Hackl2014,Engstrand2018,Manfredi2006]]. The often-quoted "~50%" was not found in the validated sources. Status: the figure depends on population, follow-up length and imaging era.
- **"Bad luck" versus environment.** Stem-cell divisions correlate with cancer risk (r = 0.81), and replication errors are proposed to cause about two-thirds of mutations [[Tomasetti2015,Tomasetti2017]]. Against this, geographic variation in mutational signatures implies environmental mutagens such as colibactin [[DiazGay2025]]. Status: contested (Hypothesis).
- **Is colibactin a driver of early-onset CRC?** Signatures are 3.3 times enriched before age 40 and imprinted early [[DiazGay2025]], with direct mutagenesis shown in organoids [[PleguezuelosManzano2020]]. Causation in humans and preventability are unproven. Status: Early evidence.
- **Is Fusobacterium a cause or a passenger?** It is enriched in tumours and persists in metastases, and metronidazole slowed xenografts [[Kostic2012,Castellarin2012,Bullman2017]]. It is also associated with prognosis [[Mima2016]]. Status: association established; causal role in humans unproven.
- **Is the CMS classification clinically actionable?** Subtypes are prognostic [[Guinney2015]], but no single mutation defines them, and their use for treatment choice remains investigational [[Dienstmann2017]].
- **Sidedness versus molecular biology.** Side is prognostic and predicts anti-EGFR benefit [[Petrelli2017,Arnold2017]], but much of its effect overlaps with MSI, BRAF and CMS [[Missiaglia2014,Guinney2015]]. Status: side is used as a practical surrogate.
