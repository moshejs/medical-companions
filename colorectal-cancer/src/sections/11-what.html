What colorectal cancer is

Colorectal cancer, also called bowel cancer, starts in the lining of the colon or rectum. It is one of the most common cancers in the world. Scrub through who gets it, where in the bowel it starts, and why doctors now worry about younger adults. Switch between new cases and deaths to see how the picture changes.

Survival has improved, and stage matters most

Five-year relative survival compares people with colorectal cancer with people of the same age and sex in the general population. These are averages over many thousands of people diagnosed years ago. They describe groups, not any one person, and they lag behind newer treatments. [[Siegel2026CRC,Siegel2026]]

Early-onset colorectal cancer: what is known

Established

It is rising, mostly on the left

In the US, incidence at ages 20–49 has risen 1–2% a year since the mid-1990s, and 3% a year from 2013 to 2022. From 1998 to 2022 sigmoid colon cancer in this age group nearly doubled (2.1 to 3.7 per 100,000) and rectal cancer nearly doubled (3.6 to 6.6), while proximal tumours rose 26%. [[Siegel2026CRC]]

Established

It is not only American

Early-onset incidence rose in 27 of 50 countries up to 2017, and in 20 of those the rise was confined to, or faster than, that in older adults. The highest rates (2013–17) were in Australia (16.5 per 100,000), Puerto Rico, New Zealand, the USA and South Korea. In 20 European countries, incidence at ages 20–29 rose 7.9% a year from 2004 to 2016. [[Sung2025,Vuik2019]]

Established

Often found late, but survival is not worse

Three in four patients under 50 are diagnosed with advanced disease, 27% with distant metastases against 21–23% of older patients. Five-year relative survival is similar or higher in younger people; for distant-stage disease it is 23% under 50, 17% at 50–64 and 10% at 65 or older. [[Siegel2026CRC]]

Established

Mostly not inherited

An inherited (germline) gene change such as Lynch syndrome explains 16–20% of colorectal cancer before 50; most early-onset cases are sporadic. See Lynch syndrome & FAP. [[Siegel2026CRC]]

Hypothesis

Why? Nobody knows yet

Proposed drivers include a Western-style diet, obesity, inactivity and antibiotics, especially early in life, acting through the gut microbiome and immunity. Known risk factors do not fully fit: obesity and inactivity relate more to colon than rectal cancer, yet the rise is led by the rectum. Ultra-processed foods and microplastics are being studied. [[Akimoto2021,Patel2022EOCRC,Siegel2026CRC]]

Established

Not an artefact

A 2013 coding change made some appendix tumours reportable, which inflates counts in young people. Studies that leave out the appendix still show a real rise that started earlier, with rising deaths. Imaging done for other reasons is unlikely to explain it, because it mostly finds early-stage disease, the opposite of what is seen Early evidence. [[Sung2026,Siegel2026CRC]]

Right and left: two diseases?

Tumours of the proximal (right) colon and the distal (left) colon and rectum differ in how often they occur, how they develop, their molecular pathways and their outcome. Right-sided tumours more often show mismatch-repair defects and flat growth; left-sided tumours more often follow the chromosomal-instability route (APC, KRAS, PIK3CA, TP53) and grow as polyps.
