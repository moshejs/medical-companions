Part 1 · The disease

From polyp to cancer

Most colorectal cancers grow out of a polyp over many years, picking up gene changes one after another. Scrub through two decades of one schematic lesion, switch between the conventional and the serrated pathway, and between chromosomal and microsatellite instability.

How fast does a large polyp become cancer?

Two kinds of evidence. Before colonoscopy was routine, a Mayo Clinic series followed 226 people whose polyps of 10 mm or more were left in place. Much later, German screening data from 840,149 colonoscopies were used to estimate how often an advanced adenoma turns into cancer each year. How common are polyps?

Pooled from 18 studies of average-risk North Americans. Press a button to light up each group among 1,000 people screened. These pooled estimates come from different sets of studies, so the groups do not add up neatly. [[Heitman2009]]

Why removing polyps works. In the National Polyp Study, removing adenomas lowered colorectal cancer incidence by 76–90% compared with three reference groups (1,418 patients, mean 5.9 years). Long-term follow-up found colorectal cancer mortality of 0.47 (95% CI 0.26–0.80) compared with that expected from SEER, a 53% reduction. Reviews class the evidence for the adenoma–carcinoma sequence as epidemiological, clinicopathological and genetic. [[Winawer1993,Zauber2012,Leslie2002]]

Three kinds of genomic instability

Colorectal cancers break their genomes in at least three ways. Chromosomal instability (CIN), the commonest, means whole chromosomes and arms are gained and lost. Microsatellite instability (MSI) means the DNA "spell-checker", mismatch repair, has failed. The CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) means many gene promoters are silenced by methylation. Cancer cells without MSI gained or lost chromosomes at rates above 10⁻² per chromosome per generation. [[Pino2010,Lengauer1997,Toyota1999]]

MSI is found in about 15% of colorectal cancers [[Boland2010,Vilar2010]]

Four consensus molecular subtypes

Six gene-expression classifications converged on four consensus molecular subtypes (CMS). No subtype is defined by a single mutation, and no genetic change is limited to one subtype. [[Guinney2015]]

Microbes in the mix

Established association

Fusobacterium nucleatum

Its sequences were enriched in colorectal carcinomas against matched normal tissue in two 2012 studies, and tumour Fusobacterium went with lymph-node metastasis. In a cohort of 1,069, a high tumour load went with higher colorectal-cancer mortality (HR 1.58) and with MSI-high (Early evidence). It persists in distant metastases, and metronidazole slowed tumour growth in mice. Cause or passenger is unproven. [[Kostic2012,Castellarin2012,Mima2016,Bullman2017]]

Established mechanism

Colibactin from pks+ E. coli

Commensal E. coli with the pks island make colibactin, which breaks DNA. Five months of exposure left a distinct mutational signature in human intestinal organoids; the same signature appeared in a subset of cancer genomes, mainly colorectal, and in 5.0% of 2,208 colorectal cancers. 5.3% of APC driver mutations matched the colibactin motif. [[Nougayrede2006,PleguezuelosManzano2020,DziubanskaKusibab2020]]

Early evidence

Colibactin and early-onset cancer

In 981 colorectal cancer genomes from 11 countries, colibactin signatures were 3.3 times more common in people diagnosed before 40 than after 70, and were imprinted early in tumour development. The authors suggest early-life exposure may contribute to rising early-onset cancer; causation in humans is unproven. [[DiazGay2025]]

Early evidence

Biofilms and inflammation

People with FAP had patchy colonic biofilms of E. coli and Bacteroides fragilis with colibactin and B. fragilis toxin genes; co-colonised mice developed tumours faster. In colitis-prone mice, pks+ E. coli promoted invasive carcinoma. [[Dejea2018,Arthur2012]]

Hypothesis

Microbes as markers

Reviews propose the microbiota as screening, prognostic or predictive biomarkers, and "polymorphic microbiomes" have been proposed as an enabling characteristic of cancer. Neither is in clinical use. [[Wong2019,Hanahan2022]]

Where the evidence disagrees

Controversy

How often does colorectal cancer reach the liver?

A figure of about 50% is often quoted, but population registries give lower numbers: synchronous liver metastases in 14.5% (Burgundy) and about 17.7% (Germany); liver metastases at any time in 24.7% (Germany) and 26.5% within 5 years (Stockholm). The 5-year metachronous rate rose with stage: 3.7% (I), 13.3% (II), 30.4% (III). Figures depend on population, follow-up and imaging era. [[Manfredi2006,Hackl2014,Engstrand2018]]

Controversy

Late or early spread?

Comparative lesion sequencing suggests about 17 years from a large benign tumour to an advanced cancer, and under 2 years more to a metastatic clone. Other modelling suggests that in 17 of 21 patients, cells seeded metastases while the primary was still under 0.01 cm³, and a "Big Bang" model holds that tumours grow as one early expansion. All are model-based (Early evidence) and may describe different tumours. [[Jones2008,Hu2019,Sottoriva2015]]

Controversy

What share of cancers is serrated?

Estimates run from at least 15% to about 35% (one estimate splits colorectal cancer into roughly 60% conventional, 35% serrated and 5% Lynch). No study has followed individual serrated lesions over time, so all are indirect. Post-colonoscopy cancers may arise from serrated polyps even more often. [[IJspeert2015,Bettington2017,Snover2011]]

Controversy

Is CMS ready for the clinic?

The subtypes carry prognosis, but no single mutation defines them, and using them to choose treatment remains investigational. A "multi-gene, multi-drug" model combining tumour, stroma and immune subtypes is a hypothesis. [[Guinney2015,Dienstmann2017]]

Established

Hallmarks, in brief

Cancer is described by hallmark capabilities: sustained growth signals, evading growth suppressors, resisting cell death, replicative immortality, angiogenesis, and invasion and metastasis, enabled by genome instability and inflammation. A 2022 update proposed phenotypic plasticity, epigenetic reprogramming and microbiomes as additions (provisional). [[Hanahan2011,Hanahan2022]]
