Finding Lynch syndrome

Family criteria

Amsterdam II: "3-2-1-0"

At least three relatives with a Lynch-associated cancer (colorectal, endometrium, small bowel, ureter or renal pelvis), and all of: one is a first-degree relative of the other two; two successive generations affected; at least one diagnosed before 50; FAP excluded; tumours verified where possible. 516,554

Tumour testing criteria

Revised Bethesda

Includes colorectal cancer before 50; synchronous or metachronous Lynch-related tumours at any age; MSI-H histology before 60; colorectal cancer with one or more first-degree relatives with a Lynch-related cancer, one diagnosed before 50. 516,540

Universal tumour testing. ACG 2015: "All newly diagnosed colorectal cancers (CRCs) should be evaluated for mismatch repair deficiency" by immunohistochemistry and/or MSI; tumours with MLH1 loss should have BRAF or MLH1 promoter-methylation testing. 516 The European 2021 guideline says all colorectal and endometrial cancers should be tested. 479 In 10,206 probands, universal testing had 100% sensitivity against 87.8% for the Bethesda guidelines, and 28% of Lynch cases in an Ohio cohort did not meet them. 217,372 Each Lynch proband had on average three relatives who also carried the variant. 217

What helps carriers

Established

Colonoscopy: 6% vs 16%

In a controlled 15-year Finnish trial in Lynch families, colorectal cancer developed in 6% of those having colonoscopy every 3 years against 16% without (a 62% reduction). There were no colorectal cancer deaths in the screened group against 9 in controls. 256

Established

Aspirin: CAPP2, 9% vs 13%

861 carriers took aspirin 600 mg or placebo for about 2 years. At about 10 years, colorectal cancer occurred in 9% (40/427) vs 13% (58/434): ITT HR 0.65 (0.43–0.97); per-protocol HR 0.56. No effect on other Lynch cancers. 83 Europe 2021 tells carriers there is a "high probability that daily aspirin will reduce their cancer risk", minimum 75–100 mg daily; the UK advises it with the best dose uncertain; ACG 2015 (before this report) did not recommend standard use. 370,479,516

Early evidence

Weight in CAPP2

Obese carriers had a 2.41-fold colorectal cancer risk, seen only in the placebo arm. 379

Gynaecological risk

Hysterectomy timing

Risk-reducing hysterectomy at 25 would prevent endometrial cancer before 50 in 15% (MLH1), 18% (MSH2), 13% (MSH6) and 0% (PMS2) of carriers; little is gained before 40. 132

Polyposis syndromes

FAP · APC · dominant

Hundreds of adenomas

Germline APC variants on 5q21 cause hundreds of adenomas; untreated, cancer follows at an average 35–40. 187 About 1 in 8,300 births, under 1% of colorectal cancer; about 70% have a family history and 15–30% of variants arise de novo. 210,593 St Mark's: average age at cancer 39; 87% by 45, 93% by 50. 516 Risk is "virtually 100% by 60 years" without prophylactic treatment. 593

FAP · care

From age 12, then surgery

Europe 2024: colonoscopy from 12 in classic FAP, by 18–20 in attenuated FAP; UK 2020: from 12–14, every 1–3 years, upper GI surveillance from 25. 370,593 Prophylactic colectomy is usually advised by the late teens or early twenties; in 303 surgical cases, ages predicting cancer were 27 (profuse), 31 (sparse) and 46 (attenuated). 210,593 Registry-found patients had 3–10% cancer at presentation against 50–70% when they presented with symptoms. 370

FAP · beyond the colon

Duodenum and desmoids

Duodenal adenomas reach 90% by 70; duodenal cancer 4.5% by 57; desmoids in about 8% of men and 13% of women; thyroid cancer 1.5–12%; also CHRPE, osteomas and hepatoblastoma. After colectomy, duodenal cancer and desmoids are the two main causes of death. 81,210,593

Attenuated FAP

10 to 100 adenomas

Usually 10–100 adenomas, appearing later, with a lower cancer risk. 210

MAP · MUTYH · recessive

Both copies needed

Biallelic MUTYH variants cause about 7% of adenomatous polyposis and 0.7% of colorectal cancer; 77.4% have fewer than 100 adenomas; lifetime CRC risk about 50.5%, mean age 47.85. 593 ACG: 19% by 50 and 43% by 60. 516 One in 45 people carries a MUTYH variant, mostly one copy; single-copy carriers have 7.2% (men) and 5.6% (women) risk to 70 against 2.9% and 2.1% in the general population, yet UK guidance does not advise extra colonoscopy for them. 370,577,578

Serrated polyposis

Most common, often missed

WHO 2019: at least 5 serrated lesions proximal to the rectum, all ≥5 mm with 2 or more ≥10 mm; or more than 20 serrated lesions anywhere with at least 5 proximal, counted across colonoscopies. 370 No common germline cause is known; first-degree relatives should have colonoscopic screening. 448

Hamartomatous

Peutz–Jeghers and juvenile polyposis

Peutz–Jeghers: autosomal dominant, usually STK11, with mucocutaneous pigmentation; 1 in 50,000–200,000 births; lifetime colorectal cancer risk 39%. 49,516 Juvenile polyposis: dominant, five or more juvenile polyps; CRC RR 34.0, lifetime risk 38.7%, mean age 43.9. 78,516

Where the evidence disagrees

Controversy

How often to look?

ACG says every 1–2 years, Europe every 2–3 years with consensus split, the UK every 2 years; PMS2 may be 5-yearly in Europe. Colorectal cancer still occurs in about half of MLH1/MSH2 carriers by 75 under surveillance, which challenges the idea that adenomas are always the precursor. 131,370,479,516

Controversy

Penetrance depends on how carriers were found

Clinic-based PLSD cohorts and family-based studies differ, especially for MSH6 and PMS2: for example PMS2 to 70, 3.4% in PLSD vs 19% in men and 11% in women in PMS2 families; MSH6 to 70, 12% in French families. 63,131,524

Controversy

Aspirin dose

European and UK guidelines endorse aspirin; ACG 2015 did not. The best dose is unknown; the CAPP3 dose trial had no results in PubMed at the time of this pass. 370,479,516
