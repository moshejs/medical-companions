# E · Medicines for AUD: approved and off-label

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: medicines, offlabel.

This pass covers medicines for alcohol use disorder (AUD), the condition once called "alcoholism" in lay use. It includes the four US-approved products (oral naltrexone, extended-release injectable naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram) and their labels, the trials and meta-analyses behind them, guideline positions, off-label and non-US medicines, use in liver disease, and how rarely these medicines are prescribed. Alcohol withdrawal management (benzodiazepines, CIWA-Ar, thiamine) belongs to pass D, psychosocial treatment to pass F, and GLP-1 and other frontier work to pass H; this file mentions them only where a medicine trial needs them.

Every dose below is quoted as published, from the named US label or the named trial. None of it is an instruction. Medicines for AUD are started and adjusted by a clinician as part of a care plan. Stopping alcohol suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous, so get medical help to stop safely. Mixing alcohol with opioids, benzodiazepines or other sedatives is dangerous.

## Findings

### medicines · The approved medicines at a glance
- The US FDA has approved four products for AUD: disulfiram, oral naltrexone, long-acting injectable naltrexone and acamprosate. Patients with AUD most often receive counselling instead [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- First US approvals in Drugs@FDA: Antabuse (disulfiram) NDA007883, 28 Aug 1951. ReVia (naltrexone) NDA018932 was first approved on 20 Nov 1984, and an efficacy supplement was approved on 30 Dec 1994. Campral (acamprosate) NDA021431, 29 Jul 2004. Vivitrol (naltrexone extended-release injectable suspension) NDA021897, 13 Apr 2006 [[DrugsFDA-AUD]].
- One review dates disulfiram's first approval for AUD to 1949 [[KranzlerSoyka2018]]. The earliest Drugs@FDA record for Antabuse is 1951 [[DrugsFDA-AUD]]. (The two sources disagree; see Open questions.)
- An earlier 2001 meta-analysis called naltrexone FDA-approved for alcohol dependence. At that time acamprosate was used in many other countries but not approved in the US [[KranzlerVanKirk2001]].
- Each label says the medicine should be part of a wider programme. Vivitrol and acamprosate name "a comprehensive management program that includes psychosocial support". Oral naltrexone says it has not been shown to help "except as part of an appropriate plan of management for the addictions" [[FDA-Vivitrol,FDA-Campral,FDA-Naltrexone]].
- The labels use the older DSM-IV term "alcohol dependence". The disulfiram label also uses outdated wording for people with AUD [[FDA-Naltrexone,FDA-Campral,FDA-Antabuse]].

### medicines · Oral naltrexone: mechanism and label
- Naltrexone is a pure opioid receptor antagonist. The label says its mechanism in AUD "is not understood", but preclinical data suggest the endogenous opioid system is involved. It may block the effects of endogenous opioids [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- The oral naltrexone label says it "is not aversive therapy" and causes no disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol or opioids [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- Labelled dose: "A dose of 50 mg once daily is recommended for most patients". The placebo-controlled trials used 50 mg once daily for up to 12 weeks, and other regimens or durations were not evaluated in them [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- Oral bioavailability is 5% to 40% because of large first-pass metabolism. Both naltrexone and its metabolite 6-β-naltrexol are thought to be active [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- In blockade studies, 50 mg blocked the effects of 25 mg of intravenous heroin for up to 24 hours. Doubling the dose blocked them for 48 hours and tripling it for about 72 hours [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- Contraindications: current opioid analgesics; current opioid dependence, including methadone or buprenorphine maintenance; acute opioid withdrawal; a failed naloxone challenge or positive urine opioid screen; and hypersensitivity [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- People dependent on short-acting opioids are advised to be opioid-free (including tramadol) for at least 7 to 10 days first. After switching from buprenorphine or methadone, precipitated withdrawal can occur for up to 2 weeks [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- Overdose risk: after naltrexone stops or a dose is missed, a person may respond to lower opioid doses than before. Trying to overcome the blockade with large opioid doses can cause fatal overdose. Prescribers are told to discuss an opioid overdose reversal agent such as naloxone or nalmefene [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- People taking naltrexone may get no benefit from opioid-containing cough, antidiarrhoeal or pain medicines. If opioid analgesia is needed in an emergency, larger doses may be required, with deeper and longer respiratory depression [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- Liver: the current oral label describes hepatitis and clinically significant liver dysfunction during development and after marketing. It notes that other causes, such as alcohol-associated liver disease, hepatitis B or C, or other hepatotoxic drugs, were often present [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- The label says placebo-controlled studies of up to 300 mg/day, about five times the blockade dose, showed hepatocellular injury in "a substantial proportion" of patients at those higher doses [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- The current oral naltrexone and Vivitrol labels carry no boxed warning [[FDA-Naltrexone,FDA-Vivitrol]]. An earlier FDA boxed warning for hepatotoxicity was removed in 2013 [[Thompson2024JHEP,Stoddard2015]].
- The label calls the combination of naltrexone and disulfiram, two potentially hepatotoxic medicines, of unknown safety and efficacy. It is "not ordinarily recommended unless the probable benefits outweigh the known risks" [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- Tolerability: in two 12-week label trials, 93 patients took 50 mg/day. Five stopped because of nausea, and no serious adverse events were reported [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- In pregnancy, the oral label says to use naltrexone only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk; there are no adequate human studies [[FDA-Naltrexone]]. Pregnancy decisions are made with the care team.

### medicines · Oral naltrexone: efficacy
- In the first 12-week trial (70 men, after detoxification), 23% of the naltrexone 50 mg/day group met relapse criteria, against 54.3% on placebo. Among those who drank any alcohol, 50% (8/16) relapsed on naltrexone and 95% (19/20) on placebo [[Volpicelli1992]].
- In a second 1992 trial (n=97), naltrexone did better than placebo on abstention, drinking days, relapse and alcohol-related problems. Among those who started drinking, the people least likely to relapse were those on naltrexone plus coping-skills therapy [[OMalley1992]].
- A 2010 Cochrane review (50 RCTs, 7,793 patients) found naltrexone lowered the risk of heavy drinking to 83% of placebo risk (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.76–0.90). It cut drinking days by about 4% (MD −3.89, 95% CI −5.75 to −2.04) [[Rosner2010Naltrexone]].
- In the same review, the effect on return to any drinking just missed statistical significance (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.92–1.00) [[Rosner2010Naltrexone]].
- Side effects in that review were mainly gastrointestinal (nausea RD 0.10, 95% CI 0.07–0.13) and sedative (daytime sleepiness RD 0.09, 95% CI 0.05–0.14) [[Rosner2010Naltrexone]].
- Industry-funded trials (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.78–1.05) and non-profit trials (RR 0.84, 95% CI 0.77–0.91) gave similar results, and the regression test did not suggest publication bias (P = 0.765) [[Rosner2010Naltrexone]].
- Jonas 2014 (122 RCTs and 1 cohort; 22,803 participants) gave oral naltrexone 50 mg/d an NNT of 20 (95% CI 11–500) to prevent return to any drinking (RD −0.05) and an NNT of 12 (95% CI 8–26) to prevent return to heavy drinking (RD −0.09) [[Jonas2014JAMA]].
- The McPheeters 2023 update (118 trials, 20,976 participants) gave oral naltrexone 50 mg/d an NNT of 18 (95% CI 4–32) to prevent return to any drinking and 11 (95% CI 5–41) to prevent return to heavy drinking [[McPheeters2023JAMA]].
- In McPheeters 2023, naltrexone raised nausea (RR 1.73, 95% CI 1.51–1.98) and vomiting (RR 1.53, 95% CI 1.23–1.91) against placebo [[McPheeters2023JAMA]].
- In Jonas 2014, the number needed to harm (one extra withdrawal from a trial because of adverse events) was 48 for naltrexone (95% CI 30–112) [[Jonas2014JAMA]].
- A JAMA review summarised naltrexone as reducing the likelihood of a return to any drinking by 5% and binge-drinking risk by 10% [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- A meta-analysis of 64 trials found naltrexone had a larger effect than acamprosate on heavy drinking and craving. Requiring abstinence before naltrexone started was linked to larger effects [[Maisel2013]].

### medicines · Extended-release injectable naltrexone (Vivitrol)
- Labelled AUD indication: treatment of alcohol dependence in patients "able to abstain from alcohol in an outpatient setting prior to initiation". Patients "should not be actively drinking" at the first injection [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Labelled dose: 380 mg as a deep intramuscular gluteal injection every 4 weeks or once a month, alternating buttocks. It must be prepared and given by a healthcare provider, using only the needles in the carton [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- The label requires needle length (1½ or 2 inches) to be checked against body habitus before each injection [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Pharmacokinetics: a first plasma peak about 2 hours after injection, a second about 2–3 days later, and a slow decline from about day 14, with levels measurable for more than 1 month [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Total naltrexone exposure after one 380 mg injection is 3- to 4-fold higher than after 28 days of oral 50 mg daily. The elimination half-life is 5–10 days and depends on erosion of the polymer [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Vivitrol blocks the effects of opioids for about 28 days. As the blockade wears off, or after a missed dose, a person is vulnerable to fatal opioid overdose [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Before starting, an opioid-free interval of at least 7–10 days is recommended to avoid precipitated withdrawal "that may be severe enough to require hospitalization" [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Injection-site reactions can include pain, tenderness, induration, swelling, erythema, bruising or pruritus. Some were severe: induration, cellulitis, abscess or necrosis, some needing surgical debridement, with significant scarring in some cases [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Other label warnings: hepatotoxicity (stop if acute hepatitis develops), depression and suicidality monitoring, eosinophilic pneumonia, and hypersensitivity including anaphylaxis [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Common adverse reactions in the label's alcohol-dependence table (380 mg, n=205, vs placebo, n=214): nausea 33% vs 11%, any injection-site reaction 69% vs 50%, injection-site tenderness 45% vs 39%, injection-site induration 35% vs 8% [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- In controlled trials of 6 months or less, 9% on Vivitrol and 7% on placebo stopped because of an adverse event. Injection-site reactions caused 3% of Vivitrol and 1% of placebo dropouts [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Pivotal trial (Garbutt 2005): 624 actively drinking adults with alcohol dependence at 24 US sites received 380 mg, 190 mg or placebo monthly for 6 months, plus 12 sessions of low-intensity psychosocial support [[Garbutt2005JAMA]].
- Against placebo, 380 mg gave a 25% fall in the event rate of heavy drinking days (P = .02) and 190 mg a 17% fall (P = .07). Effects were larger in men and in those abstinent during the lead-in [[Garbutt2005JAMA]].
- Discontinuation for adverse events was 14.1% at 380 mg, 6.7% at 190 mg and 6.7% on placebo [[Garbutt2005JAMA]].
- The label notes that only 8% of the trial (n=53) had abstained in the week before the first dose. In that group, 380 mg reduced drinking days and heavy drinking days and improved complete abstinence. These effects were not evident in the 92% (n=571) still drinking at the start [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Meta-analysis: injectable naltrexone was not associated with return to any drinking (RD −0.04, 95% CI −0.10 to 0.03) but was associated with fewer heavy drinking days (WMD −4.6%, 95% CI −8.5% to −0.56%) [[Jonas2014JAMA]].
- McPheeters 2023: injectable naltrexone was associated with fewer drinking days over the 30-day treatment period (WMD −4.99 days, 95% CI −9.49 to −0.49) [[McPheeters2023JAMA]].

### medicines · Acamprosate
- Labelled indication: "maintenance of abstinence from alcohol in patients with alcohol dependence who are abstinent at treatment initiation". Efficacy has not been shown in people who were not detoxified and abstinent before starting [[FDA-Campral]].
- Mechanism (label): "not completely understood". Chronic alcohol is thought to upset the balance between neuronal excitation and inhibition. Animal studies suggest acamprosate may act on glutamate and GABA systems, which leads to the hypothesis (Hypothesis) that it restores this balance [[FDA-Campral]].
- Reviews describe acamprosate as modulating glutamatergic neurotransmission [[KranzlerSoyka2018]] or as a glutamate antagonist [[Rosner2010Acamprosate]].
- Labelled dose: two 333 mg tablets (666 mg) three times daily. Treatment should start "as soon as possible after the period of alcohol withdrawal" and "should be maintained if the patient relapses" [[FDA-Campral]].
- Renal dosing (label): in moderate renal impairment (creatinine clearance 30–50 mL/min), the starting dose is one 333 mg tablet three times daily. Acamprosate is contraindicated when creatinine clearance is ≤30 mL/min [[FDA-Campral]].
- Pharmacokinetics: absolute bioavailability about 11%. Steady state comes within 5 days. Acamprosate is not metabolised and is excreted unchanged by the kidneys, with a terminal half-life of about 20–33 hours [[FDA-Campral]].
- In moderate and severe renal impairment, peak levels were about 2-fold and 4-fold higher than in healthy people, and the half-life was about 1.8-fold and 2.6-fold longer [[FDA-Campral]].
- The label says acamprosate "does not eliminate or diminish withdrawal symptoms" [[FDA-Campral]]. Withdrawal needs its own medical care.
- Suicidality (label): suicidal events were uncommon but more frequent on acamprosate than placebo: 1.4% vs 0.5% in studies of 6 months or less, and 2.4% vs 0.8% in year-long studies [[FDA-Campral]].
- Deaths by suicide in the pooled controlled studies: 3 of 2,272 (0.13%) on acamprosate and 2 of 1,962 (0.10%) on placebo. The label asks families and caregivers to watch for and report depression or suicidal thinking [[FDA-Campral]].
- Diarrhoea was the only adverse event that led more than 1% of people to stop: 2% on acamprosate vs 0.7% on placebo [[FDA-Campral]].
- Across 38 adverse events reviewed, diarrhoea was the only one more frequent on acamprosate than placebo (24.9% vs 13.9%) [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- The label's efficacy base is three placebo-controlled trials (998 patients, detoxified as inpatients and abstinent at randomisation, 90–360 days). A fourth study, which did not require detoxification or abstinence, did not show benefit over placebo [[FDA-Campral]].
- Mann 2004 meta-analysis (17 RCTs, 4,087 people): continuous abstinence at 6 months was 36.1% on acamprosate vs 23.4% on placebo (relative benefit 1.47, 95% CI 1.29–1.69) [[Mann2004Acamprosate]].
- At 12 months in Mann 2004, the success-rate difference was 13.3% (95% CI 7.8–18.7%), an NNT of 7.5. Effect sizes for abstinence rates rose from 1.33 at 3 months to 1.50 at 6 months and 1.95 at 12 months [[Mann2004Acamprosate]].
- 2010 Cochrane review (24 RCTs, 6,915 participants): acamprosate reduced the risk of any drinking (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.81–0.91; NNT 9.09) and increased cumulative abstinence duration (MD 10.94 days, 95% CI 5.08–16.81). It did not significantly reduce heavy drinking or GGT [[Rosner2010Acamprosate]].
- In that review, diarrhoea was the only side effect more common than on placebo (RD 0.11, 95% CI 0.09–0.13) [[Rosner2010Acamprosate]].
- Jonas 2014: the NNT for acamprosate to prevent return to any drinking was 12 (95% CI 8–26; RD −0.09). Adverse-event withdrawals were not significantly increased [[Jonas2014JAMA]].
- McPheeters 2023: the NNT for acamprosate to prevent return to any drinking was 11 (95% CI 1–32). Diarrhoea was increased (RR 1.58, 95% CI 1.27–1.97) [[McPheeters2023JAMA]].
- A meta-analysis of 64 trials found acamprosate had a larger effect than naltrexone on keeping abstinence. Detoxification before starting was linked to better abstinence outcomes [[Maisel2013]].
- US vs European results: the first US multicentre trial (n=601, 6 months, recruited mostly by advertisement) found no difference in the a priori analysis of percent abstinent days: placebo 54.3%, 2 g 56.1%, 3 g 60.7% [[Mason2006Acamprosate]].
- In post-hoc analyses of that trial, results favoured acamprosate (52.3%, 58.2%, 62.7%; P = 0.01). The difference was larger in the 241 people whose baseline goal was abstinence (58.1%, 70.0%, 72.5%) [[Mason2006Acamprosate]].

### medicines · Disulfiram
- Mechanism: disulfiram blocks alcohol oxidation at the acetaldehyde stage. After drinking on disulfiram, blood acetaldehyde can be 5–10 times higher than with the same alcohol alone. This causes the disulfiram-alcohol reaction [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Reviews name the enzyme it inhibits as aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH), which normally breaks down acetaldehyde [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- Boxed warning (label): disulfiram "should never be administered to a patient when he is in a state of alcohol intoxication, or without his full knowledge". The physician should tell relatives this [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Labelled start: never until the person has abstained from alcohol for at least 12 hours [[FDA-Antabuse]]. Anyone who drinks heavily every day should get medical help to stop safely first.
- Labelled dosing: up to 500 mg once daily for 1–2 weeks, then an average maintenance dose of 250 mg daily (range 125–500 mg), not more than 500 mg daily [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Reactions can occur with alcohol up to 14 days after the last disulfiram dose. The label warns about hidden alcohol in sauces, vinegars, cough mixtures, aftershave lotions and back rubs [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Reaction signs (label): flushing, throbbing headache, breathing difficulty, nausea, heavy vomiting, sweating, chest pain, palpitations, tachycardia, hypotension, fainting, vertigo, blurred vision and confusion [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Severe reactions can include respiratory depression, cardiovascular collapse, arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, acute heart failure, unconsciousness, convulsions and death [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Dose and alcohol thresholds (label): mild reactions can start at a blood alcohol of 5–10 mg/100 mL, symptoms are fully developed at 50 mg/100 mL, and unconsciousness usually occurs at 125–150 mg. A reaction lasts 30–60 minutes, several hours if severe, or as long as alcohol is in the blood [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Tolerance does not develop. The label says the longer a person stays on it, the more sensitive to alcohol they become [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- The label states the old practice of a supervised test reaction with alcohol "has been largely abandoned" and should never be done in anyone over 50 [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Contraindications (label): recent metronidazole, paraldehyde, alcohol or alcohol-containing preparations; severe myocardial disease or coronary occlusion; psychoses; and hypersensitivity to thiuram derivatives [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Liver (label): hepatitis, including fulminant hepatitis and liver failure leading to transplantation or death, has been reported, sometimes after many months. Baseline and follow-up liver tests at 10–14 days are suggested [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Other label adverse reactions: optic neuritis, peripheral neuropathy, and psychotic reactions (most often with high doses, metronidazole or isoniazid). Mild drowsiness, fatigue or a metallic or garlic-like aftertaste may occur in the first two weeks [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- VA cooperative trial (605 men; 250 mg vs 1 mg vs none, 1 year): no difference in total abstinence or time to first drink. Among those who drank and were fully assessed, the 250 mg group reported fewer drinking days (49.0 vs 75.4 vs 86.5) [[Fuller1986VA]].
- Skinner 2014 meta-analysis (22 RCTs): disulfiram had a higher success rate than controls overall (Hedges' g = 0.58, 95% CI 0.35–0.82), but only open-label trials showed superiority (g = 0.70, 95% CI 0.46–0.93). Blinded trials showed no efficacy [[Skinner2014Disulfiram]].
- The included trials were 89% male and very heterogeneous [[Skinner2014Disulfiram]].
- Supervised ingestion was linked to a clearly better outcome (g = 0.82, 95% CI 0.59–1.05) than unsupervised treatment (9 studies; g = 0.26, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.53) [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- In a 12-week supervised open-label Finnish trial (n=243), disulfiram 200 mg/day beat naltrexone 50 mg and acamprosate 1,998 mg on heavy drinking days, weekly intake, time to first drink and abstinent days [[Laaksonen2008]].
- Network meta-analysis (156 trials, 27,334 people): disulfiram improved total abstinence (RR 1.71, 95% CI 1.39–2.10) and reduced heavy drinking (RR 0.19, 95% CI 0.10–0.35) against placebo [[Bahji2022NMA]].
- A gastroenterology guideline states disulfiram is contraindicated in liver disease of any spectrum, because it is entirely metabolised by the liver and is hepatotoxic [[Jophlin2024ACG]].

### medicines · Combining medicines and the COMBINE trial
- COMBINE (2001–2004; 1,383 recently abstinent people with alcohol dependence at 11 US sites) tested 16 weeks of naltrexone 100 mg/d, acamprosate 3 g/d, both or placebos, with medical management, with or without a combined behavioural intervention (CBI) [[Anton2006COMBINE]].
- Percent days abstinent during treatment: naltrexone + medical management 80.6, CBI + medical management + placebos 79.2, naltrexone + CBI + medical management 77.1, and placebos + medical management only 75.1 [[Anton2006COMBINE]].
- Naltrexone reduced the risk of a heavy drinking day (HR 0.72, 97.5% CI 0.53–0.98), most clearly with medical management alone [[Anton2006COMBINE]].
- Acamprosate showed no significant effect on drinking against placebo, alone or combined with naltrexone, CBI or both [[Anton2006COMBINE]].
- CBI with no pills and no medical management gave the lowest percent days abstinent (66.6). Placebo pills plus meetings with a health professional helped beyond CBI [[Anton2006COMBINE]].
- One year after treatment, the between-group differences were similar but no longer significant [[Anton2006COMBINE]].
- No combination worked better than naltrexone or CBI alone when medical management was given [[Anton2006COMBINE]].
- In a German 12-week trial (n=160 after detoxification), naltrexone, acamprosate and the combination all beat placebo, and the combination had the lowest relapse rates [[Kiefer2003]].
- Comparisons of acamprosate with naltrexone found no significant difference for return to any drinking (RD 0.02, 95% CI −0.03 to 0.08) or heavy drinking (RD 0.01, 95% CI −0.05 to 0.06) [[Jonas2014JAMA]].
- In a 14-week trial in people with depression and AUD, sertraline 200 mg/day plus naltrexone 100 mg/day gave 53.7% abstinence. The rates were 21.3% on naltrexone alone, 27.5% on sertraline alone and 23.1% on placebo [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].

### medicines · Guidelines and choosing a medicine
- APA 2018 practice guideline: as quoted in reviews, it recommends that naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram be offered to patients with moderate to severe AUD [[Reus2018APA,KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- The APA guideline also suggests topiramate or gabapentin for patients who prefer them, or who cannot tolerate or have not responded to the FDA-approved medicines [[Reus2018APA,Kranzler2019Gabapentin]].
- The APA guideline gives naltrexone and acamprosate a stronger first-line position (grade 1B) and disulfiram, topiramate and gabapentin a weaker one (grade 2C) [[Reus2018APA]] (unverified: grades not read from the guideline text).
- McPheeters 2023 concluded that, with psychosocial interventions, the findings support oral naltrexone 50 mg/d and acamprosate as first-line medicines for AUD [[McPheeters2023JAMA]].
- Jonas 2014 suggested choosing between medicines by dosing frequency, possible adverse events and availability [[Jonas2014JAMA]].
- A JAMA review said people diagnosed with AUD should receive brief counselling and a first-line medicine (for example naltrexone) or referral to more intensive psychosocial care [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- The US Department of Veterans Affairs/Department of Defense guideline lists topiramate, disulfiram, acamprosate and naltrexone as appropriate first-line treatments [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- Two clinical reviews conclude current evidence does not support pharmacogenetic testing to choose an AUD medicine [[KranzlerSoyka2018,Fairbanks2020Mayo]].

### medicines · Underuse of AUD medicines
- AUD medicines are prescribed to fewer than 9% of patients likely to benefit from them [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- A gabapentin trial report states the approved medicines are prescribed to fewer than 9% of US adults with alcohol dependence [[Mason2014Gabapentin]].
- Fewer than 9% of patients who receive any form of AUD treatment get medicines [[Fairbanks2020Mayo]].
- US retail prescriptions for AUD medicines rose from 393,000 in 2003 ($30 million) to an estimated 720,000 in 2007 ($78 million). Most of the growth came after acamprosate launched [[Mark2009]].
- In 2007, buprenorphine for opioid use disorder had 1.9 million prescriptions ($327 million), far more than AUD medicines [[Mark2009]].
- In the 2019 NSDUH, about 1.6% of US adults with past-year AUD received a medicine for AUD [[Han2021JAMAPsych]] (unverified: the figure could not be read from the letter's text).
- A Lancet seminar links undertreatment of AUD partly to stigma and partly to too little systematic screening in primary care. It says effective, cost-effective psychosocial and medicine treatments exist [[Carvalho2019Lancet]].
- The ACG guideline notes that people with alcohol-associated liver disease are rarely treated for AUD. It calls for integrated care across hepatology, addiction medicine and social work [[Jophlin2024ACG]].

### medicines · Medicines in liver disease
- ACG 2024: naltrexone in either form may be used in early alcohol-associated liver disease and compensated cirrhosis. It should be avoided in decompensated cirrhosis or liver failure and used with caution in kidney disease [[Jophlin2024ACG]].
- ACG 2024: acamprosate does not undergo hepatic metabolism, and a retrospective analysis suggests it is safe in cirrhosis. It needs a dose cut at creatinine clearance 30–50 and should be avoided below 30 mL/min [[Jophlin2024ACG]].
- ACG 2024: baclofen is the non-FDA-approved medicine most studied in advanced liver disease. The guideline describes a start of 5 mg three times a day, raised every 3–5 days as tolerated, to a maximum of 15 mg three times a day (quoted as published) [[Jophlin2024ACG]].
- ACG 2024: gabapentin and topiramate showed acceptable safety in alcohol-associated liver disease, but large prospective trials are needed [[Jophlin2024ACG]].
- Baclofen RCT in cirrhosis (Rome; 84 people; 12 weeks): 71% (30/42) on baclofen achieved and kept abstinence vs 29% (12/42) on placebo (OR 6.3, 95% CI 2.4–16.1). No hepatic side effects were recorded [[Addolorato2007Lancet]].
- Cumulative abstinence in that trial was about twice as long on baclofen: mean 62.8 vs 30.8 days [[Addolorato2007Lancet]].
- VA cohort (3,285 people with cirrhosis starting naltrexone): only 2% of those tested had liver enzyme rises. No case was classed as possible, probable or highly probable drug-induced liver injury, and no deaths or new decompensations were attributed to naltrexone [[Thompson2024JHEP]].
- Safety-net cohort (160 people on naltrexone for AUD; 100 with liver disease, 47 with cirrhosis): mean AST and ALT were lower after naltrexone was prescribed than before [[Ayyala2022]].
- Two-year survival in that cohort (a population statistic): 97.7% without liver disease, 95.4% with liver disease but no cirrhosis, 90.8% with cirrhosis and 81.3% with decompensated cirrhosis. A prescription of 30 days or less predicted hospitalisation (HR 2.50) [[Ayyala2022]].
- Mass General Brigham Biobank (9,635 people with AUD; mean follow-up 9.2 years): AUD medicine was associated with lower incidence of alcohol-associated liver disease (aOR 0.37, 95% CI 0.31–0.43) (Early evidence) [[Vannier2022]].
- In people with cirrhosis in that cohort, AUD medicine was associated with less hepatic decompensation (aOR 0.35, 95% CI 0.23–0.53), including naltrexone (aOR 0.27) and gabapentin (aOR 0.36). These are observational associations, not proof of cause (Early evidence) [[Vannier2022]].

### offlabel · Topiramate
- Topiramate is an anticonvulsant thought to reduce alcohol's reinforcing effects by boosting GABA and inhibiting glutamate pathways (Hypothesis) [[Johnson2007Topiramate]]. Its US approvals are for seizures, migraine prevention and, with phentermine, weight loss; AUD is not one of them [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- Single-site RCT (n=150, 12 weeks, escalating 25–300 mg/day): topiramate gave 2.88 fewer drinks per day, 27.6% fewer heavy drinking days and 26.2% more days abstinent than placebo [[Johnson2003Lancet]].
- Multisite RCT (n=371, 14 weeks, up to 300 mg/day, 17 US sites): topiramate reduced percent heavy drinking days more than placebo (mean difference 8.44%, 95% CI 3.07–13.80, with dropouts counted as relapse). The prespecified mixed model gave a difference of 16.19% [[Johnson2007Topiramate]].
- Adverse events, topiramate vs placebo: paresthesia 50.8% vs 10.6%, taste perversion 23.0% vs 4.8%, anorexia 19.7% vs 6.9%, difficulty concentrating 14.8% vs 3.2% [[Johnson2007Topiramate]].
- Meta-analysis (7 RCTs, 1,125 participants): topiramate improved abstinence (g = 0.468) and heavy drinking (g = 0.406), with a non-significant effect on craving (g = 0.312, p = 0.07) [[Blodgett2014Topiramate]].
- Jonas 2014: topiramate reduced percent heavy drinking days (WMD −9.0%, 95% CI −15.3% to −2.7%) and drinks per drinking day (WMD −1.0), with moderate strength of evidence [[Jonas2014JAMA]].
- In heavy drinkers aiming to cut down (n=138, up to 200 mg/day), topiramate reduced heavy drinking days. In European-American participants the effect appeared only in rs2832407 C-allele homozygotes of GRIK1 (Early evidence) [[Kranzler2014GRIK1]].
- The network meta-analysis found topiramate caused more adverse-event dropouts than placebo (RR 2.18, 95% CI 1.36–3.51) [[Bahji2022NMA]].

### offlabel · Gabapentin
- Dose-ranging RCT (n=150, 12 weeks): complete abstinence was 4.1% on placebo, 11.1% on 900 mg/d and 17.0% on 1,800 mg/d (NNT 8 for 1,800 mg) [[Mason2014Gabapentin]].
- In the same trial, no heavy drinking was 22.5%, 29.6% and 44.7% (NNT 5 for 1,800 mg). Sleep, mood and craving also improved with dose, and adverse-event terminations did not differ between groups [[Mason2014Gabapentin]].
- In people with recent alcohol withdrawal symptoms (n=90 evaluable; up to 1,200 mg/d; 16 weeks), 27% on gabapentin had no heavy drinking days vs 9% on placebo (NNT 5.4), and 18% vs 4% were totally abstinent (NNT 6.2) [[Anton2020Gabapentin]].
- In that trial, the benefit appeared only in the high-withdrawal subgroup (NNT 3.1 for no heavy drinking; 2.7 for abstinence) [[Anton2020Gabapentin]].
- The extended-release prodrug gabapentin enacarbil (600 mg twice daily, n=346, 6 months) did not beat placebo: 28.3% vs 21.5% had no heavy drinking days (p = 0.157) [[Falk2019GabapentinEnacarbil]].
- Meta-analysis (7 RCTs): every outcome pointed toward gabapentin, but only percent heavy drinking days showed clear benefit (g = −0.64, 95% CI −1.22 to −0.06) [[Kranzler2019Gabapentin]].
- Misuse: about 1% of the general population has misused gabapentin, sometimes with alcohol. Reviewers cite this, with small trials and high loss to follow-up, against first-line use [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].

### offlabel · Baclofen
- Baclofen is a GABA-B receptor agonist approved in the US for spasticity [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- France: in March 2014 the national drug agency granted a Temporary Recommendation for Use (TRU) allowing doses up to 300 mg/d. Data were collected on 6,939 people from 2014 to 2017 [[deBeaurepaire2022TRU]].
- In July 2017 the French maximum dose was cut to 80 mg/day, and a marketing authorisation followed in October 2018 [[Payet2026Baclofen]].
- French baclofen starts for AUD peaked at 98,552 people in 2014 and fell to 48,471 in 2021. The 2018 authorisation had no impact on starts [[Payet2026Baclofen]].
- ALPADIR (n=320 after detoxification; target 180 mg/day): abstinence over 20 weeks was 11.9% on baclofen vs 10.5% on placebo (OR 1.20, 95% CI 0.58–2.50; not significant). Craving fell more on baclofen (P = 0.017) [[Reynaud2017Alpadir]].
- Dutch multicentre RCT (n=151; high dose up to 150 mg, low dose 30 mg, or placebo): no difference in time to first relapse. Only 15.5% of the high-dose group reached 150 mg (mean 93.6 mg) [[Beraha2016Baclofen]].
- Bacloville (n=320 in 62 French primary care centres; titrated up to 300 mg/day; 12 months): 57% on baclofen vs 36% on placebo reached no or low-risk drinking in the last month (difference 21 points, 95% CI 8–34) [[Rigal2020Bacloville]].
- Bacloville safety: serious adverse events affected 85 people on baclofen (7 deaths) vs 36 on placebo (3 deaths) [[Rigal2020Bacloville]].
- 2023 Cochrane review (17 RCTs, 1,818 participants): baclofen probably lowers relapse risk (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.77–0.99; moderate certainty) and increases percent days abstinent (MD 9.07, 95% CI 3.30–14.85; high certainty), mainly in detoxified participants [[Agabio2023Cochrane]].
- The same review found no effect on heavy drinking days (SMD −0.18) or drinks per drinking day. Fatigue, dizziness, sleepiness, dry mouth, paraesthesia and muscle spasms or rigidity were more common [[Agabio2023Cochrane]].
- Pierce 2018 meta-analysis (13 RCTs): baclofen lengthened time to lapse (SMD 0.42) and raised abstinence at endpoint (OR 1.93, 95% CI 1.17–3.17). Low doses (30–60 mg) did no worse than high doses, and tolerability of high doses was low [[Pierce2018Baclofen]].
- Baclofen causes sleepiness. Taking it with alcohol or other sedatives is a safety concern [[Agabio2023Cochrane,Rigal2020Bacloville]].

### offlabel · Nalmefene (EU-approved, not US-approved for AUD)
- Nalmefene is a mu- and delta-opioid receptor antagonist and kappa-opioid partial agonist. In the US it is approved only to reverse opioid effects [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- The EU authorised it to reduce drinking in adults with alcohol dependence at a high drinking-risk level (men >60 g/day; women >40 g/day) who are still at that level 2 weeks after first assessment [[vandenBrink2013Nalmefene]].
- ESENSE 1 (n=604; 18 mg as needed; 24 weeks): at month 6, nalmefene cut heavy drinking days by 2.3 days/month (95% CI −3.8 to −0.8) and total alcohol by 11.0 g/day more than placebo [[Mann2013ESENSE1]].
- ESENSE 2 (n=718): heavy drinking days fell by 1.7 days/month more than placebo (95% CI −3.1 to −0.4). The fall in total alcohol (−5.0 g/day) was not significant [[Gual2013ESENSE2]].
- In the pooled subgroup with a high drinking-risk level at screening and randomisation (n=667), the differences were −3.2 heavy drinking days and −14.3 g/day [[vandenBrink2013Nalmefene]].
- Reviewers called the approval controversial because the efficacy evidence came from a retrospectively defined subgroup [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- In Jonas 2014, nalmefene cut heavy drinking days by 2.0 per month (WMD, 95% CI −3.0 to −1.0), but its number needed to harm for adverse-event withdrawal was 12 [[Jonas2014JAMA]].

### offlabel · Sodium oxybate (GHB)
- Sodium oxybate oral solution has been approved in Italy since 1991 and Austria since 1999 for alcohol withdrawal and maintaining abstinence [[Guiraud2022SMO]].
- 2010 Cochrane review (13 RCTs, 11 from Italy): GHB beat placebo at mid-term for abstinence (RR 5.35, 1.28–22.4) and relapse (RR 0.36). The authors raised concern about the risk of addiction or misuse, especially in people who use several drugs [[Leone2010Cochrane]].
- International RCT (n=314 detoxified people; 3.3–3.9 g/day for 6 months): cumulative abstinence was 43.1 days longer than placebo in the prespecified analysis, or 32.4 days in a site-level random-effects analysis [[Guiraud2022SMO]].
- GHB is a central nervous system depressant. Taking it with alcohol or other sedatives is a safety concern [[Leone2010Cochrane]].

### offlabel · Varenicline, ondansetron, prazosin and doxazosin
- Varenicline (n=200; titrated to 2 mg/d, 13 weeks): lower weekly percent heavy drinking days than placebo (adjusted mean difference 10.4). Drinks per day and craving also fell, with similar effects in smokers and non-smokers [[Litten2013Varenicline]].
- Ondansetron in early-onset AUD (n=271; 11 weeks): 4 µg/kg twice daily raised percent days abstinent to 70.10 vs 50.20 on placebo [[Johnson2000JAMA]].
- Ondansetron by genotype (n=283): people with the 5-HTT LL genotype on ondansetron had 1.62 fewer drinks per drinking day and 11.27% more days abstinent than on placebo (Early evidence) [[Johnson2011Ondansetron]].
- Prazosin (n=92 without PTSD; target 4 mg morning, 4 mg afternoon and 8 mg at bedtime, 12 weeks): drinks and heavy drinking days fell faster than on placebo, with more drowsiness and oedema (Early evidence) [[Simpson2018Prazosin]].
- Doxazosin (n=41; up to 16 mg/day): no overall difference from placebo. It reduced drinking only in people with a high family history density of AUD and increased it in those with a low density (Early evidence) [[Kenna2016Doxazosin]].
- GLP-1 receptor agonists (covered by pass H): once-weekly exenatide did not reduce heavy drinking days in 127 patients overall, though exploratory analyses suggested benefit when BMI was above 30 (Early evidence) [[Klausen2022Exenatide]].
- Low-dose semaglutide (n=48, 9 weeks) reduced lab alcohol self-administration, drinks per drinking day and craving, but not drinking days (Early evidence) [[Hendershot2025Semaglutide]].

### offlabel · Pharmacogenetics of naltrexone
- In a pooled secondary analysis (European descent), carriers of the OPRM1 Asp40 allele had lower relapse rates on naltrexone than Asn40 homozygotes (p = 0.044) [[Oslin2003OPRM1]].
- In COMBINE (medical management, no CBI), 87.1% of Asp40 carriers on naltrexone had a good clinical outcome vs 54.8% of Asn40/Asn40 (OR 5.75) [[Anton2008OPRM1]].
- The prospective genotype-stratified trial (n=221, naltrexone 50 mg) found no genotype × treatment interaction (P = .32). The authors judged it premature to use Asn40Asp as a biomarker [[Oslin2015JAMAPsych]].

### offlabel · Comparing everything: network meta-analysis
- Bahji 2022 (156 trials, 27,334 people): medicines that improved total abstinence over placebo were GHB (RR 1.90), baclofen (1.80), disulfiram (1.71), gabapentin (1.66), acamprosate (1.33) and oral naltrexone (1.15) [[Bahji2022NMA]].
- Medicines that reduced heavy drinking were disulfiram (RR 0.19), baclofen (0.72), acamprosate (0.78) and oral naltrexone (0.81) [[Bahji2022NMA]].
- The authors concluded that acamprosate (2–3 g/d), disulfiram (250–500 mg/d), baclofen (30 mg/d) and oral naltrexone (50 mg/d) had the best evidence [[Bahji2022NMA]].
- Nalmefene (RR 3.26), fluvoxamine (3.08) and topiramate (2.18) caused more adverse-event dropouts than placebo [[Bahji2022NMA]].

### Exam hooks
- Naltrexone is a mu-opioid antagonist. It precipitates opioid withdrawal, so check for opioid use first: the label advises 7–10 opioid-free days [[FDA-Naltrexone,FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Acamprosate is renally excreted unchanged: dose cut at CrCl 30–50 mL/min, contraindicated at ≤30 mL/min [[FDA-Campral]].
- Acamprosate is the AUD medicine that is not metabolised by the liver [[FDA-Campral,Jophlin2024ACG]].
- Disulfiram inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase, so acetaldehyde builds up 5–10-fold after drinking [[FDA-Antabuse,KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- Disulfiram boxed warning: never give during intoxication or without the person's full knowledge [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Disulfiram-alcohol reactions can occur up to 14 days after the last dose [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Disulfiram works in open-label or supervised trials, not blinded ones [[Skinner2014Disulfiram]].
- NNT to prevent return to any drinking (Jonas 2014): acamprosate 12, oral naltrexone 20. Naltrexone's NNT to prevent heavy drinking: 12 [[Jonas2014JAMA]].
- Acamprosate best supports abstinence. Naltrexone best reduces heavy drinking and craving [[Maisel2013]].
- In COMBINE, naltrexone + medical management worked and acamprosate did not [[Anton2006COMBINE]].
- Vivitrol: 380 mg deep IM gluteal every 4 weeks, given by a healthcare provider [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Vivitrol's main AUD trial showed a 25% fall in the heavy drinking day rate at 380 mg [[Garbutt2005JAMA]].
- Acamprosate does not treat withdrawal [[FDA-Campral]].
- Naltrexone in cirrhosis: allowed in compensated disease, avoided in decompensated cirrhosis or liver failure (ACG) [[Jophlin2024ACG]].
- Disulfiram is contraindicated in liver disease (ACG) [[Jophlin2024ACG]].
- Baclofen has a cirrhosis RCT (71% vs 29% abstinent) [[Addolorato2007Lancet]].
- Topiramate's signature side effect is paresthesia (50.8% vs 10.6%) [[Johnson2007Topiramate]].
- Gabapentin shows a dose-response: 17.0% abstinent at 1,800 mg vs 4.1% on placebo [[Mason2014Gabapentin]].
- Nalmefene is approved in the EU, taken as needed, and its goal is reduced drinking [[vandenBrink2013Nalmefene,Mann2013ESENSE1]].
- AUD medicines reach fewer than 9% of the people who could benefit [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].

### In plain words
- AUD is a medical condition, and there are medicines that help. They work best together with counselling or support [[FDA-Vivitrol,FDA-Campral,McPheeters2023JAMA]].
- Naltrexone can make drinking less rewarding and lower the chance of going back to heavy drinking [[Rosner2010Naltrexone,Jonas2014JAMA]].
- Naltrexone blocks opioid painkillers. Tell every doctor, dentist and pharmacist you take it, and ask about having naloxone on hand [[FDA-Naltrexone]].
- After naltrexone stops, even a smaller opioid dose than before can cause an overdose [[FDA-Naltrexone,FDA-Vivitrol]].
- The naltrexone shot is given once a month by a nurse or doctor. A sore spot where it went in is common, and a worsening lump or skin change should be checked [[FDA-Vivitrol]].
- Acamprosate is taken three times a day and helps people who have already stopped drinking stay stopped. It does not treat withdrawal [[FDA-Campral]].
- Disulfiram makes you very sick if you drink, even from hidden alcohol in sauces, cough syrups, aftershave or rubbing lotions, for up to two weeks after the last pill [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Nobody should ever give disulfiram to someone without their knowledge. It must be the person's own choice [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- If you drink heavily every day, do not stop suddenly on your own. Get medical help to stop safely [[FDA-Antabuse,FDA-Campral]].
- Some medicines for other conditions, like topiramate and gabapentin, can also help with drinking. A clinician decides if one fits you [[Reus2018APA,KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- Mixing alcohol with sedating medicines, opioids or sleeping pills can be dangerous [[FDA-Naltrexone,Agabio2023Cochrane]].
- Liver disease does not rule out every AUD medicine. Some are used in people with cirrhosis under a specialist's care [[Jophlin2024ACG,Thompson2024JHEP]].
- If you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, plan medicine choices with your care team [[FDA-Naltrexone,FDA-Campral]].
- Very few people with AUD are offered these medicines. It is fine to ask about them [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Why acamprosate worked in Europe but not in COMBINE or the first US trial: European trials and meta-analyses found benefit after inpatient detoxification [[Mann2004Acamprosate,Rosner2010Acamprosate]]. COMBINE found no effect [[Anton2006COMBINE]], and the US trial was negative in its a priori analysis [[Mason2006Acamprosate]]. Explanations include prior detoxification and the person's goal of abstinence [[Maisel2013,Mason2006Acamprosate]]. Status: unresolved; both remain first-line in the US evidence review [[McPheeters2023JAMA]].
- Disulfiram's true efficacy: blinded trials show no effect, but open-label and supervised trials do [[Skinner2014Disulfiram,KranzlerSoyka2018]]. The VA trial found no gain in abstinence [[Fuller1986VA]]. Supporters argue blinding cancels the deterrent that is disulfiram's point. Status: used mainly with supervision and the person's full consent [[FDA-Antabuse]].
- Baclofen dose: titrated high doses helped in Bacloville but with more serious adverse events [[Rigal2020Bacloville]]. Fixed high doses failed in ALPADIR and the Dutch trial [[Reynaud2017Alpadir,Beraha2016Baclofen]]. A meta-analysis found low doses no worse [[Pierce2018Baclofen]]. France capped doses at 80 mg/day in 2017 [[Payet2026Baclofen]]. Status: dose-response unsettled.
- Nalmefene's approval rested on a post-hoc, high-risk subgroup [[KranzlerSoyka2018,vandenBrink2013Nalmefene]]. The full-population effects were modest [[Mann2013ESENSE1,Gual2013ESENSE2]]. Status: approved in the EU, not the US.
- OPRM1 Asp40 and naltrexone response: positive retrospective analyses [[Oslin2003OPRM1,Anton2008OPRM1]] versus a negative prospective trial [[Oslin2015JAMAPsych]]. Status: not ready for clinical use [[KranzlerSoyka2018]].
- Gabapentin: positive single-site trials [[Mason2014Gabapentin,Anton2020Gabapentin]] versus a negative multisite trial of the enacarbil prodrug [[Falk2019GabapentinEnacarbil]]. The meta-analysis is positive only for percent heavy drinking days [[Kranzler2019Gabapentin]]. Misuse potential is a further concern [[KranzlerSoyka2018]]. Status: second-line.
- Naltrexone in decompensated cirrhosis: the ACG says avoid it [[Jophlin2024ACG]], while a VA cohort reported no drug-induced liver injury in compensated or decompensated cirrhosis [[Thompson2024JHEP]] and a smaller cohort asked for more data in decompensated disease [[Ayyala2022]]. Status: observational evidence growing; no RCT.
- Disulfiram's first approval date: one review says 1949 [[KranzlerSoyka2018]], while the earliest Drugs@FDA record for Antabuse is 1951 [[DrugsFDA-AUD]]. The difference may reflect records that predate the current database. Status: unresolved here.
- Sodium oxybate: benefits for abstinence [[Leone2010Cochrane,Guiraud2022SMO]] are weighed against misuse risk [[Leone2010Cochrane]]. Status: approved in Italy and Austria only [[Guiraud2022SMO]].
