# D · Alcohol withdrawal: syndrome, CIWA-Ar, management & Wernicke

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: withdrawal, ciwa, detox, wernicke, nursing.

This pass covers the alcohol withdrawal syndrome (time course, frequency, risk prediction, kindling), the CIWA-Ar and other scales, withdrawal management (setting, benzodiazepines, regimens, phenobarbital and other agents, seizures, delirium, resistant withdrawal and ICU care), Wernicke encephalopathy and Korsakoff syndrome (thiamine, diagnosis, glucose question, magnesium), and nursing care. Every dose, regimen and threshold below is quoted as published, with the guideline or label named. None of it is an instruction. The CIWA-Ar and PAWSS material is for teaching only. It is not a self-test and is not for reading one's own results.

## Findings

### withdrawal · What it is and how common it is
- ASAM 2020 says alcohol withdrawal happens in people who are physically dependent on alcohol when they stop or cut down. The cause is a sudden reversal of alcohol's depressant effects on the brain. Its most severe results are seizure, delirium and death [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 says withdrawal management alone "is not an effective treatment for alcohol use disorder". It should be used as a way into AUD treatment [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- "Withdrawal management" has replaced the older word "detoxification" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- The ASAM 2020 guideline was built with the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method from 19 existing guidelines, 112 reviews and 150 research studies (literature January 2012 to October 2017), rated by an expert physician panel [[Lindsay2020ASAM]].
- DSM-5 alcohol withdrawal, as reproduced in ASAM 2020, requires stopping or cutting down after heavy and prolonged use. Two or more of eight signs must then appear within several hours to a few days. The eight signs are:
  - autonomic hyperactivity (for example sweating or pulse >100 bpm)
  - increased hand tremor
  - insomnia
  - nausea or vomiting
  - transient visual, tactile or auditory hallucinations or illusions
  - psychomotor agitation
  - anxiety
  - generalized tonic-clonic seizures [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]
- ASAM 2020 estimates that 2-7% of hospital patients with heavy alcohol use go on to develop moderate to severe withdrawal [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Mirijello 2015 states that up to 50% of patients with AUD have withdrawal symptoms, and only a minority need medical treatment [[Mirijello2015]].
- In clinical studies of people admitted for detoxification, 13 to 71 percent developed significant symptoms (Saitz 1998 review, citing Victor & Adams 1953 and Saitz 1994) [[Saitz1998AHRW]].
- A 1995 estimate quoted by ASAM found that about 10% of people with alcohol withdrawal syndrome need inpatient treatment [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Maldonado 2015: AUD affects more than 40% of hospitalized medically ill patients. Most have uncomplicated withdrawal, but complicated withdrawal (seizures, delirium tremens) occurs "in up to 20% of cases" [[Maldonado2015]].
- Jesse 2017 review: complicated withdrawal (seizures and/or DT) occurs in up to 15% of patients with AUD [[Jesse2017]].
- In surgical ICU patients, reported withdrawal rates range from 8% to 40% [[Mirijello2015,Seshadri2022AAST]].
- Across ICU studies, reported withdrawal rates run from below 1% in all ICU patients to 60% in highly selected alcohol-dependent ICU patients [[Awissi2013]].
- COGA interview study of 1648 alcohol-dependent men and women: 211 people (12.8%) reported at least one lifetime episode of DT and/or withdrawal convulsions [[Schuckit1995]].

### withdrawal · Time course (0-120 h)
- ASAM 2020: symptoms usually begin 6-24 h after stopping or cutting down. Early features are:
  - anxiety, sleep disturbance, anorexia, vivid dreams, headache and nausea
  - tachycardia, hyperactive reflexes, sweating, raised blood pressure and hyperthermia [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]
- ASAM 2020 on seizures: they may begin as early as 8 h after the last drink, can continue for up to 48 h, and peak at about 24 h [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 on hallucinations: they develop within 12-24 h and usually resolve within 24-48 h, unless signs of delirium appear [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 is not consistent on when delirium begins:
  - The initial-assessment section puts onset at 72-96 h after the last drink, lasting from a few hours to, usually, 2-3 days.
  - The differential-diagnosis section says onset "typically occurs 24-48 hours" after cessation but "can develop as many as 3-5 days later" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Mirijello 2015 stage table (hours after the last drink):
  - stage I, minor withdrawal (tremor, sweating, nausea or vomiting, hypertension, tachycardia, hyperthermia, tachypnea): 6-12 h
  - stage II, hallucinosis: 12-24 h
  - stage III, seizures: 24-48 h
  - stage IV, delirium tremens: 48-72 h [[Mirijello2015]]
- Mirijello 2015 on DT: it usually appears 48-72 h after the last drink but "could begin up to 10 days later", and symptoms normally last 5-7 days [[Mirijello2015]].
- Mirijello 2015: mild to moderate withdrawal often resolves within 2-7 days of the last drink. Some symptoms can last weeks to months after that ("protracted AWS") [[Mirijello2015]].
- Jesse 2017: symptoms start about 6 h after stopping, and early withdrawal lasts 4-48 h. Seizures can appear 6-48 h after the last drink. DT begins 48-72 h after the last drink and may last up to 2 weeks [[Jesse2017]].
- Hillbom 2003: seizures usually occur 6-48 h after stopping drinking [[Hillbom2003]].
- Saitz 1998:
  - DTs can develop 1-4 days after withdrawal begins and last up to 3-4 days.
  - Hallucinosis may occur within 1-2 days, and it is distinct from DT.
  - Seizures may occur within 1-2 days even without other withdrawal signs.
  - A second seizure, if it occurs, "generally happens within 6 hours of the first" [[Saitz1998AHRW]].
- ASAM 2020: not every patient passes through the stages in order. A seizure can happen without other prominent signs, and older adults can follow a different timeline [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020: a patient with mild withdrawal and low risk can be watched for up to 36 h, "after which more severe withdrawal is unlikely to develop" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Ng 1988 case-control study (308 patients with a first seizure, 294 controls): 16% of first seizures in drinkers fell outside the conventional withdrawal period, and the rest showed "seemingly random timing" after the last drink. The authors argue that alcohol-related seizures can be dose-related, separately from withdrawal. The adjusted OR for unprovoked seizures was about 3 at 51-100 g/day, 8 at 101-200 g/day, and almost 20 at 201-300 g/day [[Ng1988NEJM]].

### withdrawal · Seizures
- Jesse 2017: more than 90% of acute symptomatic seizures appear within 48 h of stopping prolonged drinking [[Jesse2017]].
- Jesse 2017: more than half of affected people have repeated seizures, and in up to 5% the seizures progress to status epilepticus [[Jesse2017]].
- Mirijello 2015: about 10% of patients with withdrawal symptoms develop withdrawal seizures. These are generalized tonic-clonic seizures with little or no postictal period [[Mirijello2015]].
- Hillbom 2003: alcohol misuse precipitates 9-25% of cases of status epilepticus [[Hillbom2003]].
- Alldredge 1993: of 249 adults with generalized convulsive status epilepticus, alcohol was the only identifiable cause in 27 (10.8%). For 12 of them (44%), status epilepticus was the first alcohol-related seizure [[Alldredge1993]].
- ASAM 2020: attribute a seizure to withdrawal only if the person recently stopped or cut down. The EFNS guideline recommends neuroimaging (CT or MRI) after a first seizure [[ASAM2020Withdrawal,Brathen2005EFNS]].
- D'Onofrio 1999 RCT (186 ED patients after a witnessed alcohol-related generalized seizure):
  - Recurrent seizure within 6 h: 3/100 (3%) with IV lorazepam 2 mg vs 21/86 (24%) with placebo. OR for seizure with placebo 10.4 (95% CI 3.6-30.2).
  - Admissions: 29% vs 42% [[DOnofrio1999NEJM]].
- EFNS 2005: observe for at least 24 h after a withdrawal seizure. Benzodiazepines are recommended for primary and secondary seizure prevention [[Brathen2005EFNS]].
- Hillbom 2003 meta-analytic summary: lorazepam was effective for secondary seizure prevention and phenytoin was not. Long-term antiepileptic drugs are unnecessary for withdrawal seizures in people who stay abstinent [[Hillbom2003]].
- ASAM 2020: phenytoin should not be used unless there is a separate underlying seizure disorder. Beta-blockers can lower the seizure threshold [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].

### withdrawal · Delirium tremens (alcohol withdrawal delirium)
- DSM-5 alcohol withdrawal delirium, as reproduced in ASAM 2020, has these features:
  - a disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over hours to a few days and fluctuates
  - an added disturbance in cognition
  - evidence that heavy, prolonged alcohol use and withdrawal are the physiological cause [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]
- Saitz 1998: DTs involve disorientation, usually with severe agitation, tachycardia, high blood pressure and fever. The colloquial "shakes" are a different, milder condition [[Saitz1998AHRW]].
- Mirijello 2015: DT occurs in about 5% of patients with withdrawal syndrome, often as the result of no treatment or undertreatment [[Mirijello2015]].
- Ferguson 1996, inner-city hospital cohort (200 patients treated for withdrawal):
  - 48 (24%) developed DT.
  - Independent predictors: more days since the last drink (OR 1.3) and an acute medical illness (OR 5.1).
  - DT rates by number of these factors: 9% with neither, 25% with one, 54% with both [[Ferguson1996]].
- DT mortality varies by source and era. Each figure is a group statistic:
  - Saitz 1998: about 5% of people with DTs died from metabolic or cardiovascular complications, trauma or infection (citing Victor & Adams 1953 and Cutshall 1964) [[Saitz1998AHRW]].
  - Mirijello 2015: 1-5% [[Mirijello2015]].
  - Kattimani 2013: about 8% [[Kattimani2013]].
  - Jesse 2017: about 1% or less with early detection and appropriate treatment [[Jesse2017]].
- Mayo-Smith 2004 ASAM meta-analysis of 9 prospective controlled trials:
  - Sedative-hypnotics beat neuroleptics for shortening delirium and reducing mortality. The relative risk of death with neuroleptics was 6.6.
  - No deaths were reported in 217 patients treated with benzodiazepines or barbiturates [[MayoSmith2004Delirium]].
- Monte 2010, Spanish general-hospital cohort, 1987-2003 (539 admissions for withdrawal in 436 patients; 71.1% had DT):
  - 29 deaths, a 6.6% mortality rate (95% CI 4.2-9.1%).
  - Independent risk factors: cirrhosis (OR 4.8), DT at diagnosis (OR 3.5), other chronic illness (OR 2.5) and intubation (OR 2.9) [[Monte2010]].
- Khan 2008 case-control study (35 deaths from DT, 2000-2006): in the adjusted model, restraint use (OR 5.8) and hyperthermia in the first 24 h (OR 6.1) were associated with death [[Khan2008AEM]].
- Mainerova 2015: untreated or late-recognized DT can be fatal through malignant arrhythmia, respiratory arrest, sepsis, severe electrolyte disturbance, or prolonged seizures and trauma. DT should be managed in an ICU or a ward with vital-sign monitoring [[Mainerova2015]].
- ASAM 2020: if a patient has been delirious for longer than 72 h, assess for drug-induced delirium and withdrawal from another GABAergic agent (for example gabapentin or carisoprodol) [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].

### withdrawal · Who is at risk of complicated withdrawal
- ASAM 2020 risk factors for complicated withdrawal:
  - past withdrawal delirium or withdrawal seizure
  - numerous prior withdrawal episodes
  - medical or surgical illness, especially traumatic brain injury
  - age >65
  - long duration of heavy, regular drinking
  - a seizure in the current episode
  - marked autonomic hyperactivity
  - physiological dependence on benzodiazepines or barbiturates [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]
- ASAM 2020 further factors that may raise risk: other substance use, a positive blood alcohol level alongside withdrawal signs, and an active co-occurring psychiatric disorder of moderate severity. Several risk factors together raise the risk further [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Goodson 2014 meta-analysis (15 studies) of what predicts complicated withdrawal:
  - A past similar event was the strongest predictor: OR 2.58 for DT and 2.8 for seizures.
  - A lower platelet count (mean difference -45.64/mm3) and lower potassium (-0.26 mEq/l) predicted DT.
  - Neither sex nor liver disease was predictive [[Goodson2014]].
- Eyer 2011 cohort (827 inpatients treated for moderate to severe withdrawal):
  - DT at admission was predicted by lower potassium (OR 0.33 per mmol/l), lower platelets (OR 0.42 per 100,000) and structural brain lesions (OR 5.8).
  - Seizures were predicted by structural brain lesions (OR 6.5) and by a later peak of withdrawal severity [[Eyer2011]].
- Wood 2018 JAMA Rational Clinical Examination (14 high-quality studies, 71,295 patients, 1355 cases of severe withdrawal):
  - A history of DT (LR 2.9, 95% CI 1.7-5.2) raised the likelihood of severe withdrawal, as did baseline systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg (LR 1.7, 95% CI 1.3-2.3).
  - No single sign ruled it out [[Wood2018JAMA]].
- Schuckit 1995: people with a history of DT or convulsions reported more drinks in their heaviest 24 h (40.9 vs 24.9) and more withdrawal episodes (28.2 vs 15.9) than other alcohol-dependent people [[Schuckit1995]].

### withdrawal · PAWSS (Prediction of Alcohol Withdrawal Severity Scale)
- PAWSS was built from a systematic review that screened 2802 articles and kept 233. It found 10 items associated with complicated withdrawal. In a 68-patient pilot, sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV were all 100% at a threshold of 4 [[Maldonado2014]].
- The ten PAWSS items, as summarized by the AAST 2022 consensus:
  - previous withdrawal
  - previous withdrawal seizures
  - history of DT
  - history of alcohol rehabilitation
  - previous blackouts
  - concomitant use of CNS depressants or illicit substances
  - recent intoxication
  - elevated blood alcohol level at admission
  - evidence of increased autonomic activity
  - The AAST text lists nine named factors for the ten items, and the published PAWSS splits concomitant substances into two items. The builders should take item wording from the original scale [[Seshadri2022AAST,Crepeault2025]].
- PAWSS opens with a screening question: "Have you consumed any amount of alcohol within the last 30 days" or "did the patient have a positive BAL upon admission". It can be used before any withdrawal signs appear [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Maldonado 2015 prospective validation (403 medically ill inpatients), using a cut-off of 4:
  - sensitivity 93.1% (95% CI 77.2-99.2%)
  - specificity 99.5% (95% CI 98.1-99.9%)
  - PPV 93.1% and NPV 99.5%
  - inter-rater concordance 0.963 [[Maldonado2015]]
- ASAM 2020 describes the PAWSS end-point as complicated withdrawal, defined as a CIWA-Ar score ≥15 [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Wood 2018 found PAWSS the most useful of the combined tools:
  - With ≥4 findings: LR 174 (95% CI 43-696), specificity 0.93.
  - With ≤3 findings: LR 0.07 (95% CI 0.02-0.26), sensitivity 0.99.
  - Most of the tools reviewed were not fully validated [[Wood2018JAMA]].
- Crepeault 2025 tested modified PAWSS versions in a Vancouver community detox unit (228 people, 75% with severe withdrawal):
  - The original cut-off of 4 did not perform well. The optimal cut-offs were about 6-7.
  - Sensitivity was 44-55% and specificity 71-85%, depending on the version [[Crepeault2025]].
- ASAM 2020 also lists the Luebeck Alcohol-Withdrawal Risk Scale (LARS). A LARS score ≥17 separated severe from mild-to-moderate withdrawal with 100% sensitivity and 88% specificity in 100 psychiatric inpatients [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].

### withdrawal · Kindling (repeated withdrawals)
- Kindling is a process in which a repeated weak stimulus that first causes no behavioral effect comes to cause effects such as seizures. Clinical and experimental evidence supports a kindling mechanism in alcohol withdrawal (Early evidence in humans; Established in animal models) [[Becker1998Kindling]].
- Brown 1988 (25 men with withdrawal seizures vs 25 without): 12/25 (48%) of those with seizures had 5 or more previous detoxifications, compared with 3/25 (12%) of controls. The authors found no link with drinking history [[Brown1988Kindling]].
- Becker 1998: animal studies show that NMDA-receptor sensitivity and the severity of withdrawal seizures both increase with the number of past withdrawal episodes. Some experimental evidence suggests that treating early withdrawal may delay later kindled seizures (Hypothesis for humans) [[Becker1998Kindling]].
- Malcolm 2002 RCT (136 outpatients): in people with two or more past medicated detoxifications, carbamazepine-treated patients drank less than 1 drink a day after treatment, compared with about 5 a day on lorazepam (P=.033). Lorazepam showed rebound symptoms after treatment [[Malcolm2002]].

### ciwa · The CIWA-Ar scale
- CIWA-Ar is a 10-item shortened version of the CIWA-A scale. It was designed to be efficient while keeping clinical usefulness, validity and reliability [[Sullivan1989CIWA]].
- CIWA-Ar items and score ranges (Mirijello 2015 table, modified from Sullivan 1989):
  - nausea or vomiting 0-7
  - tremor 0-7
  - paroxysmal sweats 0-7
  - anxiety 0-7
  - agitation 0-7
  - tactile disturbances 0-7
  - auditory disturbances 0-7
  - visual disturbances 0-7
  - headache 0-7
  - orientation and clouding of sensorium 0-4
  - Nine items score 0-7 and one scores 0-4, so the maximum total is 67 [[Mirijello2015,Sullivan1989CIWA]].
- Item anchors in the same table:
  - Tremor runs from 0 (none) to 7 ("severe tremors, even with arms not extended").
  - Orientation runs from 0 ("orientated, can do serial additions") to 4 ("disorientated for place and/or person") [[Mirijello2015]].
- Published severity bands differ by source:
  - ASAM 2020: mild <10, moderate 10-18, severe ≥19, and complicated ≥19 with seizure or delirium signs. ASAM adds that classification "is ultimately up to the judgment of clinicians" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
  - Mirijello 2015: <8 mild, 8-15 moderate, >15 severe. A score >15 also predicts seizures and delirium [[Mirijello2015]].
  - Saitz 1998: scores of 8 or less with no added seizure risk can be managed without specific drugs. Scores of 8-15 or more call for medication [[Saitz1998AHRW]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation I.6: withdrawal scales including CIWA-Ar "should not be used as a diagnostic tool", because conditions other than withdrawal can raise the score [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VI.9: CIWA-Ar is not recommended in delirium, because it relies on patient-reported symptoms. Use CAM-ICU, DDS, RASS or MINDS instead [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VII.16: in general medical or surgical patients, low scores can usually be trusted but high scores should be read with caution. Fever from infection, some medicines and communication problems can all confound the score [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Hecksel 2008 (124 randomly chosen Mayo Clinic inpatients on a CIWA-Ar protocol):
  - Only 60 (48%) met both requirements: recent drinking and intact verbal communication.
  - Of the rest: 9 (14%) were drinkers who could not communicate, 35 (55%) could communicate but had not been drinking, and 20 (31%) met neither [[Hecksel2008]].
- CAM-ICU validation (111 ventilated ICU patients): sensitivity 100% and 93% and specificity 98% and 100% for the two study nurses, kappa 0.96, and a mean of 2 minutes to administer [[Ely2001CAMICU]].
- RASS is a 10-level scale running from +4 ("combative") to -5 ("unarousable"), with high inter-rater reliability (kappa 0.64-0.82 across ICU types) [[Sessler2002RASS]].
- The Short Alcohol Withdrawal Scale (SAWS) is a 10-item self-administered scale for ambulatory care. It records symptoms over the prior few hours, rather than in the moment as CIWA-Ar does [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Glasgow Modified Alcohol Withdrawal Scale (5 items, maximum 10), as reported in ASAM Appendix III, for predicting CIWA-A ≥8:
  - GMAWS ≥1: sensitivity 100%, specificity 12%
  - GMAWS ≥2: sensitivity 98%, specificity 39% [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]

### ciwa · Symptom-triggered vs fixed-schedule dosing
- Saitz 1994 double-blind RCT (101 inpatients on a VA detox unit; people with a history of seizures excluded), symptom-triggered vs fixed-schedule chlordiazepoxide:
  - median treatment duration 9 h vs 68 h (P<.001)
  - chlordiazepoxide 100 mg vs 425 mg (P<.001)
  - no difference in withdrawal severity, seizures or DT [[Saitz1994JAMA]]
- Daeppen 2002 double-blind RCT (117 patients in Lausanne and Geneva), symptom-triggered vs fixed-schedule oxazepam:
  - treated with oxazepam: 22 (39%) vs 100%
  - mean dose 37.5 mg vs 231.4 mg
  - mean duration 20.0 h vs 62.7 h (all P<.001)
  - One seizure occurred in the symptom-triggered group. Comfort did not differ [[Daeppen2002]].
- Mayo-Smith 1997: individualizing therapy with withdrawal scales gives significantly less medication and shorter treatment (P<.001) [[MayoSmith1997]].
- Reoux 2000 chart review: patients managed with a CIWA-Ar PRN protocol received significantly fewer chlordiazepoxide-equivalent mg over a shorter time than patients on other units [[Reoux2000]].
- Amato 2010 Cochrane review: one 159-participant trial favored symptom-triggered dosing for end-of-treatment CIWA-Ar scores (MD -1.10, 95% CI -3.27 to 1.07). The authors warn that single-trial differences should be read "very cautiously" [[Amato2010Cochrane]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation V.23: inpatient symptom-triggered treatment "is the preferred benzodiazepine dosing method". A fixed scheduled taper may be used if symptom-triggered dosing cannot be [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Appendix V sample symptom-triggered regimen, quoted as published, not as an instruction: chlordiazepoxide "25-100 mg PO q4-6h when CIWA-Ar ≥ 10. Additional doses PRN" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Saitz 1998 sample regimen: CIWA-Ar every hour, with medication each hour while the score is at least 8-10 [[Saitz1998AHRW]].

### detox · Setting: ambulatory vs inpatient
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation III.1: withdrawal can usually be managed safely in an ambulatory setting when risk factors are limited or mitigated. The aim is the least intensive level of care that is safe and effective [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Table 2, Level 1-WM (outpatient): mild withdrawal (CIWA-Ar <10) is appropriate, moderate (10-18) is neutral or uncertain, and severe or complicated (≥19) is inappropriate [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Table 2, Level 2-WM (extended on-site monitoring): CIWA-Ar <18 is appropriate, severe but not complicated is uncertain, and complicated is inappropriate. In both levels, drinking >8 standard drinks a day is flagged as neutral or uncertain [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation IV.1: outpatients check in daily with a qualified provider for up to 5 days after stopping. Remote check-ins can alternate with in-person visits [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation IV.5, reasons to transfer to a more intensive level of care:
  - agitation or tremor that does not resolve despite several doses
  - persistent vomiting, marked agitation, hallucinations, confusion or seizure
  - worsening illness or over-sedation
  - return to drinking
  - syncope or unstable vital signs [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VII.8, ED discharge to ambulatory care needs all of the following:
  - mild withdrawal, or moderate withdrawal without complicating factors
  - not intoxicated
  - no history of seizures or delirium in withdrawal
  - no significant comorbidity
  - able to attend follow-up [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VII.27: consider inpatient care for every pregnant patient who needs withdrawal management, and offer it at CIWA-Ar ≥10. Consult an obstetrician. Benzodiazepines and barbiturates are the medicines of choice, because the risks of severe withdrawal are weighed as greater than the small first-trimester risks [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations II.20 and III.2: assess suicide risk at intake. Patients with active suicide risk need a setting that can manage it [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].

### detox · Benzodiazepines
- Mayo-Smith 1997 meta-analysis (ASAM, 1997):
  - Benzodiazepines reduced delirium by 4.9 cases per 100 patients (95% CI 0.7-9.0; P=.04).
  - They reduced seizures by 7.7 per 100 patients (95% CI 3.5-12.0; P=.003).
  - Beta-blockers, clonidine and carbamazepine eased symptoms, but the evidence was inadequate for their effect on seizures and delirium [[MayoSmith1997]].
- Amato 2010 Cochrane review (64 RCTs, 4309 participants): benzodiazepines vs placebo protected against seizures (3 studies, 324 participants, RR 0.16, 95% CI 0.04-0.69). Heterogeneity prevented "definite conclusions" on other outcomes [[Amato2010Cochrane]].
- Amato 2011 Cochrane overview (5 reviews, 114 studies, 7333 participants):
  - Benzodiazepines beat antipsychotics for seizures (4 studies, 633 participants, RR 0.24).
  - Evidence quality was high for 3% of results, moderate for 28%, low for 48% and very low for 20% [[Amato2011Cochrane]].
- Jesse 2017 and Mirijello 2015: within the first 2 days, benzodiazepines reduce seizures by up to 84% and prevent DT [[Jesse2017,Mirijello2015]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations IV.20 and V.19: no single benzodiazepine is more effective than another, but longer-acting agents are preferred for their longer action. In significant liver disease, use an agent with less hepatic metabolism [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Saitz 1998: diazepam, chlordiazepoxide and lorazepam are the best studied. Long-acting agents give a smoother course with less rebound. Short-acting agents carry more risk of rebound symptoms, including late seizures [[Saitz1998AHRW]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations IV.29 and V.24: front loading is recommended for severe withdrawal (CIWA-Ar ≥19), and diazepam or chlordiazepoxide are preferred. The sample front-loading regimen, quoted as published, is chlordiazepoxide "50-100 mg PO q1-2h until CIWA-Ar < 10" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Appendix V sample fixed-dose regimen: "Taper daily total dose by 25-50% per day over 3-5 days" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Valium (diazepam) label, quoted as published: in acute alcohol withdrawal, Valium "may be useful in the symptomatic relief of acute agitation, tremor, impending or acute delirium tremens and hallucinosis". The usual adult dose is "10 mg, 3 or 4 times during the first 24 hours, reducing to 5 mg, 3 or 4 times daily as needed" [[FDA-Valium]].
- Chlordiazepoxide capsules label: indicated for "withdrawal symptoms of acute alcoholism". The suggested oral starting dose is "50 to 100 mg, to be followed by repeated doses as needed until agitation is controlled up to 300 mg per day" [[FDA-Chlordiazepoxide]].
- Oxazepam label: "15 to 30 mg, 3 or 4 times daily" for acute inebriation, tremulousness or anxiety on withdrawal (label wording uses the older term) [[FDA-Oxazepam]].
- Lorazepam injection label indications are status epilepticus and preanesthetic use only. Alcohol withdrawal is not a labeled indication, although guidelines use IV lorazepam [[FDA-LorazepamInj,ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Every benzodiazepine label checked (Valium, chlordiazepoxide, oxazepam) carries a boxed warning that use with opioids "may result in profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death" [[FDA-Valium,FDA-Chlordiazepoxide,FDA-Oxazepam]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation IV.26: patients and caregivers should be taught about:
  - the danger of combining benzodiazepines with alcohol or other CNS depressants
  - not driving for the first few days
  - reducing the dose if drowsy [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VII.25: monitor people on chronic opioids (including opioid agonist treatment) closely when benzodiazepines are given, because of respiratory depression [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations IV.42 and V.38: "Oral or intravenous alcohol should not be used for the prevention or treatment of alcohol withdrawal" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VII.3 (primary care): if withdrawal does not settle below CIWA-Ar 10 after an adequate dose ("e.g., 80 mg diazepam"), or the patient appears sedated, transfer to an ED or an inpatient setting [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].

### detox · Carbamazepine, gabapentin and other agents
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations IV.16-IV.17 and V.15-V.16: for mild withdrawal, carbamazepine or gabapentin are appropriate. For moderate withdrawal, benzodiazepines come first and carbamazepine or gabapentin are alternatives [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020: valproic acid only as an adjunct, and not in liver disease or in women of childbearing potential [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Minozzi 2010 Cochrane review (56 studies, 4076 participants): anticonvulsants showed no significant benefit over placebo. Carbamazepine beat oxazepam or lorazepam on CIWA-Ar scores (3 studies, 262 participants, MD -1.04). Overall the evidence did not support anticonvulsants for withdrawal [[Minozzi2010Cochrane]].
- Myrick 2009 double-blind RCT (100 outpatients, CIWA-Ar ≥10):
  - High-dose gabapentin (1200 tapering to 800 mg) was statistically superior to lorazepam (6 tapering to 4 mg) on CIWA-Ar but clinically similar (p=0.009).
  - Gabapentin groups drank less during and just after withdrawal, with less craving, anxiety and sedation [[Myrick2009]].
- Malcolm 2002 RCT (136 outpatients in moderate withdrawal): carbamazepine (600-800 mg on day 1) and lorazepam (6-8 mg on day 1) were equally effective. 20% of lorazepam patients had dizziness, incoordination or ataxia they did not recognize. 20% on carbamazepine reported itching without rash [[Malcolm2002]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation IV.32: gabapentin is a favorable choice when the clinician also plans to use it for ongoing AUD treatment [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations IV.40-IV.41 and V.36-V.37: alpha-2 agonists (clonidine, dexmedetomidine) and beta-blockers are adjuncts only, for autonomic signs not controlled by benzodiazepines. They "should not be used alone" to prevent or treat seizures or delirium [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations IV.43-IV.44: the evidence is insufficient for baclofen, and magnesium as prophylaxis or treatment for withdrawal itself "has no supporting evidence". This is separate from correcting low magnesium (see nursing) [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Mayo-Smith 1997: phenothiazines ease symptoms but are less effective than benzodiazepines at preventing delirium (P=.002) and seizures (P<.001) [[MayoSmith1997]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VI.20: antipsychotics are adjuncts only, for delirium and hallucinations not controlled by benzodiazepines, and are not recommended as monotherapy [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].

### detox · Phenobarbital
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations IV.37 and V.32: phenobarbital should be used only by clinicians experienced with it, "given its narrow therapeutic window". Parenteral phenobarbital belongs only in highly supervised settings such as the ICU or CCU [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Appendix V phenobarbital examples, quoted as published, not as instructions:
  - single dose: "10 mg/kg IV infused over 30 minutes or 60-260 mg PO/IM"
  - ICU symptom-triggered: "130 mg IV q30m to target a RASS score of 0 to -1" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]
- Rosenson 2013 double-blind RCT (102 ED patients): one dose of IV phenobarbital 10 mg/kg plus a symptom-guided lorazepam protocol, compared with placebo plus the same protocol. ICU admissions were 8% vs 25%, with no difference in adverse events [[Rosenson2013]].
- Hendey 2011 RCT (44 ED patients with mild to moderate withdrawal): IV phenobarbital (mean 509 mg) and lorazepam (mean 4.2 mg) were similarly effective, with CIWA scores falling from 15.0 to 5.4 and from 16.8 to 4.2 [[Hendey2011]].
- Lee 2024 ED meta-analysis (8 studies, 1507 patients) found no significant difference for phenobarbital vs benzodiazepines:
  - ICU admission RR 0.92 (95% CI 0.54-1.55)
  - hospital admission RR 0.98
  - adverse events RR 1.1 [[Lee2024AEM]]
- Tidwell 2018 retrospective ICU cohort, phenobarbital protocol vs CIWA-Ar benzodiazepine protocol:
  - ICU stay 2.4 vs 4.4 days
  - mechanical ventilation 2% vs 23%
  - dexmedetomidine use 7% vs 28% [[Tidwell2018]]
- Rizwan 2026 ICU meta-analysis (16 non-randomized studies):
  - Phenobarbital-based pathways did not significantly change intubation (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.20-1.87) or hospital stay.
  - ICU stay was 0.60 days shorter (95% CI 0.41-0.79).
  - Evidence certainty was very low (Early evidence) [[Rizwan2026]].

### detox · Resistant withdrawal and the ICU
- ASAM 2020 definition: resistant alcohol withdrawal "is not well defined". Prior reviews used uncontrolled symptoms despite more than 150-200 mg diazepam or 30-40 mg lorazepam in the first 3-4 hours [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations VI.27-VI.29:
  - Phenobarbital may be added to benzodiazepines with close monitoring.
  - Propofol may be used in ICU patients with resistant withdrawal who already need mechanical ventilation.
  - Dexmedetomidine may be used in the ICU [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Gold 2007 ICU before-and-after study (54 patients before the guideline, 41 after):
  - Escalating diazepam boluses plus phenobarbital raised the maximum single diazepam dose from 32 to 86 mg and the total from 248 to 562 mg.
  - Phenobarbital use rose from 17% to 58%.
  - Mechanical ventilation fell from 47% to 22% (p=.008) [[Gold2007CCM]].
- Mueller 2014 RCT (24 ICU patients with CIWA ≥15 despite ≥16 mg lorazepam over 4 h):
  - Adjunct dexmedetomidine reduced 24-h lorazepam use more than placebo (-56 vs -8 mg).
  - 7-day totals did not differ (159 vs 181 mg).
  - Bradycardia occurred in 25% vs 0% [[Mueller2014]].
- Pizon 2018 retrospective study (63 ICU patients with DT): adjunct ketamine infusion was associated with less intubation (OR 0.14) and an ICU stay 2.83 days shorter (Early evidence) [[Pizon2018]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VI.17: very large benzodiazepine doses may be needed in DT, "much higher than typically seen in other patient populations". Clinicians should watch for over-sedation, respiratory depression and accumulation in older adults and in liver disease [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VI.15: patients receiving repeated high IV doses of lorazepam or diazepam should be watched for hyponatremia and metabolic acidosis [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VI.14: continuous IV infusion "is considerably more expensive and there is no evidence of therapeutic superiority" over intermittent IV doses [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Awissi 2013: early, aggressive, symptom-guided titration was the only feature associated with better treatment outcomes in ICU withdrawal. The benefit of prophylaxis was unproven [[Awissi2013]].
- Long 2017 ED review: benzodiazepines with symptom-triggered dosing remain first-line, with rapid escalation. Phenobarbital or propofol are for resistant cases, and dexmedetomidine and ketamine need more study [[Long2017AJEM]].

### wernicke · Thiamine deficiency, the triad and how often it is missed
- Wernicke encephalopathy (WE) is an acute neuropsychiatric syndrome caused by thiamine deficiency. Autopsy studies show it is still greatly underdiagnosed [[Sechi2007]].
- Harper 1986 (131 cases diagnosed at necropsy):
  - 80% had not been diagnosed in life.
  - Only 16% had the classic triad.
  - 19% had no documented clinical signs [[Harper1986JNNP]].
- Kattimani 2013 states that WE rarely shows the classic triad of confusion, ataxia and ophthalmoplegia and so goes undiagnosed "in nearly 90% of the cases" [[Kattimani2013]].
- Harper 1979 (Perth): WE was found in 1.7% of all necropsies (51 cases), and only 7 had been diagnosed in life [[Harper1979JNNP]].
- Torvik 1982 (Oslo): 70 of 8735 autopsies (0.8%) showed WE, and stupor and coma dominated in active cases [[Torvik1982]].
- Harper 1998 (Sydney, 2212 autopsies, 1996-1997):
  - WKS prevalence was 1.1%, mostly among the 5.9% with a history suggesting heavy drinking.
  - Only 4 cases (16%) had been diagnosed in life.
  - Prevalence had fallen since bread flour was enriched with thiamine [[Harper1998MJA]].
- Dingwall 2022: more than 90% of WKS cases are reported in alcohol-dependent patients [[Dingwall2022RCT]].
- Arts 2017: in modern cohorts at least 90% of patients with WE or Korsakoff syndrome have heavy alcohol use. WE "usually (in 50%-70%) has an unobtrusive symptomatology and course" [[Arts2017]].
- Isenberg-Grzeda 2012: most cases of WE are missed, because patients do not show the classic signs [[IsenbergGrzeda2012]].

### wernicke · Diagnosis: Caine criteria, imaging
- Caine 1997 operational criteria need 2 of 4 signs:
  - dietary deficiencies
  - oculomotor abnormalities
  - cerebellar dysfunction
  - an altered mental state or mild memory impairment
  - Tested on 106 cases from a necropsy sample, rater classification was 100% reliable. WE was under-recognized only when hepatic encephalopathy was also present (50% sensitivity) [[Caine1997]].
- EFNS 2010 Recommendation 2 adopts the same 2-of-4 rule for people with heavy alcohol use (Level B). The guideline also advises measuring total blood thiamine immediately before giving thiamine (good practice point) [[Galvin2010EFNS]].
- Jesse 2017: Caine/EFNS criteria apply, and in severe withdrawal with DT the distinction between DT and WE can be hard [[Jesse2017]].
- Antunez 1998 (15 patients with acute WE): MRI sensitivity 53% and specificity 93%. CT "proved not useful", and a normal MRI does not exclude WE [[Antunez1998]].
- EFNS 2010: MRI should be used to support the diagnosis (Level B) [[Galvin2010EFNS]].

### wernicke · Thiamine regimens (as published, not instructions)
- EFNS 2010 Recommendation 5 for suspected or manifest WE: give thiamine before any carbohydrate, "200 mg thrice daily, preferably intravenously" (Level C). The overall safety of thiamine is very good (Level B) [[Galvin2010EFNS]].
- EFNS 2010 Recommendation 8: give parenteral thiamine to all at-risk people admitted to the emergency room (good practice point) [[Galvin2010EFNS]].
- RCP London 2002 (A&E guidance): people showing evidence of chronic heavy drinking with a suspected poor diet should get IV or IM B vitamins at the outset, especially when intoxication at presentation masks the clinical signs [[Thomson2002RCP]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation V.7, inpatient prevention:
  - IV or IM thiamine preferred, typically "100 mg IV/IM per day for 3-5 days"
  - oral thiamine can also be offered
  - thiamine and glucose may be given "in any order or concurrently" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation IV.9, ambulatory care: oral thiamine can be offered, "Typical dosing is 100 mg PO per day for 3-5 days" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 ICU discussion, citing one study: for patients whose signs mimic or mask WE, it suggests "200-500 mg IV thiamine every 8 hours, 64 mg/kg magnesium sulfate, and 400-1,000 mcg IV folate" instead of the "banana bag" multivitamin infusion [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Kattimani 2013 states that everyone in withdrawal should receive at least 250 mg parenteral thiamine daily for 3-5 days, and 500 mg/day for 3-5 days if WE is suspected. Sources disagree on dose [[Kattimani2013]].
- Thiamine injection label, as published:
  - In WKS, it "has been administered IV in an initial dose of 100 mg, followed by IM doses of 50 to 100 mg daily until the patient is consuming a regular, balanced diet."
  - Patients with marginal thiamine status receiving dextrose "should receive 100 mg thiamine hydrochloride in each of the first few liters of IV fluid" [[FDA-ThiamineInj]].
- The same label warns that "serious hypersensitivity/anaphylactic reactions can occur, especially after repeated administration. Deaths have resulted from IV or IM administration of thiamine" [[FDA-ThiamineInj]].
- Day 2013 Cochrane review found only 2 eligible RCTs:
  - One (Ambrose 2001, n=107, five IM doses for 2 days) favored 200 mg/day over 5 mg/day on one memory test (MD -17.90, 95% CI -35.4 to -0.40).
  - Evidence is "insufficient to guide clinicians" on dose, frequency, route or duration [[Day2013Cochrane]].
- Dingwall 2022 double-blind RCTs (Australia):
  - prevention arm, 393 at-risk patients: 100 mg daily vs 100 mg three times daily vs 300 mg three times daily for 3 days
  - treatment arm, 127 symptomatic patients: 100 vs 300 vs 500 mg three times daily for 5 days
  - No dose was clearly better on cognition or neurological signs, and the study had acknowledged limitations [[Dingwall2022RCT]].
- Jesse 2017: the earlier thiamine is started, the faster the recovery, whatever the initial presentation [[Jesse2017]].

### wernicke · Thiamine before glucose? Magnesium and refeeding
- Schabelman 2012 reviewed 19 papers (13 case reports or series, 4 animal studies, 2 expert opinions) and found no evidence above case-report level:
  - Prolonged glucose without thiamine may be a risk factor for WE.
  - Hypoglycemia treatment should not be delayed. Thiamine should follow promptly, after or together with the return to normal glucose [[Schabelman2012]].
- Jasti 2025 (VA EDs 2010-2019): in 120 encounters where people with alcohol intoxication received dextrose before thiamine, no WE cases were found within 90 days (Early evidence) [[Jasti2025]].
- EFNS 2010 and EFNS 2005 still advise giving thiamine before any carbohydrate-containing fluids or food. ASAM 2020 allows any order. This is a real difference between guidelines [[Galvin2010EFNS,Brathen2005EFNS,ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Dingwall 2015 (105 patients with heavy alcohol use, Alice Springs):
  - Magnesium deficiency (<0.80 mmol/L) was present in 48% of those tested.
  - Serum magnesium, but not thiamine pyrophosphate, correlated with cognitive scores.
  - Low magnesium may impair thiamine use (Hypothesis) [[Dingwall2015Mg]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation V.9: give magnesium to patients with hypomagnesemia, arrhythmias, electrolyte disturbances, or past withdrawal seizures [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation V.10: supplement phosphorus if it is <1 mg/dL. For moderate hypophosphatemia (1-2 mg/dL), correct it through proper nutrition [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Thomson & Marshall 2006: when WE comes from thiamine deficiency without heavy drinking, Korsakoff psychosis rarely follows treatment. In people with alcohol dependence, outcome may also depend on alcohol-related neurotoxicity [[Thomson2006Natural]].

### wernicke · Korsakoff syndrome
- Kopelman 2009: Korsakoff syndrome is a preventable memory disorder that usually follows an episode of WE.
  - Episodic memory is severely affected, while implicit memory is preserved.
  - People can learn new things in a calm, structured, cued environment.
  - People with Korsakoff syndrome "are reported to have a normal life expectancy if they remain abstinent" [[Kopelman2009]].
- Arts 2017: the often-quoted progression rate of about 84% from WE to Korsakoff syndrome in people with heavy alcohol use comes from the Victor monograph and was not meant as a general estimate. A later study found 56%, so the 85% figure is "an unreliably high estimation" [[Arts2017]].
- Arts 2017 epidemiology: Korsakoff prevalence was 4.8 per 10,000 inhabitants in The Hague (1987) and about 3 per 10,000 in North-Holland (1992). Annual incidence was about 0.5 per 10,000 in Glasgow (1990-1995) [[Arts2017]].
- Kattimani 2013 states that WE carries a 20% mortality, with 75% going on to a permanent amnestic (Korsakoff) syndrome. These are review figures quoted without a primary source here [[Kattimani2013]].
- Torvik 1982: a pure Korsakoff psychosis was present in about a third of inactive WE cases. Most of the rest had more global dementia, which the authors linked to inactive WE plus other lesions [[Torvik1982]].
- Arts 2017: memory rehabilitation is possible in Korsakoff syndrome, using agendas, memory cards, smartphones and smartwatches [[Arts2017]].

### nursing · Monitoring and reassessment
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation V.1 inpatient monitoring schedule:
  - For moderate to severe withdrawal, or anyone on medication: "re-assess every 1-4 hours for 24 hours".
  - Once stable (for example CIWA-Ar <10 for 24 h): "every 4-8 hours for 24 hours" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Across inpatient vs outpatient studies reviewed by ASAM, monitoring intervals ranged from 30 minutes to 8 hours [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Saitz 1998 sample monitoring: CIWA-Ar every 4-8 h until the score has been below 8-10 for 24 h, and hourly in symptom-triggered regimens [[Saitz1998AHRW]].
- Mirijello 2015: repeat CIWA-Ar at least every 8 h, and hourly when scores are >8-10 or treatment is given [[Mirijello2015]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VI.2: after a withdrawal seizure, admit to a closely monitored setting and reassess every 1-2 h for 6-24 h. Watch for delirium and the need for IV fluids [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations V.2-V.3: monitor vital signs, hydration, orientation, sleep, emotional state and suicidal thoughts. Watch for over-sedation and respiratory depression on medication [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VII.15: monitor fluid intake and output and serum electrolytes as indicated in hospitalized patients [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VII.7: ED patients should get a CBC and a complete metabolic panel including liver enzymes and magnesium, without delaying withdrawal treatment for the results [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].

### nursing · Safety, delirium care and supportive care
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation V.5: frequent reassurance, re-orientation to time and place, and nursing care are recommended. Severe withdrawal should be cared for "in an evenly lit, quiet room", and patients "should be offered hope and the expectation of recovery" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation V.6: supportive care includes safety measures such as assessing fall and syncope risk. Hospital delirium protocols can be used [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations VI.7-VI.8: delirium needs close nursing observation, often in ICU. Agitated, disoriented patients need continuous one-to-one observation, with resuscitation equipment at hand when high benzodiazepine doses are used [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations VI.10 and VI.12: provide immediate IV access in withdrawal delirium. Use restraints "only when required to prevent injuries", in line with state law [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Restraint use was independently associated with death in DT in one case-control study (OR 5.8). The study shows association, not cause [[Khan2008AEM]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation VI.13: the sedation target in withdrawal delirium is "light somnolence" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal,MayoSmith2004Delirium]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendations IV.6-IV.8 for ambulatory patients and caregivers:
  - Teach them what to expect and how to spot worsening withdrawal.
  - Keep a low-stimulation, reassuring home.
  - Drink non-caffeinated fluids. A daily multivitamin may help [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation V.12: use the withdrawal period to engage the person in AUD treatment, including starting AUD medication when cognition allows and a "warm handoff" to treatment providers [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- ASAM 2020 Recommendation IV.24: in ambulatory care, limit benzodiazepine supplies to the minimum needed to manage misuse or diversion risk. Carbamazepine or gabapentin are alternatives [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].

### Exam hooks
- Minor withdrawal starts 6-24 h after the last drink, seizures peak at about 24 h (window 8-48 h), and hallucinosis appears at 12-24 h with a clear sensorium [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Delirium tremens is the late complication: onset 48-72 h (Mirijello; Jesse) or 72-96 h (ASAM), with fluctuating attention plus autonomic overactivity [[Mirijello2015,Jesse2017,ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Alcoholic hallucinosis means hallucinations without clouding of consciousness, and it is distinct from DT [[Saitz1998AHRW,ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Benzodiazepines are first line because they reduce both seizures and delirium. In Mayo-Smith 1997, seizures fell by 7.7 and delirium by 4.9 per 100 patients [[MayoSmith1997]].
- Symptom-triggered dosing gives less drug for a shorter time (Saitz 1994: 100 vs 425 mg chlordiazepoxide, 9 vs 68 h), with similar safety [[Saitz1994JAMA]].
- CIWA-Ar has 10 items, nine scored 0-7 and orientation 0-4, for a maximum of 67 [[Mirijello2015]].
- ASAM bands: mild <10, moderate 10-18, severe ≥19 [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- CIWA-Ar needs a patient who can talk and has recently been drinking. Use RASS or CAM-ICU in delirium or in people who cannot communicate [[ASAM2020Withdrawal,Hecksel2008]].
- PAWSS ≥4 means high risk of complicated withdrawal (Maldonado 2015: sensitivity 93.1%, specificity 99.5%) [[Maldonado2015]].
- The strongest predictor of DT or seizures is a past DT or seizure (OR 2.58 and 2.8) [[Goodson2014]].
- Low platelets and low potassium at admission predict DT [[Goodson2014,Eyer2011]].
- Alpha-2 agonists and beta-blockers only mask autonomic signs. They do not prevent seizures or DT [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Phenytoin is not used for alcohol withdrawal seizures [[ASAM2020Withdrawal,Hillbom2003]].
- Antipsychotics alone raise the risk of death in DT (RR 6.6 vs sedative-hypnotics) [[MayoSmith2004Delirium]].
- The Wernicke triad (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia) was present in only 16% at autopsy [[Harper1986JNNP]].
- Caine criteria: 2 of 4 (diet, eyes, cerebellum, mental state) [[Caine1997,Galvin2010EFNS]].
- EFNS: thiamine 200 mg three times daily IV for suspected WE, before carbohydrate. ASAM: glucose and thiamine in any order [[Galvin2010EFNS,ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Korsakoff syndrome means severe anterograde episodic memory loss with confabulation. It is a residual syndrome after WE, linked to lesions of the mammillary bodies and anterior thalamus [[Kopelman2009,Arts2017]].
- Repeated withdrawals "kindle" worse withdrawal: in Brown 1988, 48% of people with seizures had ≥5 prior detoxifications, compared with 12% without [[Brown1988Kindling]].
- Never treat withdrawal with alcohol [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].

### In plain words
- If your body has got used to alcohol and you stop suddenly, your brain can be over-excited for a few days. This is called withdrawal [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Early signs are shaking hands, sweating, a fast heartbeat, feeling anxious or sick, and poor sleep. They usually start 6 to 24 hours after the last drink [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Stopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous. It can cause seizures or delirium tremens. Get medical help to stop safely [[ASAM2020Withdrawal,Mirijello2015]].
- Most people can be helped to withdraw safely at home or at a clinic with daily check-ins. Some need a hospital bed [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Delirium tremens ("DTs") means severe confusion, seeing or hearing things, fever and a racing heart, usually two to four days after the last drink. It is a medical emergency [[Saitz1998AHRW,Mirijello2015]].
- With modern hospital care, most people with DTs survive. This is a group statistic, not a promise for any one person [[Jesse2017,MayoSmith2004Delirium]].
- Nurses may ask questions and score signs on a checklist called the CIWA-Ar. That helps them give medicine only when it is needed [[Saitz1994JAMA,ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Do not mix alcohol with sleeping pills, benzodiazepines, opioids or other sedatives. Together they can slow or stop breathing [[FDA-Valium,ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Drinking alcohol is not a treatment for withdrawal [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Heavy drinking can use up vitamin B1 (thiamine). Very low B1 can cause Wernicke encephalopathy, with confusion, unsteady walking and eye problems [[Galvin2010EFNS,Sechi2007]].
- Thiamine is cheap and is given early, often by injection, to protect the brain [[Galvin2010EFNS,ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- If Wernicke encephalopathy is not treated, it can leave lasting memory problems (Korsakoff syndrome). People can still learn with structure and memory aids [[Kopelman2009,Arts2017]].
- Getting through withdrawal is a first step. Ongoing treatment for alcohol use disorder is what helps recovery last [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]].
- Help line text for the disclaimer and home page: "If you or someone else may have alcohol poisoning or severe withdrawal (seizure, confusion, hallucinations), call emergency services. For help with drinking in the US, SAMHSA's National Helpline is free and confidential: 1-800-662-4357. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123)."

## Open questions and controversies
- Severity cut-offs on CIWA-Ar: ASAM 2020 uses <10, 10-18 and ≥19, and says the choice is a matter of clinical judgment [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]. Older reviews use <8, 8-15 and >15 [[Mirijello2015,Saitz1998AHRW]]. Status: no single validated set. The builders should label every band with its source.
- When does DT begin? It is put at 48-72 h by [[Mirijello2015,Jesse2017]] and 72-96 h by one ASAM passage, while another ASAM passage gives "24-48 hours... as many as 3-5 days later" [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]. Saitz says 1-4 days after withdrawal begins [[Saitz1998AHRW]]. Status: unresolved. A timeline should show a band, not a point.
- How deadly is DT? Figures run from ≤1% with early treatment [[Jesse2017]] through 1-5% [[Mirijello2015]] and about 5% (historical series) [[Saitz1998AHRW]] to about 8% [[Kattimani2013]]. A Spanish hospital withdrawal cohort reported 6.6% [[Monte2010]]. The brief's "15-20% untreated" figure could not be traced to a source I could read (unverified). Status: era, setting and comorbidity drive the spread.
- Phenobarbital vs benzodiazepines. One ED RCT showed fewer ICU admissions with add-on phenobarbital [[Rosenson2013]], but the ED meta-analysis found no difference [[Lee2024AEM]]. In the ICU, observational data favor phenobarbital pathways for ICU stay, with very-low-certainty evidence on intubation [[Rizwan2026,Tidwell2018]]. ASAM keeps benzodiazepines first line [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]. Status: randomized ICU trials needed.
- Thiamine before glucose. EFNS 2010 and 2005 say thiamine before carbohydrate [[Galvin2010EFNS,Brathen2005EFNS]]. ASAM 2020 says any order [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]. The evidence is case reports only [[Schabelman2012]], and a VA series found no WE after dextrose-first [[Jasti2025]]. Status: never delay glucose for hypoglycemia, and give thiamine promptly.
- Thiamine dose. ASAM 2020 gives a typical 100 mg/day for 3-5 days for prevention [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]. EFNS 2010 gives 200 mg three times daily IV for suspected WE [[Galvin2010EFNS]], and Kattimani gives 250-500 mg/day [[Kattimani2013]]. The RCT evidence does not settle the dose [[Day2013Cochrane,Dingwall2022RCT]]. Status: unresolved.
- Does PAWSS work outside general hospitals? It was excellent in medically ill inpatients [[Maldonado2015]], but poor in a community detox unit, where severe withdrawal was common [[Crepeault2025]]. Status: setting-specific.
- Kindling: is early, adequate treatment protective? Animal data and clinical series support kindling [[Becker1998Kindling,Brown1988Kindling]]. Human evidence that treatment prevents later kindling is limited (Hypothesis).
- Prophylaxis in the ICU. ASAM recommends prophylaxis for people suspected of physiological dependence [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]. A systematic review found the benefit of prophylaxis "unproven" [[Awissi2013]].
