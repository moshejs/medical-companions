# C · Screening, diagnosis & biomarkers

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: screening, criteria, biomarkers.

This pass covers how unhealthy alcohol use is found (AUDIT, AUDIT-C, the single question, CAGE, CRAFFT and other brief tools; USPSTF 2018; brief intervention and SBIRT), how alcohol use disorder (AUD) is diagnosed (DSM-5-TR criteria, severity and remission; the DSM-IV to DSM-5 change; ICD-11 alcohol dependence and harmful pattern of use, and how the systems agree), and what laboratory tests can and cannot show (ethanol in breath and blood, EtG/EtS, PEth, CDT, GGT, MCV, AST:ALT, hair EtG). Every screening tool and threshold below is quoted as published. They are teaching material for students, not self-tests, and a score is never a diagnosis.

Access notes:

## Findings

### screening · Why screen, and the gap in practice
- AUD is undertreated, partly because of stigma and partly because of insufficient systematic screening in primary care; the Lancet seminar recommends routine screening for alcohol use in primary care with a stepped response from brief advice to medicines [[Carvalho2019Lancet]].
- In NESARC-III (US adults, 2012–2013, N = 36,309), only 19.8% of people with lifetime DSM-5 AUD had ever been treated [[Grant2015NESARC]].
- In 2017 BRFSS data from 13 US states and DC, 81.4% of adults said a health professional asked about alcohol use at a checkup in the past 2 years, but only 37.8% were asked about binge-level drinking (age-standardised) [[McKnightEily2020MMWR]].
- Among adults in that survey who reported current binge drinking, only 41.7% were advised about the harms of drinking too much and only 20.1% were advised to reduce or quit [[McKnightEily2020MMWR]].
- In the 2015–2016 US National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey, 71.7% of office-based primary care physicians reported screening for alcohol misuse, and fewer than 40% of those who screened said they always intervened when a patient screened positive [[Green2022PCP]].
- The USPSTF defines "unhealthy alcohol use" as a spectrum of behaviours, from risky drinking to AUD, that raise the risk of health consequences [[USPSTF2018Alcohol]].
- WHO terminology used with the AUDIT: "hazardous use" is a pattern that increases the risk of harmful consequences for the user, even without any current disorder; "harmful use" is a pattern already causing damage to health [[HigginsBiddle2018USAUDIT]].

### screening · USPSTF 2018 recommendation
- The USPSTF recommends screening for unhealthy alcohol use in primary care in adults 18 years or older, including pregnant women, and providing people engaged in risky or hazardous drinking with brief behavioural counselling (B recommendation) [[USPSTF2018Alcohol]].
- For adolescents aged 12 to 17 years, the USPSTF found the evidence insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms (I statement) [[USPSTF2018Alcohol]].
- The USPSTF judged the net benefit of screening plus brief counselling in adults, including pregnant women, to be moderate [[USPSTF2018Alcohol]].
- The statement cites an estimated 88,000 alcohol-attributable deaths a year in the United States in 2006–2010 [[USPSTF2018Alcohol]].
- The supporting evidence review included 113 studies (N = 314,466); no study directly examined the benefits or harms of a screening programme itself [[OConnor2018JAMA]].
- In adults (35 studies, n = 114,182), brief screening instruments commonly had sensitivity and specificity between 0.70 and 0.85 [[OConnor2018JAMA]].
- In adolescents, one study (n = 225) reported AUDIT-C sensitivity 0.73 (95% CI 0.60–0.83) and specificity 0.81 (95% CI 0.74–0.86) for the full spectrum of unhealthy alcohol use [[OConnor2018JAMA]].
- USPSTF recommendations for alcohol screening and brief intervention in adult primary care were issued in 2004, 2013 and 2018 [[McKnightEily2020MMWR]].

### screening · Brief intervention effect sizes
- Across 68 studies (n = 36,528), counselling interventions reduced drinks per week (weighted mean difference −1.6 drinks/week, 95% CI −2.2 to −1.0; 32 studies, n = 15,974) at 6 to 12 months [[OConnor2018JAMA]].
- Counselling reduced the proportion of people exceeding recommended limits (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.53–0.67) and the proportion reporting a heavy-use episode (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.58–0.77) at 6 to 12 months [[OConnor2018JAMA]].
- In pregnant women, counselling increased reported abstinence (OR 2.26, 95% CI 1.43–3.56; 5 studies, n = 796) [[OConnor2018JAMA]].
- Health outcomes were sparsely reported and generally showed no group differences, and there was no evidence that the interventions caused harm [[OConnor2018JAMA]].
- In two adolescent trials (n = 588) results were mixed: one found benefit in high-risk but not moderate-risk drinkers; the other found reduced drinking frequency in boys but not girls [[OConnor2018JAMA]].
- The 2018 Cochrane review (69 RCTs, 33,642 participants in primary care and emergency settings) found moderate-quality evidence that people given a brief intervention drank about 20 g/week less after one year than those given minimal or no intervention (MD −20 g/week, 95% CI −28 to −12; 34 studies, 15,197 participants; I² = 73%) [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- Mean baseline drinking in those trials was 244 g/week (30.5 UK units) [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- Cochrane defined a brief intervention as five or fewer sessions totalling less than 60 minutes; longer ("extended") counselling added little (MD 2 g/week vs brief, 95% CI −42 to 45; low-quality evidence) [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- Both men and women reduced drinking after brief intervention in the Cochrane subgroup analysis [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- An earlier primary-care meta-analysis (19 trials, 5,639 people) found a pooled reduction of 38 g ethanol per week (about 4 drinks; 95% CI −51 to −24 g/week) at 6 and 12 months [[Bertholet2005BI]].
- In a level 1 trauma-centre RCT, brief intervention cut drinking by 21.8 drinks/week at 12 months vs 6.7 in controls (p = 0.03), with a 47% reduction in injuries needing emergency or trauma admission (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.26–1.07, p = 0.07) [[Gentilello1999Trauma]].
- In that trauma centre, 46% of 2,524 screened patients screened positive [[Gentilello1999Trauma]].
- A meta-analysis of 9 RCTs found no evidence that brief alcohol interventions increase the use of specialty alcohol services (pooled RR 1.08, 95% CI 0.92–1.28) [[Glass2015Referral]].
- Brief intervention is aimed at hazardous and harmful drinkers who are not seeking help; it usually involves feedback, information on harms and benefits of cutting down, advice, and a personal plan [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].

### screening · SBIRT
- SBIRT (Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment) is an integrated public-health approach built on universal screening for people with, or at risk of, substance use disorders [[Babor2007SBIRT]].
- More than a hundred clinical trials have tested alcohol screening and brief intervention in primary care, emergency departments and trauma centres; short-term individual benefit is well supported, while long-term population effects have not been demonstrated (Established for short-term effects; Hypothesis for population-level effects) [[Babor2007SBIRT]].
- The "referral to treatment" step is the weakest link in the evidence, because brief interventions have not been shown to increase uptake of specialty care [[Glass2015Referral]].

### screening · AUDIT (10 items)
- The AUDIT was developed in a six-country WHO collaborative project as a screen for hazardous and harmful drinking; its items were chosen from a 150-item schedule given to 1,888 primary care attenders [[Saunders1993AUDIT]].
- It has 10 questions; each response is scored 0 to 4, giving a maximum score of 40 [[Saunders1993AUDIT]].
- In the development study, 92% of people with hazardous or harmful use scored 8 or more, and 94% of those with non-hazardous drinking scored less than 8 [[Saunders1993AUDIT]].
- The AUDIT was first released in 1989 [[Babor2016AUDITZones]].
- The items cover three domains: consumption (items 1–3: frequency, typical quantity, frequency of heavy drinking), dependence symptoms (items 4–6: impaired control, increased salience, morning drinking) and harms (items 7–10: guilt, blackouts, alcohol-related injuries, others concerned) [[HigginsBiddle2018USAUDIT,Babor2016AUDITZones]].
- WHO zones as published: 0–7 Zone I (abstinence or lower-risk drinking; feedback); 8–15 Zone II (hazardous use; brief intervention); 16–19 Zone III (harmful use; brief intervention, monitoring and diagnostic evaluation); 20–40 Zone IV (possible dependence; referral to specialist treatment) [[Babor2016AUDITZones]].
- Validation data for the zones are limited: a 2016 review found only three studies of them [[Babor2016AUDITZones]].
- In 625 primary care patients, the WHO cut-offs for zones III and IV had 100% specificity but very low sensitivity (men: 0.16 and 0.06; women: 0.12 and 0.03) [[Babor2016AUDITZones]].
- A score of 20 or more, or endorsing any dependence symptom, signals only that a diagnostic assessment is needed, not that AUD is present [[HigginsBiddle2018USAUDIT]].
- In family medicine outpatients, AUDIT ranges of 5–10, 11–14 and 15–40 in men were linked to post-screening probabilities of past-year DSM-IV alcohol dependence from 18% to 87%; in women, ranges 3–4, 5–8, 9–12 and 13–40 were linked to 6% to 94% [[Rubinsky2010AUDITZones]].
- The AUDIT assumes a 10 g standard drink, and the WHO manual recommends adapting items 2 and 3 to local drink sizes; the US standard drink (14 g) is 40% larger [[HigginsBiddle2018USAUDIT]].
- The USAUDIT adapts the consumption items to a 14 g drink and US guidelines; its consumption items can total 18 points and the full USAUDIT 46, versus 40 for the WHO AUDIT [[HigginsBiddle2018USAUDIT]].
- Across many countries, optimal AUDIT cut-offs vary, and lower cut-offs for women are often supported [[Babor2016AUDITZones]].
- The English AUDIT shows sensitivities and specificities comparable, and typically superior, to other self-report screens, with good test-retest reliability and internal consistency [[Reinert2002AUDIT]].
- The weight of evidence supports a two-factor structure for the AUDIT items [[Babor2016AUDITZones]].

### screening · AUDIT-C (3 items)
- The AUDIT-C is the first three AUDIT questions (frequency, typical quantity, frequency of heavy drinking), each scored 0 to 4, for a total of 0 to 12 [[Saunders1993AUDIT,Bush1998AUDITC,Bradley2007AUDITC]].
- In male VA patients (n = 243), AUDIT-C detected heavy drinking slightly better than the full AUDIT (AUROC 0.891 vs 0.881) and performed similarly for heavy drinking and/or active abuse or dependence (0.880 vs 0.881) [[Bush1998AUDITC]].
- In an academic family practice (392 men, 927 women), AUDIT-C AUROCs for alcohol misuse were 0.94 in men and 0.90 in women [[Bradley2007AUDITC]].
- The thresholds that best balanced sensitivity and specificity were ≥4 in men (sensitivity 0.86, specificity 0.89) and ≥3 in women (sensitivity 0.73, specificity 0.91) [[Bradley2007AUDITC]].
- In female VA patients (n = 393), the standard AUDIT-C had sensitivity 0.81 and specificity 0.86 for past-year hazardous drinking and/or active abuse or dependence [[Bradley2003Women]].
- In the same study, the standard AUDIT question 3 alone was specific (0.96) but insensitive (0.45) in women; a sex-specific binge question (≥4 drinks) had sensitivity 0.69 and specificity 0.94 [[Bradley2003Women]].
- In US national survey data, the derived AUDIT-C had AUROCs among past-year drinkers of 0.887 for alcohol dependence, 0.860 for any AUD and 0.966 for risk drinking, and sex-specific cut-points improved performance [[Dawson2005AUDITC]].
- A 2008 meta-analysis (14 studies) found no statistically significant difference in overall accuracy between AUDIT and AUDIT-C in primary care [[Kriston2008AUDITC]].
- The same meta-analysis pooled positive likelihood ratios of 6.62 (AUDIT) vs 2.99 (AUDIT-C) for risky drinking, and 4.03 vs 3.82 for any AUD [[Kriston2008AUDITC]].
- AUDIT-C ranges in men of 5–6, 7–9 and 10–12 were linked to post-screening probabilities of past-year alcohol dependence from 22% to 75%; in women, 3, 4–6, 7–9 and 10–12 were linked to 9% to 88% [[Rubinsky2010AUDITZones]].
- Higher AUDIT-C scores therefore mean higher probability of dependence, but even a positive screen leaves wide uncertainty and needs a diagnostic assessment [[Rubinsky2010AUDITZones]].

### screening · Single alcohol screening question (NIAAA)
- The NIAAA single question is: "How many times in the past year have you had X or more drinks in a day?" with X = 5 for men and 4 for women; an answer of 1 or more is positive [[Smith2009JGIM]].
- In 286 primary care patients, it was 81.8% sensitive (95% CI 72.5–88.5%) and 79.3% specific (95% CI 73.1–84.4%) for unhealthy alcohol use [[Smith2009JGIM]].
- For a current AUD it was 87.9% sensitive (95% CI 72.7–95.2%) but only 66.8% specific (95% CI 60.8–72.3%) [[Smith2009JGIM]].
- Its accuracy was similar to a commonly used three-item screen and was little affected by demographics [[Smith2009JGIM]].
- The FAST, a four-item tool derived from AUDIT items, was built for busy settings; its first item alone classifies more than half of patients [[Hodgson2002FAST]].

### screening · CAGE and its limits
- CAGE is four interview questions: Cut down, Annoyed by criticism, Guilty, Eye-opener [[Ewing1984CAGE]].
- At the usual cut-off of 2 or more, pooled sensitivity was 0.87 in inpatients, 0.71 in primary care and 0.60 in ambulatory patients; overall LR+ was 3.44 and LR− 0.18 (pooled AUC 0.87) [[Aertgeerts2004CAGE]].
- The meta-analysis concluded that CAGE is of limited value for screening at the recommended cut-point of 2 or more [[Aertgeerts2004CAGE]].
- CAGE has high test-retest reliability (0.80–0.95) and an average sensitivity of 0.71 and specificity of 0.90 for abuse or dependence in medical, surgical and psychiatric settings [[Dhalla2007CAGE]].
- CAGE has performed poorly in white women, prenatal women and college students, and is not suitable for detecting less severe drinking [[Dhalla2007CAGE]].
- CAGE asks about problems, not amounts, so it is not suited to finding less severe risky drinking; AUDIT-C outperformed an augmented CAGE in primary care (p < 0.001) [[Bradley2007AUDITC,Dhalla2007CAGE]].
- In adolescents, CAGE sensitivity was only 0.37 (95% CI 0.29–0.44), and CAGE is not recommended for this age group [[Knight2003ACER]].

### screening · Adolescents (CRAFFT, S2BI)
- CRAFFT stands for Car, Relax, Alone, Forget, Friends, Trouble [[Shenoi2019CRAFFT]].
- In 538 clinic patients aged 14 to 18, a CRAFFT score of 2 or more was optimal: sensitivity 0.76 and specificity 0.94 for any problem; 0.80 and 0.86 for any disorder; 0.92 and 0.80 for dependence [[Knight2002CRAFFT]].
- About one in four of those adolescents scored 2 or more, and validity did not differ by age, sex or race [[Knight2002CRAFFT]].
- In a comparison of adolescent screens for alcohol problems, sensitivities were AUDIT 0.88, POSIT 0.84, CRAFFT 0.92 and CAGE 0.37 [[Knight2003ACER]].
- In 4,753 pediatric emergency patients aged 12–17, a baseline CRAFFT of 2 or more was associated with substance use disorder at baseline and at 1, 2 and 3 years, but with limited sensitivity [[Shenoi2019CRAFFT]].
- Frequency-based adolescent screens (S2BI, BSTAD, TAPS) had AUCs of 0.89 to 1 for nicotine, alcohol and cannabis use disorders in 798 adolescents aged 12–17 [[Levy2023Adolescent]].
- In 517 adolescents aged 14–18, S2BI had 53.3% sensitivity and 94.2% specificity for any AUD, but 100% sensitivity and 93.6% specificity for moderate/severe AUD [[Levy2021S2BI]].

### screening · Pregnancy
- T-ACE (Tolerance, Annoyed, Cut down, Eye-opener) scores 2 points for tolerance (more than 2 drinks to feel "high") and 1 each for A, C and E; a total of 2 or more is positive [[Sokol1989TACE]].
- In 971 pregnant women, T-ACE identified 69% of risk drinkers (positive predictive value 23%) [[Sokol1989TACE]].
- A systematic review of pregnancy screens found sensitivity for risk drinking of 69–88% for T-ACE, 71–91% for TWEAK and 95% for AUDIT-C, while CAGE performed poorly [[Burns2010Pregnancy]].
- In pregnancy, AUDIT-C at 3 or more had 96% sensitivity and 71% specificity for alcohol use disorder [[Burns2010Pregnancy]].
- Screening and counselling in pregnancy are covered by the USPSTF B recommendation, and counselling increased reported abstinence [[USPSTF2018Alcohol,OConnor2018JAMA]].

### criteria · DSM-5-TR alcohol use disorder
- DSM-5-TR defines AUD as a problematic pattern of alcohol use causing clinically significant impairment or distress, shown by at least 2 of 11 criteria within a 12-month period [[APA2022,Hasin2013DSM5]].
- The 11 criteria, paraphrased: (1) drinking more or longer than intended; (2) persistent wish or failed efforts to cut down; (3) much time spent getting, using or recovering; (4) craving; (5) failure to meet major role obligations; (6) continued use despite social or interpersonal problems; (7) giving up important activities; (8) use in physically hazardous situations; (9) continued use despite physical or psychological problems; (10) tolerance; (11) withdrawal [[APA2022]].
- Severity is set by the number of criteria met: mild 2–3, moderate 4–5, severe 6 or more [[Hasin2013DSM5,APA2022]].
- Early remission: at least 3 but less than 12 months without meeting any AUD criterion except craving; sustained remission: 12 months or more without meeting any criterion except craving [[Hasin2013DSM5,APA2022]].
- A specifier "in a controlled environment" applies when the person is in a setting where access to alcohol is restricted [[APA2022]].
- The work group chose the threshold of 2 or more criteria because it gave the best agreement with the combined prevalence of DSM-IV abuse and dependence [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- The criteria form a dimensional condition with no natural cut-point; the count (2 to 11) is used as the severity indicator [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- Biomarkers were considered but not included in the DSM-5 criteria, because they were not yet suitable as diagnostic tests [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- The DSM-5 physiological specifier was eliminated [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- Tolerance and withdrawal that occur only during appropriate supervised medical use are not counted toward a substance use disorder [[Hasin2013DSM5]].

### criteria · From DSM-IV abuse and dependence to DSM-5 AUD
- DSM-5 merged DSM-IV "alcohol abuse" and "alcohol dependence" into a single disorder, based on consistent findings from more than 200,000 study participants [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- Factor analyses showed the abuse and dependence criteria formed one factor or two highly correlated factors, supporting one disorder [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- DSM-IV's hierarchy created "diagnostic orphans": people with two dependence criteria and no abuse criteria received no diagnosis [[Hasin2013DSM5,Peer2013DSM5]].
- Legal problems were dropped as a criterion because of very low prevalence, low discrimination and poor fit with the other criteria [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- Craving was added; its psychometric benefit was equivocal, but the view that craving may become a biological treatment target prevailed [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- In 7,543 people from genetic studies of substance dependence, switching to DSM-5 modestly increased prevalence, mostly by capturing diagnostic orphans; adding craving did not substantially change diagnosis rates [[Peer2013DSM5]].
- In a US national sample of past-year drinkers (n = 23,013), DSM-IV alcohol dependence agreed best with DSM-5 AUD at 4 or more criteria (the moderate threshold), with sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV generally above 85% [[Compton2013Crosswalk]].
- Treatments tested in DSM-IV dependence therefore apply best to DSM-5 AUD of at least moderate severity [[Compton2013Crosswalk]].
- In an Australian population survey, the proportion meeting DSM-5 moderate-to-severe AUD was far higher than the proportion meeting dependence in ICD-10, ICD-11 or DSM-IV [[Lago2016ICD11]].

### criteria · How common each severity level is
- In NESARC-III, 12-month and lifetime DSM-5 AUD prevalences were 13.9% and 29.1% (population estimates) [[Grant2015NESARC]].
- Twelve-month prevalence was 7.3% mild, 3.2% moderate and 3.4% severe; lifetime prevalence was 8.6% mild, 6.6% moderate and 13.9% severe [[Grant2015NESARC]].
- Mean age at AUD onset was 26.2 years, falling with severity: 30.1 (mild), 25.9 (moderate), 23.9 (severe) [[Grant2015NESARC]].
- Treatment seeking rose with severity among people with 12-month AUD: 2.7% mild, 4.9% moderate, 21.3% severe [[Grant2015NESARC]].

### criteria · ICD-11: alcohol dependence and harmful pattern of use
- ICD-11 was formally published in May 2019; its alcohol diagnoses include alcohol dependence, harmful pattern of use of alcohol, intoxication, withdrawal and alcohol-induced mental disorders, with hazardous alcohol use listed separately as a health risk factor [[Saunders2019ICD11]].
- ICD-11 condenses the six ICD-10 dependence criteria into three essential features, and at least two are needed for dependence [[Degenhardt2019ICD11]].
- The three features are impaired control over use (onset, level, circumstances or stopping, sometimes with craving), use becoming an increasing priority in life, and physiological features (tolerance, withdrawal, or use to prevent or relieve withdrawal) [[Degenhardt2019ICD11]].
- Harmful pattern of use means a pattern causing damage to physical or mental health that has not reached dependence; it is typically evident over at least 12 months [[Degenhardt2019ICD11]].
- ICD-11 harmful use added "harm to others" (health harm to others caused by substance-induced behaviour); endorsement was very low and its validity is questionable [[Degenhardt2019ICD11]].
- In WHO World Mental Health Surveys in 10 countries (12,182 regular drinkers), lifetime ICD-11 harmful use was 21.6% (95% CI 20.5–22.6%) and dependence 7.0% (95% CI 6.4–7.7%) [[Degenhardt2019ICD11]].
- ICD-11 agreed excellently with ICD-10 and DSM-IV (all κ ≥ 0.9); agreement with DSM-5 was good for dependence vs severe AUD but poor for harmful use vs mild AUD [[Degenhardt2019ICD11]].
- ICD-11 classification was in almost perfect agreement with ICD-10 and DSM-IV in an Australian survey, whereas DSM-5 selected a different group of people [[Lago2016ICD11]].

### biomarkers · What lab tests can and cannot do
- Questionnaires and lab tests answer different questions: tests can confirm recent or heavy drinking, but no test diagnoses AUD, which is a clinical diagnosis based on criteria [[Hasin2013DSM5,Niemela2016Biomarkers]].
- Indirect markers (GGT, MCV, CDT) are affected by age, sex, other substances and non-alcohol illness and do not cover the whole drinking timeline; direct ethanol metabolites (EtG, EtS, PEth, FAEE) are formed only when ethanol is present [[Wurst2015Metabolites]].
- Traditional markers (GGT, AST, ALT, MCV) have imperfect sensitivity and specificity, but help detect complications and comorbid conditions and can help motivate change and monitor treatment [[Conigrave2003Markers]].
- Traditional markers are more likely to be raised in people over 30 and in regular drinkers with a longer drinking history; obesity and hepatitis C must be considered when reading liver enzymes [[Conigrave2003Markers]].
- A single PEth value gives only an approximate estimate of how much and how recently someone drank [[Helander2019PEthDose,Helander2019PEthElimination]].
- Combining a self-report with a highly specific biomarker (either positive) raises sensitivity without losing specificity when over-reporting is unlikely [[Hahn2016PEth]].

### biomarkers · Ethanol in breath and blood
- Ethanol in blood, breath or urine shows only current or very recent drinking, because it is eliminated at about 0.15 g/L/h (range 0.1–0.3 g/L/h) [[Jones2024BiomarkerHistory]].
- Elimination is close to zero-order above about 20 mg/100 mL: 10–15 mg/100 mL/h after drinking on an empty stomach and 15–20 mg/100 mL/h in non-fasted people [[Jones2010Elimination]].
- In people with AUD during detoxification, elimination may be 25–35 mg/100 mL/h because CYP2E1 activity is raised; the physiological range is 10–35 mg/100 mL/h [[Jones2010Elimination]].
- A blood ethanol above 1.5‰ (33 mmol/L) without obvious signs of intoxication indicates tolerance, a typical sign in alcohol dependence [[Niemela2016Biomarkers]].
- Widmark-type BAC estimates carry real uncertainty: one analysis proposed a ±21% coefficient of variation, and another author argued that the error must be calculated for each case [[Searle2015Uncertainty,Posey2007Widmark]].
- Several published BAC-estimation models give different answers for the same scenario [[Posey2007Widmark]].
- When Fell and Voas wrote in 2014, the US per se driving limit was 0.08; their review found virtually all drivers are impaired on at least some driving measures at 0.05 [[Fell2014BAC]].
- The relative risk of dying in a single-vehicle crash at BAC 0.05–0.079 is 7 to 21 times that at 0.00 [[Fell2014BAC]].

### biomarkers · Urine EtG and EtS
- EtG and EtS are minor conjugated metabolites of ethanol that stay in urine much longer than ethanol itself [[Helander2009EtG]].
- In 32 patients in alcohol detoxification, urinary EtG stayed above 0.5 mg/L for about 40 to 130 hours (median 78 h), with a similar time course for EtS [[Helander2009EtG]].
- Measured from the estimated time ethanol reached zero, EtG detection lasted about 30 to 110 hours (median 66 h) [[Helander2009EtG]].
- In a dose-ranging challenge study, EtG was always positive 12 hours after drinking at 100 and 200 ng/mL cut-offs; at 24 hours sensitivity was poor after a low dose, and at 48 hours it was poor regardless of dose or cut-off [[Jatlow2014EtG]].
- In clinical trial participants, EtG sensitivity for drinking 24–48 hours earlier was under 40%, and raising the cut-off to 500 ng/mL substantially reduced sensitivity [[Jatlow2014EtG]].
- Measuring both EtG and EtS is usually unnecessary [[Jatlow2014EtG]].
- A positive urine EtG at 100 ng/mL (research or clinical use) or a more conservative 500 ng/mL (forensic use) indicates recent drinking over roughly the prior 1 to 3 days [[Hahn2016PEth]].
- Incidental exposure can produce positive EtG: 1 g and 3 g of ethanol gave peak creatinine-normalised EtG of 0.32 and 1.53 mg/L, values many workplace laboratories would call positive [[Thierauf2009TraceEtG]].
- Drinking 2.5 L of "non-alcoholic" beer (up to 0.5% ethanol) produced urine EtG of 0.30–0.87 mg/L, above the 0.1 mg/L abstinence cut-off often applied [[Thierauf2010BeerEtG]].
- Non-beverage ethanol exposure is the main barrier to wider clinical use of EtG, so results need careful interpretation [[Jatlow2010EtG]].
- In patients with liver disease, urinary EtG had 70–89% sensitivity and 93–99% specificity for any alcohol use in the past days [[Arnts2021LiverBiomarkers]].

### biomarkers · PEth (phosphatidylethanol)
- PEth is a phospholipid formed from phosphatidylcholine by phospholipase D in red blood cell membranes, only in the presence of ethanol [[Hahn2016PEth]].
- PEth 16:0/18:1 is the main homologue, about 40% of total PEth; LC-MS/MS detection limits are 2–5 ng/mL and quantitation limits 8–10 ng/mL [[Hahn2016PEth]].
- Average PEth half-life is about 4 days, with reported half-lives up to 12 days [[Hahn2016PEth]].
- After single low doses (0.25 or 0.50 g/kg), mean half-life of combined PEth was 4.6 ± 3.5 days (range 1.0–13.1) [[Javors2016PEth]].
- In 47 heavy drinkers during detoxification, all tested PEth-positive on admission (100% sensitivity for recent heavy drinking), and half-lives were 3.7–10.4 days for PEth 16:0/18:1 [[Helander2019PEthElimination]].
- In 100 patients with AUD in withdrawal treatment, mean half-life was 7.24 days (95% CI 6.98–7.53) for 16:0/18:1 and 4.55 days for 16:0/18:2, and PEth was still detectable in the fourth week of abstinence [[deBejczy2026PEth]].
- PEth correlates best with drinking in the past 2 weeks [[Helander2019PEthDose,deBejczy2026PEth]].
- A change of about 20 g/day in ethanol intake shifted PEth by about 0.10 µmol/L on average, with large differences between people [[Helander2019PEthDose]].
- Proposed interpretive bands: under 20 ng/mL, light or no consumption; 20–199 ng/mL, significant consumption; over 200 ng/mL, heavy consumption [[Ulwelling2018PEth]].
- Swedish laboratories use PEth above 0.3 µmol/L (about 210 ng/mL) as the cut-off for "excessive" use [[Hahn2016PEth]].
- Someone starting abstinence with very high PEth (for example over 2,000 ng/mL), or with a long half-life, may still test positive after 30 days, so PEth alone cannot date the last drink [[Hahn2016PEth]].
- In a 3-month randomised study, PEth was detected in everyone assigned to daily moderate wine drinking (16 g/day for women, 32 g/day for men) and was the only marker that clearly separated moderate drinking from abstinence [[Kechagias2015PEth]].
- After 1 week of one or two glasses of wine daily (16 or 32 g), all 21 participants had measurable PEth, while CDT, AST, ALT and GGT changed little [[Walther2026PEth]].
- In 300 volunteers, no PEth was detected in those reporting abstinence, and 95% of "moderate drinkers" by AUDIT-C had PEth 16:0/18:1 from not detected to 112 ng/mL [[Schrock2017PEth]].
- In 222 people with liver disease, PEth at 20 ng/mL was 73% sensitive and 96% specific for any drinking in the past month; at 80 ng/mL it was 91% sensitive and 77% specific for an average of 4 or more drinks a day [[Stewart2014PEth]].
- In a mixed cohort including critically ill patients, PEth AUROC was 0.927 for AUDIT-defined misuse, with optimal cut-points of 250 ng/mL (any misuse) and 400 ng/mL (severe misuse) [[Afshar2017PEth]].
- A meta-analysis found mean total PEth of 0.288 µM in social drinkers (≤60 g/day) vs 3.897 µM in heavy drinkers (>60 g/day) [[Viel2012PEth]].
- In liver disease, whole-blood PEth showed 73–100% sensitivity and 90–96% specificity for any alcohol use in the previous weeks, independent of liver disease stage [[Arnts2021LiverBiomarkers]].
- Self-report and PEth can disagree in both directions, and positive PEth after reported abstinence should be read cautiously [[Hahn2016PEth]].

### biomarkers · CDT, GGT, MCV and AST:ALT
- CDT (carbohydrate-deficient transferrin) is a specific marker of chronic heavy drinking but lacks sensitivity for population screening, because it needs at least 50–80 g ethanol a day for several weeks to rise [[Niemela2016Biomarkers]].
- Serum CDT has a half-life of about 15 days and detects heavy use over the prior few weeks [[Arnts2021LiverBiomarkers]].
- In liver disease, CDT showed 40–79% sensitivity and 57–99% specificity for excessive use in the past weeks [[Arnts2021LiverBiomarkers]].
- In studies before 1998, commercial CDT assays were not significantly better than GGT in head-to-head comparisons [[Scouller2000CDT]].
- The IFCC standardised the CDT measurand and methods; in large general-population groups, CDT's diagnostic performance is superior to traditional markers [[Wielders2024CDT]].
- GGT is sensitive to drinking but not specific (it also rises with liver disease from other causes and with obesity); raised values in heavy drinkers usually return to normal within 2–3 weeks of abstinence [[Niemela2016Biomarkers]].
- MCV rises with ethanol intake in a dose-dependent way and may take 2–4 months to normalise after stopping [[Niemela2016Biomarkers]].
- In 165 heavy drinkers vs reference people, sensitivities were GGT-CDT 90%, CDT 63%, GGT 58%, ALT 50%, AST 47% and MCV 45% [[Hietala2006GGTCDT]].
- Combining CDT and GGT gave the best accuracy for detecting 60 g/day or more in men; in women, GGT alone was best [[Chen2003CDTGGT]].
- In 1979, an AST:ALT ratio above 2 was found in 70% of patients with alcohol-related hepatitis and cirrhosis vs 26% with postnecrotic cirrhosis, 8% with chronic hepatitis and 4% with viral hepatitis [[Cohen1979SGOTSGPT]].
- In people with AUD admitted for withdrawal, AST:ALT was 1.0 or less in 64% and only exceptionally 2 or more; in alcohol-related cirrhosis, 69% had a ratio of 2 or more [[Nyblom2004ASTALT]].
- A high AST:ALT ratio therefore suggests advanced alcohol-related liver disease, not heavy drinking itself [[Nyblom2004ASTALT]].

### biomarkers · Hair EtG
- EtG is stable in hair and can show drinking over months; hair grows about 1 cm per month, so a 3 cm proximal segment reflects roughly 3 months [[Crunelle2014HairEtG,Arnts2021LiverBiomarkers]].
- 2026 Society of Hair Testing consensus: in proximal scalp hair, EtG ≤5 pg/mg does not contradict self-reported abstinence, and EtG ≥30 pg/mg strongly suggests chronic excessive drinking [[Favretto2026SoHT]].
- The previous (2016) SoHT cut-offs for EtG were 7 pg/mg and 30 pg/mg [[Pragst2017SoHT]].
- Ethyl palmitate can complement hair EtG, with chronic-excessive cut-offs of ≥350 pg/mg (0–3 cm) and ≥450 pg/mg (0–6 cm) [[Favretto2026SoHT]].
- Hair results must be read in light of hair segment, growth, cosmetic or heat treatment and method [[Favretto2026SoHT,Crunelle2014HairEtG]].
- In liver disease, scalp hair EtG had 85–100% sensitivity and 97–100% specificity for chronic excessive use in the past 3 to 6 months [[Arnts2021LiverBiomarkers]].

### Exam hooks
- AUDIT: 10 items, 0–40; 8 or more flags hazardous or harmful use; WHO zones 0–7, 8–15, 16–19, 20–40 [[Saunders1993AUDIT,Babor2016AUDITZones]].
- AUDIT-C positive: ≥4 in men, ≥3 in women (primary care sensitivity/specificity 0.86/0.89 and 0.73/0.91) [[Bradley2007AUDITC]].
- NIAAA single question: "How many times in the past year have you had 5 (men) / 4 (women) or more drinks in a day?"; 1 or more is positive (sensitivity 81.8%, specificity 79.3%) [[Smith2009JGIM]].
- USPSTF 2018: screen adults 18+ including pregnant people, then brief counselling (grade B); adolescents 12–17 = I statement [[USPSTF2018Alcohol]].
- Brief intervention: about −1.6 drinks/week (USPSTF review) or −20 g/week at 1 year (Cochrane) [[OConnor2018JAMA,Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- Brief intervention does not reliably increase uptake of specialty treatment [[Glass2015Referral]].
- CAGE: asks about problems, not amounts; limited value for screening, especially in women, pregnancy and adolescents [[Aertgeerts2004CAGE,Dhalla2007CAGE,Knight2003ACER]].
- CRAFFT ≥2 is the usual adolescent cut-off [[Knight2002CRAFFT]].
- DSM-5 AUD: 2 or more of 11 criteria in 12 months; mild 2–3, moderate 4–5, severe 6+ [[Hasin2013DSM5,APA2022]].
- DSM-5 added craving and removed legal problems; abuse and dependence were merged [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- Early remission 3 to <12 months, sustained 12+ months; craving may persist in both [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- DSM-IV dependence maps best to DSM-5 AUD of 4 or more criteria (moderate) [[Compton2013Crosswalk]].
- ICD-11 alcohol dependence: 2 of 3 features (impaired control, increasing priority, physiological features) [[Degenhardt2019ICD11]].
- Urine EtG: detection median about 3 days (78 h) after heavy drinking [[Helander2009EtG]].
- PEth: half-life about 4–7 days; detectable for weeks after heavy drinking [[Hahn2016PEth,deBejczy2026PEth]].
- GGT normalises in 2–3 weeks; MCV in 2–4 months [[Niemela2016Biomarkers]].
- AST:ALT >2 suggests advanced alcohol-related liver disease, not just heavy drinking [[Cohen1979SGOTSGPT,Nyblom2004ASTALT]].
- Hair EtG ≥30 pg/mg strongly suggests chronic excessive drinking [[Favretto2026SoHT]].

### In plain words
- Doctors and nurses ask everyone about alcohol because problems are common and easy to miss [[USPSTF2018Alcohol,Carvalho2019Lancet]].
- A short talk with a health worker helps many people drink less on average [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- A screening score only says "let's talk more." It is not a diagnosis [[HigginsBiddle2018USAUDIT]].
- The quizzes on this site are for learning. They are not a test of you or someone you love. If you are worried about drinking, talk with a health professional.
- Alcohol use disorder is a medical condition. Doctors diagnose it by asking about 11 kinds of problems over the past year [[APA2022]].
- Having 2 or 3 of these means mild, 4 or 5 means moderate, and 6 or more means severe [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- "Remission" means going at least 3 months without the problems on the list. Cravings can still happen [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- Blood and urine tests can show recent drinking, but no blood test can diagnose alcohol use disorder by itself [[Hasin2013DSM5]].
- A urine test (EtG) can show drinking from the last few days. A blood test (PEth) can show drinking from the last few weeks [[Helander2009EtG,Hahn2016PEth]].
- Some urine tests can turn positive after tiny amounts of alcohol or "non-alcoholic" beer, so results need a careful talk, not a quick judgment [[Thierauf2009TraceEtG,Thierauf2010BeerEtG]].
- The body removes alcohol at a fairly steady pace, so it can take many hours to clear [[Jones2010Elimination]].
- No calculator can tell you if it is safe to drive. Even at 0.05, almost all drivers are impaired in some way [[Fell2014BAC]].
- If you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, your care team will ask about alcohol. This is routine, and they are there to help you plan [[USPSTF2018Alcohol]].
- Stopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous. Get medical help to stop safely.

## Open questions and controversies
- AUDIT vs AUDIT-C: a meta-analysis found no significant difference in primary care accuracy, but pooled likelihood ratios and some population studies suggest the full AUDIT may be better for severe problems [[Kriston2008AUDITC]]; AUDIT-C matched or beat the full AUDIT for heavy drinking in VA men [[Bush1998AUDITC]]; status: both acceptable, choice depends on setting.
- AUDIT zones: WHO zones are widely taught [[Babor2016AUDITZones]], but they rest on little validation, and zones III and IV have very low sensitivity for dependence [[Babor2016AUDITZones,Rubinsky2010AUDITZones]]; status: unresolved.
- Drink size: the WHO AUDIT assumes a 10 g drink; the US drink is 14 g, and the USAUDIT was proposed to fix the mismatch [[HigginsBiddle2018USAUDIT]]; status: USAUDIT not yet widely validated.
- DSM-5 threshold of 2 criteria: chosen for best agreement with DSM-IV abuse plus dependence [[Hasin2013DSM5]]; critics feared it was too low, and DSM-5 moderate-to-severe AUD captured a different and larger group than dependence in other systems [[Lago2016ICD11]]; status: retained in DSM-5-TR [[APA2022]].
- DSM-5 vs ICD-11: high agreement for severe AUD vs dependence, but poor agreement for mild AUD vs harmful use [[Degenhardt2019ICD11]]; status: the two systems will label some people differently.
- Craving as a criterion: added largely for its potential as a treatment target, although its psychometric benefit was equivocal [[Hasin2013DSM5]] (Hypothesis that it will become a biological treatment target).
- Brief intervention in adolescents: evidence insufficient (USPSTF I statement) and trial results mixed [[USPSTF2018Alcohol,OConnor2018JAMA]]; status: open.
- Referral to treatment: SBIRT assumes linkage [[Babor2007SBIRT]], but brief interventions do not increase specialty care use [[Glass2015Referral]]; status: linkage strategies need better evidence.
- PEth cut-offs: proposed bands (<20, 20–199, >200 ng/mL) [[Ulwelling2018PEth]], Swedish 210 ng/mL [[Hahn2016PEth]], and critical-care optima of 250 and 400 ng/mL [[Afshar2017PEth]] differ; single values misclassify moderate vs heavy drinking [[Helander2019PEthDose]]; status: context-dependent (Early evidence for any single universal cut-off).
- Low reporting threshold for PEth: the Swedish 35 ng/mL reporting threshold had high specificity but low sensitivity for regular daily drinking of 16–32 g [[Walther2026PEth]]; status: debated.
- Urine EtG cut-offs: 100–200 ng/mL catches drinking the night before, but incidental exposures can exceed low cut-offs, while 500 ng/mL loses sensitivity [[Jatlow2014EtG,Thierauf2009TraceEtG,Thierauf2010BeerEtG]]; status: choose by setting, confirm with history.
- Hair EtG abstinence cut-off: lowered from 7 pg/mg (2016) to 5 pg/mg (2026) [[Pragst2017SoHT,Favretto2026SoHT]]; status: newly revised.
- BAC calculators: models disagree and carry large uncertainty [[Posey2007Widmark,Searle2015Uncertainty]]; status: teaching only, never a guide to whether it is safe to drive.

## Provenance
- All 73 journal sources were checked with PubMed E-utilities esummary (authors, title, journal, year, volume, pages, PMID, PMCID, DOI copied from the record). Babor2016AUDITZones and APA2022 were checked with Crossref. No source is marked unverified.
- Numbers were taken from PubMed abstracts, except where full text was read in PMC: Hasin2013DSM5 (threshold, severity, remission, legal problems, craving, biomarkers decisions), Hahn2016PEth (homologues, half-life, LOD/LOQ, Swedish cut-off, EtG cut-offs), HigginsBiddle2018USAUDIT (item domains, drink size, USAUDIT scoring), Niemela2016Biomarkers (Table 1 windows, CDT threshold, GGT and MCV normalisation, tolerance BAC), Grant2015NESARC (severity prevalences, onset ages, treatment seeking), Degenhardt2019ICD11 (ICD-11 features and harmful-use definition) and Arnts2021LiverBiomarkers (CDT half-life, hair growth). Babor2016AUDITZones was read from the publisher's open-access PDF.
- Dropped: the WHO AUDIT manual (Babor 2001; no PubMed or Crossref record), the 2022 Basel PEth consensus (no abstract, full text not accessible), the DSM-5 field-trial AUD kappa (not in abstract), Saunders 2005 NEJM review (no abstract), and the ICD-11 rule on continuous use over shorter periods (not found in a validated source read).
- DSM-5-TR criteria are paraphrased, not quoted, and cited to the book record (APA2022) and the work group paper.
- The US 0.08 per se limit is reported as of the 2014 source; state laws have since changed in places, and the BAC calculator must never be used to judge fitness to drive.
- Safety framing: screens, criteria counters and biomarker tables here are teaching tools, not self-tests. Stopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous: get medical help to stop safely.
