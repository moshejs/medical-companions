# F · Psychosocial treatment, mutual help, course & recovery

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: therapy, recovery, families.

This pass covers talking and behavioural treatments for alcohol use disorder (AUD), mutual-help groups, harm-reduction and drinking-reduction goals, the natural course of AUD and recovery, families and children, and stigma and language. Every number below was read from the PubMed abstract (or the PubMed Bookshelf record) of the cited work. All outcome and recovery figures are group (population) statistics from the studies named. They are not predictions for any one person.

Access notes:
- Full texts were not fetched (Europe PMC full-text service returned errors). All figures come from abstracts, so some well-known numbers that appear only in full texts were dropped (see Provenance).
- Medicines are covered by pass E and withdrawal by pass D. Medicines appear here only where a trial combined them with a talking therapy (COMBINE, Foa 2013, Ray 2020).
- Safety line for builders: next to any content about stopping drinking, say that stopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous (seizures, delirium tremens), so get medical help to stop safely.

## Findings

### therapy · Brief interventions (screening and brief advice)
- The USPSTF recommends screening for unhealthy alcohol use in primary care for adults 18 or older, including pregnant women, and offering brief behavioural counselling to people engaged in risky or hazardous drinking (B recommendation). For adolescents aged 12 to 17 the evidence is insufficient (I statement) [[USPSTF2018Alcohol]].
- In the 2018 Cochrane review of brief interventions in primary care (69 RCTs, 33,642 participants), people who got a brief intervention drank 20 g/week less than people who got minimal or no intervention at one year (mean difference −20 g/week, 95% CI −28 to −12; 34 studies, 15,197 participants; moderate-quality evidence; I² = 73%) [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- Mean baseline consumption in those trials was 244 g/week (30.5 UK units), so a 20 g/week reduction is about 8% of baseline intake (derived from [[Kaner2018Cochrane]]).
- In that review, brief interventions had little effect on binges per week (MD −0.08), drinking days per week (MD −0.13) or drinking intensity (−0.2 g per drinking day) [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- Longer ("extended") counselling added little: extended versus brief intervention gave MD 2 g/week (95% CI −42 to 45; low-quality evidence) [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- Most of the trials were in general practice (55%) or emergency care (39%), and both men and women cut down after a brief intervention [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- An earlier meta-analysis found small-to-medium effects for brief interventions versus control in people not seeking treatment. In treatment-seeking samples, brief and extended treatment differed little [[Moyer2002]].
- For people admitted to general hospital wards (14 studies, 4,041 mostly male participants), brief interventions reduced drinking at 6 and 9 months but not at one year. Deaths were fewer at 6 months (RR 0.42, 95% CI 0.19–0.94) and at one year (RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.40–0.91) [[McQueen2011]].
- Brief interventions are not proven for people with alcohol dependence or very heavy drinking. Of 16 primary-care RCTs (6,839 patients), 14 excluded some or all such people, and the two that included them showed no efficacy [[Saitz2010]].
- Brief interventions did not increase the use of specialist alcohol services (pooled RR 1.08, 95% CI 0.92–1.28; 9 RCTs) [[Glass2015]].
- A classic 1977 trial in people with alcohol dependence compared one counselling ("advice") session with several months of inpatient and outpatient treatment and found no significant difference in outcome at one year [[Edwards1977]].

### therapy · Motivational interviewing and motivational enhancement therapy (MI/MET)
- The 2023 Cochrane review of MI (93 studies, 22,776 participants) found a small-to-moderate effect versus no intervention right after the intervention (SMD 0.48, 95% CI 0.07–0.89; low certainty). The effect shrank at short-term follow-up (SMD 0.20) and was absent at long-term follow-up (SMD 0.12, 95% CI −0.00 to 0.25) [[Schwenker2023]].
- In the same review, MI did no better than another active intervention at any follow-up (low certainty). Against assessment and feedback, MI gave small benefits at medium and long term (SMD 0.24 each; moderate certainty) [[Schwenker2023]].
- The earlier 2011 Cochrane review (59 studies, 13,342 participants) also found an effect versus no treatment that was strongest after the intervention (SMD 0.79), weaker later, and not significant at long follow-up [[Smedslund2011]].
- In a meta-analysis of brief MI for excessive drinking, the effect size versus no treatment was 0.18 (95% CI 0.07–0.29). It was 0.60 when follow-up was 3 months or less, and larger when dependent drinkers were excluded [[Vasilaki2006]].
- MET was the comparison arm in UKATT and one of the three arms in Project MATCH. Both trials found it about as effective as the other treatments tested [[ProjectMATCH1997,UKATT2005]].

### therapy · Project MATCH and UKATT: different therapies, similar results
- Project MATCH randomised people with alcohol dependence to three 12-week, manual-guided, one-to-one treatments: cognitive-behavioural coping skills therapy (CBT), MET or twelve-step facilitation (TSF). There were two parallel trials: outpatient (N = 952; 72% male) and aftercare after inpatient or day-hospital care (N = 774; 80% male) [[ProjectMATCH1997]].
- All three MATCH treatments produced significant, sustained improvements in drinking from baseline to one year after treatment, with "little difference in outcomes by type of treatment" [[ProjectMATCH1997]].
- The matching hypotheses were largely unsupported. Only psychiatric severity interacted with treatment at one year: outpatients with low psychiatric severity had more abstinent days after TSF than after CBT [[ProjectMATCH1997]].
- Secondary MATCH analyses found that outpatients high in anger did better with MET than with CBT, and that aftercare clients high in dependence did better with TSF. The authors concluded that matching effects were "not robust" [[ProjectMATCH1997Secondary]].
- At 3 years (outpatient arm), almost 30% of participants were totally abstinent in months 37–39. Those who did drink were still abstinent on about two-thirds of days. TSF showed "a possible slight advantage" [[ProjectMATCH1998]].
- At 3 years, people high in anger had 76.4% abstinent days with MET versus 66% with CBT or TSF. People whose social networks supported drinking were abstinent on 16.1% more days with TSF than with MET [[ProjectMATCH1998]].
- At 3 years, readiness to change and self-efficacy were the strongest predictors of long-term drinking outcome [[ProjectMATCH1998]].
- UKATT (742 clients at seven UK sites) compared social behaviour and network therapy with MET. Both groups improved substantially over 12 months, and the therapies did not differ significantly in effectiveness [[UKATT2005]].
- Both UKATT therapies saved about five times their cost in health, social and criminal-justice spending. Average costs were £221 for network therapy and £129 for MET, and cost-effectiveness did not differ significantly [[UKATT2005Cost]].

### therapy · CBT and relapse prevention (the Marlatt model)
- Marlatt and Gordon's relapse-prevention model names immediate triggers (high-risk situations, coping skills, outcome expectancies, the "abstinence violation effect") and hidden antecedents (lifestyle imbalance, urges and cravings). Treatment teaches coping for personal high-risk situations, builds self-efficacy, plans for lapses and reframes how a person thinks about lapses [[Larimer1999]].
- A later version of the model treats relapse as a dynamic process: many risk factors and situations interact, rather than relapse following a fixed chain [[Witkiewitz2004RP]].
- A meta-analysis of relapse prevention (26 studies, 9,504 participants) found it generally effective, especially for alcohol problems and when combined with medication [[Irvin1999]].
- In a meta-analysis of CBT for alcohol or other drug use disorders, CBT beat minimal treatment with a moderate effect. It beat non-specific therapy or usual care only at early follow-up, and it was not better than another specific therapy [[Magill2019]].
- CBT combined with medication did better than usual care (Hedges g 0.18–0.28; 30 RCTs). CBT was not better than other evidence-based therapies such as MET or contingency management [[Ray2020CBT]].
- The authors of that meta-analysis concluded that best practice should include medication plus CBT or another evidence-based therapy, rather than usual clinical management or non-specific counselling [[Ray2020CBT]].
- In COMBINE (1,383 people with alcohol dependence), percent days abstinent during treatment was 80.6 with naltrexone plus medical management, 79.2 with the combined behavioural intervention (CBI) plus medical management, 77.1 with both, and 75.1 with placebo plus medical management [[Anton2006COMBINE]].
- In COMBINE, CBI alone without pills or medical management did worse (66.6 percent days abstinent) than placebo plus medical management (73.8). The authors noted that placebo pills and meeting a health professional had a positive effect [[Anton2006COMBINE]].

### therapy · Mindfulness-based relapse prevention (MBRP)
- In an RCT of 286 people in aftercare after substance use treatment, both MBRP and cognitive-behavioural relapse prevention lowered the risk of relapse to drug use and heavy drinking at 6 months compared with usual care (12-step programming and psychoeducation). The intervention was 8 weekly group sessions [[Bowen2014]].
- At 12 months, MBRP participants reported significantly fewer days of substance use and less heavy drinking than both the relapse-prevention and usual-care groups (Early evidence) [[Bowen2014]].

### therapy · Community reinforcement approach (CRA) and contingency management (CM)
- The community reinforcement approach was first described for alcohol problems by Hunt and Azrin in 1973 and refined by Azrin in 1976 [[Hunt1973,Azrin1976]].
- A systematic review of 11 RCTs found strong evidence that CRA reduces drinking days more than usual care, but conflicting evidence on continuous abstinence. There was moderate evidence that CRA plus disulfiram reduces drinking days [[Roozen2004CRA]].
- A meta-analysis of contingency management across substance use disorders found a mean effect size of d = 0.42. Effects were larger for opiates (0.65) and cocaine (0.66), and they faded after treatment ended [[Prendergast2006]].
- In an RCT of 79 outpatients with alcohol dependence and serious mental illness, 12 weeks of contingency management with prize draws for urine samples negative for ethyl glucuronide (EtG <150 ng/mL, three times a week) made a negative sample 3.1 times more likely (95% CI 2.2–4.5) [[McDonell2017]].
- In that trial, CM gave about 1.5 extra weeks of alcohol abstinence and fewer heavy-drinking episodes, and it also reduced stimulant use and smoking. The self-reported differences in drinking lasted to the 3-month follow-up [[McDonell2017]].

### therapy · Behavioural couples therapy and network therapy
- A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (n = 754) found behavioural couples therapy (BCT) better than individual treatment overall (Cohen's d = 0.54) and better than individual CBT alone (d = 0.42) [[Powers2008]].
- BCT's advantage grew over time. At follow-up, BCT was better for frequency of use (d = 0.45), consequences (d = 0.50) and relationship satisfaction (d = 0.51) [[Powers2008]].
- A review of marital and family therapy found that, once the person who drinks enters treatment, BCT is "clearly more effective" than individual treatment for abstinence and relationship functioning, including in studies with women and with gay and lesbian couples [[OFarrell2012]].
- In Network Support II (193 adults with AUD, followed 27 months), 12 weeks of Network Support treatment gave better post-treatment percent days abstinent and fewer drinking consequences than packaged CBT, with equal gains on heavy drinking days [[Litt2016]].
- Network Support's benefit was mediated by abstinence self-efficacy and by social-network change, especially a higher share of non-drinkers in the network and AA attendance [[Litt2016]].
- UKATT's social behaviour and network therapy also targets the social network, and it was as effective as MET [[UKATT2005]].

### therapy · Digital interventions
- In the Cochrane review of personalised digital interventions (57 studies, 34,390 participants), users drank about 23 g/week less (95% CI 15–30; about 3 UK units) than people with no or minimal intervention (moderate-quality evidence) [[Kaner2017Digital]].
- In five small studies (390 participants), digital and face-to-face interventions gave similar results (MD 0.52 g/week, 95% CI −24.59 to 25.63; low-quality evidence) [[Kaner2017Digital]].
- An individual-patient-data meta-analysis of 19 RCTs (14,198 adults; baseline 38.1 standard units of 10 g per week) found internet interventions cut drinking by an extra 5.02 units/week compared with controls. They also raised treatment response (OR 2.20; NNT 4.15) [[Riper2018IPD]].
- In that analysis, internet interventions with human support beat fully automated ones (an extra −6.78 units/week; OR 2.23 for response). Adults over 55 responded better [[Riper2018IPD]].
- A meta-analysis of technology-delivered CBT for alcohol (15 trials) found a small effect as a stand-alone treatment versus minimal control (g = 0.20). As an add-on to usual care, the effect was g = 0.30 and was stable to 12 months [[Kiluk2019Tech]].
- In an RCT of 68 adults, adding the web-based CBT4CBT programme to standard outpatient care increased percent days abstinent compared with usual care alone (d = 0.71) [[Kiluk2016CBT4CBT]].
- In an RCT of 349 people leaving residential treatment, the A-CHESS smartphone app cut risky drinking days over 12 months (1.39 vs 2.75 days; mean difference 1.37, 95% CI 0.46–2.27). Risky drinking was defined as more than 4 drinks for men or 3 for women in 2 hours [[Gustafson2014]].

### therapy · Integrated care for co-occurring depression and PTSD
- In NESARC-III, AUD at every severity level was linked with major depressive disorder, bipolar I disorder, other substance use disorders, and antisocial and borderline personality disorders (odds ratios 1.2–6.4) [[Grant2015NESARC]].
- In a meta-analysis of combined CBT and MI for co-occurring AUD and depression, the effect was small: g = 0.17 for less drinking and g = 0.27 for fewer depressive symptoms. Digital versions had a larger effect on depression (g = 0.73) [[Riper2014Depression]].
- In an RCT of 165 people with alcohol dependence and PTSD, all four groups (prolonged exposure or supportive counselling, each with naltrexone or placebo) cut their percentage of drinking days by about 61–70%. Naltrexone lowered drinking days further (7.93 percentage points) [[Foa2013]].
- In that trial, prolonged exposure therapy did not make drinking worse, which addressed a common worry about trauma-focused therapy in people with AUD. Six months after treatment, the prolonged exposure plus naltrexone group had the smallest rise in drinking [[Foa2013]].
- In the Cochrane review of PTSD with substance use disorder (14 studies, 1,506 participants), individual trauma-focused therapy plus a substance use intervention reduced PTSD severity more than usual care (SMD −0.41; very low-quality evidence). Fewer people completed it (RR 0.78) [[Roberts2016]].

### recovery · Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) and twelve-step facilitation (TSF)
- The 2020 Cochrane review of AA and 12-step programmes included 27 studies with 10,565 participants (21 RCTs or quasi-RCTs) [[Kelly2020Cochrane]].
- Manualised AA/TSF improved continuous abstinence at 12 months compared with other clinical treatments such as CBT (RR 1.21, 95% CI 1.03–1.42; 2 studies, 1,936 participants; high-certainty evidence). The effect held at 24 and 36 months [[Kelly2020Cochrane]].
- For percent days abstinent, AA/TSF performed about as well as other treatments at 12 months (MD 3.03, 95% CI −4.36 to 10.43). It did better at 24 months (MD 12.91) and 36 months (MD 6.64), with low-certainty evidence for both [[Kelly2020Cochrane]].
- AA/TSF probably performs as well as other treatments for drinking intensity and alcohol-related consequences at 12 months (moderate certainty) [[Kelly2020Cochrane]].
- Three of four economic studies found that AA/TSF saved more on healthcare costs than outpatient treatment, CBT or no AA/TSF. The reviewers concluded that AA/TSF "probably produces substantial healthcare cost savings" [[Kelly2020Cochrane]].
- In a matched comparison of 1,774 men treated for substance dependence, 12-step-based programmes had higher one-year abstinence than cognitive-behavioural programmes (45.7% vs 36.2%), and the cognitive-behavioural programmes had 64% higher annual costs [[Humphreys2001Cost]].
- At two years, abstinence was still higher after 12-step programmes (49.5% vs 37.0%), and costs were about 30% lower ($2,440 less per patient) [[Humphreys2007Cost]].
- An instrumental-variables re-analysis of randomised trials found that extra AA attendance caused by random assignment (free of self-selection) increased days of abstinence at 3 and 15 months. The exception was a sample that already attended AA heavily [[Humphreys2014IV]].
- In an RCT of 169 outpatients, a directive 12-step approach to encouraging AA raised AA attendance and percent days abstinent compared with usual care. A motivational approach to encouraging AA had no effect [[Walitzer2009]].
- In 461 people with AUD followed for 16 years, those who took part in AA for 27 weeks or more in the first year had better 16-year outcomes, and continued AA involvement also predicted better outcomes [[Moos2006AA]].
- AA appears to work mainly through social mechanisms. Among 1,726 Project MATCH participants, the effect of AA came mostly through changes in social networks and growth in social abstinence self-efficacy. Mediators explained 43–67% of the effect (Established) [[Kelly2012AAMech]].
- AA attendance reduced pro-drinking social ties more than it added pro-abstinent ties, and this network change explained much of its effect on abstinence [[Kelly2011AANetwork]].
- In a review of 25 years of mechanism research, spiritual change mattered mainly for a minority with high addiction severity. Social, cognitive and emotional mechanisms carried most of AA's benefit [[Kelly2017AASpiritual]].

### recovery · SMART Recovery and other mutual-help alternatives
- In the Peer Alternatives for Addiction study (647 adults with lifetime AUD, followed 12 months), deeper involvement in Women for Sobriety, LifeRing or SMART Recovery was linked with outcomes no different from deeper involvement in 12-step groups [[Zemore2018]].
- SMART members did worse at first, but the difference disappeared after adjusting for baseline recovery goal. This suggests that people with weaker abstinence goals chose SMART or LifeRing (Early evidence) [[Zemore2018]].
- The authors suggested that care plans support involvement in "a broad array of mutual help groups" [[Zemore2018]].
- In an RCT of 150 people leaving residential treatment, 15.6% of those who lived in a self-run Oxford House recovery home for 6 months or more reported substance use at 24 months. The figures were 45.7% for residents of less than 6 months and 64.8% for usual aftercare [[Jason2007]].

### recovery · Harm reduction and drinking-reduction goals (WHO risk drinking levels)
- The WHO risk drinking levels are four categories of average daily intake (low, moderate, high, very high risk). In the US NESARC 2001–02 sample of 22,005 people who drink, 90.2% were low-risk, 4.8% moderate-risk, 2.5% high-risk and 2.5% very-high-risk [[Hasin2017WHO]].
- Among very-high-risk drinkers, cutting down by one, two or three WHO levels predicted lower odds of alcohol dependence 3 years later (adjusted ORs 0.27, 0.17 and 0.07) [[Hasin2017WHO]].
- Among people who already had alcohol dependence and were very-high-risk drinkers, one-, two- and three-level reductions gave adjusted ORs for dependence 3 years later of 0.29, 0.06 and 0.04 [[Hasin2017WHO]].
- Very-high-risk drinkers who dropped 1, 2 or 3 levels had lower odds of liver disease 3 years later (aORs 0.34, 0.23, 0.17) [[Knox2018Liver]].
- In very-high-risk drinkers, one-, two- or three-level reductions predicted lower odds of depression or anxiety disorders (aORs 0.42, 0.37, 0.67) [[Knox2019Depression]].
- A reduction of 2 or more levels was linked with lower odds of cardiovascular disease in very-high-risk (aOR 0.58) and high-risk drinkers (aOR 0.81) [[Knox2020CVD]].
- In COMBINE, any reduction in WHO level during treatment was linked with fewer alcohol-related consequences and better mental health for up to a year after treatment [[Witkiewitz2017WHO]].
- Reductions were mostly maintained. Of those with at least a 1-level reduction at the end of treatment, 85.5% still had it one year later, and 77.8% of those with a 2-level reduction still had that reduction [[Witkiewitz2019WHO]].
- In COMBINE and UKATT, one- and two-level reductions were maintained at one year and were linked with better functioning and lower γ-glutamyltransferase, even in people with severe alcohol dependence [[Witkiewitz2020WHO]].
- In a meta-analysis of 16 cohorts of people with AUD, cutting below heavy drinking was linked with much lower mortality than continued heavy drinking (pooled OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.34–0.50). The OR was 0.35 for abstinence and 0.61 for substantial reduction without abstinence [[Roerecke2013Reduction]].
- In the same meta-analysis, abstinence carried lower mortality than reduced drinking (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.19–0.92). Both goals lower risk, and stopping lowers it most [[Roerecke2013Reduction]].
- These are observational associations in people who already drank heavily. They show that drinking less is better than drinking more. They do not show that any amount of drinking is good for health, and they do not set a "safe" level [[Hasin2017WHO,Roerecke2013Reduction]].
- In the Project MATCH New Mexico 10-year follow-up (n = 146), people in high-functioning profiles at 3 years had the best psychological functioning at 10 years, whatever their drinking level. Abstinence at 3 years did not by itself predict better functioning at 10 years (Early evidence) [[Witkiewitz2021Nonabstinent]].
- The 2022 NIAAA research definition of recovery has two parts: remission from DSM-5 AUD and cessation of heavy drinking. It does not require abstinence, and it stresses biopsychosocial functioning and quality of life [[Hagman2022]].

### recovery · Natural course, remission and relapse
- In NESARC 2001–02, among 4,422 US adults with DSM-IV alcohol dependence that began before the past year, the past-year status was: 25.0% still dependent, 27.3% in partial remission, 11.8% asymptomatic risk drinkers, 17.7% low-risk drinkers and 18.2% abstainers [[Dawson2005]].
- Only 25.5% of that group had ever received treatment, so much recovery happens without formal treatment [[Dawson2005]].
- Being married was linked with both abstinent and non-abstinent recovery, and higher alcohol intake with less of both. Greater severity raised the odds of abstinent recovery but lowered the odds of non-abstinent recovery [[Dawson2005]].
- A meta-analysis of 21 long-term studies found that 35.0–54.4% of people with substance use disorders reached remission over a mean follow-up of 17 years. The projected annual remission rate was 6.8–9.1% [[Fleury2016]].
- In 1,271 people entering publicly funded treatment (44% alcohol dependent), the median time from first use to last use was 27 years, and from first treatment to last use 9 years. 47% reached at least 12 months of abstinence within 3 years [[Dennis2005]].
- AUD is best managed like other chronic illnesses such as diabetes, hypertension and asthma. Adherence and relapse rates are similar across these conditions, and long-term care with continued monitoring gives lasting benefit [[McLellan2000]].
- In a 16-year cohort of 461 people with AUD who first sought help, those who got no help were less likely to reach 3-year remission and more likely to relapse afterwards. Natural remission "may be followed by a high likelihood of relapse" [[Moos2006Relapse]].
- In that cohort, relapse by 16 years was more likely in people who drank more, did not see their drinking as a problem, had less self-efficacy and relied more on avoidance coping [[Moos2006Relapse]].
- In a review of natural-recovery studies, low-risk drinking was reported as an outcome in 78.6% of alcohol samples [[Sobell2000]].
- In a 60-year cohort of inner-city Boston men, by age 70 the 72 men with alcohol dependence who were followed up were: 54% died, 32% abstinent, 1% controlled drinking, and 12% still had alcohol problems [[Vaillant2003]].
- In that cohort, prior alcohol dependence and AA attendance were the two best predictors of sustained abstinence. Alcohol problems could also persist for decades without remission [[Vaillant2003]].
- In NESARC, only 14.6% of people with lifetime AUD had ever received alcohol treatment, including mutual-help groups [[Cohen2007]].
- In NESARC-III (2012–13), lifetime AUD prevalence was 29.1%, and only 19.8% of people with lifetime AUD had ever been treated [[Grant2015NESARC]].
- Worldwide, about one in six people with AUD receive treatment (pooled 17.3%, 95% CI 12.8–22.3%). The figure is 9.3% in low and lower-middle-income countries [[Mekonen2021]].
- AUD is undertreated partly because of high stigma and too little screening in primary care, even though effective psychosocial and medical treatments exist [[Carvalho2019Lancet]].

### recovery · US National Recovery Survey
- In a probability survey of US adults (response from 39,809), 9.1% (weighted) said they used to have a problem with alcohol or drugs but no longer do [[Kelly2017NRS]].
- Of those who had resolved a problem, 46% identified as "in recovery" and 53.9% had used an "assisted" pathway [[Kelly2017NRS]].
- The supports used were mutual help (45.1%), treatment (27.6%), recovery support services (21.8%) and recovery community centres (6.2%) [[Kelly2017NRS]].
- Among 2,002 people who had resolved a problem, the median number of serious recovery attempts was 2 (IQR 1–4). The mean was 5.35 because a few people made many attempts [[Kelly2019Attempts]].
- The authors recommend communicating the median (2 attempts) rather than the mean. Presenting AUD as endlessly relapsing may weaken hope and help-seeking [[Kelly2019Attempts]].
- In the same sample, 20.3% reported continuous abstinence since problem resolution and 33.7% current abstinence. About half still used alcohol or drugs in some form [[Eddie2022]].
- Lower-risk use (including abstinence) was linked with greater self-esteem, happiness, quality of life and recovery capital. People tended toward abstinence as time since resolution grew [[Eddie2022]].
- People in recovery had higher rates of hepatitis C, COPD, heart disease and diabetes than the general population, which supports integrating addiction care with primary care [[Eddie2019]].

### recovery · Recovery capital and continuing care
- "Recovery capital" means the personal, social and community resources that support recovery, such as social support, spirituality, life meaning and 12-step affiliation. In 312 people in recovery, higher recovery capital predicted sustained recovery, better quality of life and less stress one year later [[Laudet2008]].
- In one cohort, people with 27 or more weeks of treatment in their first year of help-seeking had better 16-year alcohol outcomes than people who stayed untreated [[Moos2006AA]].

### families · Families, CRAFT and Al-Anon
- Family-focused interventions fall into three types: helping family members get the person into treatment, involving the family in that person's treatment, and supporting family members in their own right [[Copello2005]].
- In an RCT of 130 concerned significant others (CSOs), community reinforcement and family training (CRAFT) engaged 64% of initially unmotivated problem drinkers in treatment. The figures were 30% for the Johnson Institute confrontational intervention and 13% for Al-Anon facilitation [[Miller1999]].
- In that trial, 70% of families assigned to the Johnson intervention chose not to go ahead with the confrontation. Engagement usually happened after 4 to 6 sessions [[Miller1999]].
- All three family approaches improved the family member's own functioning and relationship quality similarly [[Miller1999]].
- A review of CRAFT found about three times more treatment engagement than Al-Anon/Nar-Anon (RR 3.25, 95% CI 2.11–5.02; NNT 2). It found twice the engagement of the Johnson intervention (RR 2.15, 95% CI 1.28–3.62; NNT 3). About two-thirds of loved ones entered treatment [[Roozen2010CRAFT]].
- Family members improved psychologically and physically whether they received CRAFT, Al-Anon/Nar-Anon or the Johnson intervention [[Roozen2010CRAFT]].
- When the person who drinks will not seek help, Al-Anon facilitation and spouse coping-skills training help family members cope better, and CRAFT promotes treatment entry [[OFarrell2012]].
- Al-Anon is the most widely used form of help for people concerned about someone else's drinking. In a national survey of 359 newcomers and 264 members, members were more satisfied with their quality of life than newcomers [[Timko2013]].
- Family members of people with alcohol or drug dependence were more likely than family members of people with diabetes or asthma to be diagnosed with substance use disorders, depression and trauma, and they had similar or higher healthcare costs [[Ray2009Family]].

### families · Children of parents with AUD
- In combined US surveys from 2009–2014, about 7.5 million children (10.5%) aged 17 or younger lived with at least one parent who had a past-year AUD [[Lipari2017]].
- In 8,629 adults in the ACE study, growing up with a parent with alcohol problems raised the odds of each adverse childhood experience about 2 to 13 times [[Dube2001]].
- The mean number of adverse childhood experiences was 1.4 with no parental alcohol problem, 2.6 with the father only, 3.2 with the mother only and 3.8 with both parents [[Dube2001]].
- These associations come from retrospective reports and cannot prove cause. The authors call for joined-up adult and paediatric care [[Dube2001]].
- Children in families with an adult with alcohol or drug dependence were more often diagnosed with depression and with substance use disorders than children of adults with asthma or diabetes. Their overall healthcare costs were similar or only somewhat higher [[Ray2014Children]].

### families · Stigma and language
- In an RCT of 516 mental health clinicians, people who read a vignette calling a person "a substance abuser" were more likely than those who read "having a substance use disorder" to judge the person personally culpable and to favour punishment [[Kelly2010Language]].
- In 1,288 participants, the terms "substance abuser", "addict" and "alcoholic" were strongly linked with negative associations. "Long-term recovery" and "medication-assisted recovery" were linked with positive ones [[Ashford2018]].
- "Recurrence of use" had a stronger positive association than "relapse" [[Ashford2018]].
- Compared with people with other mental disorders, people with alcohol dependence are held more responsible for their condition, provoke more social rejection and face more structural discrimination [[Schomerus2011]].
- An updated review of 16 population studies in 9 countries found that stigma toward people with AUD remains comparatively high, with a consistently greater wish for social distance [[Kilian2021]].
- In NESARC, people with lifetime AUD who perceived more stigma were less likely to use alcohol services (OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.18–0.76) [[Keyes2010]].
- In a US national survey (N = 709), the public held more negative views of people with drug addiction than of people with mental illness. They were more sceptical that treatment works and more accepting of discrimination [[Barry2014]].

### Exam hooks
- Brief intervention in primary care reduces drinking by about 20 g/week at 1 year compared with minimal or no intervention (Cochrane 2018) [[Kaner2018Cochrane]].
- Brief intervention is not proven for alcohol dependence or very heavy drinking, and it does not increase specialist treatment uptake [[Saitz2010,Glass2015]].
- USPSTF: screen adults 18 and over, including pregnant women, and give brief counselling for risky drinking (B). Evidence is insufficient for ages 12–17 (I) [[USPSTF2018Alcohol]].
- Project MATCH: CBT, MET and TSF gave similar outcomes, and matching hypotheses were largely unsupported [[ProjectMATCH1997]].
- UKATT: social behaviour and network therapy was as effective as MET, and both saved about five times their cost [[UKATT2005,UKATT2005Cost]].
- Cochrane 2020: manualised AA/TSF beat CBT and other treatments for 12-month continuous abstinence (RR 1.21, 95% CI 1.03–1.42; high certainty) [[Kelly2020Cochrane]].
- AA works mainly by changing a person's social network [[Kelly2012AAMech,Kelly2011AANetwork]].
- CRAFT engaged 64% of loved ones in treatment, versus 30% (Johnson intervention) and 13% (Al-Anon facilitation) [[Miller1999]].
- Behavioural couples therapy beats individual treatment (d = 0.54) [[Powers2008]].
- EtG-based contingency management made an alcohol-negative urine sample 3.1 times more likely in people with serious mental illness [[McDonell2017]].
- Marlatt's relapse-prevention model rests on high-risk situations, coping, self-efficacy, outcome expectancies and the abstinence violation effect [[Larimer1999]].
- Reducing drinking by 1–2 WHO risk levels is linked with less dependence, liver disease, depression/anxiety and CVD [[Hasin2017WHO,Knox2018Liver,Knox2019Depression,Knox2020CVD]].
- NESARC: of adults with past dependence, 25% were still dependent and about 36% were low-risk drinkers or abstainers. Only 25.5% had ever been treated [[Dawson2005]].
- National Recovery Survey: 9.1% of US adults have resolved an alcohol or drug problem, and the median number of serious attempts is 2 [[Kelly2017NRS,Kelly2019Attempts]].
- About one in six people with AUD worldwide get treatment [[Mekonen2021]].
- The NIAAA 2022 recovery definition is remission from DSM-5 AUD plus cessation of heavy drinking. It does not require abstinence [[Hagman2022]].
- "Substance abuser" makes clinicians more likely to favour punishment than "has a substance use disorder" [[Kelly2010Language]].
- Prolonged exposure therapy for PTSD did not worsen drinking in people with alcohol dependence [[Foa2013]].

### In plain words
- AUD is a medical condition that can be treated. It is not a moral failing.
- Many people get better. In one large US survey, about 1 in 11 adults said they used to have a problem with alcohol or drugs and no longer do. This is a group figure, not a promise for one person [[Kelly2017NRS]].
- Most people who got better did not need dozens of tries. The typical (median) number of serious attempts was two [[Kelly2019Attempts]].
- Several kinds of talking therapy help, such as CBT, motivational therapy and twelve-step facilitation. In big trials they worked about equally well, so pick one that fits you [[ProjectMATCH1997,UKATT2005]].
- Groups like Alcoholics Anonymous, SMART Recovery, LifeRing and Women for Sobriety can help, often because they connect you with people who support change. AA is free and in local communities [[Kelly2020Cochrane,Zemore2018,Kelly2012AAMech,Kelly2017AASpiritual]].
- If you are not ready to stop completely, drinking a lot less still lowers health risks. Stopping lowers them most [[Roerecke2013Reduction,Hasin2017WHO]].
- No research shows that drinking is good for your health.
- If you have been drinking heavily every day for a long time, stopping suddenly can be dangerous. Get medical help to stop safely.
- Families can learn skills (CRAFT) that help a loved one start treatment, and the family member's own wellbeing improves too [[Miller1999,Roozen2010CRAFT]].
- Al-Anon and family therapy are for the family's own wellbeing. A family is never to blame for someone else's drinking [[Timko2013,Copello2005]].
- Children who grow up with a parent's drinking problem often face extra stress, and support for the whole family helps [[Dube2001,Lipari2017]].
- Words matter. Saying "a person with alcohol use disorder" instead of labels like "alcoholic" can reduce blame [[Kelly2010Language,Ashford2018]].
- Apps and online programmes can help people cut down, especially when a real person also supports you [[Kaner2017Digital,Riper2018IPD]].
- A slip does not erase progress. Relapse-prevention therapy plans for lapses and for getting back on track [[Larimer1999]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Abstinence versus drinking reduction as a goal: the WHO-level and mortality data show real benefit from cutting down without stopping, and NIAAA's 2022 definition does not require abstinence [[Hasin2017WHO,Roerecke2013Reduction,Hagman2022]]. On the other hand, abstinence carries the lowest mortality, and abstinence goals predicted the best outcomes in mutual-help members [[Roerecke2013Reduction,Zemore2018]]. Current status: both are accepted goals, and severity and the person's preference guide the choice.
- Is AA's benefit causal or self-selection? Randomised TSF trials and instrumental-variable analyses support a causal effect [[Kelly2020Cochrane,Humphreys2014IV]]. Evidence for non-manualised AA/TSF is low-certainty [[Kelly2020Cochrane]]. Current status: high-certainty for manualised TSF on abstinence, with less certainty for other outcomes.
- Do secular mutual-help groups work as well as 12-step groups? Early longitudinal evidence says yes, but there are no RCTs and the samples are self-selected [[Zemore2018]].
- Does AUD typically follow a "chronic relapsing" course? Treatment-cohort data show long careers and many episodes of care [[Dennis2005,McLellan2000]]. General-population data show that many people remit, often without treatment, and need a median of 2 serious attempts [[Dawson2005,Kelly2019Attempts]]. Status: both are true for different groups. Clinical samples are more severe.
- Is natural (untreated) remission stable? One 16-year cohort found that untreated people were less likely to remit and more likely to relapse [[Moos2006Relapse]]. Population surveys show substantial recovery without treatment [[Dawson2005]]. Status: unresolved, and it depends on severity.
- Treatment matching: Project MATCH found few robust matching effects [[ProjectMATCH1997,ProjectMATCH1997Secondary]]. Anger and social-network findings at 3 years kept interest alive [[ProjectMATCH1998]]. Status: matching on these traits is not standard practice.
- Brief intervention in dependence and in hospital: brief intervention works for hazardous drinking in primary care [[Kaner2018Cochrane]] but is unproven for dependence [[Saitz2010]]. In hospital it reduced deaths at 6–12 months, but drinking effects faded by one year [[McQueen2011]].
- Long-term durability of MI: effects fade by long-term follow-up in Cochrane analyses [[Schwenker2023,Smedslund2011]].
- Integrated PTSD and AUD care: trauma-focused therapy appears safe for drinking outcomes, but the evidence is very low quality and fewer people complete it [[Foa2013,Roberts2016]].

- Every source above was confirmed with PubMed E-utilities esummary (authors, title, journal, year, volume, pages, PMID, PMCID, DOI), and its abstract was read with efetch. Lipari2017 is a PubMed Bookshelf record (SAMHSA CBHSQ Report), and its abstract was read via efetch.

- Not covered because no validated source was found within this pass: ASAM Criteria dimensions and levels of care (the ASAM Criteria is a book not indexed in PubMed); Alateen-specific outcomes; Kelly 2017's absolute count of Americans in recovery (millions; not in the abstract).
- Europe PMC full-text requests returned HTTP 500 and the BioC service rate-limited, so no full-text figures were used.
