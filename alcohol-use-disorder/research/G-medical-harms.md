# G · Medical harms: liver, cancer, heart, brain, pregnancy & FASD

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: liver, body, pregnancy.

This pass covers the harms of alcohol to the body outside the addiction itself: the alcohol-associated liver disease spectrum and its scores (Maddrey, MELD, Lille), treatment and transplant evidence for alcohol-associated hepatitis, survival with abstinence, cancer (IARC classification, site-specific dose-response, breast cancer, acetaldehyde), the "J-curve" debate and Mendelian randomisation, heart rhythm, blood pressure, stroke and cardiomyopathy, pancreatitis, brain and dementia, injuries and medicine interactions, and pregnancy, fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD) and breastfeeding. Every number below was read from the PubMed abstract or the open full text (PMC) of the cited source, or from the current US label in openFDA.

## Findings

### liver · the spectrum of alcohol-associated liver disease
- The AASLD's 2019 practice guidance uses the term "alcohol-associated liver diseases" in place of the older "alcoholic liver disease" [[Crabb2020AASLD]].
- ALD is a clinical-histologic spectrum: fatty liver (steatosis), alcohol-associated hepatitis, and cirrhosis with its complications (Established) [[Singal2018ACG,Seitz2018NRDP]].
- ALD is described as the most prevalent type of chronic liver disease worldwide [[Seitz2018NRDP]].
- Most people who drink more than 40 g of alcohol per day develop alcoholic fatty liver, but only a subset go on to more advanced disease [[Seitz2018NRDP]].
- Steatosis develops in about 90% of people who drink heavily and may be seen within 2 weeks of heavy, regular drinking; it resolves rapidly after complete abstinence [[Singal2018ACG]].
- With continued excessive drinking, about one-third of people with steatosis show liver inflammation on histology (alcohol-associated steatohepatitis, ASH) [[Singal2018ACG]].
- Among people with steatohepatitis on biopsy, 20–40% develop fibrosis and 8–20% develop cirrhosis, per the ACG guideline [[Singal2018ACG]].
- "Pure" fatty liver is not benign: of 88 people with biopsy-proven pure alcoholic fatty liver followed for a median 10.5 years, 9 developed cirrhosis and 7 more developed fibrosis [[Teli1995Lancet]].
- In that cohort, 8 of the 9 who developed cirrhosis were still drinking more than 40 units per week at follow-up; with continued heavy drinking, mixed macro/microvesicular fat and giant mitochondria identified a 47–61% risk of progression [[Teli1995Lancet]].
- Mechanisms include hepatic steatosis, oxidative stress, acetaldehyde-mediated toxicity and cytokine- and chemokine-driven inflammation (Established) [[Seitz2018NRDP]].
- Genetic, epigenetic and non-genetic factors are thought to explain the large person-to-person variation in who develops advanced ALD (Established) [[Seitz2018NRDP]].
- Obesity is one of the most important environmental risk factors for cirrhosis in people who drink heavily: being overweight for at least 10 years roughly doubles the risk [[Singal2018ACG]].
- At the same average intake, women have a higher risk of cirrhosis than men, and the dose-response for cirrhosis is steeper for death than for illness [[Rehm2010Cirrhosis]].
- In a Danish cohort of 55,917 adults aged 50–64, no cases of alcohol-related cirrhosis occurred among lifetime abstainers; in men, daily drinking carried a hazard ratio of 3.65 (95% CI 2.39–5.55) versus drinking 2–4 days a week [[Askgaard2015JHep]].
- In the same cohort, recent drinking (ages 40–59) was associated with cirrhosis risk, whereas drinking at ages 20–39 was not [[Askgaard2015JHep]].
- A 2023 meta-analysis of 5 studies found daily drinking (versus non-daily, same amount) had a pooled RR for cirrhosis of 1.71 (95% CI 1.23–2.23) in men and 1.56 (1.39–1.74) in women [[LlamosasFalcon2023]].
- Diagnosis of ALD needs documented chronic heavy drinking and exclusion of other liver diseases; most people are diagnosed at advanced stages [[Singal2018ACG]].
- Half of people with clinically "early" (asymptomatic) ALD in a biopsy cohort already had severe fibrosis or cirrhosis (F3/4), and their 10-year mortality was 45% versus 0% for F0–2 [[Lackner2017JHep]].
- Liver-related mortality at 5 years was 13% in early/compensated ALD and 43% in decompensated ALD (n=192, biopsy cohort) [[Lackner2017JHep]].
- Alcohol abstinence is the most effective way to prevent disease progression and the best treatment for all stages of ALD (Established) [[Singal2018ACG,Seitz2018NRDP]].

### liver · stopping drinking and survival in cirrhosis
- A meta-analysis of 7 cohort studies (1,235 people with alcohol-related cirrhosis) found abstinence improved survival, with the difference becoming significant from 1.5 years (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.33–0.81) [[Xie2014HepatolRes]].
- In that meta-analysis the HR for death with abstinence versus continued drinking was 0.48 at 0.5 years (not significant), 0.55 at 2 years, 0.54 at 3 years, 0.60 at 4 years and 0.63 (0.52–0.76) at 5 years [[Xie2014HepatolRes]].
- Abstinence during follow-up was an important predictor of survival in both early/compensated and decompensated ALD [[Lackner2017JHep]].
- In a 17-centre Baveno Cooperation cohort of 633 people with decompensated alcohol-related cirrhosis who stopped drinking, 31.1% achieved "recompensation" (cumulative incidence 12.3% at 1 year, 23.4% at 2 years, 33.8% at 5 years) (Early evidence) [[Hofer2026JHep]].
- Recompensation (Baveno VII) required at least 3 months' abstinence, resolution of ascites and encephalopathy off therapy, no variceal bleeding for 1 year, and Child-Pugh A or MELD <10 [[Hofer2026JHep]].
- Stopping within 1 month of decompensation roughly doubled the chance of recompensation (adjusted subdistribution HR 2.042), and recompensation was linked to lower all-cause mortality (adjusted HR 0.255) [[Hofer2026JHep]].
- No recompensated person who stayed abstinent died of liver causes or developed hepatocellular carcinoma during follow-up (median 36.3 months) [[Hofer2026JHep]].

### liver · alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH)
- AH presents with rapid onset or worsening of jaundice; in severe cases it can progress to acute-on-chronic liver failure, with 1-month mortality as high as 20–50% depending on the number of extra-hepatic organ failures [[Singal2018ACG]].
- STOPAH describes short-term mortality in severe AH as exceeding 30% [[Thursz2015STOPAH]].
- In a global cohort of 2,581 people hospitalised with AH in 85 centres in 11 countries, mortality was 20% at 28 days and 30.9% at 90 days [[MoralesArraez2022]].
- The (modified) Maddrey discriminant function is 4.6 × (prothrombin time − control time, seconds) + serum bilirubin (µmol/L)/17.1, i.e. bilirubin in mg/dL, as published in the 1989 methylprednisolone trial [[Carithers1989]].
- The original 1978 Maddrey trial identified prolonged prothrombin time and high serum bilirubin as independent predictors of death in AH [[Maddrey1978]].
- Severe AH is identified by a Maddrey score >32 (ACG wording; trials have used ≥32) or a MELD score >20 [[Singal2018ACG,Louvet2007Lille]].
- A Maddrey score ≥32 predicts about 20–50% mortality over 30 days [[Singal2018ACG]].
- MELD uses serum bilirubin, creatinine, INR and (originally) cause of liver disease; it predicted 3-month mortality with c-statistics of 0.78–0.87 across 4 validation cohorts [[Kamath2001MELD]].
- In a Mayo Clinic cohort with AH (n=73), MELD predicted 30-day mortality (c-statistic 0.83) at least as well as the Maddrey score (0.74); a MELD of 21 had 75% sensitivity and 75% specificity for 90-day mortality [[Dunn2005MELD]].
- In the global AH study, the Maddrey score had the lowest accuracy (AUROC 0.701 for 28-day mortality) and MELD-type scores performed best; the authors suggested the Maddrey score should no longer be used for prognosis [[MoralesArraez2022]].
- The Lille model combines age, renal insufficiency, albumin, prothrombin time, bilirubin and the change in bilirubin by day 7 of corticosteroids; its AUROC for 6-month death was 0.89 [[Louvet2007Lille]].
- A Lille score ≥0.45 defines "nonresponse"; it applied to 40% of steroid-treated people, whose 6-month survival was 25% versus 85% for responders [[Louvet2007Lille]].
- ACG wording: "Prednisolone is used in a dose of 40 mg per day for a total duration of 4 weeks", with methylprednisolone 32 mg/day IV for those unable to take oral medicine (quoted as published, not as instructions) [[Singal2018ACG]].
- In the 1989 trial, 28-day mortality was 35% (11/31) with placebo versus 6% (2/35) with methylprednisolone in severe AH (Maddrey >32 or spontaneous encephalopathy) [[Carithers1989]].
- STOPAH (1,103 randomised; 2×2 factorial): 28-day mortality was 17% placebo–placebo, 14% prednisolone, 19% pentoxifylline and 13% both [[Thursz2015STOPAH]].
- In STOPAH, the odds ratio for 28-day death with prednisolone was 0.72 (95% CI 0.52–1.01; P=0.06), with no benefit at 90 days or 1 year; pentoxifylline OR was 1.07 (0.77–1.49) [[Thursz2015STOPAH]].
- Serious infections occurred in 13% of people given prednisolone versus 7% of those not given it in STOPAH (P=0.002) [[Thursz2015STOPAH]].
- Corticosteroids give a short-term survival benefit in about half of people treated for severe AH; long-term mortality depends on the underlying liver disease and on abstinence [[Singal2018ACG]].

### liver · early liver transplantation for severe AH
- Historically, 6 months of abstinence was required before transplant listing; people whose AH did not respond to medical therapy had about 30% 6-month survival [[Mathurin2011NEJM]].
- In the French–Belgian early-transplant study, 26 highly selected people with a first episode of severe AH not responding to treatment (median Lille 0.88) had 6-month survival of 77% with early transplantation versus 23% in matched controls (P<0.001) [[Mathurin2011NEJM]].
- Fewer than 2% of people admitted with severe AH were selected, and the centres used 2.9% of available grafts for this indication; 3 recipients resumed drinking at 720–1,140 days [[Mathurin2011NEJM]].
- In the US ACCELERATE-AH consortium (12 centres, 147 recipients, median 55 days' abstinence before transplant), 1-year survival was 94% and 3-year survival 84% [[Lee2018Gastro]].
- After early transplantation, the cumulative incidence of any alcohol use was 25% at 1 year and 34% at 3 years; sustained use was 10% and 17%, and sustained use was linked to higher mortality (HR 4.59) [[Lee2018Gastro]].
- The ACG guideline notes that most centres still require 6 months of abstinence before transplant evaluation, but that this rule is a conflicting predictor of return to drinking [[Singal2018ACG]].

### liver · rising liver deaths
- US annual deaths from cirrhosis rose 65% from 1999 to 2016, to 34,174, and deaths from hepatocellular carcinoma doubled to 11,073 [[Tapper2018BMJ]].
- From 2009 to 2016, people aged 25–34 had the fastest annual rise in cirrhosis mortality (10.5% per year), "driven entirely by alcohol related liver disease" [[Tapper2018BMJ]].
- US deaths with an underlying cause of alcohol-associated liver disease rose from 24,106 in 2019 to 29,504 in 2020 (22.4%); all deaths involving alcohol rose 25.5%, from 78,927 to 99,017 [[White2022JAMA]].
- Globally, cirrhosis caused more than 1.32 million deaths in 2017 (2.4% of all deaths); eastern Europe and central Asia were the only GBD regions where the age-standardised cirrhosis death rate rose, mainly because of alcohol-related liver disease [[GBD2017Cirrhosis]].

### body · cancer
- The 2007 IARC working group concluded that ethanol in alcoholic beverages is carcinogenic to humans (IARC Group 1) and added the female breast and colorectum to the alcohol-related cancer sites (Established) [[Baan2007IARC,Brooks2009PLoSMed]].
- Alcohol causes cancer of the oral cavity and pharynx, oesophagus, colorectum, liver, larynx and female breast (seven sites) (Established) [[Bagnardi2015BJC,Connor2017Addiction]].
- A dose-response meta-analysis of 572 studies (486,538 cancer cases) defined light drinking as ≤12.5 g/day, moderate ≤50 g/day and heavy >50 g/day [[Bagnardi2015BJC]].
- Oral cavity and pharynx cancer RR versus non-drinkers: 1.13 light, 1.83 moderate, 5.13 heavy [[Bagnardi2015BJC]].
- Oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma RR: 1.26 light, 2.23 moderate, 4.95 heavy [[Bagnardi2015BJC]].
- Female breast cancer RR: 1.04 light (95% CI 1.01–1.07), 1.23 moderate, 1.61 heavy (118 studies) [[Bagnardi2015BJC]].
- Colorectal cancer RR: 1.17 moderate and 1.44 heavy; larynx 1.44 moderate and 2.65 heavy; liver 2.07 heavy [[Bagnardi2015BJC]].
- Heavy drinking was also associated with cancers of the stomach (RR 1.21), gallbladder (2.64), pancreas (1.19) and lung (1.15) [[Bagnardi2015BJC]].
- In a pooled reanalysis of 53 studies (58,515 women with breast cancer), breast cancer risk rose by 7.1% (95% CI 5.5–8.7%) for each additional 10 g of alcohol per day, the same in women who did and did not smoke [[Hamajima2002BJC]].
- In the UK Million Women Study (1.28 million women; average intake in drinkers about 1 drink/day), each extra 10 g/day was associated with 12% higher breast cancer risk and 29% higher oral cavity/pharynx cancer risk [[Allen2009JNCI]].
- Million Women Study absolute estimate: each additional regular daily drink adds about 11 breast cancers per 1,000 women up to age 75, and about 15 cancers per 1,000 women in total [[Allen2009JNCI]].
- In 2020, an estimated 741,300 new cancer cases worldwide (4.1% of all new cancers) were attributable to alcohol; 76.7% were in men [[Rumgay2021LancetOncol]].
- Oesophageal (189,700), liver (154,700) and breast (98,300) cancers made up the most alcohol-attributable cases in 2020 [[Rumgay2021LancetOncol]].
- Of these cancers, 46.7% were attributable to heavy drinking (>60 g/day), 39.4% to risky drinking (20–60 g/day) and 13.9% (103,100 cases) to moderate drinking (<20 g/day); drinking up to 10 g/day accounted for 41,300 cases [[Rumgay2021LancetOncol]].
- Acetaldehyde, the first and most toxic product of ethanol metabolism, is the main focus of research on how alcohol causes cancer; ethanol may also act by disrupting DNA methylation and retinoid metabolism (Established) [[Seitz2007NRC]].
- Other proposed pathways include oxidative stress and changes in oestrogen and androgen regulation, and ethanol acting as a solvent for tobacco carcinogens [[Rumgay2021LancetOncol]].
- About 36% of East Asians show an alcohol flushing response, mainly due to ALDH2 deficiency; the authors estimate at least 540 million ALDH2-deficient people worldwide (about 8% of the world population) [[Brooks2009PLoSMed]].
- In case-control studies from Japan and Taiwan, people with low-activity ALDH2 who drank had odds ratios for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma of 3.7 to 18.1, mostly over 10 for those drinking heavily [[Brooks2009PLoSMed]].
- There is some evidence that risk of laryngeal, pharyngeal and liver cancers falls after drinking stops (Early evidence) [[Connor2017Addiction]].
- Alcohol-attributable cancers at the seven causal sites were estimated at 5.8% of cancer deaths worldwide [[Connor2017Addiction]].
- ASCO's 2018 statement calls alcohol "an established risk factor for several malignancies" and a potentially modifiable one, and supports public education on alcohol and cancer [[LoConte2018ASCO]].

### body · how much is too much? risk curves, the J-curve and Mendelian randomisation
- GBD 2016: alcohol was the seventh leading risk factor for deaths and DALYs globally and the leading risk factor in people aged 15–49, accounting for 12.2% of male and 3.8% of female deaths at those ages [[GBD2016Alcohol]].
- GBD 2016 concluded that the level of drinking that minimised harm across health outcomes was zero (95% UI 0.0–0.8) standard drinks per week, with a standard drink defined as 10 g of alcohol [[GBD2016Alcohol]].
- In GBD 2016, the risk of all cancers rose monotonically with consumption; only ischaemic heart disease showed a significant J-shaped curve [[GBD2016Alcohol]].
- GBD 2020 made the risk curve depend on age and region: for people aged 15–39 in 2020, the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL) ranged from 0 to 0.603 standard drinks per day [[GBD2020Alcohol]].
- For people aged 40 and older, GBD 2020 found a J-shaped curve in all regions, with TMREL 0.114–1.87 standard drinks per day [[GBD2020Alcohol]].
- In GBD 2020, 59.1% of people drinking harmful amounts in 2020 were aged 15–39, and 76.9% were male [[GBD2020Alcohol]].
- In 599,912 current drinkers (83 studies, 19 high-income countries), the minimum all-cause mortality risk was around or below 100 g of alcohol per week [[Wood2018Lancet]].
- Each 100 g/week higher intake was associated with higher risk of stroke (HR 1.14), heart failure (1.09), fatal hypertensive disease (1.24) and fatal aortic aneurysm (1.15), but lower risk of myocardial infarction (0.94) [[Wood2018Lancet]].
- Compared with >0–100 g/week, drinking >100–200, >200–350 and >350 g/week was associated with roughly 6 months, 1–2 years and 4–5 years lower life expectancy at age 40 [[Wood2018Lancet]].
- Without adjustment, 87 cohort studies reproduced the classic J-curve (low-volume drinkers RR 0.86); after adjusting for "abstainer bias" (former and occasional drinkers counted as abstainers), the RR was 0.97 (0.88–1.07), i.e. no mortality benefit [[Stockwell2016JSAD]].
- Former drinkers had elevated mortality (RR 1.22), which is why mixing them into the "non-drinker" group makes light drinkers look healthier [[Stockwell2016JSAD]].
- In the 2023 update (107 cohorts, 4.8 million participants), low-volume drinking (1.3–24 g/day) had RR 0.93 (P=0.07) versus lifetime non-drinkers; 45–64 g/day had RR 1.19 and ≥65 g/day RR 1.35 [[Zhao2023JAMANetwOpen]].
- In that update, female drinkers had a higher mortality risk than female lifetime non-drinkers (RR 1.22), and risk began at lower intake for women than for men [[Zhao2023JAMANetwOpen]].
- An apparent protective association of low-volume drinking with coronary death (RR 0.80) disappeared in cohorts aged 55 or younger, in higher-quality studies, and in studies controlling for heart health [[Zhao2017JSAD]].
- Mendelian randomisation in 261,991 Europeans: carriers of the ADH1B rs1229984 A-allele drank 17.2% less and had lower odds of coronary heart disease (OR 0.90), suggesting that drinking less is better for the heart even for light-to-moderate drinkers [[Holmes2014BMJ]].
- In the China Kadoorie Biobank (512,715 adults), self-reported drinking showed U-shaped links with stroke and heart attack, but genotype-predicted intake (ALDH2 and ADH1B variants) showed no U-shape [[Millwood2019Lancet]].
- Genotype-predicted alcohol intake raised stroke risk log-linearly: RR per 280 g/week 1.58 for intracerebral haemorrhage and 1.27 for ischaemic stroke; there was no significant effect on myocardial infarction (RR 0.96) [[Millwood2019Lancet]].
- The Kadoorie authors concluded that the apparently protective effect of moderate drinking against stroke is "largely non-causal" and that alcohol uniformly raises blood pressure and stroke risk [[Millwood2019Lancet]].
- In UK Biobank (371,463 people), healthier lifestyles in light drinkers explained part of the apparent benefit; Mendelian randomisation linked genetically higher intake with 1.3-fold higher hypertension risk and 1.4-fold higher coronary artery disease risk per 1-SD increase [[Biddinger2022JAMANetwOpen]].
- Non-linear Mendelian randomisation suggested light drinking carried minimal added cardiovascular risk, with risk rising exponentially at heavier intake [[Biddinger2022JAMANetwOpen]].
- More than 40 ICD-10 categories are fully attributable to alcohol; most partly attributable diseases rise steadily with volume, with ischaemic disease and diabetes the exceptions in published curves [[Rehm2017Addiction]].
- Taken together, current evidence does not support drinking alcohol for health; the apparent benefit of light drinking is heavily disputed and may reflect bias (Established for the controversy; benefit itself unproven) [[Stockwell2016JSAD,Zhao2023JAMANetwOpen,Millwood2019Lancet]].

### body · heart and blood vessels
- "Holiday heart" was the name given in 1978 to heart rhythm disorders linked to alcohol, typically after a bout of heavy drinking [[Ettinger1978]].
- Heavy drinking in a binge pattern can trigger acute arrhythmias even in people with normal hearts; atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common [[Day2019Addiction]].
- A meta-analysis of 14 studies found an RR for AF of 1.08 (95% CI 1.05–1.10) for each 10 g/day increase in alcohol [[Kodama2011JACC]].
- In a dose-response meta-analysis of 7 prospective studies (12,554 AF cases), AF risk versus non-drinkers was 1.08 at 1 drink/day, 1.17 at 2, 1.26 at 3, 1.36 at 4 and 1.47 at 5 drinks/day (12 g/drink) [[Larsson2014JACC]].
- In 107,845 Europeans followed for a median of 13.9 years, one drink (12 g) per day was associated with a hazard ratio for AF of 1.16 (1.11–1.22), with similar effects across beverage types [[Csengeri2021EHJ]].
- In a case-crossover study of 100 people with paroxysmal AF wearing ECG and alcohol sensors, one drink in the previous 4 hours doubled the odds of an AF episode (OR 2.02) and two or more drinks more than tripled it (OR 3.58) [[Marcus2021AnnIntMed]].
- In an Australian RCT of 140 people with AF who drank ≥10 drinks/week, abstinence reduced AF recurrence over 6 months: 53% versus 73% (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.36–0.84) [[Voskoboinik2020NEJM]].
- In that trial, the abstinence group cut intake from 16.8 to 2.1 drinks/week, and median time in AF was 0.5% versus 1.2% (P=0.01) [[Voskoboinik2020NEJM]].
- Across 23 cohort studies, hypertension risk rose almost linearly with intake: RR 1.11 at 24 g/day, 1.22 at 36 g/day and 1.33 at 48 g/day, versus 12 g/day [[Cecchini2024Hypertension]].
- In 36 trials (2,865 participants), cutting drinking lowered blood pressure only in people who drank more than 2 drinks/day; people drinking ≥6 drinks/day who halved intake lowered systolic BP by 5.50 mm Hg and diastolic BP by 3.97 mm Hg [[Roerecke2017LPH]].
- Important blood pressure reductions can be expected after as little as 1 month of abstinence [[Day2019Addiction]].
- Heavy drinking (>4 drinks/day) was associated with RR 1.67 for intracerebral haemorrhage and 1.82 for subarachnoid haemorrhage; light drinking was inversely associated only with ischaemic stroke in observational data [[Larsson2016BMCMed]].
- Alcohol-related cardiomyopathy accounts for about one-third of non-ischaemic dilated cardiomyopathy, per one review [[Day2019Addiction]].
- Alcohol-induced cardiomyopathy is generally considered to need >80 g/day for over 5 years, though the definition is debated; titin-truncating variants support a "double-hit" (genes plus alcohol) model (Early evidence) [[Dominguez2024EHJ]].
- In alcohol-induced cardiomyopathy, recovery of ejection fraction in people who stop drinking is associated with an excellent prognosis; abstinence is the most important treatment step [[Dominguez2024EHJ]].
- Binge drinking and high lifetime intake raise total and cardiovascular mortality, heart failure, stroke, hypertension and arrhythmia [[FernandezSola2015NRC]].

### body · pancreas
- The risk of chronic pancreatitis rises steadily with intake, with an RR of 6.29 (95% CI 3.04–13.02) at 100 g/day [[Samokhvalov2015EBioMed]].
- An earlier meta-analysis found an approximately exponential dose-response, with an apparent threshold around 4 drinks daily in categorical analyses [[Irving2009JOP]].
- In a US multicentre study, very heavy drinking (≥5 drinks/day) was associated with chronic pancreatitis (OR 3.10, 95% CI 1.87–5.14), and smoking was an independent, dose-dependent risk factor [[Yadav2009ArchIntMed]].

### body · brain and dementia
- In a French national hospital cohort (over 1.1 million people with dementia), alcohol use disorders were the strongest modifiable risk factor for dementia onset: adjusted HR 3.34 in women and 3.36 in men [[Schwarzinger2018LPH]].
- Of 57,353 cases of early-onset dementia (<65 years), 38.9% were alcohol-related by definition and a further 17.6% had a co-diagnosis of an alcohol use disorder [[Schwarzinger2018LPH]].
- "Alcohol-related brain damage" is an umbrella term covering alcohol-related dementia and Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome; people can partly recover in brain structure and function if abstinence is maintained [[Ridley2013AlzResTher]].
- Over 30 years in Whitehall II, drinking 14–21 units/week was associated with three times the odds of right hippocampal atrophy (OR 3.4), and >30 units/week with OR 5.8, versus abstinence; light drinking showed no protective effect [[Topiwala2017BMJ]].
- In 36,678 UK Biobank adults, lower brain volumes and poorer white matter were already apparent at an average of one to two daily units, and the links grew stronger with intake [[Daviet2022NatCommun]].
- In Whitehall II (9,087 people, 23 years), above 14 units/week each extra 7 units was associated with 17% higher dementia risk; alcohol-related hospital admission had HR 4.28 [[Sabia2018BMJ]].

### body · injuries and medicine interactions
- Injury risk rises non-linearly with the amount drunk on one occasion: for motor-vehicle crashes, the odds ratio rises 1.24 per 10 g and reaches 52.0 at 120 g [[Taylor2010DAD]].
- For non-traffic injuries, the odds ratio rises 1.30 per 10 g, reaching 24.2 at 140 g; the authors state that no level of drinking is safe when driving [[Taylor2010DAD]].
- Alcohol interacts with many medicines in two ways: by changing how the liver handles them (pharmacokinetic) and by adding to their effects, especially sedation (pharmacodynamic) [[Weathermon1999]].
- Classes that interact include antibiotics, antidepressants, antihistamines, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, muscle relaxants, opioids, non-opioid pain medicines and warfarin, as well as some over-the-counter and herbal products [[Weathermon1999]].
- The US opioid label boxed warning (OxyContin) states that using opioids with benzodiazepines or other CNS depressants, "including alcohol, may result in profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death" [[FDA-OxyContin]].
- The US alprazolam (Xanax) boxed warning states that misuse of benzodiazepines often involves alcohol and other substances, which is associated with more serious adverse outcomes [[FDA-Xanax]].
- The US acetaminophen OTC label warns that severe liver damage may occur if a person takes it while having 3 or more alcoholic drinks every day [[FDA-Tylenol]].

### pregnancy · no known safe amount
- CDC: "There is no known safe amount of alcohol consumption during pregnancy"; drinking during pregnancy can cause FASD and might increase the risk of miscarriage and stillbirth [[Gosdin2022MMWR]].
- AAP clinical report: prenatal alcohol exposure is the leading preventable cause of birth defects and intellectual and neurodevelopmental disabilities [[Williams2015AAP]].
- AAP: during pregnancy no amount of alcohol should be considered safe, there is no safe trimester, all forms (beer, wine, liquor) pose similar risk, and binge drinking poses dose-related risk [[Williams2015AAP]].
- AAP: alcohol-related birth defects and developmental disabilities are completely preventable when no alcohol is used in pregnancy; the neurocognitive and behavioural effects are lifelong; early recognition and therapy improve outcomes [[Williams2015AAP]].
- In US BRFSS data 2018–2020, 13.5% of pregnant adults reported current drinking and 5.2% reported binge drinking, each about 2 percentage points higher than in 2015–2017 [[Gosdin2022MMWR]].
- Pregnant adults with frequent mental distress were 2.3 times as likely to report drinking and 3.4 times as likely to report binge drinking; those without a usual provider were 1.7 times as likely to drink, pointing to access to care and mental health support as levers [[Gosdin2022MMWR]].
- The estimated global prevalence of alcohol use during pregnancy was 9.8% (95% CI 8.9–11.1) [[Popova2017LancetGH]].
- A meta-analysis of 24 studies (231,808 pregnant women) found alcohol exposure associated with higher miscarriage risk (OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.12–1.28); at ≤5 drinks/week each extra weekly drink added about 6% (OR 1.06) [[Sundermann2019ACER]].
- In 9,719 children aged 9–10 in the ABCD study, any reported prenatal alcohol exposure was associated with more psychopathology, attention problems and impulsiveness, some dose-dependent (Early evidence for low-level exposure) [[Lees2020AJP]].

### pregnancy · fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD)
- FASD is the umbrella term for the range of effects of prenatal alcohol exposure; fetal alcohol syndrome (FAS) was first described in 1973 [[Williams2015AAP]].
- The Institute of Medicine's four FASD categories are FAS, partial FAS (PFAS), alcohol-related neurodevelopmental disorder (ARND) and alcohol-related birth defects (ARBD) [[Hoyme2016Pediatrics]].
- Hoyme 2016 criteria for FAS require ≥2 of three facial features: short palpebral fissures (≤10th centile), thin vermilion border of the upper lip, and smooth philtrum (rank 4 or 5 on a racially normed lip/philtrum guide) [[Hoyme2016Pediatrics]].
- FAS also requires growth deficiency (height and/or weight ≤10th centile), deficient brain growth or abnormal brain structure/function (for example head circumference ≤10th percentile), and neurobehavioural impairment; it can be diagnosed with or without documented exposure [[Hoyme2016Pediatrics]].
- Partial FAS in a child with documented prenatal exposure requires the facial pattern plus neurobehavioural impairment [[Hoyme2016Pediatrics]].
- The 2016 guidelines draw on the evaluation of more than 10,000 children and add a lip/philtrum guide with a 45-degree view [[Hoyme2016Pediatrics]].
- The estimated global prevalence of FASD among children and youth is 7.7 per 1,000 (95% CI 4.9–11.7); the WHO European Region was highest at 19.8 per 1,000 [[Lange2017JAMAPediatr]].
- In 4 US communities (6,639 first-graders assessed, 2010–2016), conservative FASD prevalence ranged from 11.3 to 50.0 per 1,000 (1.1% to 5.0%), and weighted estimates from 31.1 to 98.5 per 1,000 [[May2018JAMA]].
- The global prevalence of FAS (the most severe form) was estimated at 14.6 per 10,000; about one in every 67 women who drank during pregnancy would have a child with FAS, about 119,000 children a year worldwide [[Popova2017LancetGH]].
- A meta-analysis identified 428 conditions that co-occur with FASD across 18 of 22 ICD-10 chapters; in FAS, conduct disorder, receptive and expressive language disorders, chronic serous otitis media and sensory/peripheral nerve function abnormalities each had pooled prevalence of 50–91% [[Popova2016Lancet]].
- In 415 people with FAS or fetal alcohol effects (median age 14), lifetime adverse outcomes included disrupted schooling (61%), trouble with the law (60%) and alcohol/drug problems (35%); 80% had not been raised by their birth mothers [[Streissguth2004JDBP]].
- Receiving the diagnosis at a younger age and growing up in a stable, nurturing home raised the odds of avoiding these adverse outcomes 2- to 4-fold [[Streissguth2004JDBP]].

### pregnancy · breastfeeding
- LactMed (NIH) states that with casual alcohol use (such as one glass of wine or beer) it is best to wait 2 to 2.5 hours per drink before nursing (quoted as published) [[LactMed-Alcohol]].
- Breast milk alcohol levels closely follow blood alcohol levels and peak 30 to 60 minutes after a drink; food delays the peak [[LactMed-Alcohol]].
- Nursing after 1 or 2 drinks can reduce the infant's milk intake by 20–23% and cause agitation and poor sleep; 5 or more drinks can reduce milk let-down [[LactMed-Alcohol]].
- An infant receives about 5–6% of the weight-adjusted maternal dose through milk; newborns metabolise alcohol at about half the adult rate [[Haastrup2014BCPT]].
- The long-term effects of daily heavy drinking while breastfeeding on infant development are unclear [[LactMed-Alcohol,Haastrup2014BCPT]].

### Exam hooks
- Steatosis occurs in ~90% of people who drink heavily and reverses with abstinence; steatohepatitis leads to cirrhosis in 8–20% [[Singal2018ACG]].
- Maddrey DF = 4.6 × (PT − control) + bilirubin (mg/dL); ≥32 = severe AH [[Carithers1989,Louvet2007Lille]].
- MELD inputs: bilirubin, creatinine, INR; MELD >20 flags severe AH [[Kamath2001MELD,Singal2018ACG]].
- Lille ≥0.45 at day 7 of steroids = nonresponse; 6-month survival 25% vs 85% [[Louvet2007Lille]].
- STOPAH: prednisolone 28-day OR 0.72 (P=0.06), no 90-day or 1-year benefit; pentoxifylline no benefit; more infections on prednisolone (13% vs 7%) [[Thursz2015STOPAH]].
- Early transplant for nonresponding severe AH: 6-month survival 77% vs 23% [[Mathurin2011NEJM]].
- Abstinence in alcohol-related cirrhosis: survival difference becomes significant from 1.5 years (HR ~0.5–0.6) [[Xie2014HepatolRes]].
- Alcohol is an IARC Group 1 carcinogen; seven causal sites: mouth, pharynx, larynx, oesophagus, liver, colorectum, female breast [[Baan2007IARC,Bagnardi2015BJC]].
- Breast cancer risk rises ~7% per 10 g/day [[Hamajima2002BJC]].
- ALDH2 deficiency (flushing) plus drinking sharply raises oesophageal SCC risk [[Brooks2009PLoSMed]].
- Wood 2018: lowest all-cause mortality at ≤100 g/week in current drinkers; >350 g/week ≈ 4–5 years shorter life expectancy at 40 [[Wood2018Lancet]].
- "Abstainer bias" (sick quitters) explains much of the J-curve; adjusted RR for low-volume drinkers 0.97 [[Stockwell2016JSAD]].
- Mendelian randomisation (ALDH2/ADH1B) shows no protective U-shape for stroke [[Millwood2019Lancet]].
- AF risk rises ~8% per drink/day; abstinence cut AF recurrence 73% → 53% in an RCT [[Larsson2014JACC,Voskoboinik2020NEJM]].
- Holiday heart = alcohol-triggered arrhythmia, classically AF after binge drinking [[Ettinger1978,Day2019Addiction]].
- AUD was the strongest modifiable risk factor for dementia (HR ~3.3) [[Schwarzinger2018LPH]].
- FAS face: short palpebral fissures, smooth philtrum, thin upper-lip vermilion (≥2 of 3) [[Hoyme2016Pediatrics]].
- Global FASD prevalence 7.7/1,000; US first-graders 1.1–5.0% [[Lange2017JAMAPediatr,May2018JAMA]].
- No known safe amount and no safe time to drink in pregnancy [[Gosdin2022MMWR,Williams2015AAP]].

### In plain words
- Alcohol can hurt the liver in stages: first fat builds up, then the liver can become inflamed, then scarred (cirrhosis) [[Singal2018ACG]].
- Fatty liver from alcohol often clears up quickly once a person stops drinking [[Singal2018ACG]].
- Even people with serious liver damage tend to live longer, as a group, when they stop drinking; in one large study about a third of people with advanced cirrhosis got much better after stopping [[Xie2014HepatolRes,Hofer2026JHep]].
- If you have been drinking heavily every day, do not stop suddenly on your own: get medical help to stop safely, because withdrawal can cause seizures or confusion.
- Alcohol causes at least seven kinds of cancer, including breast and bowel cancer, and the risk starts at low amounts [[Bagnardi2015BJC]].
- The idea that a little alcohol is good for your heart is not proven; newer studies suggest it may come from comparing drinkers with people who stopped because they were already sick [[Stockwell2016JSAD,Millwood2019Lancet]].
- Research does not support drinking alcohol to improve your health [[Zhao2023JAMANetwOpen,GBD2016Alcohol]].
- Alcohol can trigger an irregular heartbeat, and cutting back can lower blood pressure in people who drink a lot [[Marcus2021AnnIntMed,Roerecke2017LPH]].
- Mixing alcohol with opioids, sleeping pills or anxiety medicines like benzodiazepines can slow breathing dangerously; it can cause coma or death [[FDA-OxyContin]].
- If someone cannot be woken, is breathing very slowly or is having a seizure after drinking, call emergency services.
- No amount of alcohol is known to be safe in pregnancy, at any stage; if you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, plan with your care team, and it is never too late to get support [[Gosdin2022MMWR,Williams2015AAP]].
- Children affected by alcohol before birth do better when they are diagnosed early and grow up with steady support [[Streissguth2004JDBP]].
- If you are breastfeeding and have an occasional drink, LactMed says waiting about 2 to 2.5 hours per drink before nursing is best [[LactMed-Alcohol]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Is there any health benefit from light drinking? Position A: observational cohorts show a J-curve for ischaemic heart disease and lower MI risk with higher intake [[GBD2016Alcohol,Wood2018Lancet]]; position B: the benefit disappears after correcting abstainer bias, in younger cohorts and in Mendelian randomisation [[Stockwell2016JSAD,Zhao2017JSAD,Millwood2019Lancet,Holmes2014BMJ]]. Status: unresolved for MI; no evidence supports starting to drink for health.
- What is the lowest-risk level? GBD 2016 put it at zero drinks [[GBD2016Alcohol]]; GBD 2020 found it depends on age, near zero for ages 15–39 and up to 1.87 drinks/day in older people in some regions [[GBD2020Alcohol]]; Wood 2018 found minimum all-cause mortality at ≤100 g/week in current drinkers (a comparison that excludes non-drinkers) [[Wood2018Lancet]]. Status: methods differ; all agree risk rises with intake above low levels.
- Non-linear Mendelian randomisation suggests light drinking adds minimal cardiovascular risk while heavy drinking adds exponentially more [[Biddinger2022JAMANetwOpen]], whereas linear MR in China found a steady rise in stroke risk [[Millwood2019Lancet]]. Status: open.
- Do corticosteroids help in severe AH? A 1989 trial found a large 28-day benefit [[Carithers1989]]; the much larger STOPAH found a non-significant 28-day reduction and no benefit at 90 days or 1 year [[Thursz2015STOPAH]]. Status: guidelines still allow steroids with day-7 Lille reassessment [[Singal2018ACG]].
- Which score should guide AH care? The Maddrey score is time-tested and used in trials [[Singal2018ACG]]; a global study found it the least accurate and favoured MELD [[MoralesArraez2022]].
- Should a 6-month abstinence rule apply before transplant? Early transplant gives large survival gains in highly selected people [[Mathurin2011NEJM,Lee2018Gastro]]; return to drinking after transplant is common (34% any use at 3 years) and sustained use raises mortality [[Lee2018Gastro]]. Status: selection criteria still debated [[Singal2018ACG]].
- Does beverage type matter for cirrhosis? One Danish cohort found mostly-wine drinkers had lower risk than beer/spirit drinkers [[Askgaard2015JHep]], but cancer risks were similar whatever the beverage [[Allen2009JNCI]] and AF risk did not differ by type [[Csengeri2021EHJ]]. Status: likely confounded; not a basis for advice.
- Dementia and abstinence: Whitehall II found higher dementia risk in midlife abstainers as well as in people drinking >14 units/week, partly explained by cardiometabolic disease [[Sabia2018BMJ]]; brain imaging studies show no protection from light drinking [[Topiwala2017BMJ,Daviet2022NatCommun]]. Status: abstainer groups are heterogeneous.
- Low-level prenatal exposure: ABCD found subtle effects at any reported exposure [[Lees2020AJP]], but these data are retrospective parental reports; guidance remains no alcohol in pregnancy [[Williams2015AAP,Gosdin2022MMWR]].
- Breastfeeding: a 2014 review judged that occasional drinking has not been convincingly shown to harm nursing infants and that no special recommendations are needed [[Haastrup2014BCPT]]; LactMed advises waiting 2–2.5 hours per drink and notes reduced milk intake after 1–2 drinks [[LactMed-Alcohol]]. Status: both agree heavy use is a concern.
- Acute pancreatitis in women: intake below 40 g/day was associated with lower risk than abstention (RR 0.76) [[Samokhvalov2015EBioMed]]; likely affected by the same abstainer biases as other J-curves (Hypothesis).
