Not medical adviceThe AUD Companion is an educational synthesis of published literature for study and understanding. It does not replace a doctor, nurse, pharmacist, counsellor or other clinician. The AUDIT, the criteria counter, CIWA-Ar and the other calculators are teaching aids, not self-tests, and doses, benzodiazepine and thiamine regimens and CIWA-Ar thresholds are quoted as published, never as instructions. Stopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous: get medical help to stop safely. If you or someone else may have alcohol poisoning or severe withdrawal (seizure, confusion, hallucinations), call emergency services. For help with drinking in the US, SAMHSA's National Helpline is free and confidential: 1-800-662-4357. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).

Part 1 · Understanding AUD

Alcohol and the brain: reward, adaptation, craving

Alcohol tips the brain's balance between calming GABA and exciting glutamate. With months of heavy drinking the brain pushes back, and when the alcohol suddenly goes, the push-back is left unopposed. Scrub through one synapse, then around the addiction cycle.

Before drinking

A brain in balance

Two signals keep neurons in check. GABA acting on GABA-A receptors opens a chloride channel and calms the cell (blue). Glutamate acting on NMDA receptors lets in positive ions and excites it (red). Ethanol acts on the brain from the low millimolar range up to about 100 mM: early stimulant effects are followed by depressant ones. 1

In plain wordsThe brain has a brake (GABA) and an accelerator (glutamate). Normally they balance.
Exam hookGABA-A = ligand-gated Cl− channel (inhibitory). NMDA = glutamate-gated cation channel (excitatory).
Stopping safelyStopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous: the hyperexcitable state can bring seizures and delirium tremens. 467 Get medical help to stop safely. Binge / intoxication

The rewarding effects of alcohol, incentive salience (alcohol cues gaining pull) and drug-seeking habits involve dopamine and opioid peptides in the basal ganglia. 256 Early: Early on, drinking is driven mainly by the reward itself: positive reinforcement.

In plain wordsDrinking feels rewarding, and the brain learns to notice alcohol and the places, people and moods linked to it.

How medicines aim at these circuits

Approved in the US

Naltrexone: blocking the opioid "reward"

Drinking releases the brain's own opioids in the nucleus accumbens and orbitofrontal cortex; in heavy drinkers the orbitofrontal change tracked the subjective "high", which suggests a mechanism for opioid antagonists. Early evidence 333 Naltrexone is a pure opioid receptor antagonist; the label says its mechanism in AUD "is not understood". 117

Approved medicines →
Approved in the US

Acamprosate: the glutamate side

The label says chronic alcohol is thought to upset the balance between excitation and inhibition, and animal studies suggest acamprosate acts on glutamate and GABA systems: the Hypothesis is that it restores the balance. 113 Reviews describe it as modulating glutamate transmission. 261

Not approved for AUD in the US

Topiramate and baclofen

Topiramate is thought to reduce alcohol's reinforcing effects by boosting GABA and inhibiting glutamate pathways. Hypothesis 206 Baclofen is a GABA-B receptor agonist approved in the US for spasticity. 261

Other medicines →
Early evidence

The stress system: CRF

In animals, a history of dependence leaves CRF1 receptors in the amygdala up-regulated for the long term, and CRF antagonists block excessive drinking and stress-induced relapse-like seeking. This is not established in humans. 172,254

Research frontier →

More of what imaging shows

Established

Dopamine release, then fewer D2 receptors

On PET, alcohol released dopamine in the human ventral striatum and nucleus accumbens, the first such human report. 43 In 10 people with alcohol dependence, striatal D2 receptor availability was lower than in 17 controls (2.1 against 2.7) and did not correlate with days since the last drink. 481

Hypothesis

From choice to habit

Everitt and Robbins propose a shift from voluntary use to habit and compulsion: from prefrontal to striatal control, and from ventral to dorsal striatum, with animal support. 106 In humans, heavy drinkers showed more dorsal-striatum cue activation, which tracked obsessive-compulsive craving. Early evidence 482

Established

Some brain changes recover

MRI shows white-matter loss and damage to some grey-matter structures in AUD; some changes are permanent and some reverse with abstinence. 512 In 58 men scanned repeatedly, fluid spaces shrank between about day 12 and day 32 of abstinence, and white matter fell further in those who went back to drinking. 361

Established

Even moderate amounts show up

In 36,678 UK Biobank adults, going from one to two daily units was "equivalent to the effect of aging 2 years" on brain volume. 77 In Whitehall II, more than 30 units a week carried an odds ratio of 5.8 for hippocampal atrophy, and light drinking was not protective. 464

These are brain changes, not a lack of willpower: over time, drinking can shift from seeking a good feeling to escaping a bad one. 254,256 Alcohol use disorder is a treatable medical condition, and many people recover: about 9.1% of US adults report having resolved an alcohol or drug problem (a population figure, not a promise for any one person). 230

AUD Companion is an educational synthesis of the published literature on alcohol use disorder and alcohol withdrawal, made for health-science, medical and nursing courses and for people with alcohol use disorder, their families and friends.

It is not medical advice. It cannot diagnose, and nothing here should start, stop or change a medicine or a plan for stopping drinking without your own care team. The AUDIT, the criteria counter, CIWA-Ar, PAWSS and the blood alcohol and liver calculators are for learning, not self-diagnosis or reading your own results, and the blood alcohol calculator can never tell anyone whether it is safe to drive. Doses and regimens are quoted as published in labels and guidelines, never as instructions. Alcohol poisoning and severe withdrawal are emergencies.
