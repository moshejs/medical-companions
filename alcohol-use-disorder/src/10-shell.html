Alcohol use disorder, explained

Alcohol use disorder is a medical condition defined by heavy drinking that a person finds hard to control, kept up despite harm: it is treatable, and it is not a moral failing. [[Carvalho2019Lancet]] About 3 in 10 US adults meet its criteria at some point in life and about 1 in 7 in a given year, yet fewer than 1 in 5 ever get treatment or help. [[Grant2015NESARC,Mekonen2021]] Recovery is common: about 9.1% of US adults say they once had an alcohol or drug problem and no longer do, a group figure, not a promise. [[Kelly2017Recovery]]

If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).

Alcohol use disorder at a glance

What it is
Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a treatable medical condition, not a moral failing. A Lancet review defines it by compulsive heavy drinking and loss of control over intake. [[Carvalho2019Lancet]]
Who gets it
In a large US survey, about 1 in 7 adults (13.9%) had AUD in the past year: 17.6% of men and 10.4% of women. [[Grant2015NESARC]] It was most common at ages 18 to 29. [[Grant2015NESARC]] Worldwide, about 100 million people had AUD in 2016, more than any other substance use disorder. [[GBD2016AlcDrug]]
Main symptoms
The core features are losing control over drinking, drinking becoming a growing priority in life, and body changes such as tolerance and withdrawal. [[Degenhardt2019ICD11]] Stopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous, with seizures or delirium tremens. [[ASAM2020Withdrawal,Mirijello2015]]
How it is diagnosed
AUD is diagnosed when at least 2 of 11 criteria occur within 12 months. [[APA2022]] Severity counts the criteria met: mild 2 to 3, moderate 4 to 5, severe 6 or more. [[Hasin2013]]
Main treatments
The US FDA has approved four medicines for AUD: oral naltrexone, injectable naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram. [[KranzlerSoyka2018]] Talking therapies such as CBT beat minimal treatment and work best combined with medicine. [[Magill2019,Ray2020CBT,Irvin1999]] Yet few people get help: only 7.7% of US adults with AUD in the past year had ever sought treatment. [[Grant2015NESARC]]
Outlook
Many people recover: about 9.1% of US adults report having resolved an alcohol or drug problem. [[Kelly2017Recovery]] Some brain changes reverse with abstinence. [[Zahr2017]] Without that change the toll is heavy: in Nordic registers, people hospitalised with AUD lived 24 to 28 years less than the general population. [[Westman2015]]
