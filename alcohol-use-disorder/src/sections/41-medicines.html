Approved medicines: naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram

Alcohol use disorder is a treatable medical condition, and the US FDA has approved four medicines for it: oral naltrexone, injectable naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram. Each works in a different way. Watch how, see how much each helps in the trials, and read what the labels say. [[KranzlerSoyka2018]]

Read this firstEvery dose on this page is quoted as published in the named US label or trial. None of it is an instruction: a clinician starts and adjusts these medicines as part of a care plan. If a person drinks heavily every day, stopping suddenly can be dangerous, so get medical help to stop safely. Mixing alcohol with opioids, benzodiazepines or other sedatives is dangerous. How much do they help? Numbers needed to treat

The number needed to treat (NNT) is how many people need to take a medicine, rather than a placebo, for one more person to avoid an outcome. The two big US evidence reviews pooled outpatient trials of acamprosate and oral naltrexone. Pick a review and an outcome. These are averages across trial populations, not a forecast for any one person. [[Jonas2014JAMA,McPheeters2023JAMA]]

Which medicine might a clinician consider when…

The American Psychiatric Association's 2018 guideline, as quoted in reviews, recommends that naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram be offered to patients with moderate to severe AUD. The 2023 US evidence review concluded that, with psychosocial support, oral naltrexone 50 mg/day and acamprosate are supported as first-line medicines. Jonas 2014 suggested choosing between them by dosing frequency, possible adverse events and availability. The table quotes the labels and guidelines; it is not a guide to choosing your own treatment. COMBINE: what happened when the medicines met talking therapy

Percent days abstinent during treatment, as published. MM = medical management (meetings with a health professional); CBI = combined behavioural intervention. [[Anton2006COMBINE]]

COMBINE · JAMA 2006

1,383 people, 11 US sites, 16 weeks

Recently abstinent people with alcohol dependence received naltrexone 100 mg/day, acamprosate 3 g/day, both or placebos, all with medical management, with or without CBI. [[Anton2006COMBINE]]

  • Naltrexone reduced the risk of a heavy drinking day (HR 0.72, 97.5% CI 0.53–0.98), most clearly with medical management alone.
  • Acamprosate showed no significant effect, alone or combined.
  • No combination beat naltrexone or CBI alone when medical management was given.
  • Placebo pills plus meetings with a health professional helped beyond CBI alone, which gave the lowest result (66.6).
  • One year later the differences were similar but no longer significant. [[Anton2006COMBINE]]

Why acamprosate worked in European trials but not in COMBINE or the first US trial is unresolved; prior inpatient detoxification and the person's own goal of abstinence are among the explanations. [[Mann2004Acamprosate,Mason2006Acamprosate,Maisel2013]]

The trials and the side effects

Oral naltrexone

Less heavy drinking

First trial (70 men, 12 weeks): 23% on naltrexone 50 mg/day met relapse criteria vs 54.3% on placebo. A Cochrane review of 50 trials (7,793 people) found the risk of heavy drinking fell to 83% of the placebo risk; the effect on any drinking just missed significance (RR 0.96, 0.92–1.00). [[Volpicelli1992,Rosner2010Naltrexone]]

Side effects were mainly nausea and daytime sleepiness. One extra person stopped a trial because of side effects for every 48 treated. [[Rosner2010Naltrexone,Jonas2014JAMA]]

Injectable naltrexone

A monthly injection

In the pivotal 6-month trial (624 actively drinking adults), 380 mg gave a 25% fall in the rate of heavy drinking days vs placebo. Effects were larger in men and in people abstinent during the lead-in. [[Garbutt2005JAMA]]

Label: nausea 33% vs 11%; any injection-site reaction 69% vs 50%. Some reactions were severe (abscess or necrosis, a few needing surgery), so a worsening lump or skin change should be checked. Opioid blockade lasts about 28 days. [[FDA-Vivitrol]]

Acamprosate

Staying stopped

Mann 2004 (17 trials, 4,087 people): continuous abstinence at 6 months 36.1% vs 23.4% on placebo. Cochrane (24 trials): lower risk of any drinking (RR 0.86) and about 11 more abstinent days. [[Mann2004Acamprosate,Rosner2010Acamprosate]]

Diarrhoea is the side effect that stands out (24.9% vs 13.9%). The first US multicentre trial was negative in its planned analysis. [[KranzlerSoyka2018,Mason2006Acamprosate]]

Disulfiram

A deterrent that needs consent

A meta-analysis of 22 trials found benefit only in open-label trials (g = 0.70); blinded trials showed no efficacy. Supervised ingestion did clearly better (g = 0.82) than unsupervised (g = 0.26). The VA trial of 605 men found no gain in total abstinence. [[Skinner2014Disulfiram,KranzlerSoyka2018,Fuller1986VA]]

Supporters argue that blinding cancels the deterrent that is the point of the medicine. The label's old supervised test reaction "has been largely abandoned". [[FDA-Antabuse]]

Combining

Naltrexone with disulfiram?

The naltrexone label calls this combination of two potentially liver-toxic medicines of unknown safety and efficacy, "not ordinarily recommended unless the probable benefits outweigh the known risks". [[FDA-Naltrexone]]

Gene tests to pick a medicine are not supported by current evidence. [[KranzlerSoyka2018,Fairbanks2020Mayo]]

Underused: few people are offered them

Schematic of a published proportion. [[KranzlerSoyka2018,Fairbanks2020Mayo]]

AUD medicines are prescribed to fewer than 9% of the people likely to benefit, and people with AUD most often receive counselling alone. [[KranzlerSoyka2018]] US retail prescriptions rose from 393,000 in 2003 to an estimated 720,000 in 2007, while buprenorphine for opioid use disorder reached 1.9 million in 2007. [[Mark2009]]

A Lancet seminar links undertreatment partly to stigma and partly to too little systematic screening in primary care. People with alcohol-associated liver disease are rarely treated for their AUD. [[Carvalho2019Lancet,Jophlin2024ACG]]

In plain wordsVery few people with AUD are offered these medicines. It is fine to ask a doctor or nurse about them. [[KranzlerSoyka2018]]

More options: other medicines, talking therapies, the liver, managing withdrawal safely.
