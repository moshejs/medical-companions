Alcohol and the brain: reward, adaptation, craving

Alcohol tips the brain's balance between calming GABA and exciting glutamate. With months of heavy drinking the brain pushes back, and when the alcohol suddenly goes, the push-back is left unopposed. Scrub through one synapse, then around the addiction cycle.

Schematic. Receptor numbers, dots and the beam's angle show direction of change, not measured amounts. The directions come from reviews of animal and human studies: GABA-A α1 internalised and reduced, α4 increased, NMDA receptors up-regulated with tolerance. [[Kumar2009,TsaiCoyle1998]]

Stopping safelyStopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous: the hyperexcitable state can bring seizures and delirium tremens. [[TsaiCoyle1998]] Get medical help to stop safely. See Alcohol withdrawal: the timeline. If someone has a seizure, confusion or hallucinations, call emergency services.

How medicines aim at these circuits

Approved in the US

Naltrexone: blocking the opioid "reward"

Drinking releases the brain's own opioids in the nucleus accumbens and orbitofrontal cortex; in heavy drinkers the orbitofrontal change tracked the subjective "high", which suggests a mechanism for opioid antagonists. Early evidence [[Mitchell2012]] Naltrexone is a pure opioid receptor antagonist; the label says its mechanism in AUD "is not understood". [[FDA-Naltrexone]]

Approved medicines →
Approved in the US

Acamprosate: the glutamate side

The label says chronic alcohol is thought to upset the balance between excitation and inhibition, and animal studies suggest acamprosate acts on glutamate and GABA systems: the Hypothesis is that it restores the balance. [[FDA-Campral]] Reviews describe it as modulating glutamate transmission. [[KranzlerSoyka2018]]

Approved medicines →
Not approved for AUD in the US

Topiramate and baclofen

Topiramate is thought to reduce alcohol's reinforcing effects by boosting GABA and inhibiting glutamate pathways. Hypothesis [[Johnson2007Topiramate]] Baclofen is a GABA-B receptor agonist approved in the US for spasticity. [[KranzlerSoyka2018]]

Other medicines →
Early evidence

The stress system: CRF

In animals, a history of dependence leaves CRF1 receptors in the amygdala up-regulated for the long term, and CRF antagonists block excessive drinking and stress-induced relapse-like seeking. This is not established in humans. [[HeiligKoob2007,Koob2014]]

Research frontier →

More of what imaging shows

Established

Dopamine release, then fewer D2 receptors

On PET, alcohol released dopamine in the human ventral striatum and nucleus accumbens, the first such human report. [[Boileau2003]] In 10 people with alcohol dependence, striatal D2 receptor availability was lower than in 17 controls (2.1 against 2.7) and did not correlate with days since the last drink. [[Volkow1996]]

Hypothesis

From choice to habit

Everitt and Robbins propose a shift from voluntary use to habit and compulsion: from prefrontal to striatal control, and from ventral to dorsal striatum, with animal support. [[EverittRobbins2005]] In humans, heavy drinkers showed more dorsal-striatum cue activation, which tracked obsessive-compulsive craving. Early evidence [[VollstadtKlein2010]]

Established

Some brain changes recover

MRI shows white-matter loss and damage to some grey-matter structures in AUD; some changes are permanent and some reverse with abstinence. [[Zahr2017]] In 58 men scanned repeatedly, fluid spaces shrank between about day 12 and day 32 of abstinence, and white matter fell further in those who went back to drinking. [[Pfefferbaum1995]]

Established

Even moderate amounts show up

In 36,678 UK Biobank adults, going from one to two daily units was "equivalent to the effect of aging 2 years" on brain volume. [[Daviet2022]] In Whitehall II, more than 30 units a week carried an odds ratio of 5.8 for hippocampal atrophy, and light drinking was not protective. [[Topiwala2017]]

These are brain changes, not a lack of willpower: over time, drinking can shift from seeking a good feeling to escaping a bad one. [[KoobVolkow2016,Koob2014]] Alcohol use disorder is a treatable medical condition, and many people recover: about 9.1% of US adults report having resolved an alcohol or drug problem (a population figure, not a promise for any one person).
