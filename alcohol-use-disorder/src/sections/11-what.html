Part 1 · Understanding AUD

What alcohol use disorder is

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a medical condition defined by heavy alcohol use that a person finds hard to control, kept up despite harm. It is common, it is treatable, and most people who have it never receive treatment. Press Play to fill 100 US adults from the national survey. [[Carvalho2019Lancet,Grant2015NESARC]]

Each figure is 1 in 100 US adults, rounded to whole people; the exact survey percentages are in the panel. NESARC-III, 36,309 adults interviewed face to face, 2012–2013, DSM-5 criteria. Population statistics, not a prediction for anyone. [[Grant2015NESARC,Grant2017JAMAPsych]]

Who is affected

AUD can begin or continue at any age and in any group, but the survey numbers are not even. Pick a view. Group figures describe populations, never a single person. [[Grant2015NESARC]]

How much harm alcohol causes

Burden studies count the harm from all alcohol use, not only from the disorder. Different modelling groups give different totals for similar years: always read a figure with its source. [[GBD2016Alcohol,Shield2020,Shield2025]]

Established

The most common substance use disorder

GBD 2016 counted 100.4 million people with AUD worldwide, more than any other substance use disorder, and attributed 99.2 million DALYs (4.2% of all DALYs) to alcohol use. [[GBD2016AlcDrug]]

Established

The leading risk factor at ages 15–49

Globally alcohol was the 7th leading risk factor for deaths and DALYs in 2016, and the leading one at ages 15–49, causing 12.2% of male and 3.8% of female deaths in that age group. [[GBD2016Alcohol]]

Not for health

No level was best for health

GBD 2016 found the level of drinking that minimised harm across health outcomes was zero standard drinks a week. GBD 2020 put the minimum-risk level at 0–0.6 drinks a day for people aged 15–39. The research does not support drinking for health, and neither study supports starting to drink. [[GBD2016Alcohol,GBD2020Alcohol]]

Trend

More drinking, fewer deaths per drinker?

Adult consumption worldwide rose from 5.9 L of pure alcohol a year in 1990 to 6.5 L in 2017 [[Manthey2019Lancet]]. A WHO-funded model found alcohol-attributable deaths fell 31.0% from 2000 to 2019, with the burden falling hardest on Eastern Europe, Central and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, and young people. [[Shield2025]]

A medical condition, and the words we use

Medical, not moral

AUD is a treatable medical condition, not a moral failing

A Lancet review defines AUD by compulsive heavy alcohol use and loss of control over intake, and notes that effective, cost-effective psychosocial and drug treatments exist [[Carvalho2019Lancet]]. Drug dependence has been compared with other chronic illnesses such as diabetes, hypertension and asthma, which have similar adherence and relapse rates and benefit from long-term care. [[McLellan2000]]

Recovery is common at population level: in a US probability survey, 9.1% of adults said they used to have an alcohol or drug problem and no longer do. That is a group figure, not a promise for any one person. [[Kelly2017Recovery]]

Person-first language

Why this guide says "a person with AUD"

In a randomised study of 516 mental health clinicians, a vignette calling someone "a substance abuser" made readers more likely to judge the person personally culpable and to favour punishment than "having a substance use disorder" [[Kelly2010Language]]. In 1,288 people, "alcoholic", "addict" and "substance abuser" carried strongly negative associations. [[Ashford2018]]

  • alcoholica person with alcohol use disorder
  • substance abuserhas a substance use disorder
  • addicta person in recovery

"Alcoholism" is the older, lay name. "Alcohol abuse" appears here only as the historical DSM-IV diagnosis: see the history module.

Established

Stigma is higher for AUD

Population studies show people with alcohol dependence are held more responsible for their condition than people with other mental disorders, and face more social rejection and structural discrimination [[Schomerus2011]]. A 2010–2020 update of 16 studies found the wish for social distance stayed consistently higher for AUD. [[Kilian2021]]

Established

Why people do not seek treatment

Of 1,008 European primary-care patients with AUD, 810 had no treatment. Of those who gave a reason, 55.3% named lack of problem awareness, 28.6% stigma or shame, 22.8% barriers to care and 20.9% wanting to cope alone [[Probst2015]]. In NESARC, people with AUD who perceived more stigma were less likely to use alcohol services (OR 0.37). [[Keyes2010]]

Established

Medicines are rarely used

In NSDUH 2019 only 10.9% of adults in alcohol treatment received a medicine for AUD, against 40.9% of adults in opioid treatment who received a medicine for opioid use disorder [[Bensley2025]]. In a US retail study, fewer than 9% of patients who needed AUD treatment received a single prescription for an approved medicine. [[KranzlerSoyka2018]] See Approved medicines.

Conditions that often come with AUD

AUD often travels with other conditions, so a good assessment looks at both. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) for 12-month AUD in NESARC-III; a value above 1 means the two occur together more often than chance. [[Grant2015NESARC]]

Any drug use disorder AOR 3.3 Severe AUD with any drug use disorder AOR 5.3 Nicotine use disorder AOR 2.5 Borderline personality disorder AOR 1.9 Antisocial personality disorder AOR 1.6 Bipolar I AOR 1.4 Major depression AOR 1.2 (1.4 in severe AUD) PTSD, generalised anxiety AOR 1.0, n.s.

Depression and anxiety

Pooling 22 population surveys (1990–2014), AUD was associated with major depression (OR 2.42) and any anxiety disorder (OR 2.11), more strongly for dependence than for abuse. These pooled odds ratios are higher than NESARC-III's because most surveys adjusted less. [[Lai2015,Grant2015NESARC]]

Other conditions

AUD was the most common substance use disorder in clinical samples of people with bipolar disorder, at 42% [[Hunt2016]]. In treatment populations for substance use disorders, 23.1% met criteria for ADHD [[vanEmmerikvanOortmerssen2012]]. In US veterans with lifetime PTSD, the adjusted OR for AUD was 2.1. [[Smith2016PTSD]]

AUD and suicide

In longitudinal studies, AUD was associated with higher suicide mortality (pooled OR 2.68 in males, 2.39 in females); the authors conclude that treating AUD belongs in suicide prevention [[Lange2024]]. In Nordic registers, people hospitalised with AUD died by suicide at 9.3–35.9 times the general-population rate. [[Westman2015]]

Need help now?If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).

SafetyStopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous (seizures, delirium tremens): get medical help to stop safely. [[ASAM2020Withdrawal,Mirijello2015]] Alcohol poisoning is an emergency: call emergency services. Mixing alcohol with opioids, benzodiazepines or other sedatives is dangerous: together they can slow or stop breathing. [[FDA-Valium,ASAM2020Withdrawal]] See Alcohol withdrawal.
