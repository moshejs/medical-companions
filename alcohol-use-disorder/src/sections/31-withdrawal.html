Alcohol withdrawal: the timeline

What happens in the hours and days after the last drink in a person whose brain has adapted to heavy, regular drinking: early tremor and sweating, hallucinosis, seizures and, later, delirium tremens. Scrub through 120 hours, then see why repeated withdrawals matter and how the PAWSS flags people at higher risk.

SafetyStopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can be dangerous: it can cause seizures or delirium tremens. Get medical help to stop safely. Withdrawal can be managed safely, often at home or in a clinic with daily check-ins, and sometimes in hospital. Drinking alcohol is not a treatment for withdrawal. What counts as withdrawal

Withdrawal happens in people who are physically dependent on alcohol when they stop or cut down: alcohol's depressant effect on the brain is suddenly reversed. Its most severe results are seizure, delirium and death. The older word "detoxification" has been replaced by "withdrawal management". [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]] For the brain side of this, see Alcohol and the brain.

DSM-5, as reproduced in ASAM 2020, requires stopping or cutting down after heavy and prolonged use, then two or more of these eight signs within several hours to a few days: [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]

  • Autonomic hyperactivity (for example sweating or pulse >100 bpm)
  • Increased hand tremor
  • Insomnia
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Transient visual, tactile or auditory hallucinations or illusions
  • Psychomotor agitation
  • Anxiety
  • Generalised tonic-clonic seizures
How common

Symptoms are common; severe withdrawal is not

Up to 50% of people with alcohol use disorder have withdrawal symptoms, and only a minority need medical treatment. [[Mirijello2015]] ASAM 2020 estimates that 2–7% of hospital patients with heavy alcohol use develop moderate to severe withdrawal. [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]

Complicated withdrawal

Seizures or delirium tremens

Complicated withdrawal occurs in up to 15% of people with alcohol use disorder (Jesse 2017) and "in up to 20% of cases" among medically ill inpatients (Maldonado 2015). In a family study of 1648 alcohol-dependent adults, 12.8% reported at least one lifetime episode of delirium tremens and/or withdrawal convulsions. [[Jesse2017,Maldonado2015,Schuckit1995]]

A first step

Withdrawal care is a door, not the treatment

ASAM 2020 says withdrawal management alone "is not an effective treatment for alcohol use disorder": it should be a way into treatment. Getting through withdrawal is a first step; ongoing care is what helps recovery last. [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]] See Managing withdrawal safely and Approved medicines.

Who is at higher risk of complicated withdrawal

ASAM 2020

Risk factors

  • Past withdrawal delirium or withdrawal seizure
  • Numerous prior withdrawal episodes
  • Medical or surgical illness, especially traumatic brain injury
  • Age over 65
  • Long duration of heavy, regular drinking
  • A seizure in the current episode
  • Marked autonomic hyperactivity
  • Physiological dependence on benzodiazepines or barbiturates

Other substance use, a positive blood alcohol level alongside withdrawal signs, and an active co-occurring psychiatric disorder may add risk. Several factors together raise it further. [[ASAM2020Withdrawal]]

What the studies found

Predictors, with numbers

  • A past similar event is the strongest predictor: OR 2.58 for DT and 2.8 for seizures; lower platelets and lower potassium predicted DT; sex and liver disease did not (meta-analysis of 15 studies). [[Goodson2014]]
  • Structural brain lesions predicted DT at admission (OR 5.8) and seizures (OR 6.5) in 827 inpatients. [[Eyer2011]]
  • A history of DT (LR 2.9) and systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg (LR 1.7) raised the likelihood of severe withdrawal; no single sign ruled it out. [[Wood2018JAMA]]
  • In 200 inpatients, DT developed in 9% with neither more days since the last drink nor an acute medical illness, 25% with one and 54% with both. [[Ferguson1996]]

PAWSS: a teaching checklist

The Prediction of Alcohol Withdrawal Severity Scale (PAWSS) has 10 items drawn from a systematic review of risk factors for complicated withdrawal. It predicts risk before symptoms appear, whereas the CIWA-Ar measures how severe withdrawal is once it has started. [[Maldonado2014,ASAM2020Withdrawal]]

Teaching toolThis checklist shows how the published scale is built. It is not a self-test, it is not for reading your own or anyone else's risk, and it cannot tell anyone whether it is safe to stop drinking without help. Anyone who drinks heavily every day and wants to stop should get medical help to stop safely.

Get helpIf you or someone else may have alcohol poisoning or severe withdrawal (seizure, confusion, hallucinations), call emergency services. For help with drinking in the US, SAMHSA's National Helpline is free and confidential: 1-800-662-4357. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).

Do not mix alcohol with sleeping pills, benzodiazepines, opioids or other sedatives: together they can slow or stop breathing. [[FDA-Valium,ASAM2020Withdrawal]] Next: how nurses and doctors score withdrawal with the CIWA-Ar, and why thiamine matters in Wernicke encephalopathy.
