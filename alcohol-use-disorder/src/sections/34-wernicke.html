Part 3 · Withdrawal & acute care

Thiamine, Wernicke and Korsakoff

Heavy drinking can use up vitamin B1 (thiamine). Very low thiamine can cause Wernicke encephalopathy, an acute brain emergency that is often missed, and untreated it can leave lasting memory loss: Korsakoff syndrome. Thiamine is cheap and is given early to protect the brain. [[Galvin2010EFNS,Sechi2007,Kopelman2009]]

The Caine criteria: a teaching checklist

The classic triad of confusion, unsteadiness (ataxia) and eye-movement problems was present in only 16% of 131 people found at autopsy to have had Wernicke encephalopathy. Caine and colleagues proposed a looser rule for people who drink heavily: any 2 of 4 signs. The EFNS 2010 guideline adopted it. Tick the signs in an imaginary case to see how the two rules differ. This is a teaching tool, not a way to diagnose anyone. [[Harper1986JNNP,Caine1997,Galvin2010EFNS]]

Glucose, magnesium and refeeding

Guidelines differ

Thiamine before glucose?

Older teaching, kept by EFNS 2005 and 2010, is to give thiamine before any carbohydrate. ASAM 2020 allows "any order or concurrently", so that glucose is not delayed. A review of 19 papers found nothing above case-report level: prolonged glucose without thiamine may be a risk factor, but low blood sugar should be treated at once, with thiamine promptly after or alongside. [[Galvin2010EFNS,Brathen2005EFNS,ASAM2020Withdrawal,Schabelman2012]]

Early evidence

Dextrose first, in practice

In US veterans' emergency departments (2010–2019), 120 encounters in which people with alcohol intoxication got dextrose before thiamine were followed for 90 days: no case of Wernicke encephalopathy was found. One observational series cannot prove safety. [[Jasti2025]]

Hypothesis

Magnesium and thiamine

In 105 heavy drinkers in Alice Springs, 48% of those tested had low magnesium (below 0.80 mmol/L), and serum magnesium, but not thiamine pyrophosphate, tracked cognitive scores. The authors suggest low magnesium may impair the body's use of thiamine. ASAM gives magnesium for low levels, arrhythmias, electrolyte disturbances or past withdrawal seizures. [[Dingwall2015Mg,ASAM2020Withdrawal]]

Refeeding

Feeding again after poor nutrition

A 2025 systematic review of 30 studies found past alcohol misuse among the risk factors for refeeding syndrome reported by two or more studies. In a small detox-unit cohort (36 people), none developed refeeding syndrome by day 3, although 44% had low magnesium. ASAM supplements phosphorus below 1 mg/dL and corrects moderate lows through nutrition. [[Zheng2025,Manning2014,ASAM2020Withdrawal]]

Established

Missed in life

Autopsy series keep finding Wernicke encephalopathy that was never diagnosed: 1.7% of necropsies in Perth (7 of 51 diagnosed in life), 0.8% in Oslo, and 1.1% in Sydney in 1996–97, where prevalence had fallen since bread flour was enriched with thiamine. [[Harper1979JNNP,Torvik1982,Harper1998MJA]]

Established

Scans help, but cannot rule it out

In 15 people with acute Wernicke encephalopathy, MRI had a sensitivity of 53% and specificity of 93%; CT "proved not useful". A normal MRI does not exclude it. EFNS advises MRI to support the diagnosis, and measuring blood thiamine just before giving it. [[Antunez1998,Galvin2010EFNS]]

Korsakoff syndrome is preventable, and people can still learn. It is a memory disorder that usually follows an episode of Wernicke encephalopathy. Episodic memory is badly affected while skills learned without conscious recall are preserved, and people can learn new routines in a calm, structured, cued setting, helped by agendas, memory cards, smartphones and smartwatches. People with Korsakoff syndrome "are reported to have a normal life expectancy if they remain abstinent". [[Kopelman2009,Arts2017]] For families and carers, see Families, friends and everyday life.

Get helpIf you or someone else may have alcohol poisoning or severe withdrawal (seizure, confusion, hallucinations), call emergency services. For help with drinking in the US, SAMHSA's National Helpline is free and confidential: 1-800-662-4357. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).
