Families, friends and everyday life

Alcohol use disorder touches everyone close to a person. Families are never to blame for someone else's drinking, and they can get help for themselves and learn ways that make it more likely a loved one starts treatment.

Schematic. One illustrative family. Bars show group results from the studies named, not what will happen in any family. [[Dube2001,Miller1999,Powers2008]]

What to say: words that reduce blame

In a randomised study of 516 mental health clinicians, people who read about "a substance abuser" were more likely to see the person as personally to blame and to favour punishment than people who read about someone "having a substance use disorder". In 1,288 people, "substance abuser", "addict" and "alcoholic" carried strongly negative associations, while "long-term recovery" and "medication-assisted recovery" carried positive ones. "Alcohol abuse" survives only as the historical DSM-IV term. Stigma matters: people with lifetime AUD who perceived more stigma were less likely to use alcohol services (OR 0.37), and public stigma toward AUD remains comparatively high. [[Keyes2010,Kilian2021,Schomerus2011]]

Everyday life: practical points

If you or someone else may have alcohol poisoning or severe withdrawal (seizure, confusion, hallucinations), call emergency services. For help with drinking in the US, SAMHSA's National Helpline is free and confidential: 1-800-662-4357. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, contact a crisis line (in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123).
Safety plan

Emergencies at home

  • Alcohol poisoning is an emergency: call emergency services. In the US an average of 2,221 people aged 15 or older died of alcohol poisoning each year in 2010–2012, about six a day. [[Kanny2015]]
  • Mixing alcohol with opioids, benzodiazepines or other sedatives is dangerous: the labels warn of "profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death". Alcohol was involved in 14.7% of US opioid overdose deaths in 2017. [[FDA-OxyContin,FDA-Xanax,Tori2020]]
  • Stopping suddenly after long, heavy daily drinking can cause seizures or delirium tremens: get medical help to stop safely. See withdrawal.
Driving

No calculator can say it is safe to drive

Even at a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05, virtually all drivers are impaired on at least some driving measures. Lowering the legal limit to 0.05 or lower went with an 11.1% fall in fatal alcohol-related crashes, and ignition interlocks cut re-arrests while fitted. ASAM advises people treated with benzodiazepines for withdrawal not to drive for the first few days. [[Fell2014BAC,Fell2017,Elder2011,ASAM2020Withdrawal]]

Social life

Holidays and pressure to drink

Who is around matters. In Network Support II, treatment that built a network with more non-drinkers gave more days abstinent than packaged CBT, and AA works partly by reducing pro-drinking ties. "Holiday heart", named in 1978, is a heart rhythm disorder after a bout of heavy drinking. [[Litt2016,Kelly2011AANetwork,Ettinger1978]]

Work

Testing and trace exposure

Some workplaces test urine for ethyl glucuronide (EtG). Incidental exposure to 1 g or 3 g of ethanol produced EtG values many workplace laboratories would call positive, so a single result needs context. [[Thierauf2009TraceEtG]] See blood and urine markers.

For families

Al-Anon and Alateen

Al-Anon is the most widely used help for people concerned about someone else's drinking; in a national survey, members were more satisfied with their quality of life than newcomers. Al-Anon also runs Alateen for younger family members; our research pass found no outcome studies of Alateen. [[Timko2013]]

Children

Support the whole family

About 7.5 million US children (10.5%) lived with a parent with past-year AUD in 2009–2014. Their stress is real and support helps; adult and paediatric care work best joined up. For pregnancy, plan with your care team: see pregnancy and FASD. [[Lipari2017,Dube2001]]

Suicide risk

Ask, and know where to turn

AUD is associated with suicide attempts (OR 3.13) and death by suicide (OR 2.59) in population studies. If you are worried, ask directly and help the person contact a crisis line: in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland Samaritans 116 123. [[Darvishi2015]]
