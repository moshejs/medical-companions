Lapse or relapse? The road back

A lapse is a single return to drinking; a relapse is a return to the old pattern. Relapse-prevention therapy treats the moment after a lapse as a turning point: the abstinence violation effect pulls one way, a plan and a reframe pull the other. People also respond better to the phrase "recurrence of use" than to "relapse". [[Larimer1999,Ashford2018]]

Schematic of the Marlatt relapse-prevention model and its later dynamic version; distances are not to scale. [[Larimer1999,Witkiewitz2004RP]]

Stopping safely. If someone has been drinking heavily every day for a long time, stopping suddenly can be dangerous (seizures, delirium tremens). Get medical help to stop safely, including after a return to heavy drinking. Alcohol poisoning is an emergency: call emergency services. See the withdrawal timeline.

Levels of care: the least intensive setting that is safe

For withdrawal, ASAM's 2020 guideline aims for "the least intensive level of care that is safe and effective"; most withdrawal can be managed outside hospital when risk factors are limited. What the long view shows

Recovery is common

9.1%

of US adults have resolved an alcohol or drug problem

In a national probability survey (39,809 adults), 9.1% said they used to have a problem with alcohol or drugs but no longer do. Of them, 46% called themselves "in recovery" and 53.9% had used an assisted pathway: mutual help 45.1%, treatment 27.6%, recovery support services 21.8%. Group statistics, not a promise for any one person. [[Kelly2017Recovery]]

Attempts

2

median serious attempts before resolving a problem

Among 2,002 people who had resolved a problem, the median number of serious recovery attempts was 2 (IQR 1–4); the mean of 5.35 is pulled up by a few people with many attempts. The authors advise quoting the median, because describing AUD as endlessly relapsing may weaken hope and help-seeking. [[Kelly2019Attempts]]

Definitions

Remission and recovery

DSM-5 early remission: at least 3 but less than 12 months without meeting any AUD criterion except craving; sustained remission: 12 months or more. The 2022 NIAAA research definition of recovery adds cessation of heavy drinking, and does not require abstinence. [[Hasin2013DSM5,APA2022,Hagman2022]] Count criteria in the criteria teaching counter.

Mortality

Risk falls when drinking falls

In clinical samples, people with AUD had higher all-cause mortality than people without (RR 3.38 in men, 4.57 in women). In 16 cohorts of people with AUD, cutting below heavy drinking went with lower mortality than continued heavy drinking (OR 0.41); abstinence carried the lowest (OR 0.35). In a 60-year Boston cohort, by age 70 of 72 men with alcohol dependence, 54% had died, 32% were abstinent and 12% still had alcohol problems. [[Roerecke2013Mortality,Roerecke2013Reduction,Vaillant2003]]

Treatment gap

Most people never get treatment

Only 25.5% of NESARC adults with past dependence had ever been treated; 19.8% of people with lifetime AUD in NESARC-III. Worldwide about 1 in 6 receive treatment (17.3%), 9.3% in low and lower-middle-income countries. High stigma and too little screening are part of the reason. [[Dawson2005,Grant2015NESARC,Mekonen2021,Carvalho2019Lancet]]

Abstinence or moderation

Paths after resolution

In the recovery survey, 20.3% reported continuous abstinence since resolving their problem and 33.7% current abstinence; about half still used alcohol or drugs in some form. Lower-risk use, including abstinence, went with more self-esteem, happiness and recovery capital, and people moved toward abstinence as time passed. [[Eddie2022]] Low-risk drinking was an outcome in 78.6% of natural-recovery alcohol samples. [[Sobell2000]] The research does not support drinking for health.

Controversy

A chronic relapsing illness?

Treatment cohorts show long careers: median 27 years from first use to last use, 9 years from first treatment to last use, and 47% reaching a year of abstinence within 3 years. AUD is best managed like diabetes or asthma, with continued monitoring. Population surveys show many people remit, often untreated. Both are true for different groups: clinical samples are more severe. [[Dennis2005,McLellan2000,Dawson2005,Kelly2019Attempts]]

Early evidence

Managed alcohol programmes

For people with alcohol dependence who are homeless or unstably housed, managed alcohol programmes give regulated doses through the day with housing, health and social support. In six Canadian cities both programme participants and controls cut drinks per day by similar amounts, but participants reported fewer harms; liver markers worsened after people left. [[Carver2026,Stockwell2021]]

Health in recovery

Keep primary care in the plan

People in recovery had higher rates of hepatitis C, COPD, heart disease and diabetes than the general population, which supports joining addiction care with primary care. [[Eddie2019]] Recovery housing helped too: 15.6% of people who lived in an Oxford House for 6 months or more reported substance use at 24 months, against 64.8% with usual aftercare. [[Jason2007]]
