## Findings

### course · Vocabulary of course: response, remission, relapse, recovery, recurrence
- In 1991 a MacArthur Foundation task force found the terms for change points in depressive illness were used inconsistently, and proposed one internally consistent scheme with tentative operational criteria for remission, recovery, relapse and recurrence [[Frank1991]].
- STAR*D defined remission as a QIDS-SR16 score of 5 or less (equivalent to 7 or less on the 17-item Hamilton scale) and relapse as a QIDS-SR16 score of 11 or more (Hamilton 14 or more) [[Rush2006STARD]].
- STAR*D defined response as a reduction of 50% or more from the baseline QIDS-SR score [[Trivedi2006STARD]].
- NICE's 2022 evidence review describes relapse as typically a return of an episode within 6 months of improvement or remission, and recurrence as a new episode after a more developed recovery lasting at least 4 to 6 months [[NICE2022ReviewC]].
- CANMAT 2023 keeps a two-phase treatment model (acute and maintenance). The maintenance phase aims to consolidate remission, restore functioning and quality of life to premorbid levels, and prevent recurrence [[Lam2024CANMAT]].
- CANMAT 2023 calls symptom remission a crucial target of acute treatment because people who reach remission have a lower risk of relapse than those who do not [[Lam2024CANMAT]].

### course · How long an episode lasts
- In the Dutch general-population NEMESIS cohort (survey n = 7076; newly originated episodes), the median duration of a major depressive episode was 3.0 months [[Spijker2002]].
- In NEMESIS, 50% of participants had recovered within 3 months, 63% within 6 months and 76% within 12 months; nearly 20% had not recovered at 24 months [[Spijker2002]].
- In NEMESIS, greater severity and comorbid dysthymia predicted a longer episode, and a recurrent (rather than first) episode predicted a shorter one [[Spijker2002]].
- In the East Baltimore population cohort (3481 adults sampled in 1981; 92 with a first lifetime onset; 23 years), the median episode length was 12 weeks [[Eaton2008]].
- In the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication (n = 9090), the mean episode duration was 16 weeks [[Kessler2003]].
- In the Dutch NESDA cohort of people in primary and specialised care, the median episode duration was 6 months for depression alone and more than 24 months when depression and an anxiety disorder occurred together [[Penninx2011]].
- A meta-regression of 19 wait-list and cohort studies estimated that, among adults with untreated depression in primary-care samples, 23% remit within 3 months, 32% within 6 months and 53% within 12 months [[Whiteford2013]].
- The untreated-remission estimates come mostly from mild-to-moderate cases and may not apply to people with more severe depression [[Whiteford2013]].

### course · Recovery over years (clinical cohorts)
- In the NIMH Collaborative Depression Study (431 people with major depression followed for 5 years), 50% recovered within the first 6 months and 12% had still not recovered at 5 years [[Keller1992]].
- In the same cohort the chance of recovery fell the longer a person had been ill: from 15% per month in the first 3 months to 1% to 2% per month in years 3, 4 and 5 [[Keller1992]].
- Many participants who did not recover stayed in a state that resembled dysthymia more than full major depressive disorder [[Keller1992]].
- In a Cambridge (UK) cohort followed for 8 to 11 years, 92% (60 of 65) recovered, about two-thirds of those later had a recurrence, and 17% had an episode of chronic depression lasting at least 2 years [[Kennedy2003]].
- Recovery and recurrence rates in the Cambridge cohort were very similar to those in older studies despite high levels of treatment [[Kennedy2003]].

### course · Chronic depression
- In NEMESIS, a duration of 24 months or more (the study's definition of chronicity) applied to nearly 20% of new episodes [[Spijker2002]].
- In the Baltimore cohort, about 15% of people with a first episode did not have a year free of episodes even after 23 years [[Eaton2008]].
- In a community survey using the WMH-CIDI 3.0, chronic depression of at least two years' duration had a lifetime prevalence of 4.6% (95% CI 3.9-5.3%) and was found in 29.4% (95% CI 25.6-33.3%) of people with a lifetime depressive disorder [[Murphy2012]].
- In NESDA, 24.5% of people with depression alone had a chronic 2-year course, against 56.8% of those with depression plus an anxiety disorder [[Penninx2011]].
- Nearly 80% of the 2876 outpatients analysed in STAR*D step 1 had chronic or recurrent major depression [[Trivedi2006STARD]].

### course · Recurrence: the "50/70/90" figures and what the cohorts show
- The widely taught figures are stated by NICE: at least 50% of people have a second episode, and after the second and third episodes the risk of relapse rises to 70% and 90% respectively [[NICE2022ReviewC]].
- A systematic review found recurrence in specialised mental health settings of 60% after 5 years, 67% after 10 years and 85% after 15 years [[Hardeveld2010]].
- The same review found recurrence seems lower in the general population: 35% after 15 years [[Hardeveld2010]].
- In the Baltimore general-population cohort, about 50% of people with a first episode recovered and had no further episodes over 23 years; the illness was recurrent in 35% and unremitting in 15% [[Eaton2008]].
- In the NIMH Collaborative Depression Study, a cumulative 85% (Kaplan-Meier) of 380 people who recovered from an index episode had a recurrence within 15 years [[Mueller1999]].
- Among the 105 people in that study who stayed well for at least 5 years after recovery, 58% (Kaplan-Meier) still had a later recurrence [[Mueller1999]].
- In 318 people followed for 10 years, nearly two-thirds had at least one recurrence and the mean number of episodes was 0.21 per year of follow-up [[Solomon2000]].
- The risk of recurrence rose by 16% with each successive recurrence and fell progressively the longer a person stayed recovered [[Solomon2000]].
- The high figures come from people treated in specialist settings; a conceptual review argues that research has been biased toward recurrent forms and has largely left out people with a single lifetime episode [[Monroe2011]].
- The same review concludes that depression can also be an acute, time-limited condition, because not everyone who has an episode has another [[Monroe2011]].

### course · Predictors of relapse and recurrence
- Across studies, the number of previous episodes and subclinical residual symptoms appear to be the most important predictors of recurrence [[Hardeveld2010]].
- Gender, civil status and socioeconomic status seem not to be related to recurrence [[Hardeveld2010]].
- A four-phase systematic review named childhood maltreatment, residual symptoms after treatment and a history of recurrence as strong prognostic indicators of relapse risk [[Buckman2018]].
- NICE lists as predictors each prior episode, the severity of the initial depression, residual symptoms after initial treatment and coexisting psychiatric disorders, and notes that the risk falls as the period of recovery lengthens [[NICE2022ReviewC]].
- In the 15-year NIMH cohort, female sex, a longer episode before intake, more prior episodes and never marrying predicted recurrence, but nothing predicted recurrence in people who had already been well for 5 years [[Mueller1999]].
- A review of recurrence risk factors concluded that recurrent depression reflects an underlying vulnerability that is largely genetic and that also predisposes to the psychosocial risk factors that accompany it (Hypothesis) [[Burcusa2007]].
- CANMAT 2023 states that people with risk factors for recurrence should continue antidepressant treatment for 2 years or more, as published in the guideline [[Lam2024CANMAT]].

### course · Residual symptoms
- In people followed to remission, residual symptoms (Hamilton 17-item score of 8 or more) were present in 32% (19 of 60) of those who remitted below major depression [[Paykel1995]].
- Early relapse followed in 76% (13 of 17) of people with residual symptoms and 25% (10 of 40) of those without [[Paykel1995]].
- Residual symptoms were more common after a more severe initial illness [[Paykel1995]].
- In a 10-year naturalistic cohort (82 people who recovered with residual subthreshold symptoms, 155 who recovered without symptoms), residual symptoms were associated with early relapse (OR 3.65), a stronger association than a history of more than four lifetime episodes (OR 1.64) [[Judd1998]].
- People with residual symptoms relapsed to any depressive episode more than 5 times faster (median 33 weeks against 184 weeks) [[Judd1998]].
- After a first lifetime episode, people who recovered with residual subthreshold symptoms (n = 26) relapsed faster and had more recurrences, shorter well intervals and fewer symptom-free weeks over up to 12 years than those who recovered without symptoms (n = 70) [[Judd2000]].
- The authors' reading is that ongoing subthreshold symptoms mean the illness is still active [[Judd2000]].

### course · STAR*D: remission step by step
- STAR*D gave up to four successive treatment steps to a broadly representative outpatient sample with non-psychotic major depressive disorder: 3671 people entered step 1 and 123 reached step 4 [[Rush2006STARD]].
- QIDS-SR16 remission rates were 36.8%, 30.6%, 13.7% and 13.0% for steps 1, 2, 3 and 4 [[Rush2006STARD]].
- The reported overall cumulative remission rate was 67% [[Rush2006STARD]].
- People who needed more treatment steps had higher relapse rates during the 12-month naturalistic follow-up [[Rush2006STARD]].
- After each of the first three steps, relapse was less frequent in people who entered follow-up in remission than in those who entered it improved but not remitted [[Rush2006STARD]].
- In step 1 (citalopram with measurement-based care, 2876 analysed), remission was 28% on the Hamilton scale and 33% on the QIDS-SR, and response was 47% [[Trivedi2006STARD]].
- A substantial share of people who reached response or remission in step 1 did so at or after 8 weeks of treatment [[Trivedi2006STARD]].
- In step 1, longer index episodes, more concurrent psychiatric disorders (especially anxiety disorders or drug abuse), more general medical disorders and lower baseline function were associated with lower remission rates [[Trivedi2006STARD]].
- A 2023 reanalysis of the patient-level data, using the protocol-specified blinded Hamilton scale and the protocol's inclusion rules, found a cumulative remission rate of 35.0% rather than 67% [[Pigott2023]].
- The reanalysis attributes the difference to the summary paper's use of a non-blinded clinic-administered measure and to the inclusion of 99 people who scored as remitted at study entry and 125 who scored as remitted when starting their next level [[Pigott2023]].
- In the reanalysis, step 1 remission and response on the Hamilton scale were 25.5% and 40.5% [[Pigott2023]].
- The reanalysis notes that the STAR*D investigators reported an overall relapse rate of 46.1% among the 1729 people with at least one follow-up assessment [[Pigott2023]].

### course · Staying well: maintenance treatment
- A pooled analysis of 31 randomised trials (4410 participants) found that continuing antidepressants reduced the odds of relapse by 70% (95% CI 62-78) compared with discontinuation [[Geddes2003]].
- The average relapse rate in those trials was 41% on placebo and 18% on active treatment [[Geddes2003]].
- The effect seemed to persist for up to 36 months, although most trials lasted 12 months [[Geddes2003]].
- A later meta-analysis of 40 studies (n = 8890) found relapse in 20.9% on continued antidepressant against 39.7% on placebo (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.33-0.43) [[Kato2021]].
- In that meta-analysis the difference was similar for a maintenance period of 6 months (19.6% vs 37.6%) and of more than 1 year (19.9% vs 39.8%) [[Kato2021]].
- Another meta-analysis found antidepressants more effective than placebo for preventing relapse (72 trials, n = 14 450; NNT 4.4) and recurrence (37 trials, n = 7253; NNT 3.8) [[Sim2015]].
- ANTLER randomised 478 UK primary-care patients who felt well enough to consider stopping (at least two prior episodes or 2 years or more on an antidepressant) to keep their medicine or to taper to placebo [[Lewis2021]].
- By 52 weeks relapse had occurred in 56% (135 of 240) of the discontinuation group and 39% (92 of 238) of the maintenance group (hazard ratio 2.06, 95% CI 1.56-2.70) [[Lewis2021]].
- In ANTLER, 44% of the discontinuation group had not relapsed by 52 weeks [[Lewis2021]].
- People in the ANTLER discontinuation group had more symptoms of depression, anxiety and withdrawal than those who kept their medicine [[Lewis2021]].
- CANMAT 2023 adjusted its recommendation, as published, from 6 to 9 months to maintaining antidepressants for 6 to 12 months after remission [[Lam2024CANMAT]].
- CANMAT 2023 states that maintenance medication can reduce relapse rates by 50% compared with placebo [[Lam2024CANMAT]].
- NICE notes that for people still at appreciable risk after 4 to 6 months of antidepressant treatment, maintenance treatment may halve the risk for at least up to 2 years of continued use [[NICE2022ReviewC]].
- In an individual-patient meta-analysis (1258 people with recurrent depression), mindfulness-based cognitive therapy reduced the risk of relapse over 60 weeks compared with not receiving it (hazard ratio 0.69, 95% CI 0.58-0.82) [[Kuyken2016]].
- NICE notes some evidence that psychological treatments do not carry an increased risk of relapse after they end when compared with antidepressants, which raises the possibility of a lasting protective effect (Early evidence) [[NICE2022ReviewC]].

### course · Function recovers more slowly than symptoms
- In pooled data from 10 treatment studies (N = 827), work impairment at baseline showed as unemployment in 11% and on-the-job performance problems in 44% [[Mintz1992]].
- Work outcomes were generally good when treatment worked on symptoms, but work recovery appeared to take considerably longer than symptom remission [[Mintz1992]].
- A systematic review of 35 controlled studies found that antidepressant treatment improved functional outcomes but that symptomatic and functional improvement were not synchronised [[Sheehan2017]].
- In the NCS-R, 59.3% of people with 12-month major depressive disorder had severe or very severe role impairment [[Kessler2003]].
- US workers with depression reported a mean of 5.6 hours per week of health-related lost productive time against an expected 1.5 hours per week without depression [[Stewart2003]].
- Reduced performance while at work, not absence, explained 81% of the lost-productive-time cost [[Stewart2003]].
- That 2003 analysis estimated the cost to US employers at 44 billion dollars a year, 31 billion more than for peers without depression [[Stewart2003]].

### comorbidity · How common comorbidity is
- In the NCS-R, 72.1% of people with lifetime major depressive disorder and 78.5% of those with 12-month disorder had another CIDI/DSM-IV disorder [[Kessler2003]].
- In the NCS-R, 51.6% of 12-month cases received health care for depression and treatment was adequate in 41.9% of those, so 21.7% of 12-month cases were adequately treated [[Kessler2003]].
- In World Health Surveys data from 245 404 people in 60 countries, an average of between 9.3% and 23.0% of people with one or more chronic physical diseases had comorbid depression [[Moussavi2007]].
- In the same surveys, depression produced the greatest decrement in health scores compared with angina, arthritis, asthma and diabetes, and depression together with a chronic disease gave the worst scores of all [[Moussavi2007]].
- A meta-analysis of 40 articles (n = 381 527) found the risk of a depressive disorder twice as great in people with multimorbidity as in those without (RR 2.13, 95% CI 1.62-2.80) [[Read2017]].
- The odds of a depressive disorder rose by 45% with each additional chronic condition (OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.28-1.64) [[Read2017]].
- Contributors to comorbid depression in medical disease include shared genetic factors, converging biological pathways, social factors, health behaviours and psychological factors [[Gold2020]].
- Symptom overlap makes depression harder to diagnose in people with a medical disorder, and symptoms of depression may lower adherence to treatment of both conditions [[Gold2020]].

### comorbidity · Anxiety disorders
- In NESDA (N = 1783), 67% of people with a depressive disorder had a current anxiety disorder and 75% had one in their lifetime [[Lamers2011]].
- Of people in NESDA with a current anxiety disorder, 63% had a current and 81% a lifetime depressive disorder [[Lamers2011]].
- When both were present, anxiety came first in 57% and depression first in 18% [[Lamers2011]].
- In STAR*D step 1, 53.2% of participants had anxious depression (Hamilton anxiety/somatisation factor score of 7 or more) [[Fava2008]].
- In STAR*D, remission was less likely and took longer in anxious depression, and side-effect burden was greater, in step 1 and again in step 2 [[Fava2008]].
- In the US NESARC-III survey, the DSM-5 anxious/distressed specifier characterised 74.6% of cases of major depressive disorder [[Hasin2018]].
- In NESARC-III, adjusted odds ratios for other psychiatric disorders ranged from 2.1 (specific phobia) to 5.7 (generalised anxiety disorder) [[Hasin2018]].
- Comorbid depression and anxiety was associated with more childhood trauma, higher neuroticism, earlier onset and higher symptom severity [[Lamers2011]].

### comorbidity · Substance use, sleep and pain
- A meta-analysis (48 articles, 348 550 people) found any substance use disorder in 25.0% of people with major depressive disorder: alcohol use disorder in 20.8%, illicit drug use disorder in 11.8% and cannabis use disorder in 11.7% [[Hunt2020]].
- Alcohol use disorder was more frequent in men with major depressive disorder (36%) than in women (19%) [[Hunt2020]].
- In NESARC-III, adjusted odds ratios linking major depressive disorder with substance use disorders ranged from 1.8 for alcohol to 3.0 for any drug [[Hasin2018]].
- A review concluded that the most plausible causal link runs from alcohol use disorder to major depression, so that increasing involvement with alcohol raises the risk of depression [[Boden2011]].
- In 21 longitudinal studies, people with insomnia and no depression had about twice the risk of developing depression (fixed-effects OR 2.10, 95% CI 1.86-2.38) [[Baglioni2011]].
- Pain and depression occur together more often than either alone would predict, pain lowers the recognition and treatment of depression, and each worsens the outcome of the other [[Bair2003]].

### comorbidity · Heart disease in both directions
- In 21 aetiological cohort studies, depression was associated with a pooled relative risk of 1.81 (95% CI 1.53-2.15) for future coronary heart disease [[Nicholson2006]].
- In 34 prognostic studies of people with coronary heart disease, the pooled relative risk was 1.80 (95% CI 1.50-2.15); adjustment for left ventricular function cut the estimate from 2.18 to 1.53 in the eight studies that had it [[Nicholson2006]].
- The authors concluded that depression had yet to be established as an independent risk factor because adjustment for conventional risk factors and disease severity was incomplete and biased [[Nicholson2006]].
- In pooled individual data on 563 255 people in 22 cohorts, each 1-SD higher depressive symptom score carried a hazard ratio of 1.11 for coronary heart disease and 1.10 for stroke in UK Biobank; the associations were described as modest [[Harshfield2020]].
- Major depression was identified by structured interview in 19.8% (95% CI 19.1-20.6%) of survivors of acute myocardial infarction (8 studies, N = 10 785) [[Thombs2006]].
- Depression within 3 months after myocardial infarction was associated with all-cause mortality (OR 2.25, 95% CI 1.73-2.93), cardiac mortality (OR 2.71, 95% CI 1.68-4.36) and cardiac events (OR 1.59, 95% CI 1.37-1.85) within 24 months (29 studies, 16 889 patients) [[Meijer2011]].
- In 896 people after myocardial infarction there was a long-term dose-response relationship between depressive symptoms during admission and 5-year cardiac mortality [[Lesperance2002]].
- A 2014 American Heart Association statement reviewed 53 studies and 4 meta-analyses and recommended that depression be elevated to the status of a risk factor for adverse medical outcomes in people with acute coronary syndrome [[Lichtman2014]].
- SADHART (369 people with major depression after myocardial infarction or unstable angina) found sertraline did not worsen cardiac measures; clinical global response was 67% against 53% on placebo [[Glassman2002]].
- ENRICHD (2481 people after myocardial infarction) found that a psychosocial intervention improved depression but did not change event-free survival (75.8% vs 75.9% after a mean 29 months) [[Berkman2003]].
- EsDEPACS (300 people with depression after acute coronary syndrome, South Korea) found that 24 weeks of escitalopram was followed by fewer major adverse cardiac events over a median 8.1 years: 40.9% vs 53.6% on placebo (hazard ratio 0.69, 95% CI 0.49-0.96) [[Kim2018]].
- In EsDEPACS, all-cause mortality did not differ significantly (20.8% vs 24.5%), and the authors called for research on generalisability (Early evidence) [[Kim2018]].

### comorbidity · Diabetes, obesity, stroke, cancer and dementia
- Diabetes doubles the odds of comorbid depression (OR 2.0, 95% CI 1.8-2.2, in 20 controlled studies) [[Anderson2001Diabetes]].
- Depression prevalence in diabetes was 11% by standardised diagnostic interview and 31% by self-report questionnaire, and higher in women (28%) than in men (18%) [[Anderson2001Diabetes]].
- Prospectively, baseline depression was associated with a 60% higher risk of incident type 2 diabetes (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.37-1.88), while diabetes was associated with a modest rise in incident depression (RR 1.15, 95% CI 1.02-1.30) [[Mezuk2008]].
- In TEAMcare (214 people with depression plus poorly controlled diabetes, coronary heart disease or both), nurse-led collaborative care improved glycated haemoglobin (difference 0.58%), LDL cholesterol (6.9 mg/dL), systolic blood pressure (5.1 mm Hg) and depression scores at 12 months [[Katon2010]].
- Obesity at baseline raised the odds of later depression (OR 1.55, 95% CI 1.22-1.98), and depression raised the odds of later obesity (OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.33-1.87) [[Luppino2010]].
- Overweight raised the odds of later depression less (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.07-1.51), and baseline depression did not predict overweight [[Luppino2010]].
- Across 28 prospective cohorts (317 540 people), depression was associated with a hazard ratio of 1.45 (95% CI 1.29-1.63) for total stroke [[Pan2011]].
- The estimated absolute difference was 106 extra cases of stroke per 100 000 people per year [[Pan2011]].
- After stroke, the pooled frequency of depression was 31% (95% CI 28-35%) across 61 studies of 25 488 people [[Hackett2014]].
- In oncology and haematology settings (70 interview-based studies, 10 071 people), DSM-defined major depression was found in 14.9% (95% CI 12.2-17.7) [[Mitchell2011]].
- In palliative-care settings (24 studies, 4007 people), DSM-defined major depression was found in 14.3% (95% CI 11.1-17.9) [[Mitchell2011]].
- A history of depression was associated with later Alzheimer's disease, with pooled odds ratios of 2.03 (95% CI 1.73-2.38) in case-control studies and 1.90 (95% CI 1.55-2.33) in cohort studies [[Ownby2006]].
- A longer interval between the depression and the dementia diagnosis went with higher risk, which the authors read as favouring a risk factor over a prodrome (Hypothesis) [[Ownby2006]].
- The 2020 Lancet Commission on dementia listed later-life depression among 12 modifiable risk factors, with a relative risk of 1.9 (95% CI 1.6-2.3) and a weighted population attributable fraction of 3.9% [[Livingston2020Commission]].

### comorbidity · Mortality gap
- Across studies with 1 813 733 participants from 35 countries, the unadjusted relative risk of death in people with depression was 1.64 (95% CI 1.56-1.76), falling to 1.52 (95% CI 1.45-1.59) after adjustment for publication bias [[Cuijpers2014]].
- The excess was similar in community samples and in samples with heart disease, cancer or kidney disease, and was smaller in higher-quality and longer studies [[Cuijpers2014]].
- For mental disorders as a whole (148 studies), the pooled relative risk of death was 2.22 (95% CI 2.12-2.33) and the median years of potential life lost was 10 [[Walker2015]].
- Among people with mental disorders, 67.3% of deaths were from natural causes and 17.5% from unnatural causes [[Walker2015]].
- A meta-review found every mental disorder studied carried a higher all-cause mortality than the general population and listed depression among the disorders with the highest suicide risks [[Chesney2014]].

### suicide · Burden and the link with depression
- An estimated 746 000 people (95% UI 692 000-800 000) died by suicide worldwide in 2021 [[GBD2021Suicide]].
- The global age-standardised suicide mortality rate fell from 14.9 per 100 000 in 1990 to 9.0 per 100 000 in 2021 [[GBD2021Suicide]].
- In 17 countries (84 850 adults), the lifetime prevalence of suicidal ideation was 9.2%, of a plan 3.1% and of an attempt 2.7% [[Nock2008]].
- About 60% of transitions from ideation to a plan or an attempt happened within the first year after ideation began [[Nock2008]].
- In psychological-autopsy case series, the median proportion of people who died by suicide and had a mental disorder was 91% (95% CI 81-98%); in case-control studies it was 90% of cases against 27% of controls [[Cavanagh2003]].
- Depression is the most common psychiatric disorder in people who die by suicide [[Hawton2013]].
- Record-linkage studies give a pooled suicide rate ratio of 12.3 (95% CI 8.9-17.1) for mood disorders compared with the general population [[Too2019]].
- In the Global Burden of Disease 2010 analysis, depression accounted for the largest share (46.1%) of the suicide burden attributed to mental and substance use disorders [[Ferrari2014]].
- Most people with depression do not die by suicide: lifetime suicide prevalence was 2.2% in mixed inpatient and outpatient samples with affective disorders, 4.0% in those ever hospitalised, and 8.6% in those ever hospitalised for suicidality, against less than 0.5% in people without affective illness [[Bostwick2000]].
- In a Danish national register cohort followed for up to 36 years from first psychiatric contact (median 18 years), the absolute risk of suicide in men with unipolar affective disorder was 6.67% (95% CI 5.72-7.78%), against 0.72% in men without psychiatric contact [[Nordentoft2011]].
- In people with major depressive disorder followed for 5 years, the rate of suicide attempts per 1000 patient-years was 332 during major depressive episodes, 62 during partial remission and 16 during full remission [[Holma2010]].
- The share of suicide attempts attributable to time spent in a depressive episode was 78%, which makes time spent unwell the main modifiable driver of long-term risk [[Holma2010]].

### suicide · Risk and protective factors in depression
- In people with depression, factors associated with suicide were male gender (OR 1.76), family history of psychiatric disorder (OR 1.41), previous suicide attempt (OR 4.84), more severe depression (OR 2.20), hopelessness (OR 2.20), comorbid anxiety (OR 1.59) and misuse of alcohol and drugs (OR 2.17) [[Hawton2013]].
- In the Finnish 5-year cohort, low perceived social support predicted attempts (hazard ratio 3.57, 95% CI 1.09-11.1), as did a history of attempts (hazard ratio 4.39); lack of a partner pointed the same way (hazard ratio 2.33, 95% CI 0.97-5.56), so social support acts in the protective direction [[Holma2010]].
- The first 3 months after discharge from psychiatric hospital are a period of marked risk: the pooled suicide rate was 1132 per 100 000 person-years, falling to 654 at 3 months to 1 year, 494 at 1 to 5 years, 366 at 5 to 10 years and 277 beyond 10 years [[Chung2017JAMAPsych]].
- In US Medicaid data on adults aged 18 to 64, the suicide rate in the 90 days after discharge was 235.1 per 100 000 person-years for people admitted with a depressive disorder, against 14.2 in the US general population [[Olfson2016]].
- People with no outpatient care in the 6 months before admission had a higher short-term risk after discharge (adjusted hazard ratio 1.7) [[Olfson2016]].
- About three in four people who died by suicide had contact with primary care in the year before, and on average 45% within 1 month; about one in five had contact with mental health services within a month [[Luoma2002]].
- In 5894 health-plan members who died by suicide, 83% received health care in the year before death, but only 24% had a mental health diagnosis recorded in the last 4 weeks [[Ahmedani2014]].
- Exposure to the suicide of a close contact is associated with a higher risk of suicide in bereaved partners and in mothers who lose an adult child, of psychiatric admission in bereaved parents and of depression in children who lose a parent; some evidence shows more rejection and shame than after other violent deaths (narrative review of 57 studies) [[Pitman2014]].
- Media reporting of a celebrity's death by suicide was followed by a 13% rise in suicides (rate ratio 1.13, 95% CI 1.08-1.18); the authors name guidelines for responsible reporting as the best available population-level response [[Niederkrotenthaler2020]].

### suicide · Why prediction performs poorly
- A meta-analysis of 365 studies over 50 years found that prediction of suicidal thoughts and behaviours from risk factors was only slightly better than chance and had not improved over time [[Franklin2017Suicide]].
- For risk scales, the pooled positive predictive value of a "high-risk" result was 5.5% (95% CI 3.9-7.9%) for suicide; the authors judged no high-risk classification clinically useful [[Carter2017PPV]].
- Low prevalence puts a ceiling on positive predictive value, whatever the instrument [[Carter2017PPV]].
- Across 64 prediction models (17 cohort studies, more than 14 million participants), overall classification accuracy was good (0.80 or more in most) but the positive predictive value for death by suicide was 0.01 or less in most models [[Belsher2019]].
- A Lancet seminar states there is no effective algorithm to predict suicide in clinical practice [[Turecki2016Lancet]].
- No risk factor, including diagnostic subtype, has been reliably shown to predict suicide in affective disorders [[Bostwick2000]].
- Item 9 of the PHQ-9 still carries information: over one year, suicide attempts occurred in 0.4% of outpatients answering "not at all" and 4% of those answering "nearly every day"; deaths by suicide in 0.03% and 0.3% (84 418 outpatients) [[Simon2013]].
- That excess risk emerged over several days and kept growing for several months, which points to an enduring vulnerability more than a short crisis [[Simon2013]].
- The USPSTF recommends screening adults for depression (B recommendation) but finds the evidence insufficient to assess screening for suicide risk in adults (I statement) [[USPSTF2023Depression]].

### suicide · Asking, assessing and crisis lines
- A review of studies in adults and adolescents, in general and at-risk populations, found none that showed a statistically significant increase in suicidal ideation after people were asked about suicidal thoughts [[Dazzi2014]].
- The same review suggests that acknowledging and talking about suicide may reduce rather than increase suicidal ideation [[Dazzi2014]].
- The Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale was validated in three multisite studies (124 adolescents after a suicide attempt, 312 adolescents with depression, 237 adults in an emergency department) and showed good convergent and divergent validity and sensitivity to change [[Posner2011]].
- On the Columbia scale, people at the two highest levels of ideation severity (intent, or intent with a plan) at baseline had higher odds of a suicide attempt during the study [[Posner2011]].
- Among 1085 callers to eight US crisis centres who were assessed during their calls, suicidality decreased significantly over the course of the call, and hopelessness and psychological pain kept decreasing in the following weeks [[Gould2007]].
- The US 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline launched in July 2022 [[Purtle2025]].

### suicide · What works: brief interventions and follow-up
- The Safety Planning Intervention is a brief clinical intervention that produces a prioritised list of coping skills and strategies; in the trial it was combined with telephone follow-up [[Stanley2018SafetyPlan]].
- In a cohort comparison at 9 US Veterans Health Administration emergency departments, suicidal behaviour over 6 months occurred in 3.03% (36 of 1186) of people who received safety planning with follow-up and 5.29% (24 of 454) of those who received usual care [[Stanley2018SafetyPlan]].
- That is 45% fewer suicidal behaviours (odds ratio 0.56, 95% CI 0.33-0.95), and people in the intervention group had double the odds of attending at least one outpatient mental health visit (odds ratio 2.06) [[Stanley2018SafetyPlan]].
- A meta-analysis of safety-planning-type interventions (6 studies, 3536 participants) found a relative risk of suicidal behaviour of 0.570 (95% CI 0.408-0.795; number needed to treat 16) and no significant effect on suicidal ideation [[Nuij2021]].
- Brief suicide prevention interventions delivered in a single acute-care encounter (14 studies, 4270 patients) were associated with fewer later suicide attempts (pooled odds ratio 0.69, 95% CI 0.53-0.89) and more linkage to follow-up care (odds ratio 3.04) [[Doupnik2020]].
- In ED-SAFE (1376 participants), screening plus a brief intervention during and after the emergency visit lowered the share who attempted suicide over 52 weeks from 23% to 18% and the total number of attempts by 30% [[Miller2017EDSAFE]].
- In a trial of 843 people who had declined ongoing care after hospitalisation, those sent letters at least four times a year for five years had a lower suicide rate, significantly so for the first two years [[Motto2001]].
- The WHO brief intervention and contact trial (1867 people seen in emergency units in five low- and middle-income countries) recorded fewer deaths by suicide at 18 months with the intervention (0.2%) than with usual treatment (2.2%) [[Fleischmann2008]].
- A meta-analysis of brief contact interventions found a non-significant reduction in any repeat self-harm or attempt (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.74-1.04) and fewer repeat episodes per person (incidence rate ratio 0.66), and concluded they could not yet be recommended for widespread use [[Milner2015]].
- A meta-analysis of randomised trials found the WHO brief intervention and contact associated with lower odds of death by suicide (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.09-0.42; 3 trials, n = 2028) and no other intervention with a statistically significant effect on deaths [[Riblet2017]].

### suicide · What works: psychological treatment and collaborative care
- In 120 adults who had recently attempted suicide, a 10-session cognitive therapy was followed by a repeat attempt in 24.1% over 18 months, against 41.6% with usual care (hazard ratio 0.51, 95% CI 0.26-0.997) [[Brown2005]].
- In that trial depression severity and hopelessness were lower with cognitive therapy, while rates of suicidal ideation did not differ [[Brown2005]].
- PROSPECT (598 older primary-care patients with depression in 20 practices) compared care management with guideline-based treatment against usual care [[Bruce2004]].
- At 4 months in PROSPECT, suicidal ideation fell by 12.9 percentage points with the intervention (29.4% to 16.5%) and by 3.0 points with usual care (20.1% to 17.1%) [[Bruce2004]].
- Among PROSPECT patients who reported suicidal ideation, resolution was faster with the intervention, with the largest difference at 8 months (70.7% vs 43.9%) [[Bruce2004]].
- A 2021 systematic review concluded that cognitive-behavioural therapy and dialectical behaviour therapy prevent suicidal behaviour, and that active outreach after discharge or a suicidal crisis prevents suicidal behaviour [[Mann2021Suicide]].
- Evidence for specific interventions for people bereaved by suicide (postvention) is scant and of weak quality; approaches that show promise are supportive, therapeutic and educational, involve the person's social environment, and run as a series of sessions led by trained facilitators [[Andriessen2019]].

### suicide · Population measures
- A 2005 systematic review found that two measures prevented suicide: education of physicians in recognising and treating depression, and restricting access to means [[Mann2005]].
- The 2021 update reached the same two conclusions (training primary care physicians in depression recognition and treatment, and means restriction) and added education of young people about depression and suicidal behaviour [[Mann2021Suicide]].
- A 10-year European review assessed seven kinds of intervention (public and physician education, media strategies, screening, restricting access to means, treatments, and internet or helpline support) and concluded that no single strategy clearly stands above the others [[Zalsman2016]].
- That review calls effective pharmacological and psychological treatment of depression important in prevention, and finds insufficient evidence for screening in primary care, general public education and media guidelines [[Zalsman2016]].
- The 2021 review notes that active screening for suicidal ideation is not proven to be better than screening for depression alone [[Mann2021Suicide]].

### suicide · Medicines and suicidality
- In FDA's pooled adult trials, the odds ratio for suicidal behaviour or ideation on antidepressants against placebo was 1.62 (95% CI 0.97-2.71) under age 25, 0.79 (95% CI 0.64-0.98) at ages 25 to 64 and 0.37 (95% CI 0.18-0.76) at 65 and over [[Stone2009]].
- Modelled continuously, the odds ratio for suicidal behaviour or ideation fell by 2.6% per year of age [[Stone2009]].
- In 24 paediatric trials there were no deaths by suicide; the risk ratio for suicidality (ideation or behaviour) was 1.66 (95% CI 1.02-2.68) for SSRIs in depression trials and 1.95 (95% CI 1.28-2.98) for all drugs and indications, a risk difference of 0.02 [[Hammad2006]].
- US antidepressant labels carry a boxed warning that antidepressants increased the risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviour in children, adolescents and young adults in short-term studies, with no increase over age 24 and a reduction at 65 and older [[FDA-Prozac]].
- The label table gives the drug-placebo difference per 1000 people treated: 14 more cases of suicidal thoughts or behaviours under 18, 5 more at 18 to 24, 1 fewer at 25 to 64 and 6 fewer at 65 and over [[FDA-Zoloft]].
- The label states that no suicides occurred in the paediatric studies, and asks for close monitoring of everyone starting an antidepressant for clinical worsening and for the emergence of suicidal thoughts [[FDA-Zoloft]].
- A person-level reanalysis of fluoxetine and venlafaxine trials found that the medicines decreased suicidal thoughts and behaviour in adult and geriatric patients through improvement in depression, with no significant effect in either direction in youths [[Gibbons2012]].
- In randomised trials in mood disorders (48 trials, 6674 participants), lithium was associated with fewer suicides than placebo (odds ratio 0.13, 95% CI 0.03-0.66); in unipolar depression the odds ratio was 0.36 (95% CI 0.13-0.98) [[Cipriani2013]].
- In InterSePT, in people with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder at high risk, clozapine reduced suicidal behaviour compared with olanzapine (hazard ratio 0.76, 95% CI 0.58-0.97) [[Meltzer2003]].
- Clozapine's US label includes reducing the risk of recurrent suicidal behaviour in schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder; this is not an indication in depression [[FDA-Clozaril]].

### suicide · Ketamine and esketamine (supervised medical or research treatment only)
- In an individual-participant meta-analysis of 167 people with baseline suicidal ideation, a single intravenous dose of ketamine reduced suicidal ideation within 1 day and for up to 1 week, with effect sizes of d = 0.48 to 0.85 (Early evidence) [[Wilkinson2018]].
- The effect on ideation stayed significant after adjusting for change in depressive symptoms, and the authors asked for more research on long-term safety and on suicide risk before clinical implementation [[Wilkinson2018]].
- In a midazolam-controlled trial, 55% of the ketamine group and 30% of the midazolam group had a 50% or greater fall in a suicidal ideation scale score at day 1 (number needed to treat 4.0) (Early evidence) [[Grunebaum2018]].
- ASPIRE I (226 adults with major depressive disorder and active suicidal ideation with intent) found a greater fall in MADRS score at 24 hours with esketamine nasal spray plus standard care than with placebo plus standard care (difference -3.8, 95% CI -6.56 to -1.09) [[Fu2020,NCT03039192]].
- ASPIRE II (230 adults enrolled) found a similar difference in MADRS score at 24 hours (-3.9, 95% CI -6.60 to -1.11) [[Ionescu2021,NCT03097133]].
- In both ASPIRE trials, the between-group difference in clinician-rated severity of suicidality was not statistically significant; both groups improved rapidly with comprehensive standard care [[Fu2020,Ionescu2021]].
- The Spravato label includes depressive symptoms in adults with major depressive disorder with acute suicidal ideation or behaviour, in conjunction with an oral antidepressant [[FDA-Spravato]].
- The label states, as a limitation of use, that effectiveness in preventing suicide or in reducing suicidal ideation or behaviour has not been demonstrated, and that use does not preclude the need for hospitalisation [[FDA-Spravato]].
- Esketamine is available only through a restricted programme, with monitoring for at least two hours after each administration [[FDA-Spravato]].
- The 2021 prevention review summarises ketamine as reducing suicidal ideation within hours but untested for preventing suicidal behaviour (Early evidence) [[Mann2021Suicide]].

### Exam hooks
- Median duration of a major depressive episode in the general population: 3.0 months; nearly 20% not recovered at 24 months [[Spijker2002]].
- STAR*D remission by step: 36.8%, 30.6%, 13.7%, 13.0%; reported cumulative 67% [[Rush2006STARD]].
- The 2023 patient-level reanalysis of STAR*D gives a cumulative remission of 35.0% on the protocol-specified blinded Hamilton scale [[Pigott2023]].
- Continuing antidepressants after response: relapse 18% against 41% on placebo across 31 trials [[Geddes2003]].
- ANTLER at 52 weeks: relapse 56% after discontinuation, 39% on maintenance (hazard ratio 2.06) [[Lewis2021]].
- Residual symptoms after remission predict early relapse: 76% against 25% [[Paykel1995]].
- The "50/70/90" recurrence figures describe specialist-care samples; in the general population about half of people with a first episode have no further episode [[NICE2022ReviewC,Eaton2008]].
- Recurrence in specialist settings: 60% at 5 years, 67% at 10 years, 85% at 15 years; general population 35% at 15 years [[Hardeveld2010]].
- The two strongest predictors of recurrence are the number of previous episodes and residual symptoms [[Hardeveld2010]].
- About two-thirds of people with a depressive disorder in a clinical cohort have a current anxiety disorder [[Lamers2011]].
- Depression after myocardial infarction: major depression in about 1 in 5; all-cause mortality OR 2.25 within 24 months [[Thombs2006,Meijer2011]].
- ENRICHD treated depression after myocardial infarction and did not improve event-free survival [[Berkman2003]].
- Diabetes doubles the odds of depression; depression raises the risk of incident type 2 diabetes by 60% [[Anderson2001Diabetes,Mezuk2008]].
- Depression and obesity predict each other (OR 1.55 and 1.58) [[Luppino2010]].
- Depression carries a relative risk of death of about 1.5 after correction for publication bias [[Cuijpers2014]].
- Asking about suicide does not increase suicidal ideation [[Dazzi2014]].
- Risk scales cannot usefully sort people into "high risk": pooled positive predictive value for suicide 5.5% [[Carter2017PPV]].
- Safety planning with follow-up after an emergency visit: 45% fewer suicidal behaviours over 6 months [[Stanley2018SafetyPlan]].
- Antidepressants and suicidality depend on age: odds ratio 1.62 under 25, 0.79 at 25 to 64, 0.37 at 65 and over [[Stone2009]].
- The Spravato label says effectiveness in preventing suicide has not been demonstrated [[FDA-Spravato]].

### In plain words
- Depression is a common, treatable illness. Half of new episodes in the general population lift within about three months [[Spijker2002]].
- About one in five episodes lasts two years or more, so an episode that drags on is a reason to ask for more help, not a personal failing [[Spijker2002]].
- About half of people who have a first episode never have another one [[Eaton2008]].
- The risk of another episode is higher after several episodes, and when some symptoms are still there after treatment [[Hardeveld2010]].
- Getting fully well matters. People who still have some symptoms are more likely to become unwell again soon [[Paykel1995]].
- If the first treatment does not work, the next one may. In a large US study, more people got well with each step they tried [[Rush2006STARD]].
- Staying on an antidepressant after getting well lowers the chance of the illness coming back. Stopping suddenly can cause discontinuation symptoms, so any change is planned with the prescriber [[Geddes2003,Lewis2021]].
- Getting back to work and everyday life often takes longer than feeling better in mood [[Mintz1992]].
- Depression often comes together with anxiety, and with long-term physical illnesses such as heart disease and diabetes. Each can make the other harder to manage, and both deserve treatment [[Lamers2011,Gold2020]].
- Suicidal thoughts are a symptom that can be treated. They are far more common during an episode and fall as the depression lifts [[Holma2010]].
- Asking someone whether they are thinking about suicide does not put the idea in their head. It can bring relief [[Dazzi2014]].
- Most people with depression do not die by suicide [[Bostwick2000]].
- A written safety plan made with a clinician, plus follow-up calls, roughly halved suicidal behaviour in one large study [[Stanley2018SafetyPlan]].
- No questionnaire can tell who will or will not harm themselves. A score never replaces a conversation with a clinician [[Carter2017PPV]].
- In the US, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) has been running since July 2022 [[Purtle2025]].

## Open questions and controversies
- How recurrent is depression? Position A: at least 50% after one episode, 70% after two and 90% after three [[NICE2022ReviewC]]; position B: these figures come from specialist-care samples, and in the general population about half of first episodes are never followed by another [[Eaton2008,Hardeveld2010,Monroe2011]]. Status: both are true of their populations; the companion should name the population with every figure.
- What did STAR*D show? Position A: 67% cumulative remission over four steps [[Rush2006STARD]]; position B: 35.0% when the protocol-specified blinded outcome and inclusion rules are used [[Pigott2023]]. Status: unresolved in print; present both with the outcome measure named.
- Does ANTLER measure relapse or withdrawal? The trial found more relapse after discontinuation and also more withdrawal symptoms in that group [[Lewis2021]]. Whether some relapse events were withdrawal effects is debated (Hypothesis); the abstract does not settle it.
- Is depression a causal risk factor for coronary disease? Position A: associations are consistent enough for the AHA to name depression a risk factor for poor prognosis after acute coronary syndrome [[Lichtman2014]]; position B: confounding by disease severity is not excluded [[Nicholson2006]], and treating depression did not improve cardiac outcomes in ENRICHD [[Berkman2003]], though one single-country trial of escitalopram did [[Kim2018]]. Status: open.
- Is depression a risk factor for dementia or an early sign of it? Risk factor reading [[Ownby2006,Livingston2020Commission]]; the prodrome reading is not excluded by observational data. Status: open (Hypothesis).
- Do antidepressants raise suicidality in young people? Pooled FDA trial data show an increase in suicidal thoughts or behaviour under 25 with no deaths by suicide in the paediatric trials [[Hammad2006,Stone2009]]; a person-level reanalysis of two medicines found no increase in youths [[Gibbons2012]]. Status: the boxed warning stands [[FDA-Prozac]]; the absolute difference is small and the comparison is with untreated illness.
- Can suicide be predicted? Risk-factor research, scales and algorithms all give low positive predictive values [[Franklin2017Suicide,Carter2017PPV,Belsher2019]]; single items such as PHQ-9 item 9 still mark graded risk [[Simon2013]]. Status: assessment guides care but cannot classify individuals.
- Should adults be screened for suicide risk? The USPSTF finds the evidence insufficient [[USPSTF2023Depression]]; reviews find screening for ideation unproven beyond screening for depression [[Mann2021Suicide,Zalsman2016]]. Status: open.
- Do brief contact interventions prevent deaths? One meta-analysis found no significant effect on suicide [[Milner2015]]; the WHO brief intervention and contact trials did [[Fleischmann2008,Riblet2017]]. Status: promising, setting-dependent.
- Do ketamine and esketamine prevent suicide? They reduce depressive symptoms and, for intravenous ketamine, suicidal ideation within a day [[Wilkinson2018,Grunebaum2018]]; esketamine did not separate from placebo on severity of suicidality, and the label says prevention has not been demonstrated [[Fu2020,Ionescu2021,FDA-Spravato]]. Status: (Early evidence) for ideation, none for deaths.
- How many people who die by suicide had a mental disorder? Psychological autopsies give about 90% [[Cavanagh2003]]; health records show that half had no mental health diagnosis recorded in the year before death [[Ahmedani2014]]. Status: the methods measure different things (retrospective interview against recorded diagnosis).
