## Findings

### what · What major depressive disorder is
- Major depressive disorder (MDD) is described as a debilitating disease "characterized by depressed mood, diminished interests, impaired cognitive function and vegetative symptoms, such as disturbed sleep or appetite" [[Otte2016MDD]]
- DSM-5-TR (2022) defines a major depressive episode as five or more of nine symptoms present during the same 2-week period, at least one of which is depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure, causing clinically significant distress or impairment and not attributable to a substance or another medical condition; MDD requires that there has never been a manic or hypomanic episode [[APA2022DSM5TR]]
- The two core symptoms, of which at least one is required, are depressed mood and loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia); the word anhedonia entered official English-language psychiatry with DSM-III [[Paykel2008]]
- The modern concept of depression in the official classifications is "a clinical syndrome, defined by presence of a number of clinical features", not a diagnosis defined by a known cause [[Paykel2008]]
- DSM criteria index the illness; they do not exhaust it. A review of textbooks from 1900–1960 found 18 symptoms and signs of depression, 10 of which are covered by the DSM criteria for major depression or melancholia; changes in volition, slowed speech, anxiety, other physical symptoms and depersonalisation are described clinically but are not DSM criteria [[Kendler2016Phenomenology]]
- MDD "affects one in six adults in their lifetime" and "occurs about twice as often in women than it does in men" [[Otte2016MDD]]
- The aetiology is multifactorial, with heritability estimated at approximately 35%, and childhood sexual, physical or emotional abuse is strongly associated with risk; "no established mechanism can explain all aspects of the disease" (Established) [[Otte2016MDD]]
- Depression is treatable: management "primarily comprises psychotherapy and pharmacological treatment", and electroconvulsive therapy has the best empirical evidence for people whose illness has not responded to several treatment attempts [[Otte2016MDD]]
- In detection and management, depression poses challenges "because of its various presentations, unpredictable course and prognosis, and variable response to treatment" [[Malhi2018Lancet]]

### what · Names and classifications (DSM-5-TR, ICD-11, PDD, 'clinical depression')
- ICD-11 was approved by the World Health Assembly in May 2019, with reporting of health statistics under the new system to begin on 1 January 2022 [[Reed2019]]
- In ICD-11, a depressive episode is defined by "the concurrent presence of at least five out of a list of ten symptoms, which must occur most of the day, nearly every day, for at least 2 weeks", one of which must be depressed mood or markedly diminished interest or pleasure [[Stein2020BMC]]
- ICD-11 adds a tenth symptom, hopelessness about the future, that is not in the DSM-5 list; the depressive episode is one of the few ICD-11 descriptions that gives explicit thresholds for the number and duration of symptoms, and the five-symptom threshold was made consistent with DSM-5 [[Stein2020BMC]]
- ICD-11 codes: 6A70 Single episode depressive disorder (one depressive episode with no history of prior depressive episodes) and 6A71 Recurrent depressive disorder; a depressive episode is also a building block of bipolar type I (6A60) and type II (6A61) disorder; 6A73 is Mixed depressive and anxiety disorder [[Shevlin2023]]
- ICD-11 allows qualifiers for the current episode that include prominent anxiety symptoms, melancholia, current perinatal episode and seasonal pattern, and it grades current episode severity [[Stein2020BMC]]
- Persistent depressive disorder (PDD) is "a chronic mood disorder that is common and often more disabling than episodic major depression"; in DSM-5 the term subsumes dysthymia with or without superimposed major depressive episodes, chronic major depression, and recurrent major depression without recovery between episodes [[Schramm2020]]
- Dysthymia requires fewer symptoms than a major depressive episode but a longer duration of 2 years, and represents a form of what was earlier called neurotic depression [[Paykel2008]]
- In GBD, "depressive disorders" means major depressive disorder plus dysthymia; published GBD totals for depressive disorders therefore exceed those for MDD alone [[GBD2019Mental]]
- "Clinical depression" is an informal name for depression at the level of a diagnosable disorder; it is used in historical and clinical writing but is not the title of a DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 category [[Jansson2022,Kendler2016Phenomenology,Shevlin2023]]
- "Unipolar" depression means depressive episodes without mania or hypomania. The modern separation into unipolar and bipolar disorder followed empirical research by Angst and Perris in the 1960s [[Paykel2008,Angst2001]]
- The distinction matters in practice: in a 2000 survey of 600 support-group members with bipolar disorder, 69% reported having been misdiagnosed, most often with unipolar depression, and over one third waited 10 years or more for an accurate diagnosis [[Hirschfeld2003]]

### what · Depression, grief and ordinary sadness
- ICD-11 states that a depressive episode is differentiated from a normal reaction to adverse life events (for example divorce or job loss) "by the severity, range and duration of symptoms" [[Stein2020BMC]]
- From DSM-III (1980) recent bereavement could exclude the diagnosis of a major depressive episode unless symptoms were severe and very impairing; DSM-5 removed this exclusion and leaves the judgement about a response to a significant loss to the clinician [[Zisook2012,Stein2020BMC]]
- The argument for removal: a 2012 review concluded that "bereavement-related depression is not different from MDE that presents in any other context" in genetic influence, past and family history, personality and comorbidity [[Zisook2012]]
- The argument against: longitudinal studies cited by ICD-11 authors found that the risk of later depressive episodes was significantly lower after bereavement-related than after other major depression, and ICD-11 keeps bereavement in its exclusion wording [[Stein2020BMC]]
- In the US NESARC-III survey, 12.9% of adults with lifetime MDD had episodes that all occurred just after the death of someone close and lasted less than 2 months [[Hasin2018]]

### what · Global prevalence and burden
- GBD 2019: 279.6 million people (95% UI 251.6–310.3) were living with a depressive disorder worldwide in 2019, up from 170.8 million (152.7–190.4) in 1990 [[GBD2019Mental]]
- The rise is driven by population growth and ageing: the age-standardised prevalence of depressive disorders was 3486.2 per 100 000 (3140.8–3855.7) in 1990 and 3440.1 per 100 000 (3097.0–3817.6) in 2019 [[GBD2019Mental]]
- By sex in 2019: 170.4 million females (153.6–188.7; 4158.4 per 100 000 age-standardised) and 109.2 million males (98.0–121.4; 2713.3 per 100 000) [[GBD2019Mental]]
- All mental disorders together accounted for 970.1 million cases (900.9–1044.4) in 2019, up 48.1% from 654.8 million in 1990 [[GBD2019Mental]]
- Depressive disorders accounted for the largest share of mental disorder DALYs in 2019, 37.3% (32.3–43.0), followed by anxiety disorders (22.9%) and schizophrenia (12.2%) [[GBD2019Mental]]
- In 2019 depressive disorders ranked 13th among the top 25 leading causes of DALYs and second among the leading causes of YLDs (years lived with disability) at the disorder level [[GBD2019Mental]]
- Mental disorders caused 125.3 million DALYs (93.0–163.2) in 2019, 4.9% (3.9–6.1) of global DALYs (3.1% in 1990), and 14.6% (12.2–16.8) of global YLDs [[GBD2019Mental]]
- The regional age-standardised prevalence of depressive disorders in 2019 was high in sub-Saharan Africa (4540.4 per 100 000) and in north Africa and the Middle East (4348.9 per 100 000), as well as in Australasia, Tropical Latin America and high-income North America [[GBD2019Mental]]
- By age, depressive disorders were the sixth-ranked cause of DALYs in adults aged 25–49 years in 2019 and were in the top ten for ages 10–24; among females aged 10–24, headache disorders, depressive disorders and anxiety disorders were the top three causes [[GBD2019Diseases]]
- GBD 2021: among the 25 leading Level 3 causes, age-standardised DALY rates between 2010 and 2021 rose most for anxiety disorders (16.7%), depressive disorders (16.4%, 95% UI 11.9–21.3) and diabetes (14.0%) [[GBD2021Diseases]]
- GBD 2023: 1.17 billion (1.06–1.31) prevalent cases of mental disorders in 2023; mental disorders caused 171 million DALYs (6.1% of all-cause DALYs, the fifth leading cause, up from 12th in 1990) and were the leading cause of YLDs (17.3%) [[GBD2023Mental]]
- GBD 2023: major depressive disorder ranked 15th for DALYs among 304 Level 4 diseases and injuries (anxiety disorders 11th, schizophrenia 41st); mental disorder DALY rates were higher in females and peaked at ages 15–19 years [[GBD2023Mental]]
- GBD 2010: depressive disorders were the second leading cause of YLDs; MDD accounted for 8.2% (5.9–10.8%) of global YLDs and 2.5% (1.9–3.2%) of global DALYs, dysthymia for 1.4% and 0.5% [[Ferrari2013]]
- GBD 2010 also attributed 16 million suicide DALYs and almost 4 million ischaemic heart disease DALYs to MDD as a risk factor, which would raise the share of depressive disorders from 3.0% to 3.8% of global DALYs [[Ferrari2013]]
- New (incident) cases of depression worldwide rose from 172 million in 1990 to 258 million in 2017, an increase of 49.86% (GBD 2017) [[Liu2020Burden]]
- A WHO report restated in a peer-reviewed source estimated that depression affected 322 million people globally in 2015, an 18.4% increase in the number of people living with it between 2005 and 2015 [[Greenberg2021]]
- WHO ranked major depression as the third cause of burden of disease worldwide in 2008 and projected that it would rank first by 2030 [[Malhi2018Lancet]]
- Earlier WHO projections (2002 baseline) placed unipolar depressive disorders among the three leading causes of burden of disease in 2030, with HIV/AIDS and ischaemic heart disease [[Mathers2006]]
- Burden estimates differ by method: one re-analysis argued that mental illness accounts for 32.4% of YLDs and 13.0% of DALYs rather than 21.2% and 7.1% [[Vigo2016]]; another estimated 418 million DALYs (16% of global DALYs) and an economic value of about US$5 trillion for 2019 [[Arias2022]]; the GBD 2019 figure is 4.9% of DALYs [[GBD2019Mental]]

### what · The COVID-19 pandemic
- The COVID-19 Mental Disorders Collaborators estimated an additional 53.2 million (44.8–62.9) cases of major depressive disorder globally in 2020 due to the pandemic, an increase of 27.6% (25.1–30.3) [[Santomauro2021]]
- Prevalence of MDD in 2020 was 2470.5 per 100 000 (193 million people) before adjustment for the pandemic and 3152.9 per 100 000 (246 million people) after it [[Santomauro2021]]
- Females had the larger increase: 35.5 million additional cases (a 29.8% increase) against 17.7 million in males (24.0%); younger age groups were more affected than older ones [[Santomauro2021]]
- MDD caused 49.4 million (33.6–68.7) DALYs globally in 2020 [[Santomauro2021]]
- In US adults, the prevalence of depression symptoms on a screening questionnaire was higher in every severity category during COVID-19 than before (for example moderate 14.8% vs 5.7%; severe 5.1% vs 0.7%), "more than 3-fold higher" overall [[Ettman2020]]
- In 29 studies of 80 879 children and adolescents in the first year of the pandemic, the pooled prevalence of clinically elevated depression symptoms was 25.2% (95% CI 21.2–29.7%), described as double pre-pandemic estimates [[Racine2021]]
- Symptom-screen figures are not diagnoses: they count people above a questionnaire cut-off, which is why they are much higher than interview-based MDD prevalence [[Ettman2020,Brody2025]]

### what · United States
- NESARC-III (36 309 adults, DSM-5): 12-month prevalence of MDD 10.4% and lifetime prevalence 20.6% [[Hasin2018]]
- In NESARC-III, odds of 12-month MDD were lower in men (OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.46–0.55) and higher in adults aged 18–29 (OR 3.0, 2.48–3.55) and in those with incomes of US$19 999 or less (OR 1.7, 1.49–2.04) [[Hasin2018]]
- Most lifetime MDD in NESARC-III was moderate (39.7%) or severe (49.5%); almost 70% of people with lifetime MDD had received some type of treatment; the anxious/distressed specifier characterised 74.6% of cases and mixed features 15.5% [[Hasin2018]]
- NCS-R (DSM-IV): lifetime prevalence 16.2% (95% CI 15.1–17.3; 32.6–35.1 million US adults) and 12-month prevalence 6.6% (5.9–7.3; 13.1–14.2 million) [[Kessler2003]]
- In NCS-R, 12-month cases were 10.4% mild, 38.6% moderate, 38.0% severe and 12.9% very severe; mean episode duration was 16 weeks; 59.3% had severe or very severe role impairment [[Kessler2003]]
- In NCS-R, 72.1% of lifetime and 78.5% of 12-month cases had another comorbid DSM-IV disorder [[Kessler2003]]
- In NCS-R, 51.6% of 12-month cases received health-care treatment for MDD, and treatment was adequate in 41.9% of those, so 21.7% of all 12-month MDD was adequately treated [[Kessler2003]]
- The earlier National Comorbidity Survey (DSM-III-R) found major depressive episode among the most common disorders; fewer than 40% of people with a lifetime disorder had ever received professional treatment [[Kessler1994NCS]]
- NSDUH: in 2020, 9.2% of Americans aged 12 or older had a past-year major depressive episode (7.3% in 2015, 8.6% in 2019); the highest rates were in young adults aged 18–25 (17.2%) and adolescents aged 12–17 (16.9%), with no change in adults aged 35 or older [[Goodwin2022Trends]]
- Depression prevalence rose significantly in the USA from 2005 to 2015, and the rise was faster in youth than in all older age groups [[Weinberger2018]]
- NHANES, August 2021–August 2023: 13.1% of people aged 12 and older had depression defined as a PHQ-9 score of 10 or more in the past 2 weeks; prevalence fell with increasing age and with increasing family income, and had risen since 2013–2014 [[Brody2025]]
- Among those screening positive in NHANES 2021–2023, 87.9% reported at least some difficulty with work, home or social activities, and 43.0% of females and 33.2% of males had received therapy or counselling in the past 12 months [[Brody2025]]

### what · Cross-national prevalence
- WHO World Mental Health surveys (18 countries, n = 89 037, DSM-IV major depressive episode): average lifetime and 12-month prevalence 14.6% and 5.5% in ten high-income countries and 11.1% and 5.9% in eight low- to middle-income countries [[Bromet2011]]
- Lifetime prevalence of a major depressive episode by country in those surveys ranged from 6.5% (Shenzhen, China) and 6.6% (Japan) to 19.2% (United States) and 21.0% (France); 12-month prevalence ranged from 2.2% (Japan) to 10.4% (São Paulo, Brazil) [[Bromet2011]]
- An earlier ten-site study (DSM-III) found lifetime rates from 1.5 per 100 adults (Taiwan) to 19.0 per 100 (Beirut) and annual rates from 0.8 (Taiwan) to 5.8 per 100 (New Zealand); rates were higher for women than men in every country [[Weissman1996]]
- Across 17 WMH countries (85 052 respondents), lifetime prevalence of any mood disorder ranged from 3.3% to 21.4% (IQR 9.8–15.8%) [[Kessler2007WMH]]
- Variation between countries may reflect both substantive and methodological differences; the cross-national data are consistent in showing meaningful lifetime prevalence, wide variation in age of onset and a high risk of chronic-recurrent persistence [[Kessler2013Cultures]]
- In high-income countries younger age was associated with higher 12-month prevalence, whereas in several low- to middle-income countries older age was; the strongest demographic correlate was separation from a partner (high-income) or being divorced or widowed (low- to middle-income) [[Bromet2011]]
- Europe (27 countries, 258 888 people): prevalence of current depressive disorder 6.38% (95% CI 6.24–6.52), from 2.58% in the Czech Republic to 10.33% in Iceland; 7.74% in women and 4.89% in men [[AriasDeLaTorre2021]]
- Canada, 2012: past-year prevalence of MDD 3.9% (95% CI 3.5–4.2%); among those receiving treatment, 37.5% perceived that others held negative opinions about them or treated them unfairly because of the disorder [[Patten2015]]

### what · Sex ratio and when it emerges
- Meta-analysis of nationally representative samples (65 articles, 1 716 195 people for diagnoses): odds ratio for major depression in females vs males 1.95 (95% CI 1.88–2.03); for symptoms d = 0.27 [[Salk2017]]
- The gender difference in diagnoses was present by age 12 (OR 2.37), peaked at ages 13–15 (OR 3.02), then declined and remained stable in adulthood [[Salk2017]]
- Larger gender differences in major depression were found in nations with greater gender equity; the authors add that the size of the difference means "depression in men should not be overlooked" [[Salk2017]]
- The female:male ratio was about 2:1 across the WMH countries [[Bromet2011]]
- Women are about twice as likely as men to develop depression during their lifetime; a developmental subtype is proposed as the strongest contributor to the gap, and evidence that any risk factor is specific to depression is limited (Early evidence) [[Kuehner2017]]
- Across WMH cohorts, gender differences in major depression narrowed in recent cohorts, and the narrowing was related to changes in the traditionality of female gender roles [[Seedat2009]]

### what · Age of onset
- Meta-analysis of 192 studies (n = 708 561): for depressive disorders (62 studies) the peak age at onset was 19.5 years and the median 30 years (25th–75th percentile 21–44) [[Solmi2022]]
- The proportion of people whose depressive disorder had begun before ages 14, 18 and 25 was 3.1%, 13.2% and 36.9% [[Solmi2022]]
- For mood disorders as a block: 2.5%, 11.5% and 34.5% before ages 14, 18 and 25; peak 20.5 years; median 31 years (IQR 21–46) [[Solmi2022]]
- WMH surveys: median age of onset of a major depressive episode 25.7 years (IQR 17.3–37.2) in high-income and 24.0 years (17.0–34.8) in low- to middle-income countries, ascertained retrospectively [[Bromet2011]]
- NCS-R: median age of onset 30 years for mood disorders, against 11 years for anxiety and impulse-control disorders and 20 years for substance use disorders [[Kessler2005Onset]]
- Mean age at onset of major depression varied little across ten countries (range 24.8–34.8 years) [[Weissman1996]]

### what · Children and adolescents
- Worldwide pooled prevalence of any depressive disorder in children and adolescents: 2.6% (95% CI 1.7–3.9), from 41 studies in 27 countries [[Polanczyk2015]]
- Adolescents worldwide: point prevalence of MDD 8% (95% CI 2–13%), one-year prevalence 8% (5–12%) and lifetime prevalence 19% (12–26%) [[Shorey2022]]
- Point prevalence of elevated self-reported depressive symptoms in adolescents rose from 24% (2001–2010) to 37% (2011–2020) [[Shorey2022]]
- US adolescents aged 12–17: 12-month prevalence of major depressive episode rose from 8.7% in 2005 to 11.3% in 2014; in young adults aged 18–25 from 8.8% to 9.6%, with little change in treatment [[Mojtabai2016]]
- US adolescents, 2009–2019: past-year major depressive episode rose from 8.1% to 15.8%; among girls from 11.4% to 23.4%; the gap between girls and boys widened from 6.4 to 14.8 percentage points [[Daly2022]]
- NSDUH (n = 611 880): past-year major depressive episode rose 52% from 2005 to 2017 in adolescents (8.7% to 13.2%) and 63% from 2009 to 2017 in young adults (8.1% to 13.2%); the authors attribute adult trends mainly to birth cohort (Early evidence for the proposed causes) [[Twenge2019]]

### what · Treatment gap
- WMH surveys, 21 countries (n = 51 547): 4.6% met 12-month criteria for MDD; of these 56.7% reported needing treatment; of those, 71.1% made at least one visit to a provider; of those treated, 41.0% received treatment meeting minimal standards [[Thornicroft2017]]
- Result: 16.5% of all people with 12-month MDD received minimally adequate treatment, "1 in 5 people in high-income and 1 in 27 in low-/lower-middle-income countries" [[Thornicroft2017]]
- Meta-regression of 149 studies (84 countries, 2000–2019): treatment coverage for any health service use ranged from 51% (95% UI 20–82%) in high-income locations to 20% (1–53%) in low- and lower-middle-income locations; mental health service use was 33% (8–66%) in high-income locations [[Moitra2022]]
- Meta-analysis of 65 studies (1.1 million participants, 79 countries): pooled treatment rate 34.8% (95% CI 29.9–39.9%): 48.3% in high-income, 21.4% in middle-income and 16.8% in low-income countries; among those treated, about 40% received minimally adequate treatment [[Mekonen2021]]
- Modelled estimates for 204 countries in 2021: 9.1% (95% UI 7.2–11.6) of people with MDD received minimally adequate treatment (females 10.2%, males 7.2%); 27.0% in high-income locations and 2.0% in sub-Saharan Africa; seven countries exceeded 30% and 90 countries were below 5% [[Santomauro2024Coverage]]
- An earlier review of 37 studies put the median treatment gap (share untreated) for major depression at 56.3% [[Kohn2004]]
- In the USA, antidepressant treatment rose from 5.84% of the population in 1996 to 10.12% in 2005 (13.3 to 27.0 million people), while the share of antidepressant users also receiving psychotherapy fell from 31.50% to 19.87% [[Olfson2009]]

### what · Economic burden
- The incremental economic burden of US adults with MDD rose 37.9%, from US$236.6 billion in 2010 to US$326.2 billion in 2018 (year 2020 values) [[Greenberg2021]]
- The number of US adults with MDD rose 12.9%, from 15.5 to 17.5 million, between 2010 and 2018; the share aged 18–34 rose from 34.6% to 47.5% [[Greenberg2021]]
- Composition of 2018 costs: 35% direct costs, 4% suicide-related costs, 61% workplace costs (2010: 47%, 5%, 48%); 37% of total costs were attributable to MDD itself as opposed to comorbid conditions [[Greenberg2021]]
- The preceding estimate was US$173.2 billion in 2005 and US$210.5 billion in 2010 (inflation-adjusted, year 2012 values), an increase of 21.5% [[Greenberg2015]]
- Global return on investment: scaling up treatment for depression and anxiety over 2016–2030 would cost US$147 billion and yield 43 million extra years of healthy life; benefit-to-cost ratios were 2.3–3.0 to 1 on economic benefits alone and 3.3–5.7 to 1 with the value of health returns included [[Chisholm2016]]

### what · Excess mortality
- Depression and mortality (293 studies, 1 813 733 participants, 35 countries): unadjusted relative risk of death 1.64 (95% CI 1.56–1.76) for people with depression vs without, 1.52 (1.45–1.59) after adjustment for publication bias [[Cuijpers2014Mortality]]
- The association was similar in community samples and in samples of people with heart disease, cancer, kidney disease or other disease; the authors caution that it "may have been overestimated because of publication bias and low study quality" [[Cuijpers2014Mortality]]
- All mental disorders (148 studies): pooled relative risk of all-cause mortality 2.22 (95% CI 2.12–2.33); 67.3% of deaths were from natural causes and 17.5% from unnatural causes; median years of potential life lost 10 years [[Walker2015]]
- The same review estimated that 14.3% of deaths worldwide, about 8 million a year, are attributable to mental disorders [[Walker2015]]
- Danish register cohort (7 369 926 people): mortality rate ratio 1.92 (95% CI 1.91–1.94) for mood disorders, the lowest of the disorder groups studied (substance use disorders 3.91) [[PlanaRipoll2019]]
- A meta-review of 20 systematic reviews (over 1.7 million patients) found raised all-cause mortality in every mental disorder studied, and listed depression among the disorders with the highest suicide risk [[Chesney2014]]
- GBD counts almost no deaths as caused directly by mental disorders, so its years-of-life-lost figures "do not reflect premature mortality in individuals with mental disorders" [[GBD2019Mental]]

### what · Recovery is common
- STAR*D (US outpatients, up to four treatment steps): QIDS-SR remission rates were 36.8%, 30.6%, 13.7% and 13.0% for steps 1–4, and the overall cumulative remission rate was 67% [[Rush2006STARD]]
- STAR*D step 1 (citalopram, 2 876 outpatients): remission 28% (HAM-D) and 33% (QIDS-SR), response 47%; many who responded or remitted did so at or after 8 weeks [[Trivedi2006STARD]]
- Without treatment, a model based on adults recruited in primary care estimated that 23% of prevalent cases remit within 3 months, 32% within 6 months and 53% within 12 months; the authors note the data over-represent mild-to-moderate depression [[Whiteford2013Remission]]
- Network meta-analysis of 522 trials (116 477 participants): all 21 antidepressants were more effective than placebo in adults with MDD, with odds ratios from 1.37 (reboxetine) to 2.13 (amitriptyline) [[Cipriani2018]]
- Network meta-analysis of 331 trials (34 285 patients): all psychotherapies studied were more efficacious than care as usual and waiting list, with standardised mean differences vs care as usual from −0.32 to −0.81 [[Cuijpers2021Psychotherapies]]
- These are group averages from trials; they describe populations and do not predict one person's course [[Rush2006STARD,Cipriani2018]]

### history · Melancholia from antiquity to the nineteenth century
- Hippocrates (460–379 BC) attributed the melancholic state to an excess of black bile, one of four humours (blood, black bile, yellow bile, phlegm); the states were marked above all by fear and sadness [[TellesCorreia2015]]
- Galen (129–216 AD) kept the humoral theory and described a melancholic temperament from black bile; "melancholia" is the Greek name for black bile [[TellesCorreia2015]]
- The early meaning of melancholia was very wide and "included all forms of quiet insanity" [[Paykel2008]]
- Aretaeus of Cappadocia (first century AD) is perhaps the first to have described mania and melancholia as two states of one disease [[Angst2001]]
- Robert Burton's The Anatomy of Melancholy was first published in 1621 and linked melancholia with the more modern idea of melancholy or despair [[Paykel2008,TellesCorreia2015]]
- From the 1780s to the 1830s authors including Cullen, Pinel and Esquirol defined melancholia as a disorder of intellect or judgement, a "partial insanity" often but not always associated with sadness [[Kendler2020Melancholia]]
- In the 1850s Guislain and Bucknill and Tuke described melancholia without delusions as a primary disorder of mood; by the 1860s–1880s Griesinger, Sankey, Maudsley, Krafft-Ebing and Kraepelin all treated melancholia as a primary mood disorder [[Kendler2020Melancholia]]
- The term "depression" began to appear in the nineteenth century to indicate a state of sadness, as melancholia was reconceptualised as a modern mood disorder [[Paykel2008,Jansson2022]]
- The alternation of melancholia and mania was first clearly described in 1854, independently, by the French psychiatrists Falret and Baillarger [[Paykel2008]]

### history · Kraepelin, Freud and the unipolar–bipolar split
- In 1899 Emil Kraepelin unified all types of affective disorder in "manic-depressive insanity", and the unitary concept was adopted worldwide [[Angst2001]]
- Kraepelin's textbook ran to nine editions and revisions between 1883 and 1926, and his concept of manic-depressive insanity evolved gradually across them [[Trede2005]]
- Kraepelin's late descriptions of the symptoms and signs of melancholia and mania align closely with those of DSM-5 [[Kendler2021]]
- Sigmund Freud drafted "Mourning and Melancholia" in 1915 and published it in 1917; with Abraham he proposed that depression arises in relation to actual or symbolic loss of a love object [[May2019,Freud1917,Paykel2008]]
- Debate about psychotic vs neurotic and endogenous vs reactive depression began in the 1920s and produced much multivariate research in the 1960s [[Paykel2008]]
- Karl Leonhard introduced the term "bipolarity", as distinct from unipolarity; in the 1960s Jules Angst, Carlo Perris and George Winokur independently showed clinical, familial and course differences that validated separating unipolar from bipolar disorders [[Shorter2015,Angst2001]]
- Kraepelin's manic-depressive concept was carried through the first DSM editions until DSM-III built a separate diagnosis of bipolar disorder [[Mason2016]]

### history · Electroconvulsive therapy
- Four somatic treatments were developed in the 1930s: insulin coma therapy, Metrazol convulsive therapy, lobotomy and electroconvulsive therapy (ECT); ECT is the only one still in use [[Endler1988]]
- Convulsive therapy was introduced to psychiatric practice in 1934 and was quickly recognised as effective in affective disorders [[Fink2001]]
- ECT was developed by Ugo Cerletti and Lucio Bini at the Clinic for Nervous and Mental Disorders in Rome in 1938; Bini reported it at the 3rd International Neurological Congress in Copenhagen in August 1939 [[Endler1988,Faedda2010]]
- ECT was displaced by the psychotropic drugs of the 1950s and 1960s and was recalled two decades later for illness that had not responded to medicines [[Fink2001]]
- Modern evidence: real ECT was more effective than simulated ECT (6 trials, 256 patients, standardised effect size −0.91, 95% CI −1.27 to −0.54) and than pharmacotherapy (18 trials, 1144 participants, −0.80, −1.29 to −0.29) [[UKECT2003]]

### history · The first antidepressants and the monoamine hypotheses
- Iproniazid and imipramine, the prototypes of the monoamine oxidase inhibitor and reuptake inhibitor antidepressants, were introduced in 1957 [[Ban2001]]
- Iproniazid was derived from the antituberculosis drug isoniazid; its use in depression followed three unexpected observations: MAO inhibition, reversal of reserpine-induced sedation, and psychostimulation as a clinical side effect [[Pletscher1991]]
- Loomer, Saunders and Kline published their evaluation of iproniazid as a "psychic energizer" in 1957 [[Loomer1957]]
- Imipramine came from the antihistamine and chlorpromazine chemical line; Roland Kuhn, a Swiss psychiatrist, identified its antidepressant effect by open clinical observation, and his English-language report appeared in 1958 [[Brown2015Imipramine,Kuhn1958,Pletscher1991]]
- Imipramine (Tofranil) NDA 011838 was approved in the USA on 2 October 1959 [[FDA-DrugsAtFDA]]
- The early MAO inhibitors were curtailed by adverse effects such as potentiation of the blood-pressure-raising action of food amines [[Pletscher1991]]
- Joseph Schildkraut published "The catecholamine hypothesis of affective disorders" in 1965 and Alec Coppen "The biochemistry of affective disorders" in 1967, the reference papers for the noradrenaline and serotonin versions of the monoamine hypothesis [[Schildkraut1965,Coppen1967]]
- The monoamine hypothesis proposes a deficiency of central noradrenergic and/or serotonergic systems; a 2000 review called it "clearly inadequate" in its original form because the pathophysiology of depression remains unknown and the clinical response is gradual while the rise in monoamines is rapid (Hypothesis) [[Hirschfeld2000Monoamine]]
- Iproniazid and imipramine were also research tools that allowed the first aetiopathogenic hypotheses of depression to be put forward [[LopezMunoz2009]]
- A 2022 umbrella review of 17 studies concluded that the main areas of serotonin research "provide no consistent evidence of there being an association between serotonin and depression, and no support for the hypothesis that depression is caused by lowered serotonin activity or concentrations" (Established for the absence of consistent evidence) [[Moncrieff2022Serotonin]]
- That conclusion is about cause, not about treatment: in randomised trials all 21 antidepressants studied were more efficacious than placebo in adults with MDD [[Cipriani2018]]

### history · Rating scales, cognitive therapy and diagnostic criteria
- Max Hamilton published "A rating scale for depression" in 1960 [[Hamilton1960]]
- Aaron Beck and colleagues published "An inventory for measuring depression" (the Beck Depression Inventory) in 1961, and Beck's "Thinking and depression: idiosyncratic content and cognitive distortions" followed in 1963 [[Beck1961,Beck1963]]
- The Montgomery–Åsberg scale (1979) took the 10 items that changed most with treatment from a 65-item scale, to be more sensitive to change than the Hamilton scale [[Montgomery1979]]
- The PHQ-9 (2001) scores each of the 9 DSM-IV criteria from 0 to 3; in 580 primary care and obstetrics-gynaecology patients a score of 10 or more had a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 88% for major depression against a clinician interview. It is a screening and severity measure scored by clinicians, not a self-diagnosis [[Kroenke2001PHQ9]]
- The Feighner criteria were published in 1972 by a Washington University (St Louis) group led by Eli Robins; for depression they required a dysphoric mood plus five of eight other criteria [[Feighner1972,Kendler2010Feighner,Shorter2015]]
- The Feighner criteria formed the basis of the Research Diagnostic Criteria (1978), which in turn were central to DSM-III [[Kendler2010Feighner,Spitzer1978RDC]]
- DSM-I appeared in 1952, DSM-II in 1968 and DSM-III in 1980; DSM-III replaced broad, aetiologically defined entities with symptom-based categorical diagnoses [[Shorter2015,Mayes2005]]
- DSM-III merged psychiatry's traditional two depressions (melancholia and non-melancholia) into one "major depression" and separated it from bipolar disorder [[Shorter2015]]
- During the 1950s and 1960s anxiety was the emblematic mental health problem in the United States and depression was considered rare; DSM-III's definition of major depressive disorder encompassed far more patients than any single anxiety disorder [[Horwitz2010]]
- The 1980s brought defined criteria into the official diagnostic schemes (DSM-III and later ICD-10) [[Paykel2008]]

### history · Fluoxetine, the SSRI era and the boxed warning
- Work on selective inhibitors of serotonin uptake began in the early 1970s and led to fluoxetine, "approved for the treatment of depression by the US FDA in 1987" [[Wong2005]]
- Fluoxetine (Prozac, NDA 018936) was approved on 29 December 1987 and sertraline (Zoloft, NDA 019839) on 30 December 1991 [[FDA-DrugsAtFDA]]
- The clinical introduction of fluoxetine in the late 1980s opened the way for new families of antidepressants, all of which still act by modulating monoaminergic neurotransmission [[LopezMunoz2009]]
- In October 2003 the FDA issued a public health advisory about the risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviour in paediatric patients taking SSRIs for depression [[Libby2007]]
- The FDA analysis behind the warning pooled 24 placebo-controlled paediatric trials (4582 patients): the risk ratio for suicidal ideation or behaviour was 1.66 (95% CI 1.02–2.68) for SSRIs in depression trials and 1.95 (1.28–2.98) for all drugs across indications, a risk difference of 0.02; no deaths by suicide occurred in any of these trials [[Hammad2006]]
- A black-box warning focused on children and adolescents younger than 18 followed in 2005 and was expanded in 2007 to include young adults [[Soumerai2024]]
- The warning is usually dated to the FDA's announcement in October 2004, which is why a 2014 NEJM perspective is titled "10 years later"; the October 2004 date was not confirmed against an FDA document [[Friedman2014]] (unverified)
- In adults the effect is age-dependent: in 372 trials (99 231 adults), odds ratios for suicidal behaviour or ideation on antidepressant vs placebo were 1.62 (0.97–2.71) under age 25, 0.79 (0.64–0.98) at ages 25–64 and 0.37 (0.18–0.76) at 65 and older [[Stone2009]]
- Current labels carry the class warning: antidepressants "increased the risk of suicidal thoughts and behavior in children, adolescents, and young adults in short-term studies", with no increase over age 24 and a reduction at 65 and older, and call for close monitoring of people of all ages starting treatment [[FDA-Prozac,FDA-Zoloft]]
- After the advisory, paediatric diagnoses of depression fell back to 1999 levels and SSRI prescription fills were 58% lower than the prior trend predicted, with no compensating rise in psychotherapy [[Libby2007]]
- In the second year after the warnings, antidepressant use fell by 31.0% in adolescents, 24.3% in young adults and 14.5% in adults relative to trend [[Lu2014]]
- A 2024 systematic review of 34 studies (11 meeting design criteria) found no evidence that monitoring increased and found unintended reductions in mental health care after the warnings; the authors call for the warnings to be re-evaluated (Early evidence) [[Soumerai2024]]

### history · STAR*D, rapid-acting treatments and brain stimulation (2000s–2020s)
- STAR*D reported its level 1 results in January 2006 and its summary of all four steps in November 2006 [[Trivedi2006STARD,Rush2006STARD]]
- A 2000 proof-of-concept trial gave intravenous ketamine 0.5 mg/kg or saline to seven people with major depression in a research setting: Hamilton scores fell by 14 points vs 0 within 72 hours [[Berman2000]]
- A 2006 NIMH crossover trial in 18 people with treatment-resistant depression found improvement within 110 minutes of a single supervised ketamine infusion; 71% met response criteria the next day and 35% maintained response for at least 1 week [[Zarate2006]]
- Ketamine and esketamine are supervised medical or research treatments; the esketamine label carries boxed warnings for sedation, dissociation, respiratory depression, and abuse and misuse, requires monitoring for at least two hours after each dose, and restricts supply to a REMS programme [[FDA-Spravato]]
- Esketamine nasal spray (Spravato, NDA 211243) was approved on 5 March 2019; in TRANSFORM-2 (227 randomised adults) the change in MADRS score at day 28 favoured esketamine plus a new oral antidepressant by 4.0 points (95% CI −7.31 to −0.64) [[FDA-DrugsAtFDA,Popova2019TRANSFORM2]]
- Esketamine supplements were approved on 31 July 2020 and 17 January 2025; the current label covers treatment-resistant depression in adults as monotherapy or with an oral antidepressant, and depressive symptoms in adults with MDD with acute suicidal ideation or behaviour [[FDA-DrugsAtFDA,FDA-Spravato]]
- Brexanolone (Zulresso, NDA 211371), a 60-hour intravenous infusion for postpartum depression, was approved on 19 March 2019 and is now listed as discontinued; in its phase 3 trials the HAM-D reduction at 60 hours was 17.7 points (90 μg/kg/h) vs 14.0 with placebo in study 1 and 14.6 vs 12.1 in study 2 [[FDA-DrugsAtFDA,MeltzerBrody2018]]
- Zuranolone (Zurzuvae, NDA 217369), an oral 14-day course for postpartum depression in adults, was approved on 4 August 2023; in SKYLARK (196 women) the HAM-D change at day 15 was −15.6 vs −11.6 (difference −4.0, 95% CI −6.3 to −1.7) [[FDA-DrugsAtFDA,FDA-Zurzuvae,Deligiannidis2023SKYLARK]]
- Dextromethorphan–bupropion (Auvelity, NDA 215430) was approved on 18 August 2022 and gepirone (Exxua, NDA 021164) on 22 September 2023 [[FDA-DrugsAtFDA]]
- Transcranial magnetic stimulation: a sham-controlled trial in 301 medication-free patients reported in 2007, and the first TMS device record for depression in the FDA database (NeuroStar TMS System, de novo DEN070003) has a decision date of 7 October 2008 [[OReardon2007,FDA-DEN070003]]
- Stanford neuromodulation therapy (SNT, formerly SAINT): in a sham-controlled trial of 29 participants with treatment-resistant depression, the mean MADRS reduction 4 weeks after a 5-day course was 52.5% with active and 11.1% with sham treatment; the Magnus Neuromodulation System with SAINT Technology (K220177) was cleared on 1 September 2022 [[Cole2022SNT,FDA-K220177]]
- Since the 2000 ketamine study, glutamatergic systems have been examined as treatment targets beyond the monoamines (Early evidence) [[Hillhouse2015]]

### Exam hooks
- DSM-5-TR major depressive episode: at least 5 of 9 symptoms in the same 2 weeks, one of which must be depressed mood or anhedonia [[APA2022DSM5TR]]
- ICD-11 depressive episode: at least 5 of 10 symptoms for at least 2 weeks; the extra symptom is hopelessness [[Stein2020BMC]]
- ICD-11 codes: 6A70 single episode depressive disorder; 6A71 recurrent depressive disorder [[Shevlin2023]]
- Dysthymia/persistent depressive disorder: fewer symptoms, at least 2 years [[Paykel2008,Schramm2020]]
- About 280 million people (279.6 million) lived with a depressive disorder in 2019 [[GBD2019Mental]]
- Depressive disorders: second leading cause of YLDs and 13th leading cause of DALYs in 2019 [[GBD2019Mental]]
- COVID-19: +27.6% cases of MDD in 2020 (an extra 53.2 million) [[Santomauro2021]]
- US adults: 12-month 10.4% and lifetime 20.6% (NESARC-III, DSM-5); lifetime 16.2% in NCS-R (DSM-IV) [[Hasin2018,Kessler2003]]
- Female:male odds ratio 1.95 overall; the gap is present by age 12 and peaks at 13–15 (OR 3.02) [[Salk2017]]
- Depressive disorders: peak onset 19.5 years, median 30 years [[Solmi2022]]
- Minimally adequate treatment reaches 1 in 5 people with MDD in high-income and 1 in 27 in low-/lower-middle-income countries [[Thornicroft2017]]
- US economic burden of MDD: US$326.2 billion in 2018, 61% of it workplace costs [[Greenberg2021]]
- Relative risk of death with depression: 1.52 after adjustment for publication bias [[Cuijpers2014Mortality]]
- STAR*D cumulative remission over four steps: 67%; step 1 remission 36.8% (QIDS-SR) [[Rush2006STARD]]
- Kraepelin, 1899: manic-depressive insanity; Angst and Perris, 1960s: unipolar vs bipolar [[Angst2001,Paykel2008]]
- ECT: Cerletti and Bini, Rome, 1938 [[Endler1988]]
- 1957: iproniazid (MAOI) and imipramine (tricyclic), both found by clinical observation [[Ban2001,Pletscher1991]]
- 1965 Schildkraut (catecholamines); 1967 Coppen (serotonin); 2022 umbrella review: no consistent evidence that low serotonin causes depression, while antidepressants still outperform placebo [[Schildkraut1965,Coppen1967,Moncrieff2022Serotonin,Cipriani2018]]
- DSM-III, 1980: operational criteria and a single "major depression" [[Shorter2015]]
- Fluoxetine approved 29 December 1987; esketamine 5 March 2019; zuranolone 4 August 2023 [[FDA-DrugsAtFDA]]

### In plain words
- Depression is a common illness, not a weakness or a character flaw. About one in six adults has it at some point in life [[Otte2016MDD]]
- Doctors diagnose it from a pattern: low mood or loss of interest, with several other symptoms, most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks [[APA2022DSM5TR,Stein2020BMC]]
- Sadness after a loss or a hard event is part of life. Depression is different in how severe it is, how many symptoms there are and how long it lasts [[Stein2020BMC]]
- Around 280 million people in the world were living with a depressive disorder in 2019 [[GBD2019Mental]]
- Depression is about twice as common in women as in men, but it is common in men too and should not be missed [[Salk2017]]
- It often starts young: for about one person in three it has begun by age 25 [[Solmi2022]]
- The pandemic year 2020 added about 53 million cases worldwide [[Santomauro2021]]
- Most people get better. In a large US study, about two in three people reached remission across up to four treatment steps [[Rush2006STARD]]
- Many people who need treatment do not get it: in rich countries about 1 in 5 gets adequate care, and in poorer countries about 1 in 27 [[Thornicroft2017]]
- Both talking therapies and antidepressant medicines work better than no treatment in trials. These are averages for groups, so the best plan is made with a clinician [[Cipriani2018,Cuijpers2021Psychotherapies]]
- The idea that depression is simply "low serotonin" is not supported by the evidence. That does not mean antidepressants do not work: trials show they do [[Moncrieff2022Serotonin,Cipriani2018]]
- Thoughts of suicide can be a symptom of depression, and they can be treated. Medicine labels ask for close follow-up when treatment starts, especially in young people [[FDA-Prozac]]
- Questionnaires such as the PHQ-9 are tools clinicians use to screen and to track symptoms. A score is not a diagnosis; talk to a clinician [[Kroenke2001PHQ9]]
- People have described this illness for more than 2000 years, first as "melancholia" [[TellesCorreia2015]]

## Open questions and controversies
- Is depression becoming more common? Age-standardised global prevalence of depressive disorders was flat from 1990 to 2019 (3486.2 vs 3440.1 per 100 000) [[GBD2019Mental]]; GBD 2021 and GBD 2023 report rising age-standardised rates since 2010 and since 1990 [[GBD2021Diseases,GBD2023Mental]]; US surveys show steep rises in adolescents and young adults [[Daly2022,Goodwin2022Trends]]. Status: unresolved; estimates depend on the pandemic adjustment and on survey methods.
- Why are adolescent rates rising in the USA? Cohort effects linked by the authors to digital media and shorter sleep [[Twenge2019]]; others document the trend without assigning a cause [[Mojtabai2016,Weinberger2018]]. Status: causes not established (Hypothesis).
- How large is the true burden? 4.9% of global DALYs in GBD 2019 [[GBD2019Mental]]; 13.0% [[Vigo2016]] or 16% [[Arias2022]] when suicide, chronic pain overlap and excess mortality are reallocated to mental illness. Status: a methods dispute; GBD remains the reference series.
- Why do country prevalences differ so much (6.6% to 21.0% lifetime)? Real differences in risk vs differences in reporting, thresholds and instruments [[Bromet2011,Kessler2013Cultures,Weissman1996]]. Status: unresolved.
- Why is depression more common in women? Developmental, biological and social explanations are all proposed, with limited evidence for depression-specific risk factors [[Kuehner2017]]; larger gaps in more gender-equal nations [[Salk2017]] but narrowing gaps in recent cohorts as gender roles change [[Seedat2009]]. Status: unresolved.
- Bereavement: DSM-5 removed the exclusion [[Zisook2012]]; ICD-11 kept bereavement in its exclusion wording and cites prospective data against the DSM-5 position [[Stein2020BMC]]. Status: the two classifications differ.
- Does DSM-III's single "major depression" lump different illnesses together? It merged melancholia and non-melancholic depression [[Shorter2015]] and extended the category to many more people [[Horwitz2010]]; DSM criteria capture only part of the classical clinical picture [[Kendler2016Phenomenology]]. Status: ongoing debate about heterogeneity.
- Serotonin: no consistent evidence that depression is caused by low serotonin [[Moncrieff2022Serotonin]]; the monoamine hypothesis was already judged inadequate in its original form in 2000 [[Hirschfeld2000Monoamine]]; antidepressant efficacy over placebo is established in trials [[Cipriani2018]]. Status: a simple deficiency model is not supported; how antidepressants work is not settled.
- Did the antidepressant boxed warning do more good than harm? Trial data show a small age-dependent increase in suicidal thoughts and behaviour under 25 [[Hammad2006,Stone2009]]; observational studies report reduced diagnosis and treatment afterwards [[Libby2007,Lu2014,Soumerai2024]]. Status: contested; the warning remains on labels [[FDA-Prozac]].
- How should spontaneous remission shape treatment-gap targets? About half of untreated cases in milder samples remit within 12 months [[Whiteford2013Remission]], while fewer than 1 in 10 people with MDD worldwide receive minimally adequate treatment [[Santomauro2024Coverage]]. Status: coverage targets are being redefined.

- Validation: every PubMed-indexed source was checked on 2026-11-13 with NCBI E-utilities esummary (authors, title, journal, year, volume, pages, PMID, PMCID, DOI). The source lines were generated from those records, not typed from memory. Open-access full text (Europe PMC fullTextXML) was read for GBD2019Mental (table 1 and results), GBD2019Diseases, Santomauro2021, Bromet2011 (table 2), Solmi2022 (table 2), Greenberg2021, Goodwin2022Trends, Stein2020BMC, Shevlin2023, Paykel2008, TellesCorreia2015 and Shorter2015.

- openFDA validated the four drug labels (label endpoint: indication and boxed-warning text read), the approval dates (drugsfda endpoint: ORIG-1 approval date for each NDA; Zurzuvae's approved original is ORIG-2) and the two device records (510k endpoint).

What these papers said is taken from the secondary sources cited beside them (Ban2001, Pletscher1991, Brown2015Imipramine, Hirschfeld2000Monoamine, Kendler2010Feighner, Shorter2015). - Not accessible, so no numbers taken: Herrman 2022 Lancet–WPA Commission (no abstract, no open full text), Friedrich 2017 JAMA news item (no abstract), WHO 2017 "Depression and other common mental disorders" (not indexed; its 322 million figure is quoted as restated in Greenberg2021), the WHO depression fact sheet, the icd.who.int browser, and the full text of Kessler2013Cultures, Hasin2018, Salk2017, Thornicroft2017 and Mojtabai2016 (abstract only).
