# F. Breathing, nutrition & symptom care
This pass covers respiratory muscle weakness in ALS, the tests used to follow it, published triggers for non-invasive ventilation (NIV), the NIV evidence, cough augmentation, diaphragm pacing, tracheostomy ventilation and withdrawal of ventilation; then weight, hypermetabolism, dysphagia, gastrostomy (timing, method, ProGas), high-calorie diet trials and texture modification; and briefly the published treatments for sialorrhoea, thick secretions, pseudobulbar affect, cramps, spasticity, pain, fatigue, sleep, mood and constipation, plus multidisciplinary clinic care. It reports what guidelines, trials and reviews say; nothing here is advice to a reader. Access limits: the EFNS 2012 guideline full text (Wiley) returned HTTP 403, so its NIV and gastrostomy thresholds are given as tabulated by the 2019 review of Dorst and Ludolph and as quoted in ProGas and ESPEN 2018, and are flagged as such. The pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov website served a browser check to command-line requests, which was not bypassed; those PMC articles were read via WebFetch or the Europe PMC full-text API instead. The AAN 1999 parameter (retired) had no abstract and was not read beyond its record. The LIPCAL-ALS full text (Ann Neurol, paywalled) was not read, so the fast-progressor post hoc result is given only as the abstract states it. NICE NG42 recommendations and update history were read on nice.org.uk; the APM 2015 withdrawal guidance was read as the November 2015 PDF; ESPEN 2018 was read in full from the University of Geneva open archive.

## Facts

### Respiratory muscles and symptoms
- Phrenic motor neurons, which supply the diaphragm (described as the major muscle of breathing), lie in the ventral horn of the mid-cervical spinal cord at C3-C5; accessory respiratory muscles are recruited to compensate when the primary respiratory muscles weaken. [Fusco2019]
- Respiratory failure is described as the leading cause of death in ALS, and most deaths are from respiratory complications. [Fusco2019; Niedermeyer2019; Radunovic2017]
- An effective cough needs a deep inspiration (inspiratory muscles), brief glottic closure (laryngeal adductors) while expiratory muscles and the diaphragm build intrathoracic pressure, then rapid glottic opening giving a 30-50 ms burst of expiratory flow; people with ALS often show a prolonged inspiratory phase, reduced flows and slower cough "spikes". [Berlowitz2023]
- Pharyngeal, laryngeal and respiratory muscles work together to keep the upper airway open, and upper airway activity precedes phrenic output; bulbar weakness therefore affects airway patency, cough and swallowing as well as speech. [Berlowitz2023]
- NICE NG42 Table 1 lists symptoms of potential respiratory impairment: breathlessness, orthopnoea, recurrent chest infections, disturbed sleep, non-refreshing sleep, nightmares, daytime sleepiness, poor concentration and/or memory, confusion, hallucinations, morning headaches, fatigue and poor appetite; signs listed are increased respiratory rate, shallow breathing, weak cough, weak sniff, abdominal paradox, use of accessory muscles and reduced chest expansion. [NICE2016]
- The AAN 2009 respiratory algorithm lists frequent arousals, morning headaches, excessive daytime sleepiness and vivid dreams as symptoms suggestive of nocturnal hypoventilation. [Miller2009AAN]
- NICE NG42 lists bulbar presentation, weight loss, poor respiratory function, older age, lower ALSFRS/ALSFRS-R score and shorter time from symptom onset to diagnosis as prognostic factors associated with shorter survival when present at diagnosis. [NICE2016]

### Measurements and their prognostic value
- NICE NG42 sets baseline tests at or soon after diagnosis: a single resting room-air SpO2, plus FVC or VC and/or SNIP and/or MIP; tests are repeated every 2-3 months, more or less often depending on symptoms, rate of progression and the person's preference [2010 recommendations, amended 2016]. [NICE2016]
- NICE NG42 says that with severe bulbar impairment or severe cognitive problems, SpO2 should still be measured but FVC, VC, SNIP and MIP not performed if interfaces are unsuitable; an October 2022 update added that pulse oximeters may overestimate saturation in people with dark skin. [NICE2016]
- The Canadian 2020 recommendations call for respiratory monitoring at baseline and every 3 months, with symptom review (dyspnoea, orthopnoea, morning headache), sitting FVC or slow VC, one or more of SNIP, supine FVC or MIP, blood gas or transcutaneous CO2 when hypercapnia is suspected, peak cough flow, and nocturnal oximetry or polysomnography when sleep-disordered breathing is suspected. [Shoesmith2020]
- In 25 people with ALS who had transdiaphragmatic pressure (Pdi) measured, supine FVC correlated best with Pdi (R2 = 0.76), and supine FVC below 75% predicted was 100% sensitive and specific for an abnormally low Pdi (<70 cmH2O); accessory muscle use had 84% sensitivity and 100% specificity. [Lechtzin2002]
- The AAN 2009 update cites studies showing that the difference between erect and supine FVC correlated with orthopnoea, and that 13 of 20 patients with FVC >70% already had an abnormal MIP (< -60 cmH2O). [Miller2009AAN]
- A commonly quoted rule that a supine fall in FVC of more than 10-20% indicates diaphragm weakness was not found as a stated threshold in any source read in this pass (unverified). [Lechtzin2002; Miller2009AAN]
- In 98 people followed 3-monthly for 3 years, SNIP correlated with Pdi (r = 0.9) and was the test most often still recordable at the last visit (96% vs 86% for FVC and 81% for MIP); SNIP <40 cmH2O was linked to nocturnal hypoxaemia, carried a hazard ratio for death of 9.1 and a median survival of 6 months. [Morgan2005]
- For predicting death within 6 months, FVC <50% had 58% sensitivity and 96% specificity, while SNIP <40 cmH2O had 97% sensitivity and 79% specificity. [Morgan2005]
- The AAN 2009 update reports that SNP below 30 cmH2O was associated with a median survival of 3 months, that no respiratory test had predictive power for hypercapnia in people with marked bulbar weakness, and that elevated bicarbonate with low chloride predicted death within 5 months in 8 of 10 patients in one study. [Miller2009AAN]
- The AAN 2009 update reports that nocturnal desaturation below 90% for 1 cumulative minute was more sensitive for nocturnal hypoventilation than FVC or MIP, and that a mean nocturnal saturation below 93% was associated with a mean survival of 7 months vs 18 months above 93% (Class IV). [Miller2009AAN]
- In 1034 people with definite or probable ALS in one US clinic, median survival was 2.91 years with a baseline FVC <75% vs 4.08 years with FVC >75%, and baseline FVC remained an independent predictor after adjustment. [Czaplinski2006]
- In 893 placebo-treated trial participants (EMPOWER, BENEFIT-ALS and PRO-ACT; mean baseline SVC 90.5%), the average SVC decline in EMPOWER over 1.5 years was 2.7 percentage points per month, and 3.6 points per month in those older than 65. [Andrews2018]
- A slower SVC decline of 1.5 points per month over the first 6 months was associated with 22% lower risk of respiratory insufficiency or death and 23% lower risk of death after 6 months. [Andrews2018]
- In the Bourke trial cohort, FVC declined at -2.2% per month before NIV and -1.1% per month after it, as summarised by the AAN 2009 update. [Miller2009AAN; Bourke2006]
- NICE NG42 recommends arterial or capillary blood gases if resting room-air SpO2 is 94% or lower (92% or lower with known lung disease), and urgent referral to a respiratory ventilation service, to be seen within 1 week, if PaCO2 is above 6 kPa. [NICE2016]

### NIV initiation criteria, guideline by guideline
- AAN 2009 (Figure 2, respiratory algorithm): NIV is considered with orthopnoea, or SNP <40 cmH2O, or MIP < -60 cmH2O, or abnormal nocturnal oximetry, or FVC <50%; the figure legend defines abnormal oximetry as printed "pO2 <4% from baseline", and FVC may be supine or erect. [Miller2009AAN]
- AAN 2009 graded the evidence as Level B that NIV should be considered to lengthen survival and to slow FVC decline, Level C that it may improve quality of life, and Level C that early initiation may improve compliance. [Miller2009AAN]
- AAN 2009 lists invasive ventilation (or hospice referral) when the person is unable to maintain pO2 >90% or pCO2 <50 mmHg, or to manage secretions, despite NIV, or if NIV is not tolerated or accepted. [Miller2009AAN]
- EFNS 2012, as tabulated by Dorst and Ludolph, recommends NIV with at least one respiratory symptom or FVC <80%, SNIP <40 cmH2O, MIP <60 cmH2O, significant nocturnal desaturation, or morning pCO2 >45 mmHg; the review notes these criteria rest on expert opinion (EFNS full text not read). [Andersen2012EFNS; Dorst2019]
- NICE NG42 Table 2 lists results that should prompt a discussion and possible referral to a respiratory ventilation service: FVC or VC <50% predicted; FVC or VC <80% with symptoms or signs, particularly orthopnoea; SNIP or MIP <40 cmH2O; SNIP or MIP <65 cmH2O in men or <55 cmH2O in women with symptoms or signs; or a fall in SNIP or MIP of more than 10 cmH2O per 3 months. [NICE2016]
- NICE NG42 (2016) amalgamated and replaced NICE CG105 (2010) on NIV in MND; NIV recommendations marked [2010] were carried over without evidence review, and later minor updates were July 2019 (gabapentin safety link), October 2019, August 2020, October 2022 (oximetry and dark skin), September 2024 (technology appraisal links, including Xeomin TA605 for sialorrhoea) and April 2026 (links to overarching guidelines). [NICE2016]
- NICE NG42 recommends offering a trial of NIV when symptoms, signs and test results suggest the person is likely to benefit, and considering it in severe bulbar or cognitive impairment only for possible benefit to sleep-related symptoms or correction of hypoventilation. [NICE2016]
- The Canadian 2020 recommendations list as NIV criteria (any one): symptoms of respiratory insufficiency including orthopnoea; SNIP or MIP 40 cmH2O or less; a reliable upright FVC <65%; sitting or supine FVC <80% with symptoms or signs; daytime pCO2 >45 mmHg; or abnormal nocturnal oximetry or symptomatic sleep-disordered breathing. [Shoesmith2020]
- The 2021 US ONMAP technical expert panel (ACCP, AARC, AASM, ATS) proposed bilevel NIV criteria for thoracic restrictive disorders including neuromuscular disease (any one): symptoms with VC <80%; awake PaCO2 45 mmHg or more, or end-tidal, transcutaneous or venous CO2 50 mmHg or more; sleep SpO2 90% or less for 5% or more of the night, or 88% or less for 5 minutes or more; VC 50% predicted or less; or MIP or SNIP at or worse than 60 or 40 cmH2O. These are proposals for US reimbursement criteria, with no ALS-specific cut-offs. [Wolfe2021ONMAP]
- The EAN 2024 guideline (GRADE method) recommends offering NIV to all people with ALS with symptoms, signs or investigations supportive of respiratory insufficiency, adds that every effort should be made to allow NIV regardless of bulbar function, and gives no numeric thresholds in its recommendation text. [VanDamme2024EAN]
- Across these documents, FVC <50% and SNIP about 40 cmH2O are shared thresholds; EFNS, NICE (with symptoms), Canada (with symptoms) and ONMAP (with symptoms) also use FVC or VC <80%, while AAN 2009 does not; the documents frame these as triggers for discussion and referral rather than automatic treatment. [Miller2009AAN; Dorst2019; NICE2016; Shoesmith2020; Wolfe2021ONMAP]

### NIV evidence, adherence and masks
- Bourke 2006 (UK, single centre): 92 of 102 eligible people were assessed every 2 months, and 41 were randomised to NIV (22) or standard care (19) when they developed orthopnoea with MIP <60% predicted or symptomatic hypercapnia. [Bourke2006]
- In Bourke 2006, NIV improved quality of life (SF-36 mental component and sleep apnoea quality-of-life symptom domain) and survival overall; in the subgroup with normal to moderately impaired bulbar function (n=20), median survival benefit was 205 days (p=0.006) with quality of life maintained for most of that period. [Bourke2006]
- The Cochrane review gives the subgroup medians as 216 days with NIV vs 11 days with standard care, and the whole-trial medians as 219 vs 171 days (48 days longer; 95% CI 12 to 91; moderate-quality evidence). [Radunovic2017]
- In people with poor bulbar function in Bourke 2006 (n=21), NIV improved some sleep-related quality-of-life measures (mean symptom domain, p=0.018) but gave no survival benefit. [Bourke2006; Radunovic2017]
- The 2017 Cochrane review found only this one usable RCT (41 participants) and states that further RCTs withholding NIV are no longer ethically justifiable. [Radunovic2017]
- In a retrospective cohort offered BiPAP when FVC fell below 50%, median survival from initiation was 14.2 months in those using it more than 4 h/day, 7.0 months in those using it less, and 4.6 months in those who declined; FVC fell -3.5% per month, -5.9% per month and -8.3% per month respectively. [Kleopa1999]
- Of 71 people offered NIV in one Italian centre, 44 (61.9%) tolerated it (use of 4 h/day or more); mild or moderate bulbar symptoms predicted tolerance (OR 6.09), and tolerance and a slower FVC decline after starting were the only independent predictors of survival. [LoCoco2006]
- The AAN 2009 update summarises observational data: use of NIV more than 4 h/day was associated with 7 months longer survival, early NIV started at more than 15 desaturations per hour on overnight oximetry with 11 months longer survival, and non-compliance was 75% in people with ALS and frontotemporal dysfunction vs 38% in classic ALS. [Miller2009AAN]
- The AAN 2009 update reports that 94% of NIV users and 81% of tracheostomy ventilation users said they would choose ventilation again, and that NIV improved energy, vitality, daytime sleepiness, concentration and sleep quality for 10 months or more in one Class III study. [Miller2009AAN]
- In a pilot sham-controlled trial of early NIV in 54 people not yet meeting guideline criteria, mean use was 3.3 h/day (active) vs 2.0 h/day (sham), the FVC change favoured active NIV by 0.44 per month (p=0.049), and survival did not differ. [Jacobs2016]
- In a German register of 1720 people with data, 72.0% received no ventilation, 20.8% NIV only and 9.5% tracheostomy ventilation; median survival was 40.8 months with NIV vs 33.6 months without ventilation. [Spittel2021]
- NICE NG42 recommends daytime acclimatisation, then regular use at night before and during sleep, with hours built up as needed, and states that NIV can be stopped at any time. [NICE2016]
- Nasal masks are better tolerated and allow easier speech but can leak through the mouth in bulbar weakness; both nasal and full-face masks can cause facial pressure ulcers, which one study found protective patches did not prevent, and mouthpiece ventilation in the daytime is an option when mouth muscles work. [Dorst2019]
- NICE NG42 lists in its pre-NIV risk assessment the ventilator and interface type, tolerance, risk of ventilator failure, power and battery back-up, travel, humidification, secretion management and carer availability, and notes possible interference with eye-gaze communication equipment. [NICE2016]
- Opioids and benzodiazepines are recommended by NICE as options for breathlessness (off-label at publication), including as-needed use at home for people on NIV. [NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN]

### Cough augmentation, secretions and diaphragm pacing
- The AAN 2009 update states that peak cough flows above 160 L/min are needed to clear secretions and that clinicians recommend assistive devices when peak cough flow falls below 270 L/min (Class III); a mean peak cough flow above 337 L/min was associated with a greater chance of being alive at 18 months. [Miller2009AAN]
- The 270 L/min threshold appears as the trigger for mechanically assisted coughing in Bach's 1990-2000 single-centre series of 101 people, in which continuous NIV with assisted coughing delayed tracheotomy by more than a year for a minority. [Bach2002]
- Mechanical insufflation-exsufflation (MI-E) raised peak cough flow by 17% in healthy controls, 26% in bulbar and 28% in non-bulbar patients, against 11-13% for manually assisted cough, as summarised by AAN 2009, which gives MI-E a Level C recommendation for clearing secretions. [Miller2009AAN]
- NICE NG42 recommends unassisted breath stacking and/or manually assisted cough first, assisted breath stacking (for example a lung volume recruitment bag) for bulbar dysfunction or when that fails, and a mechanical cough assist device if assisted breath stacking does not work or during a chest infection; EAN 2024 adopts this and adds a suction device. [NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN]
- The Canadian 2020 recommendations suggest MI-E twice daily when peak cough flow is below 270 L/min, more often during chest infection (expert consensus). [Shoesmith2020]
- In a pilot RCT of 40 people comparing breath stacking (n=21) with MI-E (n=19), chest infections (13 vs 19), hospital admissions (6 vs 6) and median survival (535 vs 266 days) did not differ significantly, and the authors suggested breath stacking as a low-cost first-line option. [Rafiq2015]
- Upper airway collapse during both insufflation and exsufflation has been observed in people with ALS using MI-E, so settings are individually adjusted; secretion problems are reported by 40-60% of people with ALS. [Berlowitz2023]
- For thick secretions, NICE recommends reviewing medicines (especially saliva treatments), advice on hydration, posture and oral care, and considering humidification, nebulisers and carbocisteine; EAN 2024 adds beta-blockers (propranolol, metoprolol) and mucolytics (acetylcysteine, guaifenesin). [NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN]
- The NeuRx RA/4 Diaphragm Pacing System received a US FDA Humanitarian Device Exemption for respiratory failure in ALS before any randomised data were available. [DiPALS2015; Dorst2019]
- In DiPALS (UK, 74 people), median survival was 11.0 months with NIV plus diaphragm pacing vs 22.5 months with NIV alone (adjusted HR 2.27, 95% CI 1.22-4.25); recruitment was stopped and pacing discontinued on the data monitoring committee's advice. [DiPALS2015]
- In RespiStimALS (France, 74 people with FVC 60-80%), NIV-free survival was 6.0 months with active stimulation vs 8.8 months with sham (HR 1.96), the trial was stopped for excess mortality, and serious adverse events occurred in 65% vs 59%. [GonzalezBermejo2016]
- EAN 2024 recommends against diaphragmatic pacing in ALS (strong recommendation against). [VanDamme2024EAN]

### Tracheostomy ventilation and withdrawal of ventilation
- In a survey of neurologists, 84% of US neurologists reported that fewer than 10% of their ALS patients had tracheostomy ventilation, compared with 32% of Japanese neurologists; over 70% in both groups said they would decline it for themselves. [Rabkin2013]
- Utilisation differs by setting: 9.5% in a German register (most of it primary tracheostomy ventilation without prior NIV) and 31.3% (87 of 279) in one Italian centre in 2001-2010; a US review states that up to 95% of people with ALS in the US choose not to have a tracheostomy. [Spittel2021; Spataro2012; Niedermeyer2019]
- In a Japanese multicentre registry (190 with and 1093 without tracheostomy ventilation, propensity-matched), median overall survival was 11.33 vs 4.61 years; older onset age and respiratory onset predicted shorter survival after tracheostomy. [Hayashi2020]
- Median survival was 82.1 months with tracheostomy ventilation in the German register and 47 vs 31 months with vs without it in the Italian centre, where the advantage was seen in people with onset at 60 or younger. [Spittel2021; Spataro2012]
- In 52 home-ventilated people in Germany, 81% of those with tracheostomy ventilation had been tracheotomised without informed consent; quality of life was rated as good by both NIV and tracheostomy users, but 30% of tracheostomy caregivers rated their own quality of life below their patient's. [KaubWittemer2003]
- EAN 2024 recommends discussing invasive ventilation as part of advance care planning so that emergency invasive ventilation can be avoided; NICE recommends end-of-life discussions at trigger points including a significant change in respiratory function or the need for gastrostomy or NIV. [VanDamme2024EAN; NICE2016]
- The APM guidance (November 2015, version 1.0) states that in UK law a refusal of treatment by a person with capacity must be respected, that assisted ventilation (invasive or non-invasive) is a medical treatment that can be refused or withdrawn, and that withdrawing it at the person's request is not assisted suicide. [APM2015]
- The APM guidance states that although withdrawal influences the timing of death, the cause of death remains the advanced neurological disease, for example "1a ventilatory failure (due to) 1b advanced motor neurone disease". [APM2015]
- In 95 UK accounts of ventilation withdrawal at the person's request (2015-2024), 94% involved NIV, 92% of people were ventilator-dependent for more than 22 hours a day, and median time from removal to death was 30 minutes (range 10 minutes to over 54 hours); 94% received medication beforehand, usually an opioid plus midazolam, with titrated dosing. [Bleazard2025]
- NICE NG42 recommends that people on continuous NIV who wish to stop are supported by professionals experienced in stopping NIV, the ventilator, palliative medicines and the relevant legal frameworks. [NICE2016]

### Weight, BMI, hypermetabolism and dysphagia
- In 92 people in Limoges, a 5% loss from usual weight at diagnosis carried an adjusted 30% higher risk of death (RR 1.30, 95% CI 1.08-1.56); during follow-up each further 5% loss raised risk by 34% and each unit of BMI lost by 24%. [Marin2011]
- In an earlier study of 55 people, 16.4% were malnourished, malnutrition carried a 7.7-fold higher risk of death, and reduced VC and malnutrition were the only independent predictors on multivariate analysis. [Desport1999]
- ESPEN 2018 summarises cohort data: at diagnosis each BMI point lost raised the risk of death by 9-23%, weight loss of more than 5% roughly doubled it (HR 1.92), and 10% weight loss raised it by 45%. [Burgos2018ESPEN]
- In 79 Japanese people, a post-diagnosis BMI decline of 2.0 kg/m2 per year or more was linked to shorter survival and was an independent prognostic factor; weight gain after diagnosis was linked to longer survival in women. [Shimizu2019]
- Measured resting energy expenditure exceeded predicted in 47.5% of 61 people at inclusion (about 50% hypermetabolic), and 80% did not change metabolic status over time. [Bouteloup2009]
- Hypermetabolism was found in 41% of 58 people with ALS vs 12% of matched controls (adjusted OR 5.4) and was linked to faster ALSFRS-R decline (-0.68 vs -0.39 points per month) and higher risk of death (HR 3.2). [Steyn2018]
- ESPEN 2018 suggests estimating energy needs at about 30 kcal/kg body weight in non-ventilated people, and 25-30 kcal/kg in people on NIV, which lowered resting energy expenditure by 7% in one study. [Burgos2018ESPEN]
- ESPEN 2018 reports dysphagia prevalence varying by method: 47.8-72.7% on endoscopy (mainly bulbar form), 75% on videofluoroscopy, and dysphagia as the first symptom in 6.2%; it describes five stages from normal eating to nothing by mouth. [Burgos2018ESPEN]
- In 145 people followed with FEES every 3-6 months for about 2 years, dysphagia rose to 85% overall (from 35% to 73% in spinal onset and from 95% to 98% in bulbar onset); 8% with dysphagia on FEES did not perceive it, and 44% refused thickeners or PEG. [Onesti2017]
- Dysphagia leads to choking, aspiration, weight loss and dehydration, and aspiration pneumonia is a stated risk; NICE asks for assessment of respiratory symptoms, aspiration and choking risk and fear of choking. [Miller2009AAN; Burgos2018ESPEN; NICE2016]

### Gastrostomy: guidance, timing and ProGas
- AAN 2009 (Figure 1, nutrition algorithm) places gastrostomy discussion after symptom progression or continuing weight loss and grades risk by FVC: above 50% low risk, 30-50% moderate risk (anaesthesia review, experienced endoscopist, respiratory support during the procedure), below 30% high risk; oral intake continues as tolerated, and declining PEG leads to palliative IV hydration or NG feeding. [Miller2009AAN]
- AAN 2009 found no ALS-specific evidence on timing (Level U), noted one Class III study in which PEG risk rose when FVC fell below 50%, and stated that PEG "does not eliminate oral feeding". [Miller2009AAN]
- AAN 2009 graded PEG as Level B to stabilise weight and to prolong survival, based on 2 Class II and 7 Class III studies in which PEG users were compared with people who refused it or with risk-adjusted controls; 4 Class III studies found no survival benefit. [Miller2009AAN]
- EFNS 2012 recommends that gastrostomy is placed before respiratory insufficiency develops (abstract), and, as quoted by ProGas and Dorst, after weight loss of 10% or more from premorbid weight and before VC falls below 50% predicted (full text not read). [Andersen2012EFNS; ProGas2015; Dorst2019]
- NICE NG42 recommends discussing gastrostomy early and at regular intervals, explaining the benefits of early placement and the risks of late placement (low body mass, respiratory complications, dehydration, higher mortality and complications), avoiding delay once referred, and accepting that some people will not want one. [NICE2016]
- ESPEN 2018 recommends gastrostomy before severe weight loss and before respiratory function is severely impaired, prefers PEG generally, and suggests RIG by an expert team in frailer people or those with moderate to severe respiratory impairment (good-practice and Grade 0 recommendations). [Burgos2018ESPEN]
- The Canadian 2020 recommendations name triggers for nutritional intervention, including tube referral: 5-10% or more weight loss from usual weight, a fall of 1 BMI point or more, or BMI below 18.5; FVC approaching 50% should prompt referral, FVC below 50% does not necessarily rule out a tube, and insertion within 4 weeks of the decision is suggested. [Shoesmith2020]
- EAN 2024 adds that with respiratory insufficiency NIV should be started first and the gastrostomy done with the person established on NIV, that NG feeding may be considered while waiting, and that parenteral feeding may be considered if gastrostomy is not feasible. [VanDamme2024EAN]
- ProGas (24 UK centres, 2010-2014) enrolled 345 people, of whom 330 had a gastrostomy: 163 (49%) PEG, 121 (37%) RIG, 43 (13%) PIG and 3 surgical. [ProGas2015]
- 30-day mortality in ProGas was 4% overall (12 of 330): 3% after PEG (5 of 163), 3% after RIG (4 of 121) and 7% after PIG (3 of 43; p=0.46); 6% of procedures could not be completed in each group. [ProGas2015]
- Median survival after gastrostomy in ProGas was 325 days overall (341 PEG, 361 RIG, 201 PIG); the insertion method did not affect the hazard of death after adjustment. [ProGas2015]
- In ProGas, weight loss of more than 10% from diagnosis to gastrostomy was linked to higher hazard of death (HR 2.51, 95% CI 1.49-4.24) and median survival of 7.7 months vs 12 months with 10% loss or less; odds of 30-day death were 10.7 times higher (95% CI 1.3-87.0). [ProGas2015]
- Mean FVC at gastrostomy in ProGas was 65.4% for PEG, 59% for RIG and 52% for PIG (p=0.004), so the groups differed in respiratory function. [ProGas2015]
- A 2013 meta-analysis estimated mortality after PEG as 2.1% higher than after RIG or PIG (range -6.3% to +11.2%), and a survey of 20 UK clinics found practice varied with clinician preference, availability and respiratory function. [Stavroulakis2013]
- ESPEN 2018 reports studies of PEG placed under NIV, including one in which 35 people with FVC <50% had survival similar to 40 with FVC >50%, and notes that spirometry can underestimate respiratory function in bulbar weakness. [Burgos2018ESPEN]
- The 2023 Cochrane review found no randomised or quasi-randomised trials comparing tube feeding with continued oral feeding and judged such trials unlikely for ethical reasons, so survival evidence for gastrostomy is observational only. [Sulistyo2023]
- The AAN 2009 update summarised 7 Class III studies showing weight stabilisation or modest gain over 2-24 months with PEG feeding. [Miller2009AAN]

### High-calorie diet trials
- Wills 2014 (US, phase 2, double-blind) randomised 24 people already fed by gastrostomy (20 started) to isocaloric control, high-carbohydrate hypercaloric or high-fat hypercaloric tube feeding for 4 months; the high-carbohydrate group had fewer serious adverse events (0 vs 9) and no deaths vs 3 of 7 controls (log-rank p=0.03) over 5 months. [Wills2014]
- Wills 2014 was a safety and tolerability trial with very small numbers, and the authors described its results as preliminary evidence supporting larger trials. [Wills2014]
- LIPCAL-ALS (12 German centres, 2015-2018) randomised 201 people to a high-calorie fatty supplement (405 kcal/day, 100% fat) or placebo with riluzole; survival probability at 28 months was 0.37 vs 0.39 (HR 0.97, p=0.44), with no evidence of benefit overall. [Ludolph2020]
- The LIPCAL-ALS abstract reports a significant survival benefit in fast-progressing participants in a post hoc analysis only (effect size not read; full text paywalled). [Ludolph2020]
- LIPCAL-ALS II (NCT06280079, University of Ulm) tests an ultra-high-calorie fatty diet against placebo with survival as primary outcome, enrolling people with disease duration under 24 months and ALSFRS-R loss of 0.33 points per month or more; 399 were enrolled, it is active and not recruiting, and estimated completion is April 2028. [LIPCALALSII]
- ESPEN 2018 recommends supplements when needs are not met by an enriched diet but states there is insufficient evidence that oral supplements improve survival, and suggests weight gain if BMI <25, stability at BMI 25-35 and weight loss above 35 (good-practice points). [Burgos2018ESPEN]
- EAN 2024 reviewed four RCTs on malnutrition (TOLCAL-ALS, LIPCAL-ALS, Wang 2022, Wills 2014); its recommendation text contains no recommendation specific to high-calorie diets. [VanDamme2024EAN]

### Texture modification, swallowing assessment and saliva
- The IDDSI framework (released November 2015) is an 8-level continuum (0-7) for drinks and foods, each level identified by a number, text label, colour code, definition and measurement method, built from surveys of 2050 respondents in 33 countries and 3190 in 57 countries. [Cichero2017]
- ESPEN 2018 recommends dysphagia screening in every person with ALS at diagnosis and about every 3 months, notes that videofluoroscopy, videofluoromanometry and FEES can detect early dysphagia, and recommends texture adaptation and postural manoeuvres such as chin tuck in moderate dysphagia (good-practice points). [Burgos2018ESPEN]
- The AAN 2009 update notes that videofluoroscopy may identify food textures that can be handled successfully. [Miller2009AAN]
- Normal saliva production is 0.5-1.5 L per day, and sialorrhoea in MND results from loss of salivary clearance due to dysphagia rather than excess production. [James2022]

### Symptom care
- Sialorrhoea, NICE: consider an antimuscarinic first line (off-label in 2016), glycopyrronium first line in people with cognitive impairment because of fewer CNS effects, and referral for botulinum toxin A as first or second line; Xeomin is recommended in NICE TA605 (2019) for chronic sialorrhoea in neurological conditions. [NICE2016]
- Sialorrhoea, EFNS (as tabulated by Dorst): amitriptyline 10 mg three times daily, atropine 0.5-1% drops 3-4 times daily, glycopyrrolate, transdermal scopolamine 1.5 mg every third day, botulinum toxin A or B to the salivary glands, and salivary gland irradiation. [Andersen2012EFNS; Dorst2019]
- Sialorrhoea, AAN 2009b: botulinum toxin B should be considered for refractory sialorrhoea (Level B) and low-dose salivary gland radiotherapy may be considered (Level C). [Miller2009AANb]
- Sialorrhoea, EAN 2024: anticholinergics (amitriptyline, atropine, glycopyrrolate, oxybutynin, scopolamine) first line; consider dextromethorphan-quinidine, especially with emotional lability; botulinum toxin in severe cases when drugs fail; radiotherapy after that; surgery not suggested. [VanDamme2024EAN]
- The 2022 Cochrane review found 4 RCTs (110 participants): low to moderate certainty evidence for botulinum toxin B injections and moderate certainty for dextromethorphan-quinidine (CNS-BFS sialorrhoea subscale MD -1.52), with evidence on radiotherapy vs botulinum toxin A and on scopolamine patches too uncertain for conclusions. [James2022]
- The Xeomin (incobotulinumtoxinA) label lists chronic sialorrhoea in patients 2 years and older; the adult dose is 100 units per session (30 units per parotid and 20 units per submandibular gland), no sooner than every 16 weeks. [FDA2026Xeomin]
- Pseudobulbar affect: in a 28-day RCT in ALS (140 randomised), dextromethorphan 30 mg plus quinidine 30 mg twice daily improved the CNS-LS score by 3.3 points more than dextromethorphan alone and 3.7 more than quinidine alone; treatment-related discontinuation was 24%. [Brooks2004]
- In the 12-week STAR trial (326 people with ALS or MS and PBA), episode rates were 46.9% lower with dextromethorphan-quinidine 30/10 mg and 49.0% lower with 20/10 mg than with placebo, and CNS-LS fell 8.2 vs 5.7 points. [Pioro2010]
- The Nuedexta label lists treatment of pseudobulbar affect, with capsules of dextromethorphan 20 mg and quinidine 10 mg, one daily for 7 days then one every 12 hours. [FDA2025Nuedexta]
- AAN 2009b gave dextromethorphan-quinidine a Level B recommendation for PBA "if approved by the FDA"; EAN 2024 suggests SSRIs, tricyclics or dextromethorphan-quinidine for emotional lability, distinguished from depression and FTD. [Miller2009AANb; VanDamme2024EAN]
- Cramps: in a 12-week RCT (60 people), mexiletine reduced cramp frequency to 31% of placebo at 300 mg/day and 16% at 900 mg/day; 32% stopped the 900 mg dose vs 5% on placebo, and ALSFRS-R and SVC decline did not differ. [Weiss2016]
- In a crossover trial of mexiletine 150 mg twice daily in 20 people, cramps fell from 5.3 to 3.5 per day and severity by 15 points on a 100-point scale, with no serious adverse events. [Oskarsson2018]
- NICE NG42 (2016) suggests quinine first line for cramps, then baclofen, then tizanidine, dantrolene or gabapentin; EAN 2024 suggests sodium-channel blockers (ranolazine, quinine, mexiletine, carbamazepine), gabapentin, pregabalin or baclofen, with low-dose quinine (100-200 mg/day) and cardiac monitoring; Canada lists mexiletine second line (expert consensus). [NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN; Shoesmith2020]
- A 2017 Cochrane overview found memantine and tetrahydrocannabinol probably ineffective for cramps (moderate quality) and found other agents uncertain. [Ng2017]
- Spasticity: NICE suggests baclofen, tizanidine, dantrolene or gabapentin and referral to a specialist service for severe spasticity; EAN 2024 adds physiotherapy, cannabinoids and focal botulinum toxin; Canada states there is insufficient evidence to recommend intrathecal baclofen. [NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN; Shoesmith2020]
- The only RCT in the Cochrane spasticity review was a trial of endurance exercise in 25 people, too small to show benefit or harm; no drug for spasticity in ALS has been tested in an RCT included there. [Ng2017]
- Pain, fatigue, sleep, mood and constipation (EAN 2024): look for causes of pain (cramps, spasticity, positioning, frozen shoulder) and consider targeted steroid joint injections; treat underlying causes of fatigue and consider pausing riluzole if implicated (AAN 2009b Level C); treat causes of insomnia, then relaxation, low-dose tricyclics or hypnotics; consider psychotherapy and/or drugs for depression; assess constipation (opioids, anticholinergics, NIV-related aerophagia, immobility, low fibre) and advise fibre, fluid and laxatives. [VanDamme2024EAN; Miller2009AANb]

### Multidisciplinary clinic care
- In the Irish register (1996-2000), people attending the multidisciplinary clinic (82 of 344) lived a median 7.5 months longer, had 29.7% lower one-year mortality and 9.6 months longer survival with bulbar onset; clinic attendance was an independent predictor (HR 1.47); the clinic group was 5 years younger and more often on riluzole (99% vs 61%). [Traynor2003]
- Across Ireland and Northern Ireland (719 incident cases, 2005-2010), attendance at the multidisciplinary clinic was linked to better survival (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.49-0.71), which persisted on multivariate analysis. [Rooney2015]
- In the Netherlands (208 people), multidisciplinary care was linked to better SF-36 mental quality of life and more adequate aids (93.1% vs 81.3%) but no difference in physical scores or caregiver quality of life. [VandenBerg2005]
- AAN 2009b recommends referral to a multidisciplinary clinic to optimise care and prolong survival (Level B) and possibly to improve quality of life (Level C); NICE and EAN 2024 recommend clinic-based specialist multidisciplinary care, with EAN suggesting reviews about every 3-6 months. [Miller2009AANb; NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN]
- The 2017 Cochrane overview found no RCT evidence on multidisciplinary care and considered such a trial unethical where care is available, so the survival evidence is observational. [Ng2017]

### Controversies and disagreements between sources
- Timing of NIV: guideline thresholds (FVC <50% in AAN 2009) contrast with the lower thresholds of EFNS, NICE and Canada (FVC <80% with symptoms) and with early-NIV studies (Jacobs 2016, Pinto via AAN); Dorst and Ludolph argue the EFNS criteria rest on opinion and that earlier NIV may be better, while adherence in people without symptoms is uncertain. [Miller2009AAN; Dorst2019; Jacobs2016; NICE2016]
- NIV with severe bulbar weakness: Bourke 2006 found no survival benefit in this subgroup, NICE limits NIV in severe bulbar impairment to symptom benefit, while EAN 2024 asks that every effort be made to allow NIV regardless of bulbar function, citing later retrospective data. [Bourke2006; NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN; Dorst2019]
- The FVC 50% gastrostomy rule: AAN (one Class III study) and EFNS link higher risk to FVC below 50%, but ESPEN, Canada and EAN cite procedures under NIV, RIG or PIG and similar outcomes below 50%, and note spirometry is unreliable in bulbar weakness. [Miller2009AAN; Burgos2018ESPEN; Shoesmith2020; VanDamme2024EAN]
- PEG vs RIG vs PIG: a 2013 meta-analysis suggested RIG and PIG might be safer than PEG, whereas the larger prospective ProGas found similar 30-day mortality across methods; ProGas was non-randomised and the groups differed in FVC and weight loss. [Stavroulakis2013; ProGas2015]
- Does gastrostomy prolong survival? AAN 2009 calls PEG "probably effective" (Level B), ESPEN reports that EFNS found no convincing survival evidence, and Cochrane 2023 found no randomised trials. [Miller2009AAN; Burgos2018ESPEN; Sulistyo2023]
- Hypermetabolism and survival: Steyn 2018 found hypermetabolism linked to shorter survival, while Bouteloup 2009 found no correlation with survival and ESPEN cites a study in which it was not associated. [Steyn2018; Bouteloup2009; Burgos2018ESPEN]
- High-calorie diets: a small phase 2 safety signal (Wills) and a fast-progressor post hoc finding (LIPCAL-ALS) have not been confirmed in a trial powered for survival; LIPCAL-ALS II is testing this. [Wills2014; Ludolph2020; LIPCALALSII]
- Tracheostomy ventilation use varies widely between countries and centres, and Rabkin's surveys suggest neurologist attitudes, not only patient preference, account for part of the gap. [Rabkin2013; Spittel2021; Spataro2012; Hayashi2020]

## Step sequence

(a) Breathing: from normal breathing to later choices
1. Normal breathing: the diaphragm, supplied by phrenic motor neurons at C3-C5, does most of the work; baseline SpO2 and FVC or VC and/or SNIP or MIP are recorded at or soon after diagnosis. [Fusco2019; NICE2016]
2. Gradual decline: tests are repeated every 2-3 months (NICE) or every 3 months (Canada); in placebo-treated trial participants, SVC fell on average 2.7 percentage points per month, faster (3.6) after age 65. [NICE2016; Shoesmith2020; Andrews2018]
3. Diaphragm weakness: breathlessness lying flat (orthopnoea), abdominal paradox and accessory muscle use appear; a supine FVC below 75% predicted identified low diaphragm pressure in a small study. [NICE2016; Lechtzin2002]
4. Hypoventilation during sleep: disturbed and non-refreshing sleep, morning headache, daytime sleepiness and poor concentration; SNIP below 40 cmH2O is linked to nocturnal hypoxaemia and a median survival of about 6 months. [NICE2016; Miller2009AAN; Morgan2005]
5. Guideline triggers crossed: FVC <50% (all guidelines), or FVC <80% with symptoms (EFNS, NICE, Canada, ONMAP), SNIP or MIP about 40 cmH2O or less, pCO2 >45 mmHg or PaCO2 >6 kPa (NICE urgent referral within 1 week), or abnormal overnight oximetry. [Miller2009AAN; Dorst2019; NICE2016; Shoesmith2020; Wolfe2021ONMAP]
6. NIV: started in the daytime, then used at night; in Bourke 2006 it extended median survival by 205 days with maintained quality of life in people with better bulbar function, and use of about 4 h/day or more was linked to longer survival in cohorts (14.2 vs 7.0 vs 4.6 months). [NICE2016; Bourke2006; Kleopa1999; LoCoco2006]
7. Cough and secretions: assisted coughing or MI-E is added when peak cough flow falls below 270 L/min (more than 160 L/min is needed to clear secretions); diaphragm pacing shortened survival in DiPALS (11.0 vs 22.5 months) and is not recommended. [Miller2009AAN; Shoesmith2020; DiPALS2015; VanDamme2024EAN]
8. Later choices: as NIV use extends toward 24 hours a day, people and families discuss tracheostomy ventilation (about 9.5% in a German register; fewer than 10% of patients for most US neurologists) or a palliative route; a person with capacity can ask for ventilation to be withdrawn, with titrated symptom control (median 30 minutes from removal to death in UK reports). [Spittel2021; Rabkin2013; APM2015; Bleazard2025]

(b) Swallowing and weight: from safe swallow to after the tube
1. Safe swallow: weight, BMI, diet and swallowing are assessed at diagnosis, with screening for malnutrition and dysphagia about every 3 months. [Burgos2018ESPEN; NICE2016]
2. Dysphagia: slow meals, coughing and fatigue at meals; over about 2 years, FEES-detected dysphagia rose from 35% to 73% in spinal onset and from 95% to 98% in bulbar onset, and 8% did not notice it. [Onesti2017; Miller2009AAN]
3. Adapting food: texture-modified food and thickened drinks graded on the 8-level IDDSI scale, chin-tuck posture, and videofluoroscopy or FEES to guide safe textures. [Cichero2017; Burgos2018ESPEN]
4. Weight loss and energy gap: a 5% loss from usual weight at diagnosis carried 30% higher risk of death; about 41-50% of people are hypermetabolic, and ESPEN estimates needs at about 30 kcal/kg. [Marin2011; Steyn2018; Bouteloup2009; Burgos2018ESPEN]
5. Gastrostomy window: discussed early and repeatedly; triggers include 5-10% weight loss, BMI below 18.5 or FVC approaching 50%; AAN rates risk low above FVC 50%, moderate at 30-50% and high below 30%, and EAN advises NIV during the procedure if breathing is weak. [NICE2016; Shoesmith2020; Miller2009AAN; VanDamme2024EAN]
6. Procedure: PEG, RIG or PIG had 30-day mortality of 3%, 3% and 7% in ProGas (p=0.46); more than 10% weight loss before gastrostomy was linked to median survival of 7.7 vs 12 months. [ProGas2015]
7. After the tube: weight stabilises or rises modestly in cohort studies, eating by mouth can continue as tolerated, and survival benefit is supported only by observational data; high-calorie feeding is still being tested (LIPCAL-ALS II). [Miller2009AAN; Sulistyo2023; LIPCALALSII]

### Notes on verification
- All 52 PMIDs above were checked in one PubMed esummary call (first author, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, DOI, PMCID), and every DOI matched the PMID record; abstracts were read from Europe PMC (resultType=core). Titles are copied from the PubMed or Europe PMC record.
- Oskarsson2018: the PubMed esummary record lacks volume, issue and pages; Crossref (10.1002/mus.26117) gives 58(1):42-48, which matches the EAN 2024 reference list. Bleazard2025: PubMed gives pages 396-399, and the article number is e005389. Fusco2019 has no DOI in PubMed; it is an NIH author manuscript with article number 100011.
- Full texts read: ProGas2015, VanDamme2024EAN, Dorst2019 and Berlowitz2023 (Europe PMC full-text XML); Miller2009AAN (PMC HTML, with the two algorithm figures viewed as images); Wolfe2021ONMAP, Shoesmith2020 (first 100,000 characters, covering the recommendation table), Fusco2019 and Bleazard2025 (WebFetch of PMC pages); NICE NG42 recommendations and update history (nice.org.uk); APM2015 (PDF, version 1.0); Burgos2018ESPEN (PDF, Geneva open archive). Labels from openFDA; trial record from ClinicalTrials.gov API v2.
- EFNS 2012 (Andersen2012EFNS) full text could not be accessed (Wiley HTTP 403; no repository copy found). Its NIV criteria come from the table in Dorst2019, its 10% weight-loss gastrostomy threshold from ProGas2015, and its FVC 50% threshold from Dorst2019 and Burgos2018ESPEN. All three are secondary attributions.
- Miscitations found in secondary sources: (1) Dorst2019 Table 1 lists the NICE SNIP/MIP threshold with symptoms for women as <40 cmH2O, but NICE NG42 Table 2 says <55 cmH2O. (2) Dorst2019 text gives the DiPALS survival difference as "-11.5 months; 95% CI 8.3-13.6", but the DiPALS abstract reports medians of 11.0 months (95% CI 8.3-13.6) with pacing vs 22.5 months without, so that CI belongs to the pacing-arm median. (3) Dorst2019 Table 1 leaves the AAN column for hypersalivation empty, but AAN 2009b recommends botulinum toxin B (Level B) and radiotherapy (Level C). (4) Burgos2018ESPEN states that the AAN "recommends to refuse gastrostomy when FVC was lower than 30%" and that AAN and EFNS recommend gastrostomy with FVC >50%; in fact the AAN 2009 Figure 1 labels FVC <30% "high risk" with both accept and decline branches, and the AAN text grades timing Level U.
- The AAN 2009 Figure 2 legend prints abnormal nocturnal oximetry as "pO2 <4% from baseline". It is reproduced verbatim because its intended meaning (probably a desaturation of 4% or more) is not stated.
- Radunovic2017 reports the Bourke better-bulbar subgroup medians as 216 vs 11 days. The 11-day control median is as printed in the Cochrane abstract; the Bourke abstract gives only the 205-day difference (p=0.006).
- Plan seeds checked: the 205-day Bourke benefit is confirmed. "ProGas linked >10% weight loss with worse survival" is confirmed (HR 2.51; 7.7 vs 12 months). The seed "FVC about 2-3.5% predicted lost per month" is partly supported: 2.7 points per month for SVC in placebo-treated participants (Andrews2018), -2.2% per month before NIV in Bourke's cohort, and -3.5% to -8.3% per month after FVC <50% depending on NIV use (Kleopa1999); no pooled FVC figure for trial placebo arms was read. The "Shimizu" seed is the 2019 J Neurol paper (Shimizu2019); no 2012 Shimizu paper was found.
- Not found or not verified: a published supine-minus-upright FVC fall threshold (often quoted as >10-20%); a numeric figure for diaphragm pacing use in the US (only the HDE approval and statements of wide use); an ESPEN 2020 ALS guideline (only a 2020 reply letter, PMID 31771773, was found, so ESPEN 2018 is cited); LIPCAL-ALS fast-progressor effect sizes; the AAN 1999 parameter content.
- Fusco2019 describes intercostals as expiratory muscles. That is a simplification (external intercostals are inspiratory), so it is cited only for the C3-C5 location and accessory-muscle recruitment.
- Source count (57) and fact count (about 135) exceed the 25-45 and 60-90 targets because the brief spans three domains and asks for each guideline item by item; Miller1999AAN is listed for completeness only (retired, no abstract, not cited for any fact).
