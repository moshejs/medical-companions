## Facts

### Motor system anatomy (for the animation)
- Upper motor neurons are defined as the Betz cells in layer 5 of the primary motor cortex. Lower motor neurons are the brainstem cranial motor nerve nuclei and the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord [Feldman2022].
- In ALS autopsy tissue, pTDP-43 pathology was seen in the Betz pyramidal cells of layer Vb and in smaller projection neurons of layers II, III, V and VI of the primary motor area (Brodmann area 4). Lipofuscin remnants in the neuropil marked where Betz cells had been lost ("pigment incontinence") [Brettschneider2013].
- The corticospinal tract is the principal motor pathway for voluntary movement. Most of its axons cross the midline at the junction of the brainstem and spinal cord, forming the pyramidal decussation [Welniarz2017]. The commonly quoted proportion of crossing fibres (about 75-90%) was not stated in any source read in this pass (unverified).
- Direct cortico-motoneuronal (CM) connections from cortex onto spinal motor neurons run in parallel with less direct routes through interneurons. They differ widely between species and may serve adaptive skilled movements of the distal limbs [Lemon2008].
- The direct corticomotoneuronal system is unique to primates and markedly enhanced in humans. Eisen and colleagues argue that its failure in ALS may explain the split hand, gait disturbance, split leg and bulbar symptoms of vocalisation and breathing [Eisen2017].
- A motor unit is one motor neuron plus all the skeletal muscle fibres its axon innervates. Motor units fall into fast-fatigable (FF; type IIb/x fibres), fast fatigue-resistant (FR; type IIa) and slow (S; type I) classes [Moloney2014].
- The neuromuscular junction (NMJ) is a specialised synapse between nerve and muscle. Normal electrical activity is needed for acetylcholine receptors to be distributed correctly on the muscle membrane as the NMJ matures. In ALS, many molecular changes occur at the NMJ before symptoms begin [Moloney2014].
- In humans, the phrenic motor neurons that supply the diaphragm sit in lamina IX of cervical segments C3-C5 (the source cites Keswani 1955). They receive rhythmic excitatory drive from premotor neurons in the medulla [Mantilla2008].
- At the lowest burden of pTDP-43 pathology (stage 1), inclusions are found in the agranular motor cortex, in brainstem motor nuclei of cranial nerves V, VII and X-XII, and in spinal alpha-motor neurons. The bulbar somatomotor nuclei scored were hypoglossal, ambiguus, facial and trigeminal motor; the oculomotor nuclei were scored separately [Brettschneider2013].

### UMN and LMN signs and their physiology
- UMN dysfunction shows as increased and pathological reflexes (including Hoffmann, Babinski and snout), pathological spread of reflexes, reflexes that are preserved in a weak limb, and spasticity [Feldman2022].
- LMN dysfunction shows as muscle weakness, atrophy and fasciculations [Feldman2022]. Cramps and reduced reflexes are usually listed as LMN features, but no source read in this pass states this explicitly (unverified in this pass).
- The textbook explanation of spasticity and hyperreflexia as loss of descending (supraspinal) inhibition of spinal reflex circuits was not stated in any source read in this pass (textbook-level; unverified here).
- UMN signs are often hard to elicit in a limb that is also wasting. The reasons are complex and depend on how the spinal cord's motor neurons and interneurons respond to damage to descending pathways, not simply on how much LMN wasting there is [Swash2012].
- UMN assessment in ALS remains essentially clinical. This contributes to diagnostic delay, and biomarkers of UMN involvement are still needed [Huynh2016].
- Pseudobulbar affect is uncontrollable emotional outbursts, including laughing, crying and excessive yawning. "Pseudobulbar palsy" is a less common form of bulbar onset with mostly UMN bulbar features that spread slowly to the limbs [Feldman2022].
- In an outpatient chart review, pseudobulbar affect was documented in 31 of 50 people with primary lateral sclerosis (PLS) and 12 of 37 with ALS. Mood during episodes matched the affect but was excessive in magnitude [Floeter2014].
- Controversy, pseudobulbar affect pathway: it is classically attributed to corticobulbar (UMN) damage. On DTI, Floeter and colleagues additionally found disrupted corticopontocerebellar pathways (transverse pontine fibres, middle cerebellar peduncle) in people with pseudobulbar affect. They proposed it as a "dysmetria" of emotional expression from cerebellar dysmodulation [Floeter2014].

### Denervation, reinnervation and what EMG shows
- To support an ALS diagnosis, needle EMG should show reduced recruitment (a reduced number of motor units firing rapidly) and/or large-amplitude, long-duration motor unit potentials (MUPs), with or without remodelling (more phases). These occur together with abnormal spontaneous activity: positive sharp waves, fibrillations and/or fasciculation potentials [Joyce2013].
- Under the Awaji consensus, EMG evidence of chronic neurogenic change counts the same as clinical information for a muscle. In suspected ALS, fasciculation potentials count the same as fibrillations and positive sharp waves as evidence of denervation. Instability of fasciculation potentials and MUPs should be sought [deCarvalho2008].
- Collateral sprouting from surviving axons enlarges motor units. MUNE studies have shown that this reinnervation can prevent loss of strength and CMAP amplitude even when up to half the motor units are lost. This is a secondary statement in Joyce 2013 citing Daube 1985; the primary source was not read [Joyce2013].
- Controversy, gradual versus abrupt loss: 19 asymptomatic SOD1 carriers were followed with MUNE every 6 months for 3 years. 17 kept normal motor neuron numbers. In 2, MUNE fell suddenly several months before weakness began, which suggests a trigger rather than lifelong gradual loss [Aggarwal2002].
- Collateral sprouting leaves nerve terminals immature, which makes neuromuscular transmission unstable in ALS. This shows as decrement on repetitive stimulation and as abnormal jitter, blocking and fibre density on single-fibre EMG. These changes correlate with atrophy and weakness [Joyce2013].
- Most people with ALS have fasciculation potentials, but these are not specific to ALS. Sensory conduction studies are generally normal in ALS [Joyce2013].
- Fasciculation origin: time-locked fasciculation potentials on two needles, which point to a spinal origin, were most frequent in benign fasciculation syndrome (44%). They occurred in 27% in ALS muscle without reinnervation and in 14% in reinnervated tibialis anterior in ALS. In chronic partial denervation, fasciculations are therefore more likely to arise distally [deCarvalho2013].
- Slow motor neurons can compensate for a while by reinnervating denervated fibres, until later in the disease they also degenerate [Nijssen2017].
- FF motor units on type IIb/x fibres are the most vulnerable and are lost earliest, in both human and rodent ALS [Moloney2014; Kanning2010].

### Cortical hyperexcitability, dying-forward and dying-back
- The dying-forward hypothesis holds that corticomotoneurons drive excitotoxic death of anterior horn cells through the glutamatergic system [Vucic2006; Vucic2008].
- Threshold-tracking TMS in 23 people with MND found short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI) reduced to 3.6 +/- 0.8%, against 8.5 +/- 1.0% in controls (P < 0.001), most prominently in limb-onset disease. The reduction came with other signs of cortical hyperexcitability (stimulus-response curve, cortical silent period, resting motor threshold) and was present even in advanced disease [Vucic2006].
- In that study, cortical hyperexcitability correlated with peripheral disease burden: CMAP amplitude r = -0.6, neurophysiological index r = -0.6, and strength-duration time constant r = -0.6 [Vucic2006].
- SICI was -1.2 +/- 0.6% in SOD1 familial ALS and -0.7 +/- 0.3% in sporadic ALS, against 9.8 +/- 1.5% in asymptomatic SOD1 carriers and 8.5 +/- 1.0% in controls. In three carriers SICI was absent or reduced by 32% before symptoms began, and all three later developed ALS [Vucic2008].
- Cortical hyperexcitability across ALS phenotypes distinguishes ALS from non-ALS disorders and correlates with the affected body regions and with disease spread. SICI change was the primary endpoint of a phase 2 trial of retigabine [Feldman2022].
- Eisen and colleagues argue that TDP-43 pathology in sporadic ALS is restricted to cortical areas and to subcortical nuclei under direct corticofugal control. In their view this places its origin in the cortex, with possible prion-like spread at corticofugal synaptic terminals [Eisen2017].
- Dying-back evidence: in SOD1(G93A) mice, 40% of end-plates were denervated at 47 days with no ventral-root or cell-body loss. At 80 days, 60% of ventral-root axons were lost with no motor neuron loss. Mice became weak at 80 days, motor neuron loss was under way by 100 days, and death came at 131 +/- 5 days [Fischer2004].
- In the same study, microglial and astrocytic activation around motor neurons appeared only after distal axon degeneration had begun. One person with sporadic ALS who died unexpectedly had denervation and reinnervation changes in muscle but normal-appearing motor neurons [Fischer2004].
- Controversy: the two hypotheses rest mainly on different evidence, human TMS (dying-forward) versus the SOD1 mouse and NMJ studies (dying-back). The sources read do not settle which comes first [Vucic2008; Fischer2004; Moloney2014].

### The split hand
- In ALS, wasting preferentially affects the thenar or lateral hand (abductor pollicis brevis, APB, and first dorsal interosseous, FDI) and relatively spares the hypothenar abductor digiti minimi (ADM). All three are supplied from C8-T1, and FDI and ADM are both ulnar-innervated [Eisen2012].
- Proposed mechanisms for the split hand are both cortical and peripheral. On the cortical side, magnetic motor evoked potentials are smaller from thenar than hypothenar muscles. On the peripheral side, APB/FDI axons have larger persistent sodium currents and so higher axonal excitability. Heavy precision-grip use may add oxidative and metabolic stress. The sign is reported to have high specificity in early ALS [Eisen2012].

### Spared motor neuron groups
- The ocular motor neurons (oculomotor, trochlear and abducens nuclei; cranial nerves III, IV and VI) persist throughout the disease, which is why eye-tracking devices can support communication late in ALS [Nijssen2017].
- Extraocular muscles have a unique set of myosin heavy chains and several neuromuscular innervation sites per fibre, which sets them apart from other motor units [Nijssen2017].
- In 5 ALS cases, Onuf's nucleus (the sacral "X group" that controls the sphincters) was remarkably well preserved. This fits with bladder and rectal sphincter function often staying intact until the latest stage [Mannen1977].
- In ALS autopsy tissue, calbindin-D28k and parvalbumin were absent from motor neuron groups lost early (cortical, spinal and lower cranial). They were present at much higher levels in groups damaged late or rarely (Onuf's nucleus and the oculomotor, trochlear and abducens neurons) [Alexianu1994].
- Fast-fatigable units degenerate early in ALS, SMA and ageing, while motor neurons for slow muscles, eye movement and pelvic sphincter control are strikingly preserved [Kanning2010].
- In a mouse microarray, 18 genes differed more than tenfold between resistant motor neurons (ocular and Onuf's) and vulnerable spinal motor neurons. IGF-2 was protective, and viral IGF-2 extended the lifespan of SOD1(G93A) mice by 10% [Nijssen2017].
- Proposed vulnerability factors in spinal motor neurons include calcium-permeable AMPA receptors, low intrinsic calcium buffering, and heavy reliance on mitochondria for calcium buffering [Nijssen2017].

### TDP-43: normal structure and function
- TDP-43 is a 414-amino-acid protein encoded by TARDBP on chromosome 1. Its domains are an N-terminal region (aa 1-102) containing a nuclear localisation signal (NLS, aa 82-98); RRM1 (aa 104-176); RRM2 (aa 192-262); a reported nuclear export signal (aa 239-250); and a C-terminal region (aa 274-414) with a Q/N-rich prion-like segment (aa 345-366) and a glycine-rich region (aa 366-414) [Prasad2019].
- Ling and colleagues describe TDP-43 as two RNA recognition motifs followed by a glycine-rich, low-complexity, prion-like domain. More than 40 ALS mutations cluster in this domain [Ling2013].
- TDP-43 is thought to exist natively as a dimer, or in monomer-dimer equilibrium, through head-to-head N-terminal interactions. Dimerisation is linked to splicing activity and solubility [Prasad2019].
- TDP-43 shuttles continuously between nucleus and cytoplasm, and this shuttling depends on transcription. Deleting the C-terminus caused large nuclear and cytoplasmic aggregates; disrupting the RNA-recognition domain reduced its presence in the nucleoplasm [Ayala2008].
- At steady state most TDP-43 is nuclear. It was first named for binding the TAR element of HIV. It acts in transcription, splicing, microRNA biogenesis, RNA transport and translation, and stress granule formation [Ling2013].
- TDP-43 was identified as the factor that binds (TG)m repeats near CFTR exon 9. Overexpressing it increased skipping of exon 9, and antisense knockdown increased its inclusion [Buratti2001].
- CLIP-seq found TDP-43 binding sites in 6,304 genes in mouse brain. Depleting TDP-43 with antisense oligonucleotides in adult mouse brain changed the levels of 601 mRNAs and altered 965 splicing events. The most depleted RNAs came from genes with very long introns that encode synaptic proteins [Polymenidou2011].
- TDP-43 autoregulates its own synthesis by binding an intron in its 3' UTR, which triggers nonsense-mediated decay of its own transcript [Polymenidou2011].
- TDP-43 represses non-conserved cryptic exons. When it was depleted, these exons were spliced into mRNAs, often disrupting translation and promoting nonsense-mediated decay. Forcing cryptic-exon repression prevented cell death, and the repression was impaired in ALS-FTD tissue [Ling2015].
- TDP-43 is an essential gene in mice [Ling2013].

### TDP-43: stress granules and phase separation
- Most TDP-43 inclusions induced in cultured cells co-localised with stress granules. Disease-linked TDP-43 mutations increased inclusion formation under stress, and drugs that block stress granule formation suppressed it. In patient CNS tissue, TDP-43 inclusions co-localised with the stress granule markers TIA-1 and eIF3 [LiuYesucevitz2010].
- Phase separation into membrane-less organelles such as stress granules is mediated by low-complexity domains in TDP-43, FUS and hnRNPA1. ALS mutations are frequently found in these domains [Taylor2016].
- Controversy, are stress granules the seed? Cytoplasmic TDP-43 formed long-lived liquid droplets that did not depend on conventional stress granules. These droplets turned into gels or solids after transient arsenite stress, recruited importin-alpha and Nup62, and mislocalised RanGap1, Ran and Nup107. The result was blocked nucleocytoplasmic transport, loss of nuclear TDP-43 and cell death [GassetRosa2019].

### TDP-43 pathology
- TDP-43 was identified as the major disease protein in both FTLD-U and ALS. Pathological TDP-43 was hyperphosphorylated, ubiquitinated and cleaved into C-terminal fragments, and was recovered only from affected CNS regions [Neumann2006].
- Independently, skein-like, intranuclear and glial inclusions in ALS spinal cord were found to be TDP-43-positive and abnormally phosphorylated, which led to the proposal of a "proteinopathy of TDP-43" [Arai2006].
- Phosphorylation sites cluster in the C-terminal region and are generated by casein kinase 1 and 2. Phosphorylation increased oligomerisation and fibrillisation. Phospho-specific antibodies stained more inclusions than ubiquitin antibodies, did not stain normal nuclei, and labelled 15 nm fibres. Insoluble fractions contained hyperphosphorylated TDP-43 at 45 kDa plus 18-26 kDa fragments [Hasegawa2008].
- C-terminal fragments were enriched in cortical and hippocampal inclusions. Inclusions in spinal motor neurons, by contrast, were positive for both N- and C-terminal epitopes, so the composition of inclusions differs between regions [Igaz2008].
- TDP-43 proteinopathy is present in about 97% of ALS and about 50% of FTD [Feldman2022]. Ling and colleagues state "over 90%" of ALS [Ling2013]. These are framings of the same evidence; neither source reports a single cohort count.
- In 111 cases, every case of sporadic ALS (n = 59), ALS with dementia (n = 26) and SOD1-negative familial ALS (n = 11) had neuronal and glial inclusions positive for both ubiquitin and TDP-43. SOD1-mutation cases (n = 15) had ubiquitin-positive inclusions but none immunoreactive for TDP-43 [Mackenzie2007].
- ALS caused by SOD1 or FUS mutations does not show TDP-43 pathology; it shows inclusions of abnormal SOD1 or FUS instead [Taylor2016].
- FUS mutations cluster in its low-complexity/prion-like domain and in its C-terminal NLS. NLS mutations increase cytoplasmic FUS, and several are associated with juvenile-onset ALS [Ling2013].
- TDP-43 inclusions are also the hallmark of inclusion body myositis, and TDP-43 is a secondary pathology in a subset of people with Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease [Taylor2016].
- LATE-NC (limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy neuropathological change) is a TDP-43 proteinopathy of older adults that causes an amnestic dementia resembling Alzheimer's. About 25% of brains in community autopsy cohorts had enough of it to be associated with cognitive impairment [Nelson2019].

### Loss of function versus gain of toxicity
- Cells with TDP-43 aggregates usually also lose nuclear TDP-43 (loss of function), while the cytoplasmic inclusions suggest a toxic gain of function. Ling and colleagues conclude that both are likely involved [Ling2013].
- Controversy: some mutant TDP-43 mouse lines lost lower motor neurons without losing nuclear TDP-43 or forming aggregates, but showed both loss and gain of splicing function in selected targets. Other lines had very different phenotypes [Ling2013].

### Cryptic exons: STMN2 and UNC13A
- In human stem-cell-derived motor neurons, 885 transcripts needed TDP-43 to keep normal levels. STMN2 (also called SCG10, a microtubule regulator) is one of the most abundant transcripts in these neurons and was particularly sensitive to TDP-43 loss [Klim2019].
- STMN2 fell after TDP-43 knockdown or mislocalisation, in motor neurons from patients with TDP-43 mutations, and in post-mortem patient spinal cord. The loss came from altered splicing (a cryptic exon). STMN2 was necessary for normal axon outgrowth and regeneration, and stabilising STMN2 protein rescued the regrowth deficits caused by TDP-43 loss [Klim2019].
- STMN2 is one of four stathmin-family microtubule-binding proteins; STMN2 and STMN4 are enriched in the CNS [Klim2019].
- With less TDP-43 bound in STMN2 intron 1, a cryptic exon ("exon 2a") with a premature polyadenylation site is used, producing a truncated, non-functional mRNA. Reduced stathmin-2 was found in motor cortex and spinal motor neurons from people with sporadic ALS and with C9orf72-expansion ALS. Restoring stathmin-2 restored axonal regeneration [Melamed2019].
- Mechanism nuance: Klim 2019 describes the STMN2 change as cryptic-exon splicing, while Melamed 2019 emphasises premature polyadenylation within the cryptic exon 2a. Both describe the same truncated transcript [Klim2019; Melamed2019].
- Loss of nuclear TDP-43 led to inclusion of a cryptic exon in UNC13A mRNA and less UNC13A protein, in human brain, neuronal cell lines and stem-cell-derived motor neurons. The top FTD and ALS risk variants lie in the intron that holds the cryptic exon and increase its splicing when TDP-43 is dysfunctional [Ma2022].
- UNC13A is critical for synapse function. Depleting TDP-43 caused robust UNC13A cryptic-exon inclusion, nonsense-mediated decay and protein loss. Two common intronic risk polymorphisms overlap TDP-43 binding sites and potentiate inclusion in patient brain and spinal cord [Brown2022].

### Spread: Brettschneider staging
- In 76 ALS autopsy cases, four sequential stages of pTDP-43 pathology were recognised. Stage 1 (n = 6): agranular motor cortex, brainstem motor nuclei of V, VII and X-XII, and spinal alpha-motor neurons. Stage 2: prefrontal neocortex (middle frontal gyrus), reticular formation, precerebellar nuclei and red nucleus [Brettschneider2013].
- Stage 3: prefrontal cortex (gyrus rectus and orbital gyri), then postcentral neocortex and striatum. Stage 4: anteromedial temporal lobe, including the hippocampus. Oligodendroglial pTDP-43 aggregates appeared at all stages [Brettschneider2013].
- The 10 C9orf72-expansion cases followed the same sequence with a greater regional burden. The authors read the pattern as consistent with propagation along axonal pathways, and as a possible basis for executive cognitive deficits [Brettschneider2013].
- Clinically, motor neuron degeneration in ALS starts focally and spreads contiguously through the three-dimensional anatomy of the UMN and LMN levels [Ravits2009]. Spread typically goes to the contralateral side and then to adjacent segments; non-contiguous spread is less common [Feldman2022].

### Other mechanisms, with evidence strength
- Glutamate excitotoxicity (human tissue plus animal data, and a modestly effective drug; causal role unproven): in synaptosomes from 13 people with ALS, maximal high-affinity glutamate transport was reduced by 59% in spinal cord, 70% in motor cortex and 39% in somatosensory cortex. It was not reduced in visual cortex, striatum or hippocampus, nor in Alzheimer's or Huntington's disease [Rothstein1992].
- The astroglial transporter GLT-1 (the human homologue is called EAAT2) was reduced by 71% in ALS motor cortex, and by 90% in about a quarter of specimens. Neuronal EAAC1 fell only 20%, while GLAST and GFAP were unchanged. The authors note the loss could be primary, secondary down-regulation, or toxic [Rothstein1995].
- When astrocytes fail to clear glutamate through EAAT2, the result is repetitive firing, calcium influx, and ER and mitochondrial stress. Astrocytes normally induce GluR2 in motor neurons, which lowers AMPA-receptor calcium permeability, and mutant SOD1 astrocytes fail to do this [Taylor2016].
- Oxidative stress (consistent biomarker evidence; therapeutic evidence weak): markers of oxidative damage are raised in ALS spinal cord and CSF. Antioxidant trials in humans have so far been disappointing. The review gives SOD1 mutations as about 20% of familial ALS, an older estimate (pass C covers current figures) [Barber2010].
- Normal SOD1 converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide or oxygen. Mutant SOD1 toxicity is an acquired property, independent of dismutase activity, and more than 170 ALS-causing mutations are known [Taylor2016].
- Feldman and colleagues group ALS mechanisms into four categories: impaired RNA metabolism, altered proteostasis and autophagy, cytoskeletal and trafficking defects, and mitochondrial dysfunction. TDP-43 is said to impair the clearance of damaged proteins, and TUBA4A and PFN1 mutations disrupt the cytoskeleton [Feldman2022].
- Proteostasis and mitochondria genes: TBK1 regulates autophagy and inflammation, and CHCHD10 encodes a mitochondrial protein. Each accounts for under 1% (or an unknown share) of ALS [Taylor2016].
- Axonal transport (strong in models, indirect in humans): motor axons can exceed one metre. SOD1 mutants slow both anterograde and retrograde transport months before neurodegeneration, and TDP-43 mutations impair axonal transport of RNA granules in Drosophila and patient-derived motor neurons [Taylor2016].
- Controversy, wild-type SOD1 in sporadic ALS: oxidised wild-type SOD1 shared a misfolded epitope (C4F6) with mutant SOD1. C4F6 immunoreactivity was found in lumbosacral motor neurons in a subset of sporadic cases, and the protein inhibited fast axonal transport [Bosco2010].
- Against this, seven conformation-specific antibodies found misfolded SOD1 below detection in spinal cord and cortex from sporadic and non-SOD1 inherited ALS [DaCruz2017].
- Prion-like spread of SOD1 (cell models): mutant SOD1 aggregates enter cells by macropinocytosis, nucleate aggregation of soluble mutant SOD1, perpetuate themselves, and transfer between cells via extracellular release [Munch2011].
- Large SOD1 aggregates are not enough to drive disease: eliminating them did not change disease in mice [Taylor2016].
- Nucleocytoplasmic transport (C9orf72 models and patient tissue): C9orf72 repeat RNA binds RanGAP1. RanGAP was mislocalised in flies, patient-derived neurons and patient brain, and nuclear import was impaired. Antisense oligonucleotides and small molecules targeting the repeat's G-quadruplexes rescued the defect [Zhang2015].
- Non-cell-autonomous toxicity, microglia (mouse genetics): in mice with a deletable mutant SOD1 gene, mutant expression in motor neurons determined disease onset and early progression. Reducing it in microglia had little effect on onset but sharply slowed later progression [Boillee2006].
- Astrocytes (cell culture): mutant SOD1 in motor neurons alone did not cause degeneration. Mutant SOD1 astrocytes killed spinal motor neurons through soluble factor(s) in a Bax-dependent way, but spared GABAergic and dorsal root ganglion neurons. Fibroblasts, microglia, cortical neurons and myocytes carrying the mutation were not overtly toxic [Nagai2007].
- Oligodendrocytes (mouse plus human tissue): grey-matter oligodendrocytes degenerated before onset in SOD1(G93A) mice, and their replacements failed to mature, so demyelination progressed. Grey-matter demyelination was also seen in patient motor cortex and spinal cord. Removing mutant SOD1 from oligodendroglia delayed onset and prolonged survival [Kang2013].
- Lower levels of the MCT1 lactate transporter in oligodendrocytes may reduce energy supply to motor axons [Taylor2016].
- Neuroinflammation (consistent association; causal weight debated): activated microglia and astroglia, pro-inflammatory lymphocytes and macrophages are seen in ALS. Dysfunctional regulatory T cells and pro-inflammatory macrophages occur even without known mutations [Beers2019]. In SOD1 mice, glial activation followed rather than preceded distal axon degeneration [Fischer2004].
- C9orf72 (one paragraph; pass C covers genetics): a GGGGCC expansion of about 400-1,600 repeats lies in the non-coding region of C9orf72 [Ling2013]. In the discovery series it was the most common genetic abnormality in familial FTD (11.7%) and familial ALS (23.5%) [DeJesusHernandez2011]. It underlay 46.0% of familial and 21.1% of sporadic ALS in Finland, and about one-third of familial ALS of European descent [Renton2011]. Three mechanisms are proposed: loss of C9orf72 protein function, toxic repeat RNA (nuclear foci), and dipeptide repeat proteins made by repeat-associated non-ATG translation [Balendra2018; DeJesusHernandez2011].

### Riluzole and edaravone (mechanism level)
- Riluzole inhibits glutamate release from cultured neurons, from brain slices and in vivo. This is thought to come partly from inactivating voltage-dependent sodium channels on glutamatergic terminals and partly from a G-protein-dependent process. It also blocks NMDA receptors noncompetitively [Doble1996].
- In rat cortical neurons, riluzole blocked the persistent sodium current (EC50 2 microM) at therapeutic concentrations. It inhibited the fast transient sodium current only at much higher levels [Urbani2000].
- The US label states that the mechanism by which riluzole works in ALS is unknown [FDARiluzoleLabel]. Feldman and colleagues call it an anti-glutamate agent and note that it is still debated at which disease stages it prolongs survival [Feldman2022].
- The glutamate rationale was tested in a trial of 155 outpatients on 100 mg/day. At 12 months, 74% on riluzole were alive against 58% on placebo (P = 0.014) [Bensimon1994].
- Edaravone is a low-molecular-weight, amphiphilic antioxidant that scavenges lipid- and water-soluble peroxyl radicals by donating an electron; its action was inferred to be peroxynitrite scavenging. It was approved in Japan for acute stroke in 2001 and for ALS in 2015, and by the FDA for ALS in 2017 [Watanabe2018].
- The US label states that the mechanism by which edaravone works in ALS is unknown [FDAEdaravoneLabel].
- In the confirmatory trial in a selected early-stage population, the change in ALSFRS-R at 24 weeks was -5.01 with edaravone and -7.50 with placebo (difference 2.49; 95% CI 0.99-3.98) [MCI186Group2017]. Its efficacy and generalisability remain contested [Feldman2022].

### Neurofilament light chain (mechanism level)
- Neurofilaments are major structural proteins of neurons, made of light (NfL), medium and heavy chains plus alpha-internexin. When the axonal membrane is disrupted they are released into interstitial fluid, then into CSF and blood [Gaiottino2013].
- Neurofilament levels rise with neuroaxonal damage whatever the cause, so NfL is not disease-specific. Fourth-generation single-molecule-array (Simoa) assays measure it reliably in blood [Khalil2018].
- In serum, NfL was 95.4 pg/ml in ALS (n = 46), 79.4 in Guillain-Barre syndrome, 30.8 in Alzheimer's disease, 4.4 in neurological controls and 3.3 in healthy controls; ALS levels were more than 20-fold higher than controls [Gaiottino2013].
- Blood NfL was about four times higher in ALS than in controls and stayed relatively constant during follow-up. The highest tertile at baseline carried a mortality hazard ratio of 3.91 (95% CI 1.98-7.94) [Lu2015].
- Serum NfL was higher in ALS than in all other groups, including ALS mimics (disease controls), except Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease. It was stable over time within individuals (pass D covers the cut-off and its accuracy) [Verde2019].
- In gene-carrier phenoconverters, NfL was raised as far back as about 12 months before the first symptoms or signs [Benatar2018]. Feldman and colleagues state that plasma neurofilaments are raised up to five years before onset in sporadic and familial ALS [Feldman2022]. The difference reflects different cohorts and is not a direct contradiction.
- In SOD1 ALS, tofersen lowered CSF SOD1 and plasma NfL against placebo over 28 weeks without a significant difference in the primary clinical endpoint. This illustrates NfL as a pharmacodynamic marker of axonal injury [Miller2022].

## Step sequence

### (a) Upper versus lower motor neuron degeneration: what examination and EMG show
1. Healthy circuit: an upper motor neuron (a Betz cell in layer 5 of primary motor cortex) sends its axon down the corticospinal tract, or to brainstem motor nuclei [Feldman2022; Brettschneider2013].
2. Most corticospinal axons cross at the pyramidal decussation, at the junction of brainstem and spinal cord. They contact anterior horn lower motor neurons directly (cortico-motoneuronal connections) or through interneurons [Welniarz2017; Lemon2008].
3. Each lower motor neuron and the muscle fibres it supplies form a motor unit (FF, FR or S type). The impulse crosses the neuromuscular junction to contract those fibres. The diaphragm's motor neurons sit at C3-C5 [Moloney2014; Mantilla2008].
4. Early cortical change: before or around symptom onset, TMS can show cortical hyperexcitability (loss of short-interval intracortical inhibition), the basis of the dying-forward hypothesis [Vucic2006; Vucic2008].
5. UMN loss: examination shows brisk and spreading reflexes, Hoffmann and Babinski signs, reflexes kept in a weak limb, and spasticity. In the bulbar system it can include pseudobulbar affect, which also involves corticopontocerebellar pathways [Feldman2022; Floeter2014].
6. LMN loss begins distally: the NMJ denervates before motor neuron cell bodies are lost (dying-back, mouse evidence). FF units go first [Fischer2004; Moloney2014].
7. Denervated fibres fire on their own, and EMG shows fibrillations and positive sharp waves. Unstable distal motor axons produce fasciculation potentials, which Awaji counts as equivalent to fibrillations in suspected ALS [Joyce2013; deCarvalho2013; deCarvalho2008].
8. Surviving neurons sprout and adopt the orphaned fibres, so motor units enlarge. EMG shows large, long-duration, polyphasic MUPs with reduced recruitment and unstable transmission. Strength can be preserved despite loss of up to half the units [Joyce2013; Nijssen2017].
9. Compensation fails: weakness and atrophy become clinically evident, and in some SOD1 carriers MUNE falls abruptly months before weakness. UMN signs may then become harder to elicit in a wasted limb [Aggarwal2002; Swash2012].
10. ALS pattern: both sets of signs appear, and spread contiguously from the onset region. Thenar muscles are lost before hypothenar (the split hand), while the oculomotor nuclei and Onuf's nucleus are relatively spared until late [Ravits2009; Eisen2012; Nijssen2017; Mannen1977].

### (b) TDP-43: from nuclear function to axon failure
1. Normal TDP-43 (414 aa: N-terminal domain with NLS, RRM1, RRM2 and a C-terminal glycine-rich prion-like domain) is mostly nuclear and is thought to work as a dimer [Prasad2019; Ling2013].
2. In the nucleus it binds UG/TG-rich RNA at thousands of genes, regulating splicing and stability (including long-intron synaptic genes), and it autoregulates its own level [Buratti2001; Polymenidou2011].
3. A key nuclear job is repressing cryptic exons. For example, its binding in STMN2 intron 1 keeps cryptic exon 2a and its premature polyadenylation site out of the mature mRNA [Ling2015; Melamed2019].
4. TDP-43 shuttles to the cytoplasm in a transcription-dependent way. Under stress it is recruited into stress granules through its low-complexity domain [Ayala2008; LiuYesucevitz2010; Taylor2016].
5. With persistent stress or raised cytoplasmic concentration, cytoplasmic TDP-43 droplets harden into gels or solids. This can happen independently of conventional stress granules, and the droplets sequester nuclear transport factors, impairing import [GassetRosa2019].
6. TDP-43 is cleared from the nucleus and builds up in cytoplasmic inclusions that are hyperphosphorylated (CK1/CK2, C-terminal sites), ubiquitinated and cleaved into 18-26 kDa C-terminal fragments. This occurs in about 97% of ALS [Neumann2006; Hasegawa2008; Feldman2022].
7. Loss of function: without nuclear TDP-43, cryptic exons enter transcripts. STMN2 is truncated and its protein falls; UNC13A gains a cryptic exon and undergoes nonsense-mediated decay, which ALS risk variants make worse [Klim2019; Melamed2019; Ma2022; Brown2022].
8. Gain of toxicity: aggregates and phase-transitioned TDP-43 may add toxicity and disrupt nucleocytoplasmic transport, so both loss and gain of function probably contribute [Ling2013; GassetRosa2019].
9. Axon failure: without stathmin-2, motor neurons cannot sustain axon outgrowth or regenerate after injury, and loss of UNC13A impairs synaptic function. Restoring stathmin-2 rescues regrowth in cell models [Klim2019; Melamed2019; Brown2022].
10. Spread: pTDP-43 pathology progresses through four Brettschneider stages, from motor cortex, brainstem and spinal motor neurons to prefrontal, then postcentral and striatal, then hippocampal regions. This is consistent with propagation along axonal pathways [Brettschneider2013].

- Rosen1993 citation trap: Europe PMC lists two records with the same title. The original paper is Nature 1993;362(6415):59-62 (PMID 8446170, doi:10.1038/362059a0). PMID 8332197 (Nature 1993;364(6435):362) is a later one-page item with the same title, probably a correction. Hardiman2017 has the same problem: the article is Nat Rev Dis Primers 2017;3:17071 (PMID 28980624); PMID 29052611 (17085) is its correction notice.
- The "about 97%" figure for TDP-43 pathology is stated in Feldman 2022 (full text). Ling 2013, often cited for 97%, says "over 90%" in its text; its Figure 1 was not examined as an image. Mackenzie 2007 supports the qualitative point that all sporadic and non-SOD1 cases in their series (n = 96) were TDP-43-positive, and none of 15 SOD1 cases were.
- Melamed 2019 frames the STMN2 defect as cryptic polyadenylation within a cryptic exon ("exon 2a"), and Klim 2019 frames it as cryptic-exon splicing. Both describe the same truncated transcript; the guide should not imply two separate mechanisms.
- Rothstein 1995 measured "GLT-1", rodent nomenclature for the astroglial transporter that Taylor 2016 calls EAAT2. The guide can say EAAT2 (GLT-1).
- The "loss of up to half of motor units before weakness" statement is secondary, from Joyce 2013 citing Daube 1985 (Neurol Clin 1985;3:473-493), which was not read. Aggarwal 2002 suggests that loss can also be abrupt just before weakness.
- The Benatar 2018 Europe PMC abstract ends with "Ann Neurol 2018;83:130-139", but the registry metadata (Europe PMC and PubMed) gives volume 84(1):130-139. Volume 84 is used here.
- Barber 2010 gives SOD1 as about 20% of familial ALS, an older estimate; pass C should give current figures.
- Not verified from any source read in this pass, so flagged in the Facts: the proportion of corticospinal fibres that cross (about 75-90%), cramps and hyporeflexia as LMN signs, and the "loss of descending inhibition" physiology of spasticity.
