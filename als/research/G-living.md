# G. Living, communication & end of life
This pass covers what published studies, guidelines and official registries say about cognition and behaviour, communication (from speech to brain-computer interfaces), mood and quality of life, practical domains (driving, work, money, home, pregnancy, multidisciplinary clinics), advance care planning and palliative care, how people with ALS die, assisted dying where it is legal (reported as law and data only), caregivers, economic burden and long survival. Every journal record was checked against PubMed (esummary/efetch) and Europe PMC in this pass. Most items were read as abstracts. Full text was read for Galvin 2016, Heidinger 2025, Van Damme 2024 (EAN guideline) and Ball 2004 (an author-hosted PDF). Three official sources were read directly: the NICE NG42 recommendations page, the Oregon 2025 Death with Dignity Act Data Summary (PDF) and UK DVLA/GOV.UK pages. The Health Canada Sixth Annual MAID Report web page was read through a summarising fetch tool, so its figures are quoted as that tool reported them. Some things could not be read: full text of the AAN 2009 practice parameter (Miller2009AANb), of Strong 2017 and of Peters 2024; the Taylor & Francis abstract page for Ball 2004 (403); and Faull & Oliver 2016 (APM/ABN ventilation-withdrawal guidance, which has no abstract), so that guidance is not cited. Pass F (symptoms, pseudobulbar affect) and pass A (survival curves) are only cross-referenced here. Nothing found in this pass gives a citable figure for "hope" in ALS, for air travel, or for locked-in state across the whole ALS population (published figures are among people on tracheostomy ventilation only). Those points are marked below. The counts (79 sources, about 115 fact bullets including controversy notes) are above the 25-45 and 60-90 targets because the brief spans nine sub-areas. Shared keys used: Strong2017, Miller2009AANb, Andersen2012EFNS, NICE2016. vanEs2017, Feldman2022 and Hardiman2017 were not read in this pass and are not cited.

## Facts

### Cognition and behaviour
- In a population-based Irish cohort of 160 people with newly diagnosed ALS, 13.8% met Neary criteria for frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Another 34.1% of those without dementia met consensus criteria for cognitive impairment, and 46.9% had no detectable cognitive abnormality [Phukan2012].
- In the Piemonte (Italy) population-based series (183 people with ALS analysed), 49.7% were cognitively normal. 12.6% had ALS-FTD, 19.7% executive cognitive impairment, 5.5% non-executive impairment, 6.0% behavioural impairment alone and 6.0% non-classifiable impairment. The authors read this as "about 50%" having some cognitive impairment, consistent with the Irish study despite different genetic backgrounds [Montuschi2015].
- In Montuschi's series, people with ALS-FTD were older, had less education and had shorter survival than every other cognitive group. Six of the nine C9orf72 carriers had ALS-FTD [Montuschi2015].
- The 2017 revised consensus criteria frame cognitive and behavioural change in ALS as a spectrum, the ALS-frontotemporal spectrum disorder (ALS-FTSD). They state that neuropsychological deficits affect "over 50%" of people with ALS, may include social-cognition and language deficits, and when present adversely affect survival [Strong2017].
- Controversy (measurement): prevalence depends on criteria and tests. About 14% FTD plus 34% non-demented impairment in Ireland [Phukan2012], about 50% any impairment in Italy [Montuschi2015], and "over 50%" in the consensus statement [Strong2017]. Figures are not directly comparable across studies.
- The Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS) is a 15-20 minute screen designed for people with physical disability. Its ALS-Specific score covers executive functions and social cognition, fluency and language. Its ALS Non-specific score covers memory and visuospatial function. A carer interview covers five behavioural domains characteristic of FTD [Abrahams2014].
- Original ECAS abnormality cut-offs, derived from healthy controls, were 77/100 (ALS-Specific), 24/36 (ALS Non-specific) and 105/136 (Total) [Abrahams2014]. Later age- and education-adjusted norms exist but were not read in this pass (unverified).
- In the ECAS development sample (48 people with ALS, none with evident dementia), 29% had abnormal ALS-Specific scores and 6% also had abnormal Non-specific scores. Language deficits were most frequent (35%), then executive functions and fluency (23% each). Carers reported behaviour change in at least one domain for 40%, and 15% met criteria for possible FTD [Abrahams2014].
- Comorbid FTD was associated with shorter survival in the Irish population-based cohort (HR 2.67, 95% CI 1.04-6.85). In people without dementia, executive dysfunction independently predicted shorter survival (multivariable HR 3.44, 95% CI 1.45-8.18). Impairment in other cognitive domains without executive dysfunction did not [Elamin2011].
- In a Torino population-based cohort (128 people), 32.0% had neurobehavioural dysfunction on the family-rated FrSBe within 4 months of diagnosis. Median survival was 3.3 years with dysfunction vs 4.3 years without, and survival after enteral nutrition and NIV was shorter in the dysfunction group [Chio2012].
- Controversy (behaviour and survival): an Irish cross-sectional population-based study of 317 people found that behavioural categorisation (none/mild-moderate/severe) was not associated with survival (P = 0.198), unlike Chio2012 [Burke2017; Chio2012].
- On the Beaumont Behavioural Inventory, 57% of 317 people with ALS had no behavioural change, 30% mild-moderate change and 13% clinically severe change suggestive of behavioural-variant FTD. The most frequently endorsed behaviours were reduced concern for hygiene (36.8%), irritability (36.2%), new unusual habits (33.4%) and increased apathy (31.1%) [Burke2017].
- In 225 people with sporadic ALS rated on the FrSBe, 39.6% had impairment in at least one behavioural domain, and apathy was the most common (31.1%). The authors link recognising such change to recognising changes in decision-making capacity [Witgert2010].
- Note on "apathy is the commonest behavioural change": Witgert2010 supports this on the FrSBe. In Burke2017 apathy (31.1%) ranked fourth by item frequency, behind hygiene, irritability and unusual habits. The claim depends on the instrument [Witgert2010; Burke2017].
- In a longitudinal study of 49 people with ALS and their caregivers, initiation apathy was the most common apathy subtype at baseline (38.8%), followed by emotional apathy (16.3%). Initiation and executive apathy increased over time and were variably linked to higher caregiver burden or strain [Radakovic2025].
- NICE advises awareness that people with MND and FTD may lack mental capacity (care in line with the Mental Capacity Act 2005). It recommends exploring cognitive or behavioural change at diagnosis and when concern arises, with referral for formal assessment if needed [NICE2016].
- The AAN 2009 practice parameter recommends that screening for cognitive and behavioural impairment be considered (Level B), "because many patients with ALS demonstrate cognitive impairment, which in some cases meets criteria for dementia" [Miller2009AANb].

### Communication
- More than 80% of people living with MND develop speech difficulties, and most eventually use an AAC device. This is stated as background in Cave & Bloch, and Ball 2004 attributes a similar 80% figure to a 1982 source not read here, so treat it as secondary [Cave2021; Ball2004].
- In 30 consecutive people with ALS followed for 2 years, speech deteriorated to "poor" and needed AAC methods in 60%. Speech stayed adequate on average for 18 months from the first bulbar symptom, and severity, articulation rate and intelligibility declined in nearly all participants, faster in bulbar-onset disease [Makkonen2018].
- Voice banking creates an approximation of a person's own voice for later use in a speech-generating device. In interviews with 12 people with MND (9 had banked, 3 had not), "preserving identity" was the main motivation. Few participants understood how a banked voice is actually used in AAC [Cave2021].
- Message banking means recording whole words and phrases in one's own voice for later playback. In a survey after a short presentation, most people with MND, significant others and speech-language pathologists had never heard of it, but all were interested [Oosthuizen2018].
- In a 2021 US online survey of 216 people with ALS, over 70% reported some detectable speech disturbance and about half used aided communication face to face. Among those with severe speech impairment, over 90% used speech-generating devices and just over half used low-tech AAC. Fewer than half said their family had received communication education or support [Peters2024].
- In 50 people with ALS followed over 4 years at a US centre, 96% accepted AAC technology (90% immediately, 6% after delay), 4% (n = 2) rejected it, and none discontinued use [Ball2004].
- NICE NG42: speech and communication should be assessed by a speech and language therapist "without delay". AAC equipment should be provided without delay, including low-tech (alphabet, word or picture boards) and high-tech (PC or tablet voice-output aids). People who need or are likely to need complex technology such as eye-gaze access should be referred to a specialised NHS AAC hub, and needs reviewed as MND progresses [NICE2016].
- The 2024 EAN guideline adapts the NICE communication recommendations. It names message and voice banking and eye-gaze access among the reasons to refer to a specialised AAC hub [VanDamme2024].
- The EFNS 2012 guideline states that maintaining the ability to communicate is essential [Andersen2012EFNS].
- In 11 people with advanced ALS (mean ALSFRS-R 5.3; 82% invasively ventilated) who used eye-tracking computer systems, mean daily use was 9.1 hours (range 0.5-16). Satisfaction was high, and use was associated with higher psychological well-being [Linse2018].
- Among 70 people with ALS on tracheostomy positive-pressure ventilation (TPPV) in Japan, 11.4% developed a totally locked-in state (TLS). Among the 33 on TPPV for more than 5 years, 18.2% developed TLS and 33.1% a minimal communication state [Hayashi2003].
- A 2006 Japanese nationwide survey found TLS in 89 of 709 people with ALS on TPPV (13%). About 70% developed TLS within 5 years of starting TPPV, and 90% of those studied further had total ophthalmoplegia at TLS onset [Kawata2008].
- No source read in this pass gives the frequency of locked-in or completely locked-in state across all people with ALS. The figures above apply only to people on long-term tracheostomy ventilation [Hayashi2003; Kawata2008].
- Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) are early evidence, mostly single participants. In 2016 a fully implanted BCI with subdural electrodes over the motor cortex let one woman with late-stage ALS and locked-in syndrome type independently at about two letters per minute, supplementing her eye-tracker [Vansteensel2016].
- An intracortical speech BCI in one participant with ALS who could no longer speak intelligibly decoded attempted speech at 62 words per minute. Word error rate was 9.1% on a 50-word vocabulary and 23.8% on a 125,000-word vocabulary; natural conversation runs at about 160 words per minute [Willett2023].
- In one 45-year-old man with ALS (BrainGate2, NCT00912041) with four microelectrode arrays, accuracy was 99.6% (50 words) on day 1 and 90.2% (125,000 words) on day 2. Accuracy held at 97.5% over 8.4 months, with self-paced conversation at about 32 words per minute for more than 248 hours. Output was spoken by text-to-speech designed to sound like his pre-ALS voice [Card2024].
- In the same research programme, a brain-to-voice neuroprosthesis synthesised voice instantaneously from 256 intracortical electrodes in a man with ALS and severe dysarthria. It let him change intonation and sing short melodies [Wairagkar2025].
- In one person with ALS in a completely locked-in state, intracortical arrays with auditory neurofeedback allowed letter-by-letter spelling. The authors present this as evidence that brain-based volitional communication is possible even in that state [Chaudhary2022].
- In the first-in-human SWITCH study, an endovascular "Stentrode" BCI was implanted via the jugular vein in 4 people with ALS (plus 1 with primary lateral sclerosis, enrolled but not analysed). There were no device-related serious adverse events over 12 months, and each analysed participant controlled a computer [Mitchell2023].

### Mood, quality of life and suicide risk
- A 2021 meta-analysis of 46 studies gave a pooled depression prevalence of 34% (95% CI 27-41%): mild 29%, moderate 16%, severe 8%. Prevalence varied by instrument: 50% with the BDI, 20% with the PHQ and 15% with the HADS [Heidari2021].
- A 2007 review reported depression prevalence of 0-44% across studies, but consistent rates of 9-11% in studies using DSM-IV structured interviews. Anxiety prevalence ranged from 0% to 30% [Kurt2007].
- Controversy (measurement): self-report scales with somatic items (e.g. BDI) give much higher depression figures than structured interviews or the HADS. The spread from about 10% to 50% reflects method as much as mood [Heidari2021; Kurt2007].
- In hospice-eligible people with late-stage ALS interviewed monthly until death or tracheostomy (80 participants), 81% had no depressive disorder at baseline, 10% minor depression and 9% symptoms consistent with major depression. 57% never had a depression diagnosis at any visit, and depression did not increase as death approached [Rabkin2005].
- In 433 people with ALS without dementia, clinically significant state anxiety was found in 18.2% and trait anxiety in 13.9%. Anxiety was driven mainly by depressive features and was independent of motor and cognitive/behavioural status [Aiello2026].
- In 96 people with ALS, self-rated overall quality of life (McGill single item) did not correlate with strength or physical function. It appeared to depend on psychological, existential and spiritual factors [Simmons2000].
- Followed over 6 months (60 people), quality of life was maintained as physical function declined [Robbins2001].
- In 80 consecutive people with ALS, perceived quality of social support was the most important explanatory variable of quality of life on both SEIQoL-DW and MQOL, and physical status was not relevant [Chio2004].
- The "disability paradox" names the observation that many people with serious, persistent disabilities report good or excellent quality of life (54.3% of 153 people with moderate-to-serious disabilities in the original study). That study was not ALS-specific [Albrecht1999].
- In 19 people with ALS in a locked-in state (Polish NEEDSinALS sample, answering via eye-tracking), most reported positive quality of life and no clinically relevant depression. All would again choose their current life-sustaining treatments, wish for hastened death was low, and caregivers significantly underestimated patients' well-being [KuzmaKozakiewicz2019].
- Hope: no study quantifying hope in ALS was read in this pass. Rabkin2005 measured hopelessness but its abstract gives no figure, and Maessen2009 links hopelessness to euthanasia/PAS and to continuous deep sedation [Rabkin2005; Maessen2009].
- In a Swedish population-based cohort (6,642 people with ALS, 1965-2004), suicide risk was almost six-fold that of the general population (SMR 5.8, 95% CI 3.6-8.8; 21 observed vs 3.6 expected). It was highest in the first year after first hospitalisation (SMR 11.2) and stayed raised 3 years later. This study appeared in Brain, not NEJM [Fang2008].
- In a Korean nationwide cohort of 2,955 people with ALS, 47 attempted suicide (annual prevalence 0.29-1.12%). 42.6% of attempts occurred within 3 months of diagnosis, and prior psychiatric hospitalisation roughly tripled the risk (HR 3.17) [Keun2023].
- Fang2008 frames suicide data from countries without legal assisted dying as complementary evidence on the wish to die. Absolute numbers are small: 21 suicides among 6,642 people [Fang2008].

### Practical domains: driving, work, money, home, pregnancy, clinics
- UK: a person with MND (also known as ALS) must notify the DVLA, and failing to report a condition that affects driving can bring a fine of up to GBP 1,000 [DVLA2026].
- DVLA rules for chronic neurological disorders including MND: Group 1 (car/motorcycle) drivers may continue as long as safe vehicle control is maintained at all times. A licence of 1, 2, 3 or 5 years may be issued and may restrict driving to cars with certain controls. Group 2 (bus/lorry) licences are refused or revoked if the condition is progressive or disabling [DVLA2026].
- In a small Norwegian observational study (31 people with ALS assessed within 4 months of diagnosis), 61% were driving at baseline and 11 were cognitively impaired. Baseline cognition did not predict who had stopped driving 4 months later [Taule2025].
- A 2026 review notes that driving rules differ widely between countries. It argues that cognitive, behavioural, respiratory and other non-motor features of MND, not only weakness, affect driving safety [Kleinerova2026].
- No AAN guideline on driving in ALS was found in this pass.
- NICE lists information to give at diagnosis, including legal rights (social care support, employment rights and benefits) and disclosure duties such as notifying the DVLA. Psychological and social support should include financial advice, support to engage in work and hobbies, and respite care [NICE2016].
- NICE recommends prompt access to assessment for funding of home adaptations, and integration of equipment such as AAC aids and environmental controls with wheelchairs [NICE2016].
- In 38 published cases of ALS overlapping with pregnancy (systematic review of 26 articles), 18 were under 30. Onset was before pregnancy in 18, during it in 16 and directly after it in 4. Progression during pregnancy was rapid or severe in 55%, 95% completed the pregnancy with a live birth (preterm delivery common), and 86% of neonates were healthy. This is case-report evidence subject to publication bias [Hamad2023].
- In the Irish ALS register (1996-2000), people who attended the multidisciplinary ALS clinic (24%) had median survival 7.5 months longer than those in general neurology clinics. Bulbar-onset survival was extended by 9.6 months, and clinic attendance was an independent covariate (HR 1.47) [Traynor2003].
- In 719 incident cases across Ireland and Northern Ireland (2005-2010), multidisciplinary clinic attendance was associated with longer survival (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.49-0.71) [Rooney2015].
- Controversy (selection bias): the clinic cohort in Traynor2003 was about five years younger and more often took riluzole (99% vs 61%), so these observational survival gains may partly reflect who attends [Traynor2003].
- The AAN recommends that referral to a multidisciplinary clinic be considered to optimise care and prolong survival (Level B) and may be considered to enhance quality of life (Level C) [Miller2009AANb].
- NICE recommends coordinated MDT clinic assessments, usually every 2 to 3 months, and a face-to-face follow-up within 4 weeks of diagnosis. The core team includes a speech and language therapist and someone with palliative care expertise [NICE2016].
- Travel: no peer-reviewed source on air or long-distance travel in ALS was read in this pass.

### Advance care planning and palliative care
- A review of international guidelines states that optimal ALS management requires a palliative approach from diagnosis. It notes that late referral to palliative services is common and that integration varies widely between health systems [Bede2011].
- NICE: offer the chance to discuss end-of-life preferences at trigger points: at diagnosis, at a significant change in respiratory function, and when interventions such as gastrostomy or NIV are needed. Be prepared to discuss end-of-life issues whenever the person wishes [NICE2016].
- NICE lists advance care planning topics: how death may occur, anticipatory medicines at home, Advance Decisions to Refuse Treatment (ADRT), DNACPR orders and Lasting Power of Attorney. It advises discussing these earlier if communication, cognition or capacity is expected to worsen [NICE2016].
- EFNS 2012: advance directives for palliative end-of-life care should be discussed early with the person and carers, respecting social and cultural background [Andersen2012EFNS].
- The EAN 2024 guideline adapts NICE's end-of-life recommendations. Its additions include discussing invasive ventilation as part of advance care planning (aiming to avoid emergency invasive ventilation), palliative sedation and withdrawal of treatment. It also adds a recommendation to be prepared to discuss euthanasia and assisted suicide [VanDamme2024].
- A 2024 meta-analysis of 26 studies found advance directives signed in 60.4% of records (1,629 of 2,696 people). The proportion was lower in 2012-2024 studies (42%) than in 1999-2011 studies (78%) [Mangal2024].
- At a US academic ALS centre (513 people, 2014-2018), 30% had any advance care planning document, 16.6% had a POLST form and only 5.1% saw a palliative care physician [Takacs2022].
- In a Scottish national cohort (188 deceased people with MND), advance care planning (OR 3.94) and specialist palliative care input for 28 days or more (OR 2.66) were associated with dying outside hospital. NIV use was associated with lower odds (OR 0.31) [Quail2025].
- Gastrostomy decisions: NICE notes "some people will not want to have a gastrostomy" and advises explaining the benefits of early placement and the risks of late placement [NICE2016].
- In an Australian MND service, 73% of 33 people recommended a gastrostomy accepted it. Decisions took days to months and reflected perceived swallowing and nutrition, carer burden, independence and control [Labra2020].
- NIV continuation and withdrawal: NICE recommends regular opportunities to discuss wishes about continuing or withdrawing NIV, and discussing all such decisions with the person and, if they agree, family and carers [NICE2016].
- In a UK evaluation of 46 ventilation withdrawals (42 NIV, 4 tracheostomy ventilation), 43% took place at home and 48% in an inpatient hospice. 89% received opioid and/or sedative medication at the start, and the median time from first medication to removal was 45 minutes. People on tracheostomy ventilation died within 30 minutes; after NIV withdrawal the most common time to death was 15 minutes (range under 15 minutes to 54 hours) [Faull2022].
- In Denmark (2002-2009), 12 people with ALS on continuous invasive home ventilation chose to withdraw it, a median 22 months after starting. All cited a general loss of "meaning in life", and withdrawal was done under deep sedation with morphine and diazepam [Dreyer2012].
- In Italy, Law 219/2017 established the right to request withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment including mechanical ventilation. A survey of 34 of 40 ALS centres found withdrawals increased afterwards, with variable involvement of palliative and community services [Moglia2023].
- Tracheostomy uptake varies by setting. In the Piemonte/Valle d'Aosta register (1995-2004), 10.6% of 1,260 people had a tracheostomy, median survival afterwards was 253 days, and young men were more likely to have one [Chio2010].
- In a Japanese multicentre registry (2006-2018), propensity-matched median survival was 11.33 years with tracheostomy invasive ventilation vs 4.61 years without [Hayashi2020].
- Controversy (between-country variation): tracheostomy rates and survival after tracheostomy differ between countries (e.g. under 1 year median after tracheostomy in Piemonte vs about 7 years' extra life expectancy in Japan). Chio2010 attributes part of the difference to sociocultural factors. The populations and study designs are not comparable [Chio2010; Hayashi2020].

### How people with ALS die
- Relatives of 121 people with ALS in Munich and records of 50 people at a UK hospice: most died peacefully (Germany 88%, UK 98%), and no patient "choked to death". The commonest symptoms in the last 24 hours were dyspnoea, coughing, anxiety and restlessness [Neudert2001].
- In the same study, around half died at home (Germany 55%, UK 52%). Morphine was in use in the terminal phase in 27% (Germany) and 82% (UK), and benzodiazepines in 32% and 64% [Neudert2001].
- In a French prospective study of 302 deaths, respiratory failure was the most frequent reported cause (77%): terminal respiratory insufficiency 58%, pneumonia 14%, asphyxia due to a foreign body 3%, pulmonary embolism 2%. Suicide accounted for 1.3%, and 63% died in a medical facility [Gil2008].
- Controversy (death certificates vs autopsy): in 100 autopsies at the Salpetriere, clinical and pathological causes of death agreed in only 20%. Bronchopneumonia and pneumonia were the main causes at autopsy, heart failure 10% and pulmonary embolism 6% [Corcia2008].
- Place of death: US death certificates for 30,066 MND deaths (2018-2021) showed home deaths rising from 45.5% before COVID-19 to 54.4% during the pandemic [Raymond2026].
- In 77 ventilated people with ALS from five European hospitals, 57% died at home. The authors stress large variation between and within countries, and argue the conditions of dying may matter more than the place [Escarrabill2014].
- Symptom control: NICE advises considering opioids to relieve breathlessness and benzodiazepines when breathlessness is made worse by anxiety (both off-label in February 2016). Towards the end of life it advises prompt access to anticipatory medicines, specialist palliative care, AAC and equipment, plus bereavement support for families [NICE2016].
- The EAN 2024 guideline repeats the opioid and benzodiazepine options for people who do not tolerate or accept NIV or invasive ventilation, in whom NIV no longer works, or in end-stage disease [VanDamme2024].
- In a 6-patient prospective pilot in terminal ALS, morphine lowered respiratory rate (42 to 29/min) and dyspnoea score (7.5 to 1.8 on a 0-10 scale) at 120 minutes without significant CO2 rise or respiratory depression. Fear of choking correlated with dyspnoea intensity (r = 0.861) [Clemens2008].

### Assisted dying where legal (law and registry data, reported neutrally)
- Netherlands, 1994-1999: of 203 people with ALS whose physicians responded, 17% died by euthanasia and 3% by physician-assisted suicide (PAS). Importance of religion was associated with less use, and income, education and care characteristics were not associated [Veldink2002].
- Netherlands, 2000-2005: 16.8% chose euthanasia or PAS (vs 20.2% in 1994-1998), and 14.8% died during continuous deep sedation. Euthanasia/PAS was not associated with depressive symptoms or quality-of-care items [Maessen2009].
- Netherlands, 2012-2020: of 4,130 ALS deaths reported to the euthanasia review committees, 1,014 were by euthanasia or PAS (mean 25% per year) [vanEenennaam2023].
- In van Eenennaam's 2014-2016 clinician survey, euthanasia accounted for 40% of 356 ALS deaths. Median survival from diagnosis was the same with and without euthanasia (15.9 vs 16.1 months; HR 1.07), and caregivers reported high satisfaction with end-of-life care in both groups [vanEenennaam2023].
- Controversy (denominators): Dutch figures range from 25% (all ALS deaths reported to the review committees) to 40% (a survey with a 49% clinician response rate). The two have different denominators and possible response bias [vanEenennaam2023].
- Oregon (Death with Dignity Act, physician-assisted death only): in 2025, ALS was the underlying illness for 30 of 400 deaths (7.5%), and for 260 of 3,691 deaths (7.0%) across 1998-2025. Losing autonomy was the most frequently reported end-of-life concern overall (89.9%) [OHA2026].
- The 2025 Oregon report gives 421 DWDA deaths for 2024, revised from 376 in the 2024 report, so year totals change as late reports arrive [OHA2026].
- Canada (MAID, 2024 data): 16,499 people received MAID (5.1% of all deaths). People with ALS were 19.8% (n = 450) of recipients with a neurological condition and 2.7% of all provisions. These figures were read through a summarising fetch tool [HealthCanada2025].
- In a single Canadian (Edmonton) multidisciplinary clinic, 55 of 255 people with ALS who died in 2019-2024 (21.6%) received MAID. PEG use was inversely associated with MAID (OR 0.34), and MAID deaths more often occurred at home (62% vs 35%) [Zhu2026].
- Across 20 jurisdictions with legal assisted dying (1999-2023), 2,967 of 17,631 ALS deaths (16.8%) were by MAID, the highest share of any condition (cancer 3.7%). Adjusted for jurisdiction and year, people dying of ALS were 6.84 times as likely as people dying of cancer to receive MAID [Heidinger2025].

### Caregivers
- In an Irish cohort of 81 informal caregivers, 70% were female and 72% spouses or partners (adult children 22%), and 83% lived with the person with ALS. They gave an average of 47 hours of care a week (median 26.5) [Galvin2016].
- In Galvin's cohort, the mean Zarit Burden Interview score was 27.1 (SD 14.7). With a cut-off of 24 or more, 52% were in the "high burden" category, and burden was associated with hours of care, lower quality of life and psychological distress [Galvin2016].
- In a 1997 US survey of 98 people with ALS, primary caregivers spent a median of 11 hours a day caring even when home care was in place. 42% felt physically unwell and 48% psychologically unwell [Krivickas1997].
- In a postal survey of 140 caregivers in Australia, 48% had high burden (Zarit). Patients' abnormal behaviour (e.g. disinhibition, impulsivity) predicted high burden more strongly than physical disability (OR 1.4), followed by caregiver stress [Lillo2012].
- A 2018 systematic review (25 studies, GRADE) found high-quality evidence linking caregiver burden to the patient's behavioural impairment. Evidence was moderate for caregiver depressive feelings and patient physical functioning [deWit2018].
- Controversy (behaviour vs disability): in 60 Italian caregiver-patient pairs, caregiver burden rose with the patient's worsening disability [Chio2005]. Lillo2012 and deWit2018 rank behavioural change as the stronger predictor. The studies used different burden instruments and populations.
- People with ALS had a good objective perception of their impact on caregivers (Caregiver Burden Inventory vs Self-Perceived Burden Scale) [Chio2005].
- Bereavement: of 16 spousal carers in Western Australia interviewed 1-4 years after the death, 6 met criteria for prolonged grief disorder (PG-13). Those 6 had accessed palliative care at a later stage of the illness [Aoun2012].
- Children: 38 children aged 7-18 with a close relative with ALS showed more negative affect and hyperactivity than 210 controls, but less uncertainty about their own mental states. The authors note research on such minors is scarce [Testoni2023].

### Economic burden
- Canada: 50 people with ALS and families reported an average annual direct cost of CAD 32,337 per patient, of which CAD 19,574 (61%) was out of pocket. Disease-related home renovations were the largest direct cost, and annual indirect costs (lost wages of patients and family carers) were CAD 56,821 [Gladman2014].
- USA: estimated annual per-patient cost of ALS was USD 63,693 (medical, non-medical and lost income), with national costs of about USD 1,023 million. Non-medical costs were driven by moving or adapting housing and paid caregiving [Larkindale2014].
- A systematic review of 12 studies from 8 countries (2001-2015) found annual total cost per patient ranged from USD 13,667 (Denmark) to USD 69,475 (USA), standardised to 2015 USD [GladmanZinman2015].
- France (2024 survey of 50 people, despite full coverage of ALS-related health spending): mean annual out-of-pocket cost was EUR 7,764. Half reported paying for home modifications and 40% for vehicle adaptations, and family income loss averaged EUR 7,633, giving a total of EUR 15,397 a year [Desnuelle2026].

### Living longer
- In a population-based Lombardy cohort (483 people diagnosed 1998-2002), survival from diagnosis was 76.2% at 1 year, 23.4% at 5 years and 11.8% at 10 years. Longer survival was predicted by younger age, spinal onset, male sex and less certain diagnostic category at diagnosis [Pupillo2014].
- Very long survival such as Stephen Hawking's is often cited as atypical, but no peer-reviewed source on his case was read in this pass. Pass A covers survival curves.

## Step sequence
"Communication over the course" (each step is what studies and guidelines describe, not a recommendation):
1. Natural speech, early bulbar change. Speech may already be affected at diagnosis. In one cohort it stayed adequate on average for about 18 months from the first bulbar symptom, and intelligibility and speaking rate then fell in nearly all participants, faster in bulbar-onset disease [Makkonen2018].
2. Voice and message banking. While speech is still clear, some people record their voice to build a synthetic version (voice banking) or record whole phrases (message banking). Identity is the main motivation, and awareness is low [Cave2021; Oosthuizen2018]. Guidelines name banking among the reasons to refer to an AAC hub [VanDamme2024].
3. Assessment and low-tech AAC. NICE calls for speech and language therapy assessment and AAC provision "without delay", including alphabet, word or picture boards [NICE2016]. In a US survey, just over half of people with severe speech impairment also used low-tech AAC [Peters2024].
4. Typing and text-to-speech. Tablet- or PC-based voice output aids, which can speak in a banked voice [NICE2016; Cave2021]. In one centre 96% accepted AAC technology and none stopped using it [Ball2004], and over 90% of US survey respondents with severe speech impairment used speech-generating devices [Peters2024].
5. Eye-gaze. When hand use is lost, eye-tracking systems (referral to a specialised AAC hub per NICE) can be used for many hours a day. In one study mean use was 9.1 h/day, with use linked to well-being [NICE2016; Linse2018].
6. Locked-in states, in a minority on long-term tracheostomy ventilation. 11-13% of people on tracheostomy ventilation in Japanese studies developed a totally locked-in state [Hayashi2003; Kawata2008]. Some people in a locked-in state report good quality of life [KuzmaKozakiewicz2019].
7. Brain-computer interfaces (research, early evidence, single participants). Results run from about 2 letters/min (2016) to 62 words/min (2023), then about 32 words/min sustained over 8 months at 97.5% accuracy in a voice resembling the participant's pre-ALS voice (2024), and instantaneous voice synthesis (2025). One completely locked-in person spelled letter by letter, and an endovascular device was implanted in 4 people [Vansteensel2016; Willett2023; Card2024; Wairagkar2025; Chaudhary2022; Mitchell2023].
