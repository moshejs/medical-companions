# D. Diagnosis, criteria & differential
This pass covers the diagnostic criteria for ALS (El Escorial 1994, revised El Escorial/Airlie House 2000, Awaji-shima 2008, Gold Coast 2020), studies comparing how well they perform, the diagnostic pathway (history, examination, EMG/nerve conduction, MRI, blood tests, CSF, neurofilament light), diagnostic delay, how the diagnosis is communicated, the main mimics with the test that separates each one, misdiagnosis rates in population registers, red flags, and the ALS-FTSD (Strong 2017) categories. Access limits: the full texts of Shefner 2020 (Gold Coast), Brooks 2000 (El Escorial revisited), Brooks 1994 and de Carvalho 2008 (Awaji) were blocked (ScienceDirect, Taylor & Francis and the Clinical Neurophysiology site returned 403 or certificate errors, and the WFN website is reachable only through web.archive.org, which is blocked). So each criteria set was read where it is reproduced in open sources, and those sources are cited for it. Gold Coast: the box reproduced verbatim in the IPD meta-analysis by Shrestha 2026 and in Turner 2022, which match each other. Revised El Escorial: the WFN website text reproduced as an appendix in the CENTAUR (AMX0035) trial protocol on ClinicalTrials.gov. Awaji: the consensus table reproduced by Finsterer 2013, plus the de Carvalho 2008 abstract and the account in de Carvalho & Swash 2024, written by two Awaji authors. El Escorial 1994: the account in de Carvalho & Swash 2024. The EFNS 2012 guideline (Wiley) was paywalled, so only its abstract was read; its investigation list is taken from the reproduction in Garnier 2024. NICE NG42 recommendations were read in full on nice.org.uk (published 24 Feb 2016, last updated 23 Jul 2019). PubMed Central pages were blocked by a CAPTCHA, so open full texts were read through Europe PMC instead. The AAN has no dedicated ALS diagnostic-workup parameter that this pass could find, and none is cited.

## Facts

### Machine-readable criteria: Gold Coast 2020 (calculator logic)
- GC.diagnosis = R1 AND R2 AND R3. All three are required, and the output is binary (ALS or not ALS). The criteria have no certainty grades (no possible, probable or definite) [Shefner2020; Shrestha2026; Turner2022].
- GC.R1 (progression) = "Progressive motor impairment documented by history or repeated clinical assessment, preceded by normal motor function". The box sets no minimum duration and no minimum rate of progression [Shefner2020; Turner2022; Garnier2024].
- GC.R2 (motor neuron dysfunction) = (UMN AND LMN dysfunction in at least 1 body region, both in the same region when only one region is involved) OR (LMN dysfunction in at least 2 body regions). Interpretation note (not stated in the box): when two regions are involved, the box does not explicitly require the UMN and LMN signs to share a region [Shefner2020; Turner2022; Shrestha2026].
- GC.R3 (exclusion) = investigations exclude other disease processes. The investigations "depend on the clinical presentation" and may include nerve conduction studies, needle EMG, MRI or other imaging, biofluid (blood or CSF) studies or other tests. No fixed test list is mandated [Shefner2020; Turner2022; Shrestha2026].
- GC.regions = {bulbar, cervical, thoracic, lumbosacral} [Shefner2020; Turner2022].
- GC.UMN (clinical only; at least one of): (a) increased deep tendon reflexes, including a reflex present in a clinically weak and wasted muscle or spread to adjacent muscles; (b) pathological reflexes, including Hoffmann, Babinski, crossed adductor or snout; (c) velocity-dependent increase in tone (spasticity); (d) slowed, poorly coordinated voluntary movement not due to LMN weakness or parkinsonian features [Shefner2020; Turner2022; Shrestha2026].
- GC.LMN in a muscle = EITHER clinical weakness AND wasting, OR EMG showing BOTH chronic neurogenic change (large motor unit potentials of increased duration and/or amplitude, with polyphasia; motor unit instability is supportive but not obligatory) AND ongoing denervation (fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves or fasciculation potentials). EMG can therefore substitute for clinical LMN signs [Shefner2020; Turner2022; Garnier2024].
- GC.LMN region rule: a region counts as LMN-involved when 2 limb muscles innervated by different roots and different nerves are abnormal (cervical, lumbosacral), or 1 bulbar muscle, or 1 thoracic muscle, by clinical examination or EMG [Shefner2020; Turner2022; Garnier2024].
- Consequences of GC as written: a pure LMN syndrome in 2 or more regions (progressive muscular atrophy, PMA) counts as ALS for the first time, while a pure UMN syndrome (primary lateral sclerosis, PLS) does not meet GC [deCarvalhoSwash2024; Pugdahl2021; Vucic2021].
- Origin: GC came from a 2019 consensus meeting on the Gold Coast, Australia, convened by the World Federation of Neurology, the International Federation of Clinical Neurophysiology, the International Alliance of ALS/MND Associations, the ALS Association (US) and the MND Association. Its aim was to merge the certainty categories into a single entity [Vucic2021].

### Machine-readable criteria: revised El Escorial (Airlie House) 2000
- rEEC.requires.presence = (A1) evidence of LMN degeneration by clinical, electrophysiological or neuropathological examination AND (A2) evidence of UMN degeneration by clinical examination AND (A3) progressive spread of symptoms or signs within a region or to other regions, by history or examination [Brooks2000; CENTAURprotocol2019; Finsterer2013].
- rEEC.requires.absence = (B1) electrophysiological and pathological evidence of other disease processes that might explain the LMN and/or UMN signs AND (B2) neuroimaging evidence of other disease processes that might explain the observed clinical and electrophysiological signs [CENTAURprotocol2019; Finsterer2013].
- rEEC.regions = brainstem (bulbar), cervical, thoracic, lumbosacral. Example signs by region (WFN table): LMN = weakness, atrophy and fasciculations in jaw, face, palate, tongue and larynx (bulbar); neck, arm, hand and diaphragm (cervical); back and abdomen (thoracic); back, abdomen, leg and foot (lumbosacral). UMN = clonic jaw, gag reflex, exaggerated snout reflex, pseudobulbar features, forced yawning (bulbar); pathological or clonic deep tendon reflexes, Hoffmann reflex, spastic tone, preserved reflex in a weak wasted limb (cervical); loss of superficial abdominal reflexes (thoracic); extensor plantar response, spastic tone (lumbosacral) [CENTAURprotocol2019].
- rEEC.clinically_definite = UMN AND LMN signs, on clinical evidence alone, in 3 regions. Other reproductions phrase this as "bulbar + at least 2 spinal regions, or 3 spinal regions", which is logically the same set when there are 4 regions [Brooks2000; CENTAURprotocol2019; Finsterer2013; Vidovic2023].
- rEEC.clinically_probable = UMN AND LMN signs, on clinical evidence alone, in at least 2 regions, with some UMN signs necessarily rostral to (above) the LMN signs [CENTAURprotocol2019; Finsterer2013].
- rEEC.clinically_probable_laboratory_supported = (clinical UMN AND LMN signs in only 1 region) OR (UMN signs alone in 1 region), AND LMN signs defined by EMG criteria in at least 2 limbs, with neuroimaging and clinical laboratory protocols properly applied to exclude other causes [CENTAURprotocol2019; Vidovic2023].
- rEEC.clinically_possible = (clinical UMN AND LMN signs together in only 1 region) OR (UMN signs alone in 2 or more regions) OR (LMN signs rostral to UMN signs), when probable laboratory-supported cannot be proven. Other diagnoses must have been excluded [CENTAURprotocol2019; Finsterer2013].
- rEEC familial category: the WFN-derived table in Ghasemi 2016 adds "clinically definite ALS – laboratory supported", meaning UMN and/or LMN signs in 1 region in a carrier of a pathogenic SOD1 mutation (not confirmed against the Brooks 2000 text in this pass) [Ghasemi2016].
- 'Suspected ALS' (LMN signs in at least 2 regions, i.e. PMA) existed in El Escorial 1994 and was removed in the 2000 revision, when 'probable laboratory-supported' was added. Confirmed by three independent accounts [deCarvalhoSwash2024; Vidovic2023; Shrestha2026].
- El Escorial 1994 categories, as summarised by de Carvalho & Swash: definite (UMN + LMN in at least 3 regions), probable (UMN + LMN in 2 regions), possible (UMN + LMN in 1 region, or UMN in 2 regions, or LMN rostral to UMN), suspected (LMN signs in at least 2 regions) [deCarvalhoSwash2024; Brooks1994].
- EMG under El Escorial: needed normal motor conduction velocities and sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs), no conduction block, and fibrillations/sharp waves with neurogenic change. A cervical or lumbosacral region needed 2 abnormal muscles innervated by different roots and nerves, while the bulbar and thoracic regions needed 1 muscle. Fasciculation potentials were not required and did not count as denervation [deCarvalhoSwash2024; Vidovic2023].
- In 2000, a mild sensory neuropathy was no longer considered a finding that necessarily excludes ALS [deCarvalhoSwash2024].
- Purpose: the El Escorial criteria were developed explicitly for clinical trials, not clinical diagnosis. Their wide use in clinical practice caused "confusion and dissatisfaction" [deCarvalhoSwash2024].

### Machine-readable criteria: Awaji-shima 2008
- AW.rule1: electrophysiological evidence of chronic neurogenic change counts as equivalent to clinical information when deciding whether a muscle in a limb is involved, so EMG LMN signs equal clinical LMN signs in a region [deCarvalho2008; deCarvalhoSwash2024].
- AW.rule2: when ALS is clinically suspected, fasciculation potentials count as equivalent to fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves as evidence of denervation, provided chronic neurogenic change is present. Instability of fasciculation potentials and motor unit potentials is stressed [deCarvalho2008; Vidovic2023].
- AW.rule3: these changes "render obsolete" the probable laboratory-supported category, which is deleted. The remaining categories are definite, probable and possible [deCarvalho2008; Finsterer2013].
- AW.definite = clinical or electrophysiological evidence of LMN AND UMN signs in bulbar + at least 2 spinal regions, or in 3 spinal regions [Finsterer2013; Vidovic2023].
- AW.probable = clinical or electrophysiological evidence of LMN AND UMN signs in at least 2 regions, with some UMN signs necessarily rostral to the LMN signs [Finsterer2013].
- AW.possible = clinical or electrophysiological UMN AND LMN signs in only 1 region, or UMN signs alone in 2 or more regions, or LMN signs rostral to UMN signs. Neuroimaging and laboratory studies must have excluded other diagnoses [Finsterer2013].
- 'Awaji algorithm': this is not a separate flowchart. It is the electrodiagnostic interpretation above, applied to the rEEC category frame. An "updated Awaji" variant that restores a probable laboratory-supported category was proposed in 2016 [deCarvalhoSwash2024; Geevasinga2016].
- Methods for detecting UMN dysfunction (for example transcranial magnetic stimulation) were judged sensitive but needing more study, and were not added to the criteria [deCarvalho2008].

### PLS and ALS-FTSD criteria (related)
- PLS consensus 2020 requires: age 25 or over; progressive UMN symptoms for at least 2 years; UMN signs in at least 2 of 3 regions (lower limb, upper limb, bulbar); no unexplained sensory symptoms, no active LMN degeneration and no alternative diagnosis. Probable PLS = no significant active LMN degeneration 2-4 years from onset; definite PLS = 4 or more years [Turner2020PLS].
- The PLS criteria allow minimal increases in insertional activity and occasional fibrillations or positive sharp waves in limb muscles. In one series, 13 of 29 people with pure UMN onset developed EMG denervation and LMN signs at an average of 3-4 years [Turner2020PLS].
- ALS-FTSD (Strong 2017) keeps a diagnostic axis model: Axis I = the motor neuron disease variant; Axis II = cognitive and behavioural dysfunction; Axis III = other non-motor features. Axis IV (disease modifiers) was dropped [Strong2017].
- ALSci (ALS with cognitive impairment) = executive impairment (impaired letter verbal fluency controlled for motor and speech limits, or impairment on 2 non-overlapping executive tests, which may include social cognition) and/or language impairment (2 non-overlapping tests). On individual measures, impairment means at or below the 5th percentile of age- and education-matched norms. Isolated memory impairment does not qualify [Strong2017].
- ALSbi (ALS with behavioural impairment) = apathy with or without other change, OR at least 2 of: disinhibition; loss of sympathy or empathy; perseverative, stereotyped or compulsive behaviour; hyperorality or dietary change; loss of insight; psychotic symptoms. It is based on informant report and must not be better explained by depression, anxiety, pseudobulbar affect or reaction to the diagnosis [Strong2017].
- ALScbi = criteria for both ALSci and ALSbi are met. ALS-FTD = progressive deterioration of behaviour and/or cognition AND (at least 3 Rascovsky behavioural/cognitive features, OR at least 2 plus loss of insight and/or psychotic symptoms, OR language impairment meeting criteria for semantic or non-fluent variant primary progressive aphasia) [Strong2017].
- Strong 2017 reports neuropsychological deficits in over 50% of people with ALS. Garnier 2024 recommends screening every patient with the ECAS or the ALS-CBS [Strong2017; Garnier2024].

### Comparative performance of the criteria
- IPD meta-analysis (5 cohorts from Australia, China, Japan, Denmark/Europe and Italy; 3007 people with suspected ALS, 2443 with ALS and 564 non-ALS; reference standard = final clinical diagnosis at follow-up). Pooled sensitivity: GC 0.96 (95% CI 0.93-0.98), Awaji 0.87 (0.78-0.93), rEEC 0.87 (0.78-0.92). Pooled specificity: GC 0.68 (0.53-0.81), Awaji 0.72 (0.57-0.83), rEEC 0.73 (0.59-0.83). Certainty for specificity was rated very low [Shrestha2026].
- In the same IPD analysis, restricting to borderline cases (excluding rEEC/Awaji 'definite') gave GC sensitivity 0.95 against 0.82 for both Awaji and rEEC. Excluding PLS left GC specificity at 0.67 and raised rEEC and Awaji specificity to 0.76. GC summary AUC was 0.94 (0.84-0.97); no formal AUC comparison was made [Shrestha2026].
- Aggregate meta-analysis (9 studies, n=9656, all from ALS referral centres). GC: sensitivity about 95%, specificity 66%, AUC 0.95, diagnostic odds ratio 36.1. rEEC probable-or-better: sensitivity 59%, specificity 94%. rEEC possible-or-better: 84% and 77%. Awaji probable-or-better: 54% and 94%. Awaji possible-or-better: 85% and 82%. The authors caution that applicability in unselected neurology populations is uncertain [vonQuednow2025].
- Sydney cohort (n=506, retrospective application to consecutive referrals): sensitivity GC 92% (88.7-94.6%), Awaji 90.3%, rEEC 88.6%, with 'possible' counted as positive for the older criteria. GC held its sensitivity across onset site, disease duration and disability, and had greater sensitivity and specificity in atypical phenotypes [Hannaford2021].
- Five European centres (404 eligible referrals for EMG: 272 ALS, 94 mimics, 35 lost to follow-up): GC sensitivity 88.2% (83.8-91.8%) against 77.6% for the best older set (Awaji with 'possible'). Specificity was high for all sets. The gain was "mainly due to" 28 people with PMA [Pugdahl2021].
- Peking Union cohort (n=1185, 1162 with final ALS diagnosis): GC sensitivity 96.6% against 85.1% for rEEC and 85.3% for Awaji. Among people who met no rEEC category, GC sensitivity was 84.4% [Shen2021].
- Japanese cohort (639 consecutive referrals: 529 ALS, 110 mimics): GC sensitivity 96.8% against 89.6% (Awaji) and 89.2% (rEEC). Specificity 77.3% against 65.5% and 66.4%. GC false positives were mainly cervical spondylosis and parkinsonian syndromes [Otani2024].
- Brescia real-world cohort (131 ALS, 104 controls): GC sensitivity 93.1% at first visit and 96.1% at last follow-up, against 71.8%/87% (rEEC) and 77.8%/89.3% (Awaji). GC specificity was 28.8% against 45.2% (rEEC) and 43.3% (Awaji) [Ferullo2024].
- Calgary single-centre cohort (n=206 with ALS): 48.5% met GC but not rEEC definite/probable. GC sensitivity was 96.1% against 47.6% for rEEC definite/probable. Eligibility for three phase 3 trials rose from 31-45% to 95-100% [Jewett2022].
- Dutch and Belgian population registers (n=5957): 600 (10.1%) met GC but not rEEC, and 95 (1.6%) met only rEEC. ALSFRS-R decline was 0.84 points/month (rEEC) against 0.81 (GC), and median survival 17.8 against 18.7 months. Spread within each category was wide [deJongh2022].
- Specialised university ALS centre, retrospective (431 with suspected ALS, 426 with final ALS): GC was more sensitive than rEEC and Awaji, and adding neurofilament levels to rEEC or Awaji did not raise their sensitivity [Klyk2026].
- Awaji against rEEC, meta-analysis (8 studies, 1187 patients): Awaji classified 23% (12-33%) more people as probable or definite. Pooled sensitivity 81.1% against 62.2%; diagnostic odds ratio 35.8 against 8.7. The gain was larger in bulbar onset [Costa2012].
- Awaji against rEEC, IPD analysis (1086 patients: 881 ALS, 205 mimics): sensitivity Awaji 0.70 (0.51-0.83), updated Awaji 0.73 (0.56-0.85), rEEC 0.58 (0.48-0.68) [Geevasinga2016].
- Controversy: a 2015 meta-analysis (9 studies, 1121 people with ALS) found Awaji "not always superior". Awaji moved 20% of rEEC 'probable laboratory-supported' cases down to 'possible' [JangBae2015].
- Inter-rater study (399 patients rated blind by 8 experienced physicians from 10 countries): agreement was low for both rEEC and Awaji, poorest for 'possible' and 'probable laboratory-supported'. Sensitivity was rEEC 64% and Awaji 63% [Johnsen2019].
- Irish ALS Register (388 people, 1993-1998): 56% were definite or probable at diagnosis. Of 165 possible or suspected (trial-ineligible) cases, 67% became trial-eligible later, but 10% of the 254 who died never became eligible. Category at diagnosis did not predict prognosis. Median time from onset to trial eligibility was 12.9 months (El Escorial) against 12.8 months (Airlie House) [Traynor2000criteria].
- Criticisms of GC (controversy). Higher sensitivity is driven partly by counting PMA as ALS [Pugdahl2021]. Specificity is lower and heterogeneous, so mimic exclusion and repeat review are needed [Shrestha2026; vonQuednow2025]. Mimics such as cervical spondylosis can be misclassified [Otani2024]. The Shen cohort included very few non-ALS cases (23 of 1185) [Shrestha2026]. Several IPD authors helped write GC, a declared possible allegiance bias [Shrestha2026]. In defence, the Dutch and Belgian data showed GC barely changed population heterogeneity for trials [deJongh2022].
- Patient-facing argument for GC: the UK MND Clinical Studies Group wrote that the older terms (suspected, possible, probable) caused "often psychologically distressing" implied doubt, and that the diagnosis is now 'ALS' or 'not ALS' [Turner2022].

### Diagnostic pathway as published
- NICE NG42 (UK): healthcare professionals should be aware that MND may first appear as isolated unexplained symptoms: loss of dexterity, falls or trips; speech or swallowing problems or tongue fasciculations; weakness, wasting, twitching, cramps or stiffness; breathing problems; and effects of low respiratory function such as daytime sleepiness, early-morning headache or breathlessness when lying down. It may also first appear with behavioural change, emotional lability or frontotemporal dementia [NICE2016].
- NICE NG42 1.1.4: if MND is suspected, refer without delay, name the possible diagnosis in the referral letter, and contact the consultant neurologist directly if urgent [NICE2016].
- EFNS: people with symptoms suggestive of ALS should be assessed as soon as possible by an experienced neurologist, and investigations, including neurophysiology, done with high priority [Andersen2012EFNS].
- Core history: steadily progressive, initially often asymmetric weakness (first in a leg in about 35%, an arm in about 30%, speech and swallowing in about 30%) without significant sensory symptoms. Signs with high positive predictive value (not formally validated): widespread fasciculations, bilateral tongue wasting, 'split hand' (wasting of first dorsal interosseous and abductor pollicis brevis), head drop, emotionality, and cognitive or behavioural change [TurnerTalbot2013].
- Examination searches all four regions for UMN signs (brisk or spreading reflexes, preserved reflexes in a wasted region, Hoffmann, Babinski, clonus, spasticity, jaw jerk) and LMN signs (weakness, wasting, fasciculation, cramps, tongue wasting and fasciculation). It also looks for signs that argue against ALS: sensory loss, eye-movement palsy, sphincter disturbance. A three-generation family tree is recommended [Garnier2024].
- EMG/NCS in ALS: motor and sensory conduction is usually normal. Motor amplitudes may be reduced, and pseudo-blocks can occur with rapid motor neuron loss. Needle EMG shows fasciculation potentials, fibrillations and positive sharp waves with large, long-duration motor units. All four regions should be sampled, not only the clinically affected ones [Garnier2024].
- Published EMG protocol (Lenglet & Camdessanché, as reproduced by Garnier): nerve conduction of median, ulnar, fibular and tibial nerves; needle EMG of tibialis anterior and vastus lateralis, first dorsal interosseous and deltoid or biceps, rectus abdominis or thoracic paraspinals, and sternocleidomastoid, genioglossus or upper trapezius. Repetitive nerve stimulation is added for proximal arm weakness, isolated bulbar onset, or when there is no denervation [Garnier2024].
- Findings that support ALS: normal motor and sensory conduction, no conduction block, and widespread neurogenic change and fasciculations [Ghasemi2016; deCarvalhoSwash2024]. Findings that argue against ALS: conduction block or demyelination, and prominent sensory abnormalities. EMG denervation arranged in a nerve territory rather than a myotome favours motor neuropathy [deCarvalhoSwash2024; TurnerTalbot2013].
- EMG is not a stand-alone test: in a tertiary series it was described as "only 60% sensitive". Bulbar-onset ALS may show normal tongue and limb EMG, and over-reliance on EMG delays diagnosis [TurnerTalbot2013; Turner2022].
- MRI: brain and spinal MRI are recommended to exclude CNS lesions. Some people with ALS show T2 hyperintensity of the corticospinal tracts [Garnier2024]. Turner & Talbot list when each scan is most useful: brain MRI for isolated dysphagia or hemiparesis; cervical MRI for mixed signs with nothing above the neck; lumbar MRI for an LMN-only leg syndrome [TurnerTalbot2013].
- Controversy, mandatory versus targeted tests: the EFNS-derived tables list a standard blood panel, chest X-ray and brain/spinal MRI for all patients [Garnier2024; Ghasemi2016]. Turner & Talbot argue that "there are no mandatory investigations" and that tests should follow the clinical features to avoid delay [TurnerTalbot2013].
- Blood tests (EFNS 2012 as reproduced by Garnier 2024): electrolytes, urea and creatinine; fasting glucose; AST, ALT, LDH and albumin; calcium; TSH, free T4 and free T3; full blood count; serum protein electrophoresis and immunoelectrophoresis; CRP; vitamin B12 and folate; creatine kinase; and serology (Borrelia, viruses including HIV, syphilis) if there is exposure risk. Plus chest X-ray and brain/spinal MRI [Garnier2024; Andersen2012EFNS].
- 'Selected cases' tests in an EFNS-style table: ACE, lactate, hexosaminidase A/B, GM1 ganglioside antibodies, anti-Hu, anti-MAG, AChR and MuSK antibodies, DNA analysis (SOD1, SMN, SBMA, TDP43, FUS), CSF, urine heavy metals, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) motor evoked potentials, mammography, and muscle or nerve biopsy. Ghasemi attributes this table to Talbot 2009 (see verification notes) [Ghasemi2016].
- CSF has most value when inflammatory neuropathy is suspected (raised protein or white cells), or for oligoclonal bands when primary progressive MS or a paraneoplastic disorder is suspected. Oligoclonal bands occasionally occur in classical MND [TurnerTalbot2013].
- Genetic testing: in France, every person with ALS (sporadic or familial) is offered C9orf72 and SOD1 testing at diagnosis, with a broader panel for onset before 40, unusual progression or family history, and results given in a dedicated consultation [Garnier2024].
- After workup: if GC is met with typical UMN and LMN signs, the diagnosis can be given. In early disease, or when two diagnoses are possible, EMG is repeated at 3 or 6 months depending on the rate of worsening [Garnier2024].

### Neurofilament light (NfL) and other biomarkers
- Serum NfL (Simoa, single centre; 124 ALS against 50 non-neurodegenerative controls, 44 disease controls from the ALS differential and 65 other neurodegenerative diseases): a cut-off of 62 pg/mL separated ALS from all other conditions with 85.5% sensitivity (78-91.2%) and 81.8% specificity (74.9-87.4%). Only Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease had comparable levels [Verde2019].
- CSF NfL (prospective, 455 patients including 85 MND mimics): a cut-off of 2200 pg/mL gave 77% sensitivity, 85% specificity and 87% PPV. CSF pNfH at 560 pg/mL gave 83% sensitivity and 77% specificity [Steinacker2016].
- In people within 6 months of symptom onset (n=54, multicentre), CSF NfL, serum NfL and CSF pNfH separated ALS from other neurological diseases and MND mimics. Across these comparisons sensitivities were 78-100% and specificities 86-98% (abstract figures). Levels did not differ by El Escorial category [Feneberg2018].
- Meta-analysis: CSF NfL averaged 3.35 times (2.19-5.12) higher in ALS than in disease mimics (11 studies, 1239 ALS, 806 mimics), and serum NfL 8.15 times higher (4 studies, 458 against 181). CSF NfL had "strong discriminatory power" for ALS against mimics [Forgrave2019].
- A second meta-analysis found better ALS-versus-mimic accuracy in CSF (AUC 0.90) than in blood (AUC 0.78), and better results with Simoa than with ELISA. Its authors said a clinically usable cut-off was still lacking [Sferruzza2022].
- Across studies, NfL sensitivity ranges from 0.71 to 0.91, specificity from 0.64 to 0.95, and AUC from 0.77 to 0.98, depending on phenotype, neurofilament type and assay. NfL is also raised in stroke, MS and other neurodegenerative diseases, so it is not ALS-specific [deCarvalhoSwash2024; Xu2016].
- In a 2026 specialised-centre cohort, adding NfL did not increase the sensitivity of rEEC or Awaji. The IPD meta-analysis warns that NfL "should not be considered in isolation" [Klyk2026; Shrestha2026].

### Diagnostic delay
- US single-centre study (n=304): median time from first symptom to confirmed diagnosis was 11.5 months. 52% received an alternative diagnosis first, and patients saw an average of 3 physicians. Delay was longer with age over 60, sporadic disease and limb onset [Paganoni2014].
- Chart review in France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the UK and the US (795 patients, 2020-2021): mean time from onset to diagnosis was 8.2 months (EU5 9.6, US 5.0). 35% had at least one prior misdiagnosis, and those patients waited 10.4 against 6.9 months [Goyal2024].
- The average interval from onset to diagnosis has stayed at about 1 year, roughly a third of median survival from onset. Faster-progressing disease is referred sooner, so true mimics tend to be slow [TurnerTalbot2013].
- Meta-analysis of 13 studies: bulbar onset was diagnosed about 4.4 months faster than limb onset (mean difference -4.42 months, 95% CI -5.73 to -3.11) [Saeed2026].
- Seed check: the dossier seed of "about 10-16 months" is only partly supported. The sources read give a median of 11.5 months, a mean of 8.2 months, and about 12 months [Paganoni2014; Goyal2024; TurnerTalbot2013].

### Communicating the diagnosis
- EFNS: the diagnosis should be given by a consultant with good knowledge of the patient and the disease, followed by regular multidisciplinary support [Andersen2012EFNS].
- NICE NG42 1.2.1-1.2.7: information should be given by a consultant neurologist with up-to-date MND experience (unless urgent). Ask how much information the person wants and whom to involve. Give oral and written information. Provide a single point of contact. Offer a face-to-face follow-up with a multidisciplinary team member within 4 weeks. Inform the GP without delay. Set aside enough time for questions such as "How long will I live?" and "Will my children get MND?" [NICE2016].
- Breaking the news is described as the start of treatment and an ongoing process. It should be honest while preserving hope, avoid collusion with relatives, and use written material to support, never replace, face-to-face discussion [ChioBorasio2004].
- Australian survey (n=248, SPIKES-based): 36% were dissatisfied with how the diagnosis was delivered. Satisfaction rose with more time spent, empathic response, sharing information with realistic goals, exploring expectations, and making a plan [Aoun2016].
- UK MND Clinical Studies Group: the diagnosis should be conveyed by a clinician who knows the proposed care plan [Turner2022]. Scottish register lesson: inform people "only when it is secure" [Davenport1996].

### Misdiagnosis in population registers and red flags
- Irish ALS Register (1993-1997): 32 of 437 referrals (7.3%) were later rediagnosed. Multifocal motor neuropathy (7, 22%) and Kennedy disease (4, 13%) were the most common. 84% of mimics had met only 'suspected' or 'possible' El Escorial categories, 13% 'probable' and 3% 'definite' [Traynor2000mimic].
- Scottish MND Register: of 552 registered, 46 (8%) had an alternative diagnosis, and potentially beneficial therapy existed in half of them. Revision was prompted by failure to progress, atypical features or investigation results [Davenport1996].
- Population studies suggest nearly 10% of people diagnosed with ALS ultimately have another condition, with a few conditions accounting for most cases [TurnerTalbot2013; Ghasemi2016].
- Red flags for an alternative diagnosis: prominent sensory features; sphincter, visual or autonomic disturbance; basal ganglia signs; eye-movement abnormality; fluctuating or fatigable weakness; severe pain at onset; conduction block or demyelination on NCS; weakness out of proportion to wasting; and no progression over time [Ghasemi2016; Garnier2024; TurnerTalbot2013; Davenport1996].

### The differential: mimics, features and key test
- Multifocal motor neuropathy (MMN): pure motor, asymmetric, distal and arm-predominant, with weakness greater than wasting (often finger or wrist extensors). Male to female ratio 3:1; mean onset about 40 years, with no cases reported over 70. Bulbar involvement is absent and life expectancy normal. Key test: conduction block on NCS (diagnostic but not always found). Anti-GM1 IgM is about 50% sensitive. The condition responds to IVIG [TurnerTalbot2013; Lawson2014].
- MMN (EFNS/PNS criteria as reproduced): needs at least 2 motor nerve territories, normal sensory conduction across segments with block, and supportive features (IgM anti-GM1, mildly raised CSF protein, T2 brachial plexus signal, response to IVIG). MMN does not respond to steroids or plasma exchange [Lawson2014].
- Cervical spondylotic myelo-radiculopathy: can cause pure motor UMN-plus-LMN signs. Clues against ALS are prominent neck pain and sphincter involvement. Clues favouring ALS are signs above the neck (bulbar, emotionality), fasciculations in bulbar or lumbosacral territory, and LMN signs more diffuse than the lesion explains. Incidental spondylosis is common at ALS ages, and avoiding unnecessary surgery is a stated concern. Key test: cervical MRI read alongside EMG [TurnerTalbot2013; Ghasemi2016; Garnier2024].
- Kennedy disease (spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy, SBMA): X-linked, affects men. Very slow LMN-only bulbar and limb weakness, chin or perioral fasciculation, postural tremor, gynaecomastia, testicular atrophy, mildly raised CK. No UMN signs, and low or absent SNAPs (sensory ganglionopathy). Key test: CAG expansion in AR (more than 35 repeats in GeneReviews) [deCarvalhoSwash2024; TurnerTalbot2013; LaSpada_GeneReviews].
- SBMA natural history (223 genetically confirmed Japanese men): median age at hand tremor 33 years, weakness 44, dysphagia 54, cane 59, wheelchair 61. Onset age correlated with CAG length but rate of progression did not [Atsuta2006].
- Inclusion body myositis (IBM): usually over 50, male to female 3:1. Slow, painless, often asymmetric weakness of quadriceps and finger flexors with medial forearm wasting, dysphagia in about a third, and no fasciculations or UMN signs. CK is often over 1000 IU/L but can be normal. EMG can look neurogenic with fibrillations, a recognised pitfall. Key test: muscle biopsy (rimmed vacuoles) and muscle MRI [TurnerTalbot2013; deCarvalhoSwash2024].
- IBM serology: anti-cytosolic 5'-nucleotidase 1A (anti-cN1A) was present in 33% of 311 European patients with IBM, so a negative test does not exclude it [Lilleker2017].
- Bulbar myasthenia gravis: fluctuating or fatigable bulbar weakness, often with ptosis or diplopia, and no UMN signs or fasciculation. Key tests: repetitive nerve stimulation and AChR/MuSK antibodies. Decrement can occur in ALS, and rare false-positive AChR antibodies are reported in MND, so neither test alone excludes ALS. Cholinesterase inhibitors can give transient relief in MND [Garnier2024; TurnerTalbot2013; Ghasemi2016].
- Primary lateral sclerosis (PLS): pure UMN syndrome, rare (under 2-3% of MND), slowly progressive, and part of the MND spectrum. Separated from UMN-predominant ALS mainly by time and serial EMG (see PLS criteria). It does not meet GC, but under rEEC UMN-only signs in 2 or more regions = 'possible ALS' [Turner2020PLS; Turner2022; CENTAURprotocol2019].
- Hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP): younger onset, very slow spastic paraparesis with minimal arm and no severe bulbar involvement, sometimes dorsal-column or sphincter signs. An (often autosomal dominant) family history is the main clue, and about 40% of familial cases carry SPAST variants. Key test: family history and genetic panel [TurnerTalbot2013; Ghasemi2016].
- Benign fasciculation syndrome: fasciculations without weakness or EMG denervation. Of 121 Mayo Clinic patients followed 2-32 years, none developed MND [Blexrud1993]. In a 2021 series of 37 (24 followed for a median 4.7 years), minor chronic neurogenic EMG changes in some older men stayed stable [Montalvo2021]. People under 40 with fasciculations, no weakness and normal thyroid and electrolytes can be reassured without EMG, according to a tertiary clinic [TurnerTalbot2013].
- Adult hexosaminidase A deficiency (late-onset GM2 gangliosidosis): combined UMN and LMN involvement with cerebellar and extrapyramidal signs, psychosis or dementia. EMG may show complex repetitive discharges and abnormal SNAPs. Key test: hexosaminidase A enzyme assay or HEXA genotyping [Ghasemi2016]. Screening 115 Israeli people with sporadic ALS found only 2 heterozygous Ashkenazi carriers, no more than in the general population, so it is a "very unlikely" cause of an ALS mimic [Drory2003].
- Post-polio syndrome: new slowly progressive weakness, fatigue or atrophy after usually 15 or more years of stability following paralytic polio. Symptoms persist for at least 1 year, with a paucity of UMN signs. Key test: history of paralytic polio (March of Dimes criteria) and exclusion of other causes [LiHiShing2019; Ghasemi2016]. MND arising in someone with past polio shows no more than a coincidental relationship [TurnerTalbot2013].
- Motor-predominant CIDP (chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy): up to a third of CIDP cases. Symmetric, fluctuating or relapsing, with mild sensory features. Key test: demyelinating NCS (slowed conduction velocities). Motor CIDP responds poorly to corticosteroids, which favours IVIG first [TurnerTalbot2013].
- Radiation-induced plexopathy or radiculopathy: an LMN syndrome after pelvic or para-aortic radiotherapy, often without sensory symptoms. Latency can be decades (33 years in one case), and progression is very slow. The brachial plexus form after breast cancer usually has a shorter latency and pain. Key clues: exposure history, myokymic discharges and non-resolving conduction block on EMG/NCS [TurnerTalbot2013; Ghasemi2016].
- Hyperthyroidism: can cause hyperreflexia, fasciculations, weight loss and weakness, usually with systemic signs (heat intolerance, tremor, tachycardia). About 10% of thyrotoxic patients have florid fasciculations. Key test: thyroid function [Ghasemi2016; TurnerTalbot2013]. Hyperparathyroidism can mimic LMN-onset ALS. Key test: serum calcium (in the EFNS panel) [Ghasemi2016; Garnier2024].
- Copper deficiency myelopathy: spastic gait with prominent sensory ataxia and dorsal-column signs, resembling B12 subacute combined degeneration. Causes include prior gastric surgery, excess zinc and malabsorption. Key tests: serum copper and ceruloplasmin (and zinc) [Kumar2004; Kumar2006]. Sensory features usually separate it from ALS [TurnerTalbot2013].
- HIV: in a review of 1700 HIV-positive people with neurological symptoms, 6 had an ALS-like syndrome. They were younger, with monomelic onset and spread over weeks, and stabilised or improved on antiretroviral therapy [Ghasemi2016]. HTLV-1-associated myelopathy can present with features indistinguishable from ALS (an autopsy case with raised serum and CSF anti-HTLV-I titres). Key test: HIV or HTLV serology when there is risk [Kuroda1991; Garnier2024; deCarvalhoSwash2024].
- Others named in the read sources: neuralgic amyotrophy (severe onset pain, then arrest), Hirayama disease (young men, unilateral C7-T1 weakness, flexion MRI), adrenomyeloneuropathy (very-long-chain fatty acids), primary progressive MS (MRI, CSF bands), paraneoplastic syndromes, and adult polyglucosan body disease [TurnerTalbot2013; Ghasemi2016; Garnier2024].

## Step sequence
Illustrative composite case, not patient data: right-hand onset, with timings chosen to show how the rule sets diverge. Each step's classification follows the rule statements above.
1. Month 0, first symptom in one region: right-hand clumsiness from weakness and wasting of first dorsal interosseous, which is clinical LMN in the cervical region only, with no UMN signs. rEEC: no category (the 2000 criteria have no 'suspected'; the 1994 'suspected' needed LMN in at least 2 regions). Gold Coast: not met (needs documented progression, and UMN+LMN in 1 region or LMN in 2) [CENTAURprotocol2019; deCarvalhoSwash2024; Shefner2020; Turner2022].
2. Month 4, first neurology visit: progression documented by history. Examination adds UMN signs in the same arm (brisk reflexes in a wasted arm, Hoffmann sign). With UMN+LMN in 1 region: rEEC 'clinically possible'; Awaji 'possible'; Gold Coast R1 and R2 met, R3 pending [CENTAURprotocol2019; Finsterer2013; Turner2022].
3. Month 5, EMG: chronic neurogenic change plus fibrillations or fasciculation potentials in 2 leg muscles with different roots and nerves (lumbosacral LMN by EMG), with normal motor and sensory conduction and no conduction block. rEEC: 'clinically probable laboratory-supported' (UMN+LMN in 1 region plus EMG LMN in at least 2 limbs). Awaji: no lab-supported category, so in studies such cases often stay 'possible'. Gold Coast: R2 met two ways (UMN+LMN cervical; LMN in 2 regions) [CENTAURprotocol2019; deCarvalho2008; JangBae2015; Johnsen2019; Turner2022].
4. Month 5-6, exclusions: cervical and brain MRI, the blood panel (thyroid, calcium, B12, protein electrophoresis, CK and others) and, if relevant, anti-GM1 and CSF show no mimic. Gold Coast: all 3 requirements met, so the diagnosis is ALS. rEEC/Awaji categories are unchanged [Garnier2024; Andersen2012EFNS; Shefner2020].
5. Month 9, spread: brisk knee jerks and an extensor plantar in the weaker leg, which is now UMN+LMN in lumbosacral as well as cervical. rEEC 'clinically probable' (UMN+LMN in 2 regions, with UMN rostral to LMN). Awaji 'probable'. Gold Coast still ALS [CENTAURprotocol2019; Finsterer2013].
6. Month 14, bulbar involvement: tongue fasciculation and wasting with a brisk jaw jerk, which is UMN+LMN bulbar. rEEC 'clinically definite' (bulbar + 2 spinal regions = 3 regions). Awaji 'definite'. Gold Coast unchanged [CENTAURprotocol2019; Finsterer2013].
7. Context bar: in the Irish register, the median time from onset to trial eligibility (definite or probable) was about 12.9 months, and 10% of those who died never left the trial-ineligible categories. In a US cohort, the median time from onset to diagnosis was 11.5 months [Traynor2000criteria; Paganoni2014].
8. Toggle note: the category ladder (possible → probable lab-supported → probable → definite) reflects how many regions are involved, not prognosis. Progression rates overlap widely between categories [Traynor2000criteria; deJongh2022; Turner2022].

### Notes on verification
- Every PMID above was checked in one PubMed esummary batch in this pass, which confirmed first author, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages and DOI. Europe PMC core records were used for abstracts and open full texts. A Crossref check was done for doi:10.1136/practneurol-2021-003256 and doi:10.1016/j.clinph.2020.04.005; Crossref lists the latter's version of record under a CC BY-NC-ND licence, although the publisher site refused access. The ClinicalTrials.gov API record for NCT03127514 confirmed the CENTAUR study and sponsor. NICE NG42 dates come from the page's time elements.
- Gold Coast wording: Shrestha2026 Box 1 and Turner2022 Box 1 agree on every element, including the UMN list, the EMG definition and the region rule. Garnier2024 Table 4 agrees on the EMG and region rule.
- Miscitations and discrepancies noticed:
  - Ferullo2024 Table 1 is inaccurate as a summary. It labels Gold Coast as "Definite ALS", gives an Awaji 'definite' of UMN+LMN in at least 2 regions, and a rEEC 'definite' of "three regions" only. Do not use it as a criteria source.
  - Vidovic2023 Table 2 describes Gold Coast UMN involvement as "clinical or electrophysiological". The reproduced GC box defines UMN clinically only.
  - A 2024 Ann Med overview (Xu R, PMID 39470153, not used as a source) gives an internally inconsistent rEEC 'definite' definition.
  - Ghasemi2016 attributes its EFNS-style investigation table (with evidence classes) to Talbot 2009 Pract Neurol. Its content matches the EFNS investigation list, but this could not be checked against the EFNS full text.
  - The rEEC lab-supported rule says EMG LMN signs in "at least two limbs" in the WFN text (CENTAURprotocol2019) and in Vidovic2023 ("≥2 regions"), but de Carvalho & Swash 2024 say "at least 2 regions". A calculator should follow the WFN text and flag the ambiguity.
  - Shrestha2026's introduction says up to 22% of people die classified as 'possible ALS', citing Traynor 2000. The Traynor abstract reports 10% (25 of 254) remaining trial-ineligible (possible or suspected) at death. The 22% could not be traced in the abstract.
- Controversy: Jang & Bae (2015) attribute Awaji's down-classification to "the requirement for 2 upper motor neuron signs in the AC 'clinically probable' category". The reproduced Awaji text (Finsterer2013) says only "LMN and UMN signs in at least two regions with some UMN signs necessarily rostral". Readings of 'probable' therefore differ when UMN signs are in one region and LMN signs (by EMG) in another. The step sequence avoids this case for the probable step.
- Feneberg2018: the abstract lists 6 sensitivities and 6 specificities without labelling every pairing, so only the ranges are reported here.
- Odat2024 (PMID 39296911, PMC11409048) was read but is not cited for facts. It reports Hannaford 2021 specificities (GCC 88.5%, rEEC 96.2%, Awaji 95.5%) at second hand, and these could not be confirmed from the Hannaford abstract.
- Shared keys vanEs2017, Feldman2022, Hardiman2017 and Brown2017 were located (PMIDs 28552366, 36116464, 28980624/29052611, 28700839), but their abstracts contain no diagnostic specifics, their full texts were not accessible, and they are not cited. A 2015 WFN "revision of the El Escorial criteria" (Ludolph et al., PMID 26121170) exists, but no abstract or full text was available, so it is not described.
- Not found: a population-register misdiagnosis figure more recent than Traynor 2000 and Davenport 1996; an AAN diagnostic-investigation guideline; and a sensitivity figure for anti-GM1 from a primary MMN cohort (the figure of about 50% comes from reviews).
