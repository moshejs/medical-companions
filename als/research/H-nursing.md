# H. Nursing care
This pass covers what published nursing, multidisciplinary and drug-label sources say about the four fixed parts of the nursing module: assessment (breathing, swallowing and nutrition, skin, falls, pain, mood, pseudobulbar affect, cognition, caregiver burden, ALSFRS-R administration), giving the medicines (riluzole, edaravone, tofersen, medicines via gastrostomy, saliva drugs, end-of-life symptom drugs), teaching and communication, and documentation. It also covers documented misunderstandings and hospital-admission risks. Full text was read for NICE NG42 (recommendations page), the EAN 2024 ALS guideline (via Europe PMC), both AAN 2009 practice parameters (PMC HTML, before PMC began returning a reCAPTCHA browser check, which was not bypassed), the ESPEN 2022 home enteral nutrition practical guideline (PDF), the NHS Lothian enteral-tube medicines best practice statement (PDF), the MND Association acute care booklet PX017 (PDF), a BTS-derived local oxygen policy template, the PRISM and Tang caregiver papers, and the US labels for riluzole tablets, Tiglutik, Exservan, Radicava/Radicava ORS, Qalsody and Cuvposa, plus the EU Rilutek SmPC. Abstract only: all other journal sources. Not accessible: EFNS 2012 full text (Wiley 403; abstract only), Canadian 2020 CMAJ guideline (403 and no Europe PMC full text; dropped), BTS 2017 guideline full text (Thorax 403), ALS Association certified-centre pages (403), the NEWT guidelines and the White and Bradnam handbook (subscription), BAPEN practical guide (not retrieved). No ALS-specific source was found for: nasal-bridge pressure-injury rates under home NIV in ALS, the Braden scale in ALS, mask cleaning schedules, adapted call bells for weak hands, nurse-led NIV set-up in ALS, or a validated ALS-specific choking first-aid procedure. These gaps are stated where they arise.

## Facts

### Assessment: breathing
- NICE NG42 lists symptoms of possible respiratory impairment for monitoring: breathlessness, orthopnoea, recurrent chest infections, disturbed or non-refreshing sleep, nightmares, daytime sleepiness, poor concentration or memory, confusion, hallucinations, morning headaches, fatigue and poor appetite. The signs it lists are increased respiratory rate, shallow breathing, weak cough, weak sniff, abdominal paradox, accessory-muscle use and reduced chest expansion. It notes that weak cough "could be assessed by measuring peak cough flow" [NICE2016].
- NICE sets a baseline at or soon after diagnosis: a single SpO2 at rest on room air, plus FVC or VC and/or SNIP or MIP, done by "a healthcare professional from the multidisciplinary team who has appropriate competencies". Tests are repeated every 2 to 3 months, more or less often depending on symptoms, rate of progression and the person's preference [NICE2016].
- If bulbar impairment or cognitive problems are severe, NICE says SpO2 should still be measured, but FVC, VC, SNIP and MIP should not be done if the interfaces are not suitable for the person [NICE2016].
- NICE thresholds for discussion and possible referral: FVC or VC below 50% predicted; SNIP or MIP below 40 cmH2O; FVC or VC below 80% with symptoms (especially orthopnoea); SNIP or MIP below 65 cmH2O (men) or 55 cmH2O (women) with symptoms; or SNIP or MIP falling by more than 10 cmH2O per 3 months. Blood gases are indicated if SpO2 is 94% or below (92% or below with known lung disease). PaCO2 above 6 kPa calls for urgent referral, to be seen within 1 week [NICE2016].
- NICE adds a measurement caution: some pulse oximeters over- or underestimate saturation near borderline values, and overestimation has been reported in people with dark skin [NICE2016].
- Upright vs supine FVC: in 25 people with ALS who had transdiaphragmatic pressure measured, supine FVC correlated best with diaphragm strength (R² 0.76). Supine FVC below 75% predicted was 100% sensitive and specific for abnormally low Pdi. Accessory-muscle use had 84% sensitivity and 100% specificity [Lechtzin2002]. The AAN rated supine FVC "possibly more effective" than erect FVC for detecting diaphragm weakness (2 Class III studies) [Miller2009AAN].
- In 81 people with ALS, percent-predicted sniff nasal pressure predicted hypercapnia (PaCO2 at least 6 kPa) better than percent-predicted VC (odds ratio 25 vs 9; sensitivity 81% vs 53%). In people with significant bulbar weakness, no test had significant predictive power [Lyall2001].
- Peak cough flow: the AAN reports that flows above 160 L/min are needed to clear secretions, that clinicians recommend assistive devices below 270 L/min (Class III), and that a mean flow above 337 L/min was linked to a greater chance of being alive at 18 months (Class III) [Miller2009AAN].
- Signs of CO2 retention named for acute staff are drowsiness, anxiety, confusion and bounding pulse, along with orthopnoea, acute panic, increased or mucopurulent phlegm, falling saturations, raised respiratory rate and inability to clear secretions [MNDA2026acute].
- Nurse's role in NIV set-up and review: NICE gives NIV decisions to the MDT with the respiratory ventilation service. Before NIV it requires a risk assessment (interface, tolerance, ventilator-failure risk, power and battery back-up, humidifier need, secretion management, carer availability) and a written care plan. It also requires an assessment of carers' ability, willingness and training needs [NICE2016]. NICE names a "specialist nurse" in the core MDT but does not assign NIV set-up to nurses specifically. No ALS-specific study of nurse-led NIV initiation was found in this pass.
- NIV evidence underlying review: in an RCT (n=41 randomised to NIV or standard care), NIV improved quality-of-life measures and survival overall. In those with better bulbar function the median survival benefit was 205 days. In those with poor bulbar function NIV improved sleep-related symptoms but gave no survival benefit [Bourke2006].
- In NICE, NIV is started with daytime acclimatisation, then regular use at night, then hours built up as needed. It is continued when reviews show symptomatic or physiological improvement, or improved sleep-related symptoms where bulbar or cognitive impairment is severe [NICE2016].

### Assessment: swallowing, nutrition and hydration
- NICE expects weight, diet, nutritional and fluid intake, hydration, oral health, feeding, drinking and swallowing to be assessed at diagnosis, at each MDT assessment (usually every 2 to 3 months) and whenever there is concern. A clinical swallowing assessment is arranged if problems are suspected [NICE2016].
- The EAN 2024 guideline sets MDT reviews at "usually every 3–6 months depending on disease progression". Weight, diet, intake and swallowing are among the areas reviewed, and ALSFRS-R is named as the functional assessment [VanDamme2024EAN].
- Controversy (visit interval): NICE says usually every 2 to 3 months; EAN says every 3 to 6 months. Neither source gives a separate BMI frequency. Weight is tied to the MDT visit in both [NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN].
- NICE lists weight loss, bulbar presentation, poor respiratory function and a lower ALSFRS/ALSFRS-R score at diagnosis among factors associated with shorter survival [NICE2016].
- EAT-10 in ALS: in 70 people with ALS who had videofluoroscopy, mean EAT-10 scores were 4.28 (safe swallowers), 7.10 (penetrators) and 20.50 (aspirators). A cut-off of 3 identified penetration or aspiration (sensitivity 88%, specificity 57%, AUC 0.77). A cut-off of 8 identified aspiration (sensitivity 86%, specificity 72%, AUC 0.88, negative predictive value 95.5%) [Plowman2016].
- The AAN nutrition algorithm uses the bulbar questions of the ALSFRS-R (or another instrument) as a screening trigger. Prolonged meal times, meals ended early from fatigue, accelerated weight loss and family concern are listed as prompts [Miller2009AAN].
- In 145 people with ALS followed with endoscopic swallow studies for about 2 years, dysphagia rose to 85% overall (35% to 73% with spinal onset, 95% to 98% with bulbar onset). 8% of those with dysphagia on endoscopy did not perceive it, and 44% of people with dysphagia refused thickeners or PEG [Onesti2017].
- Gastrostomy outcomes for context: in ProGas (330 people who had gastrostomy), 30-day mortality was 4% (95% CI 2.1 to 6.2), with no significant difference between PEG, RIG and per-oral image-guided methods [ProGas2015].

### Assessment: skin, positioning, falls and pain
- No ALS-specific study of NIV-mask pressure injury rates was found. In hospitalised adults (an integrative review of 21 studies), oronasal NIV masks had a pressure-injury incidence of 63.3%, and the nasal bridge was the most affected site [Fidalgo2023].
- In 15 healthy volunteers on CPAP via an oronasal mask, interface pressure was highest at the nasal bridge. Humidification increased transepidermal water loss, meaning it disrupted skin barrier function [Alqahtani2018].
- A hospital quality-improvement project reports that some oronasal masks generate 6.37 to 12.2 kPa on the nasal bridge when tightened against leak. Using four plan-do-study-act cycles over 1466 NIV days, it significantly reduced category 2 mask-related pressure injuries [Rochester2025].
- The Braden scale has six subscales: sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, friction/shear and nutrition. Inter-rater reliability reached r = 0.99 among registered nurses. At a cut-off of 16, sensitivity was 100% in two studies and specificity 64% to 90% [Bergstrom1987]. No validation of the Braden scale in ALS was found.
- Controversy (pressure ulcers in ALS): older small case series described a relative absence of pressure sores in ALS. A Taiwanese claims cohort (514 people with ALS, 2056 controls) instead found a higher pressure-sore risk (adjusted HR 8.82, 95% CI 4.90 to 15.9), higher still in women (aHR 26.6) and in people aged 20 to 54 (aHR 27.7) [Chen2018].
- For acute settings: people with MND may not be able to lie flat for imaging, and propping at 30 to 45 degrees or using their own NIV is suggested. "Good skin pressure care is vital", and positioning, handling and lifting need care [MNDA2026acute].
- Falls: in a prospective study of 66 people with ALS, 47% of ambulatory participants fell by 3 months and 59% by 6 months, compared with 18% and 10% of wheelchair users. Lower-limb strength and fatigue predicted falls [Alencar2026]. NICE includes "mobility and avoiding falls" in the occupational therapy and physiotherapy assessment [NICE2016].
- Pain: in a population-based study (160 people with ALS vs controls), pain was more frequent in ALS (56.9% vs 33.1%). It increased with worse function and longer disease duration, and the authors judged it under-recognised and undertreated [Chio2012Pain]. NICE includes pain in the areas the MDT reviews [NICE2016].

### Assessment: mood, pseudobulbar affect, cognition and caregiver
- ADI-12 (ALS Depression Inventory, 12 items) in 39 people with ALS: correlation with the BDI was r = 0.80. A cut-off of 30 or more identified all with current major depression (sensitivity 100%, specificity 83%). A cut-off of 23 or more identified any depressive disorder (specificity 60%) [Hammer2008].
- HADS was designed as a self-assessment scale for anxiety and depression in hospital outpatients [Zigmond1983]. In 298 people with MND, the HADS anxiety and depression subscales fitted the Rasch model only after one item was removed from each. The authors give revised cut-offs for the modified subscales [Gibbons2011].
- The AAN reports depression prevalence in ALS ranging from 0 to 44%, with systematic studies suggesting about 10% in advanced ALS. It found no controlled treatment trials (Level U) [Miller2009AANb]. EAN recommends exploring signs of depression and emotional distress, and considering psychotherapy and/or medication [VanDamme2024EAN].
- The Center for Neurologic Study-Lability Scale (CNS-LS) is a 7-item self-report measure of laughing and crying lability developed in ALS (4 laughter items, 3 tearfulness items). It predicted physicians' diagnoses of affective lability [Moore1997]. A CNS-LS score of 13 or more is the threshold used for PBA symptoms. In ALS (n=99) this cut-off matched neurologists' diagnoses in 82% (sensitivity 0.84, specificity 0.81) [Brooks2013].
- In the PRISM registry (5290 people with six neurological conditions, 125 with ALS), 36.7% overall scored 13 or more on the CNS-LS. In ALS, the rates were 51.1% of women and 41.0% of men (difference not significant) [Brooks2013].
- EAN recommends distinguishing emotional lability from depression and from FTD symptoms. For treatment it lists SSRIs, tricyclics or dextromethorphan/quinidine [VanDamme2024EAN].
- ECAS takes 15 to 20 minutes and was designed "for use by health care professionals" with people with physical disability. Control-based cut-offs are 77/100 (ALS-specific), 24/36 (non-specific) and 105/136 (total). In the development sample, 29% of 48 people had abnormal ALS-specific scores, and 40% of carers reported behaviour change in at least one domain [Abrahams2014].
- The AAN recommends that screening for cognitive and behavioural impairment be considered (Level B), and that executive-function screening be considered before formal neuropsychology (Level C) [Miller2009AANb]. NICE asks for cognition to be explored at diagnosis and whenever there is concern, with formal assessment if needed, and for care in line with mental capacity law [NICE2016].
- For acute-care staff, about 50% of people with MND are described as cognitively unaffected, about 35% as having mild cognitive and/or behavioural change, and up to 15% as developing FTD [MNDA2026acute].
- The Zarit Burden Interview originates in research on relatives of impaired older adults [Zarit1980]. In one Chinese ALS home-care study (120 caregiver pairs), the 22-item ZBI (0 to 88) averaged 63.63 ± 16.36, which the authors classed as moderate-to-severe burden and higher than reports from the USA, Italy and India. Caregiver anxiety scores predicted burden [Tang2021].
- NICE asks the MDT to discuss the psychological impact of MND with family members and carers, and to tell carers about their right to a carer assessment and respite [NICE2016].

### Assessment: ALSFRS-R administration
- The ALSFRS-R added assessments of dyspnoea, orthopnoea and need for ventilatory support to the original ALSFRS. It kept internal consistency and construct validity, and scores correlated with quality of life [Cedarbaum1999]. The total score ranges from 0 to 48, with higher scores meaning better function [Miller2022].
- In a multicentre trial, inter-rater reliability was ICC 0.93, intra-rater 0.95 and telephone-vs-in-person 0.97 (95% CI 0.93 to 0.98; n=27 telephone) [Kaufmann2007].
- A self-administered version in 60 consecutive clinic patients showed ICC 0.93 (95% CI 0.88 to 0.96) and similar sensitivity to change compared with the evaluator-administered scale [Montes2006].
- Online self-assessment correlated r = 0.96 with on-site ALSFRS-R (n=127 at baseline). 75% of patients completed a follow-up online rating [Maier2012].
- None of these validation studies tested nurse raters specifically. Raters were trial or clinic evaluators, which leaves a gap for nurse-administration evidence [Kaufmann2007; Montes2006].

### Giving the medicines: riluzole
- US label: 50 mg twice daily, taken at least 1 hour before or 2 hours after a meal. Serum aminotransferases are measured before and during treatment, with monitoring for liver injury "every month for the first 3 months of treatment, and periodically thereafter". Riluzole is not recommended if transaminases exceed 5 times the upper limit of normal [FDA2026Riluzole].
- The US label gives these rates: ALT above 5 times ULN in 2% of people taking riluzole; maximum ALT rise within 3 months; about 50% and 8% with at least one ALT above ULN and above 3 times ULN [FDA2026Riluzole].
- EU SmPC: ALT "every month during the first 3 months of treatment, every 3 months during the remainder of the first year, and periodically thereafter". Riluzole is discontinued if ALT reaches 5 times ULN [EMA_RilutekSmPC].
- Controversy (labels differ): the EU SmPC gives a fuller LFT schedule than the US label. It also does not make fasting part of the posology. It reports that high-fat meals reduce Cmax by 44% and AUC by 17% [EMA_RilutekSmPC; FDA2026Riluzole].
- Other label warnings relevant to nursing observation: severe neutropenia within the first 2 months, with patients advised to report febrile illness; interstitial lung disease; and acute pancreatitis (abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, anorexia) occurring weeks to years after starting [FDA2026Riluzole].
- Tiglutik (riluzole 50 mg/10 mL oral suspension) may be taken by mouth or via PEG tube; both silicone and polyurethane PEGs can be used. The bottle is gently shaken for at least 30 seconds, the same meal timing applies, and unused suspension is discarded 15 days after opening [FDA2026Tiglutik].
- In healthy volunteers, riluzole suspension given by intragastric tube was bioequivalent to oral dosing (AUC ratio 90.6%, Cmax ratio 97.0%) [Brooks2019].
- Crushing tablets: no US or EU label statement on crushing riluzole tablets was found. In a home simulation with 15 inexperienced volunteers, crushing riluzole tablets lost a mean 6.27% of powder and 8.53% of active drug [Casiraghi2023]. Authors of an observational study described crushing and mixing tablets into food as a common practice and judged it inconsistent with safe and effective use [Onesti2017].
- Exservan (riluzole 50 mg oral film) is placed on top of the tongue, where it sticks and dissolves. It is not cut or taken with liquids, saliva is swallowed normally, and chewing, spitting and talking are avoided while it dissolves. One film is taken at a time, with the same meal timing. Oral hypoaesthesia occurred in 38% of healthy subjects on the film vs none on tablets [FDA2020Exservan].
- In 9 people with ALS, videofluoroscopy before and after a dose of riluzole oral film showed no deterioration in swallowing. Taking the film with food reduced AUC by 15% and Cmax by 45% [Wymer2023].
- EAN recommends lifelong riluzole from diagnosis. If adverse events occur, it suggests dose reduction and then stopping if they persist, and notes that preference for oral, liquid or other formulations varies [VanDamme2024EAN]. The AAN suggests withholding riluzole may be considered if fatigue develops (Level C) [Miller2009AANb].

### Giving the medicines: edaravone and tofersen
- Radicava IV: 60 mg given as two consecutive 30 mg bags over a total of 60 minutes (about 1 mg/min). It is not used if the overwrap oxygen indicator has turned blue or purple, and is used within 24 hours of opening the overwrap. No other medicines are added to the bag. The infusion is stopped at the first sign of hypersensitivity [FDA2026Radicava].
- Cycles (IV and oral): the first cycle is daily dosing for 14 days then 14 drug-free days. Later cycles are dosing on 10 of 14 days followed by 14 drug-free days [FDA2026Radicava].
- Radicava ORS: 105 mg (5 mL) by mouth or feeding tube in the morning after overnight fasting, with no food (water allowed) for 1 hour after. If food was taken, it is withheld for 8 hours before a dose (high-fat meal), 4 hours (low-fat meal) or 2 hours (250-calorie supplement), and for 1 hour after in every case [FDA2026Radicava].
- Radicava ORS via tube: the bottle is shaken for at least 30 seconds. NG or PEG tubes of silicone, PVC or polyurethane may be used, flushed with at least 30 mL of water before and after. The supplied 5 mL syringe is used (a household teaspoon is not adequate), and the bottle is discarded 15 days after opening [FDA2026Radicava].
- Radicava warnings: hypersensitivity, including anaphylaxis, and sulfite (sodium bisulfite) reactions, more frequent in people with asthma. The most common adverse reactions on IV were contusion (15% vs 9%), gait disturbance (13% vs 9%) and headache (10% vs 6%) [FDA2026Radicava].
- EAN 2024 does not recommend IV or oral edaravone outside clinical trials (an interim recommendation) [VanDamme2024EAN].
- Qalsody (tofersen): 100 mg (15 mL) intrathecally by lumbar puncture, given by or under the direction of professionals experienced in lumbar puncture. Three loading doses are given at 14-day intervals, then one dose every 28 days [FDA2023Qalsody].
- Qalsody administration steps: the vial is warmed to room temperature without external heat and not shaken. About 10 mL of CSF is removed before the dose. The drug is given undiluted as a bolus over 1 to 3 minutes, within 4 hours of drawing it up. Sedation and imaging guidance may be considered if indicated. Patients are evaluated before and after the injection for conditions related to lumbar puncture [FDA2023Qalsody].
- The label gives no fixed post-procedure observation period. The seed claim of a specific post-dose monitoring time was not confirmed [FDA2023Qalsody].
- Qalsody warnings: myelitis or radiculitis (6 patients), papilloedema or raised intracranial pressure (4 patients) and aseptic meningitis (2 serious events). Common adverse reactions vs placebo were pain (42% vs 22%), fatigue (17% vs 6%), arthralgia (14% vs 6%), raised CSF white cells (14% vs 0%) and myalgia (14% vs 6%) [FDA2023Qalsody].
- In VALOR, lumbar-puncture-related adverse events were common, and neurologic serious adverse events occurred in 7% of tofersen recipients [Miller2022]. EAN recommends tofersen first-line for progressive SOD1-ALS and says the treatment burden of intrathecal dosing should be discussed [VanDamme2024EAN].
- Lumbar puncture aftercare (not ALS-specific): a Cochrane review of 24 trials (2996 participants) found no evidence that routine bed rest after dural puncture prevents post-dural-puncture headache. Bed rest probably increased it (RR 1.24, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.48), and the role of extra fluids remained unclear [Arevalo2016].

### Giving the medicines: via gastrostomy, saliva drugs, end-of-life symptom drugs
- ESPEN: a tube used for feeding can be used for medicines if efficacy can be confirmed. Medicines are given individually, with the tube flushed before, between and after each drug using 30 mL of water. Feeds are flushed with at least 30 mL before and after a bolus, or 4-hourly if continuous [Bischoff2022].
- ESPEN also says crushing medicines "should be avoided whenever possible", liquid medicines may carry sorbitol or high osmolality, and patients and carers need information with pharmacist involvement [Bischoff2022].
- NHS Lothian best practice (2013, review date 2018, citing NEWT and BAPEN): enteric-coated, modified or slow-release, and cytotoxic or hormonal medicines are not to be crushed. Flushes for adults are at least 30 mL before medicines, at least 5 mL between each and at least 30 mL after. For drugs needing an empty stomach, feeds are stopped 15 to 30 minutes before and restarted 15 to 30 minutes after. Medicines are not mixed with each other or with feed [NHSLothian2013].
- ALS-specific link (inferred from the labels, not stated in a guideline): riluzole and Radicava ORS both carry food-timing rules, so feeding schedules affect when they can be given via tube [FDA2026Tiglutik; FDA2026Radicava]. No published ALS nursing protocol integrating these with feeding regimens was found.
- Saliva: NICE suggests considering a trial of an antimuscarinic first line (off-label in 2016), and glycopyrronium first line where there is cognitive impairment "because it has fewer central nervous system side effects". Botulinum toxin A is a first- or second-line specialist option [NICE2016].
- EAN lists amitriptyline, atropine, glycopyrrolate, oxybutynin and scopolamine as first-line anticholinergics, dextromethorphan/quinidine where there is emotional lability, botulinum toxin when medicines fail, and radiotherapy after that. EAN adds that suction devices lack evidence and "may increase the saliva production" [VanDamme2024EAN].
- Cochrane (4 RCTs, 110 people with MND) found low-certainty evidence for botulinum toxin B and moderate-certainty evidence for dextromethorphan/quinidine. Evidence on scopolamine patches was too uncertain to draw conclusions [James2022].
- Anticholinergic side effects on the glycopyrrolate oral solution label (a paediatric drooling indication; use in adult ALS is off-label): dry mouth, vomiting, constipation, flushing and nasal congestion (each 30% or more). Further cautions are constipation or pseudo-obstruction (assess within 4 to 5 days of starting or dose increase), heat prostration in hot environments, and drowsiness or blurred vision [FDA2024Cuvposa].
- Treating sialorrhoea can over-dry saliva so that it becomes thick and hard to clear [MNDA2026acute]. NICE advises reviewing saliva treatments when secretions are thick [NICE2016].
- Breathlessness: NICE suggests considering opioids for breathlessness, and benzodiazepines for breathlessness made worse by anxiety (both off-label in 2016). It also covers "as-needed" home supplies for people on NIV, and anticipatory opioids, benzodiazepines and antimuscarinics towards the end of life [NICE2016]. EAN adopts the opioid and benzodiazepine recommendations for people who do not tolerate or accept ventilation, or in end-stage disease [VanDamme2024EAN].
- In a pilot of 6 people with dyspnoeic terminal ALS, morphine reduced respiratory rate (42 to 29 per minute) and dyspnoea (NRS 7.5 to 1.8) at 120 minutes, without significant transcutaneous CO2 rise or desaturation. Nasal oxygen beforehand did not significantly reduce dyspnoea [Clemens2008].
- For acute staff, lorazepam and morphine salts are listed for panic and acute breathlessness, buccal midazolam in acute situations, and titrated opioids where ventilation is not used. Sedatives are to be "used with caution, but not withheld at end of life" [MNDA2026acute].
- The AAN noted concern that sedative or hypnotic agents may suppress respiratory drive in ALS and found no trials (Level U for insomnia, pain and dyspnoea in late-stage ALS) [Miller2009AANb].

### Teaching and communication
- NICE requires training for the person and family in NIV use and interfaces, including emergency procedures, night-time help with removing or replacing the interface, use with wheelchairs, "what to do if the equipment fails", secretion management and ongoing emotional support. Arrangements for device maintenance and 24-hour clinical and technical support belong in the care plan [NICE2016].
- NICE asks the pre-NIV risk assessment to address power supply and battery back-up, the consequences of ventilator failure, whether a humidifier is needed, and compatibility with other equipment such as eye-gaze systems [NICE2016]. No ALS-specific source on mask cleaning schedules or alarm-response teaching was found.
- Cough: NICE and EAN recommend unassisted breath stacking and/or manual assisted cough first, then assisted breath stacking (a lung-volume recruitment bag) if bulbar function is poor or cough stays ineffective, then a mechanical cough assist device. EAN adds that a suction device may be considered alongside the cough assist [NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN].
- The AAN found mechanical insufflation-exsufflation raised peak cough flow by 26% (bulbar) and 28% (non-bulbar), vs 11% to 13% with manually assisted cough (Class III). It may be considered when peak cough flow is reduced, especially during chest infection (Level C) [Miller2009AAN].
- NICE asks for advice on swallowing, diet, posture, positioning, oral care and suctioning when drooling is a problem. It suggests humidification, nebulisers and carbocisteine for thick secretions [NICE2016]. MND Association guidance for acute staff says saline nebulisers are driven by air rather than oxygen [MNDA2026acute].
- Safe-swallow strategies: in 81 people with ALS assessed by videofluoromanometry, aspiration was resolved by chin tuck in 13 of 24 aspirators and by head rotation in 6, and was not resolved in 5 [Solazzo2011]. A scoping review of 157 MND dysphagia papers found very few that reported on the efficacy of rehabilitative or compensatory strategies [Waito2017]. Evidence for small bolus and upright positioning in ALS specifically was not found beyond NICE's general advice on positioning, seating and modified consistency [NICE2016].
- Choking and laryngospasm: for acute choking, staff are told to clear the blockage (saliva, food or fluids), offer palliation for distress and reassure the person "that death from choking is rare" [MNDA2026acute]. EAN lists laryngospasm triggers (liquid or saliva at the larynx, reflux, smoke, strong smells, emotion, alcohol, cold air, spicy food) and recommends explaining that it is self-limiting [VanDamme2024EAN]. No ALS-specific validated choking first-aid protocol was found.
- Gastrostomy site care: the exit site is checked daily and kept clean and dry with aseptic care until healed (usually up to 5 to 7 days). After about one week the tube "should be rotated daily and should be moved inwards at least once a week (at least 2 cm, up to 10 cm)". Occlusive dressings are avoided [Bischoff2022].
- ESGE recommends daily tube mobilisation (pushing inward) and keeping the external bumper loose, 1 to 2 cm from the abdominal wall, to reduce buried-bumper risk (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence) [Gkolfakis2021].
- Controversy (tube movement routine): ESPEN advises daily rotation plus weekly inward movement, while ESGE advises daily inward mobilisation [Bischoff2022; Gkolfakis2021].
- Buried-bumper warning signs: difficulty moving the tube, leakage around the site on flushing, frequent pump alarms, abdominal pain, chronic site infection, or resistance when giving feed or fluids [Bischoff2022].
- Tube blockage: some experts use cola or pancreatic enzymes, but ESPEN does not recommend cola, and sodium bicarbonate is "not evidence-based". If needed, an expert may use a guide wire or declogger [Bischoff2022]. For acute care, blockage is referred to the nutrition or upper-GI team [MNDA2026acute].
- Energy conservation: in a 28-person RCT in MND, three weekly one-hour occupational therapy energy-conservation sessions improved fatigue (FSS) and occupational performance (COPM) but not quality of life [Fateh2022].
- Communication: NICE and EAN call for prompt speech and language therapy assessment and for both low-tech (alphabet, word or picture boards) and high-tech AAC. They also call for referral to a specialised AAC hub for eye-gaze, ongoing training for carers, and alternative ways to contact services (for example when a call centre is unusable) [NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN].
- In 35 regular users with late-stage ALS, tetraplegia and anarthria, an eye-transfer (ETRAN) board improved communication over no device, and eye-tracking devices improved it further, with high satisfaction [Caligari2013]. The AAN lists alphabet boards, computerised systems, Morse code and infrared eye-movement systems, but found no controlled studies (Level U) [Miller2009AANb].
- Acute staff are asked to find out whether the person uses a simple yes/no code (any reliable movement, such as blinking), not to finish sentences, not to use family as translators unless the person asks, and to use yes/no or single-word questions [MNDA2026acute].
- No published source on adapted nurse-call systems for people with weak hands in ALS was found in this pass. NICE refers only generally to environmental control systems and assistive technology [NICE2016].
- Diagnosis and prognosis: NICE says the diagnosis should be given by a consultant neurologist with MND experience, after asking how much information the person wants. A single point of contact and a face-to-face MDT follow-up within 4 weeks are offered [NICE2016]. EFNS says diagnosis is given by a consultant who knows the patient and the disease [Andersen2012EFNS].
- The AAN found insufficient evidence for any specific method of disclosing the diagnosis (Level U) [Miller2009AANb]. In an Australian survey of 248 people with MND, 36% were dissatisfied with how the diagnosis was delivered. Satisfaction rose with time spent and with empathic response, sharing information, exploring expectations and making a plan [Aoun2016]. No source defining the nurse's specific role in disclosure was found. Guidelines place disclosure with the neurologist and follow-up with the MDT [NICE2016].

### Common misunderstandings
- "Twitching means ALS": among 121 people diagnosed with benign fasciculations (normal examination and electrophysiology apart from fasciculations), none developed motor neuron disease over 2 to 32 years [Blexrud1993]. NICE does list tongue fasciculations and muscle twitching among possible presenting features alongside weakness and other signs [NICE2016].
- "A feeding tube means no more eating": the AAN states "PEG does not eliminate oral feeding" and describes it as a way to give medicines and fluids and stabilise weight [Miller2009AAN].
- "NIV cannot be stopped once started": NICE says to "explain that non-invasive ventilation can be stopped at any time", with support from professionals experienced in withdrawal [NICE2016]. In interviews with 24 UK doctors, colleagues were sometimes unclear about the distinction between palliation, treatment withdrawal and assisted death [Phelps2017].
- "People with ALS choke to death": in 121 German and 50 UK deaths, most died peacefully (88% and 98%) and "no patient choked to death". The commonest last-24-hour symptoms were dyspnoea, cough, anxiety and restlessness [Neudert2001].
- "Oxygen relieves breathlessness in ALS": MND Association guidance states "Oxygen does not help breathlessness" [MNDA2026acute]. In a pilot, nasal oxygen did not significantly reduce dyspnoea [Clemens2008].
- "Riluzole should be stopped once a gastrostomy is placed": no source documenting this belief was found (unsourced as a misconception). The Tiglutik label allows PEG administration, and EAN recommends lifelong riluzole [FDA2026Tiglutik; VanDamme2024EAN].
- "Bed rest prevents headache after lumbar puncture": Cochrane evidence found no benefit and probable harm (not ALS-specific) [Arevalo2016].

### Documentation and the specialist nurse role
- NICE asks that key decisions be shared with all professionals involved, and that a copy of the NIV care plan be offered to the person and carers. It also asks that services seeing the person in crisis (GP, emergency care) be told the person uses NIV [NICE2016].
- NICE lists for advance care planning: ADRT, DNACPR, Lasting Power of Attorney, preferred place of death, what the person does not want (for example hospital admission), who will represent them, and plans for intercurrent illness. It also covers how plans will be available when needed (for example on the Summary Care Record) and reviewing them when gastrostomy or NIV is planned [NICE2016]. EAN adopts these and adds invasive ventilation, palliative sedation and treatment withdrawal [VanDamme2024EAN].
- In acute care, staff are asked to check for an emergency healthcare plan, ReSPECT form, advance care plan, ADRT, DNACPR and an MND Alert Card or "Understanding My Needs" record [MNDA2026acute].
- For tube medicines, the dosage form and liquid volume are recorded on the prescription chart, and flushes are recorded in the fluid balance chart where relevant [NHSLothian2013].
- No guideline found specifies documenting body position with FVC. The AAN and Lechtzin data show that upright and supine values differ, which is why a record of position matters for comparison over time [Miller2009AAN; Lechtzin2002]. NICE specifies SpO2 "at rest and breathing room air", which defines the conditions to record [NICE2016]. Recording the date and position of each respiratory measure is therefore inferred, not explicitly sourced.
- Weight trend, mask skin, communication method (including the yes/no signal), medication changes and LFT results are all among the areas the guidelines and labels say to assess or monitor (see the facts above). No single source gives a nursing documentation checklist for ALS (unsourced as a checklist).
- NICE and EAN both name a specialist nurse in the core MDT [NICE2016; VanDamme2024EAN]. On admission, "an MND specialist (nurse, neurologist or care centre coordinator) should be alerted within 24 hours" [MNDA2026acute].
- Evidence for the model: a Cochrane review found no randomised or controlled trials of multidisciplinary care in MND. It found low-quality evidence of reduced hospitalisation, very-low-quality evidence for better mental-health quality of life, and conflicting survival evidence [Ng2009]. The AAN rates multidisciplinary clinic referral Level B for care delivery and survival [Miller2009AANb].
- Controversy (centralised clinic vs community worker): in all 719 incident cases in Ireland and Northern Ireland (2005 to 2010), attending the centralised multidisciplinary clinic was associated with better survival (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.71). The authors judged it superior to devolved community care supported by a specialist care worker [Rooney2015].
- In Canterbury, New Zealand, unplanned admissions and emergency visits fell after a key-worker role and multidisciplinary clinic were introduced (retrospective before/after audit), with no change in the timing of feeding tubes or NIV [Wiseman2026].
- A 2025 census of 64 UK MND nurses and allied health professionals found 14% at medium and 12% at high risk of burnout. Almost 80% engaged in 8 of the 15 Scottish MND advanced nurse specialist competencies [Chau2025].
- ALS Association certified centres (US): a 2014 institutional announcement lists the criteria as diversity of ALS expertise, access to coordinated multidisciplinary care, a relationship with the local chapter and research participation, with nurses among team members [UNMC2014]. The ALS Association's own criteria page could not be accessed.

### Hospital admission risks
- In 8 people with neuromuscular disease and diaphragm dysfunction (3 with ALS or MND), low-flow oxygen (0.5 to 2 L/min) raised PaCO2 by a mean 28.2 ± 23.3 mmHg, and by 27 mmHg or more in five people. The authors advised caution with even low-flow oxygen and consideration of assisted ventilation first [Gay1995].
- A BTS-derived oxygen policy template sets target saturations of 94 to 98% for most acutely ill patients and 88 to 92% for those at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure. It names neuromuscular disorders among the groups in whom oxygen may raise CO2 [BTS2017template; ODriscoll2017].
- MND Association guidance: saturations of 88 to 92% are acceptable in MND unless other pathology is present. Supplemental oxygen is used "only with extreme caution", because excess oxygen can cause acidaemic respiratory failure and death, and monitoring is by arterial blood gas. A person's own NIV is used to manage dyspnoea and hypercapnia [MNDA2026acute].
- NICE NG42 itself has no explicit oxygen-caution recommendation (checked by text search of the recommendations page). The oxygen caution comes from BTS and MND Association guidance [NICE2016; MNDA2026acute].
- If intubation occurs, extubation can be difficult, and weaning to NIV may be appropriate unless bulbar weakness is significant. A high NEWS-type score may be normal for the person [MNDA2026acute]. EAN recommends advance discussion of invasive ventilation "to avoid emergency invasive ventilation (e.g., unplanned hospital admission)" [VanDamme2024EAN].
- In Pennsylvania (1996 to 2009), 32% of 1748 gastrostomies and 67% of 373 tracheostomies in ALS or MND were placed during emergency hospitalisations. Emergent gastrostomy admissions cost more and led to fewer home discharges [Tsou2012].
- Other acute checks listed: for a person on riluzole with fever, check full blood count (neutropenia) and LFTs. Drug routes depend on swallowing and whether an enteral tube is present. An MND alert wristband with an oxygen warning is among the alert tools described [MNDA2026acute].

## Step sequence
A nursing assessment round (animation, 7 steps):
1. Breathing: ask about breathlessness, orthopnoea, sleep, morning headache and daytime sleepiness; look for abdominal paradox and accessory-muscle use; record SpO2 at rest on room air and FVC (upright and, where done, supine) and/or SNIP, with date and position; note peak cough flow [NICE2016; Lechtzin2002; Miller2009AAN].
2. Swallowing and nutrition: weigh and compare with the trend; ask about meal length, coughing on swallowing and fluid intake; use a screen such as EAT-10 (score of 8 or more flags possible aspiration in ALS) and refer for a clinical swallow assessment if concerned [NICE2016; Plowman2016; Miller2009AAN].
3. Skin under the mask: inspect the nasal bridge and cheeks under the NIV interface and other pressure areas; review fit, positioning and humidification [Fidalgo2023; Alqahtani2018; Bergstrom1987].
4. Medicines: check riluzole timing relative to meals or feeds, LFT dates and the formulation in use (tablet, suspension via PEG, or film); check edaravone or tofersen schedules if prescribed; check the gastrostomy site and the flushing routine (30 mL before, between and after medicines) [FDA2026Riluzole; FDA2026Tiglutik; FDA2026Radicava; FDA2023Qalsody; Bischoff2022].
5. Communication: confirm the person's communication method and yes/no signal and that their AAC aid is within reach; note any change in speech [MNDA2026acute; NICE2016].
6. Mood, emotion and caregiver: screen for depression (for example ADI-12 or HADS), emotional lability (CNS-LS score of 13 or more), cognitive or behavioural change (ECAS by trained staff), pain, and carer burden (for example Zarit) [Hammer2008; Gibbons2011; Brooks2013; Abrahams2014; Chio2012Pain; Zarit1980].
7. Documentation: record measures with date and position, the weight trend, mask skin, communication method, medication changes and LFTs, and advance decisions (ADRT, DNACPR, ventilation and feeding decisions, preferred place of care); share key decisions with the team and alert services that may see the person in a crisis [NICE2016; MNDA2026acute; NHSLothian2013].
