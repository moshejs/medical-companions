## Facts

### Familial versus sporadic ALS: how the proportion is defined and how large it is
- A meta-analysis of 33 studies put the rate of familial ALS (FALS) at 4.6% (95% CI 3.9-5.5%) across all studies and 5.1% (4.1-6.1%) when restricted to prospective population-based registries. The authors concluded this is "not 10% as is often stated". FALS rates were lower in southern Europe. [Byrne2011]
- Controversy: the 2022 Lancet Seminar says familial ALS makes up 10-15% of cases (defined as ALS or related syndromes such as FTD among family members), and that because of low-penetrance variants, small families and incomplete family histories, familial ALS "may be underreported and represent closer to 20% of cases". The population-registry meta-analysis gives about 5%. Part of the gap is definition: whether relatives with FTD count, and how many generations are asked about. [Feldman2022; Byrne2011]
- The 1993 SOD1 discovery paper described about 10% of ALS as inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, "with high penetrance after the sixth decade". [Rosen1993]
- The 2022 Seminar reports that about 70% of familial cases carry a variant in a known ALS gene, and about 15% of people with sporadic ALS carry a "private" pathogenic variant in a known gene. It proposes replacing the familial/sporadic split with "genetically confirmed versus non-genetically confirmed ALS". [Feldman2022]
- A 2018 review counted 22 genes implicated in ALS by 2014, explaining about two-thirds of familial and about 10% of sporadic cases. Seven more were added after 2014: MATR3, CHCHD10, TBK1, TUBA4A, NEK1, C21orf2 and CCNF. [Chia2018]
- By 2022, over 40 ALS-associated genes had been reported. They vary in frequency, inheritance (mostly dominant, rarely recessive) and penetrance. Some, such as ANG, ATXN2 and DCTN1, raise risk rather than cause disease. [Feldman2022]

### Heritability
- Twin study meta-analysis (171 twin pairs with at least one affected twin): 5 monozygotic pairs were concordant and 44 discordant; no dizygotic pairs were concordant and 122 were discordant. Estimated heritability of sporadic ALS was 0.61 (95% CI 0.38-0.78), with the unshared environmental component at 0.39 (0.22-0.62). [AlChalabi2010]
- Irish population-based parent-offspring study (1117 people with ALS, 2008-2017, 32 parent-child pairs): mean lifetime heritability was 52.3% (95% CI 42.9-61.7%) overall and 36.9% (19.8-53.9%) in people with no known genetic risk. It was highest in mother-daughter pairs (66.2%). [Ryan2019]
- In the same Irish study, the lifetime risk of ALS in first-degree relatives of people with ALS was 1.4% (32 of 2234), compared with 0.3% in the general population. Affected children had onset at a mean of 52.0 years, against 69.6 years in their affected parents. [Ryan2019]
- The International Alliance of ALS/MND Associations' Genetics Task Force published global best-practice recommendations in 2026 (structured consensus, approved by anonymous vote), setting minimum and aspirational practices for genetic counselling, testing and laboratory standards across resource settings. Only the abstract was read [Salmon2026].
- The 2023 consensus states that in European populations, first-degree relatives of people with apparently sporadic ALS have a lifetime risk of about 1-3%. [Roggenbuck2023]
- A cross-ancestry GWAS (29,612 people with ALS, 122,656 controls) found 15 risk loci and quoted ALS lifetime risk as one in 350. In Mendelian randomisation analysis, high cholesterol was the only environmental or lifestyle factor tested that came out as causal. [vanRheenen2021]

### Share of ALS explained by each gene, by ancestry
- A meta-analysis of 111 studies found that pathogenic variants in the four major genes (C9orf72, SOD1, TARDBP, FUS) were present in 47.7% of familial and 5.2% of sporadic ALS. [Zou2017]
- Europe in that meta-analysis: C9orf72 in 33.7% of FALS and 5.1% of SALS; SOD1 14.8% and 1.2%; TARDBP 4.2% and 0.8%; FUS 2.8% and 0.3%. [Zou2017]
- Asia in that meta-analysis: SOD1 was most common (30.0% of FALS, 1.5% of SALS), followed by FUS (6.4%, 0.9%), C9orf72 (2.3%, 0.3%) and TARDBP (1.5%, 0.2%). The authors call the Asian genetic architecture "distinct". [Zou2017]
- A 2014 review gave approximate shares: C9orf72 about 40% of familial ALS and about 7% of apparently sporadic ALS in people of European ancestry; SOD1 about 12% of familial and about 1% of sporadic; TARDBP about 4% of familial; FUS about 4% of familial; VCP 1-2% of familial. [Renton2014]
- C9orf72 is less common in Japan than in Europe, while OPTN variants show the reverse pattern. [Renton2014]
- In a UK specialist clinic, a 44-gene panel was run on 100 consecutive people with ALS (7 familial). 21% carried a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant, and 93% of those had no family history of ALS. 15% met the criteria for a current gene-therapy trial. A further 21% carried only a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). [Shepheard2021]

### C9orf72: the repeat and its size ranges
- The C9orf72 expansion is a noncoding GGGGCC hexanucleotide repeat in the first intron of C9orf72 on chromosome 9p21. [DeJesusHernandez2011; Renton2011]
- In the discovery paper, control repeat lengths ranged from 2 to 23 units. Southern blotting of patient lymphoblast DNA estimated about 700-1600 units in people with FTD/ALS. [DeJesusHernandez2011]
- In the other discovery paper, repeat-primed PCR separated samples into expanded (more than 30 repeats) and wild-type (fewer than 20 repeats) groups. The assay can detect only about 60 repeats at most, so true expansion size can be "far greater" than it shows. Controls averaged 2 repeats (range 0-22). [Renton2011]
- Controversy, intermediate alleles: a meta-analysis of 5071 cases and 3747 controls found intermediate repeats of 24-30 associated with ALS (random-effects OR 4.2, 95% CI 1.23-14.35, p=0.02). The authors proposed that "an expansion of 24 or more repeats should be considered pathogenic". [Iacoangeli2019]
- The 2023 consensus disagrees that a cut-off is settled: "There is no validated cutoff that differentiates between pathogenic and nonpathogenic alleles, and the clinical significance of intermediate size alleles is unknown". Alleles of 20-29 repeats "have contradictory evidence of association with ALS", and any risk they carry is likely lower than for long expansions. [Roggenbuck2023]
- The 2023 consensus describes the expansion as having "extreme somatic instability", with repeat size varying over time and between tissues in one person. No case is documented where blood testing was normal but the brain carried an expansion, so DNA from blood or other non-CNS tissue is judged sufficient. [Roggenbuck2023]

### C9orf72: frequency, ancestry and founder haplotype
- A cross-sectional screen of 4448 people with ALS and 1425 with FTD from 17 regions found the expansion in 7.0% (236/3377) of white people with sporadic ALS from the USA, Europe and Australia. It was found in 39.3% (217/552) of white people with familial ALS, 4.1% (2/49) of Black and 8.3% (6/72) of Hispanic people with sporadic ALS in the USA, and none of 360 people with sporadic ALS from Asia or the Pacific Islands. [Majounie2012]
- In FTD in the same screen: 6.0% (59/981) of white Europeans with sporadic FTD and 24.8% (99/400) with familial FTD carried the expansion. [Majounie2012]
- In the discovery series, C9orf72 was the most common genetic abnormality in familial FTD (11.7%) and familial ALS (23.5%). [DeJesusHernandez2011]
- In Finland the expansion explained 46.0% of familial and 21.1% of sporadic ALS. Together with SOD1 D90A, 87% of Finnish familial ALS had a single-gene cause. It was found in about one third of familial ALS of outbred European descent. [Renton2011]
- All 262 expansion carriers haplotyped in the 2012 screen carried all or part of the Finnish founder risk haplotype. This suggests a single expansion event about 1500 years ago. [Majounie2012]
- In an Irish population-based DNA bank, the expansion was found in 41% (20/49) of familial and 5% (19/386) of apparently sporadic ALS. [Byrne2012]

### C9orf72: penetrance (a real controversy)
- Family- and clinic-based estimate: the expansion was non-penetrant before age 35, 50% penetrant by 58 years and "almost fully penetrant by 80 years" (Kaplan-Meier analysis of 603 carriers). [Majounie2012]
- A 2017 pooled analysis of 1,170 carriers drawn from the literature found a median onset age of 58.0 years (95% CI 57.0-59.0) and cumulative penetrance of 99.5% by 83 years. Penetrance did not differ between familial and sporadic cases. Women, and particularly women with bulbar onset, had a later onset. When the authors raised the share of asymptomatic carriers in the model to 20%, penetrance at 83 fell to 90.9%. [Murphy2017]
- Population-based estimate: in UK Biobank, 701 of 490,331 people had more than 100 repeats. Cumulative incidence of ALS or dementia by age 80 was 66% (95% CI 57-73%) in carriers against 5.8% in matched controls. Allowing for death from other causes, the figures were 58% (50-64%) against 5.1%. The authors call the expansion "incompletely penetrant" and report that UNC13A rs12608932 C-allele homozygosity raised risk (HR 1.81). [Gao2025]
- Controversy: estimates built from affected families give near-complete penetrance by 80-83 years [Majounie2012; Murphy2017]. A population cohort gives about 58-66% for ALS or dementia by 80 [Gao2025]. The 2023 consensus says some SOD1 and FUS variants carry high risk, while C9orf72 has "much lower disease risk" [Roggenbuck2023]. Ascertainment through families is the likely source of the difference, since Gao2025 names family-based cohorts as a possible source of bias.

### C9orf72: mechanisms
- Three main disease mechanisms have been proposed: loss of function of the C9orf72 protein (haploinsufficiency), toxic gain of function from repeat RNA, and toxic dipeptide repeat proteins made by repeat-associated non-ATG (RAN) translation. Downstream cell-autonomous and non-cell-autonomous processes probably both contribute. [Balendra2018]
- The discovery paper found that the expansion leads to loss of one alternatively spliced C9orf72 transcript and to nuclear RNA foci, "suggesting multiple disease mechanisms". [DeJesusHernandez2011]
- Most C9orf72 inclusions contain poly-(Gly-Ala), with poly-(Gly-Pro) and poly-(Gly-Arg) less common. These are presumably made by non-ATG translation of the GGGGCC repeat in three reading frames. [Mori2013]
- The repeat is transcribed in both directions. Antisense GGCCCC RNA forms nuclear foci, and RAN translation of sense and antisense transcripts yields six RAN proteins: sense Gly-Ala, Gly-Arg and Gly-Pro, and antisense Pro-Arg, Pro-Ala and Gly-Pro. These form cytoplasmic aggregates in frontal and motor cortex, hippocampus and spinal cord. [Zu2013]
- C9orf72 cases, like most ALS, show TDP-43-based pathology, the hallmark protein first identified in ALS and FTLD-U inclusions in 2006. [DeJesusHernandez2011; Neumann2006]

### C9orf72: associated phenotype
- In an Irish population-based cohort, people with the expansion had earlier onset (mean 56.3 vs 61.3 years) and more co-morbid FTD (50% vs 12%). They had shorter survival in age-matched analysis (20 vs 26 months; multivariable HR 1.9, 95% CI 1.1-3.7), and 86% had a family history of ALS or FTD. [Byrne2012]
- Symptomatic carriers show higher rates of psychotic and other psychiatric symptoms than non-carriers, and their relatives show higher rates of primary neuropsychiatric disorders. The expansion "does not appear to directly cause primary psychiatric disorders". [Silverman2019]
- GeneReviews describes onset usually between 50 and 64 years (range 20-91), presenting as pure FTD, pure ALS or both. Presentations vary between and within families, and motor neuron disease correlates with earlier onset and worse prognosis. [GossyeGeneReviews]

### C9orf72: laboratory testing
- A blinded study sent DNA from 78 people to 14 laboratories. Only 5 laboratories using PCR-based methods matched the Southern blot reference in full, only 50 of 78 samples received the same result from all 14, and false positives and false negatives were frequent. Mean sensitivity of amplicon-length analysis plus repeat-primed PCR was 95.0% (73.9-100%) and mean specificity 98.0% (87.5-100%). [Akimoto2014]
- The 2023 consensus accepts several methods for detecting expansions. Southern blot is the "gold standard" and the only commercial method able to size large expansions. Repeat-primed PCR shows a "saw-tooth pattern" and should be run from both ends of the repeat when nearby sequence variants could cause false negatives. Dual-mode long-read PCR sizes from 2 to about 950 repeats. ExpansionHunter can be used on PCR-free whole-genome data, but its size estimates are not true sizes. [Roggenbuck2023]
- The consensus asks reports to state the sizes of non-expanded alleles and to state the maximum repeat number the assay can detect. Its examples of upper limits are ">55 repeats; >145 repeats; 1500-2500 depending on the method". [Roggenbuck2023]

### SOD1
- SOD1 was the first ALS gene. In 1993, tight linkage to chromosome 21q led to 11 different SOD1 missense mutations being found in 13 families with familial ALS. SOD1 encodes a cytosolic Cu/Zn-binding homodimeric enzyme that converts superoxide to O2 and H2O2. [Rosen1993]
- Number of variants: a 2022 international study states that "over 180 variants have been identified" across the gene. Earlier papers said more than 100. Reliable genetic evidence of pathogenicity exists for only some of them. [OpieMartin2022; Renton2014; Lindberg2002]
- Nomenclature: modern HGVS numbering counts the initiator methionine, so legacy A4V is p.A5V and legacy D90A is p.D91A. The two systems can cause ambiguity in the literature. [OpieMartin2022]
- In a 1997 US registry, SOD1 mutations were found in 68 of 290 screened dominantly inherited ALS families. A4V made up 50% of the mutations found and correlated with shorter survival, whereas G37R, G41D and G93C predicted longer survival. Mean onset was 46.9 years in SOD1 families and 50.5 years in non-SOD1 families. [Cudkowicz1997]
- North American natural history (175 people with SOD1-ALS at 15 centres, cared for after 2000): mean onset 49.7 years and overall median survival 2.7 years. Median survival was 1.2 years with A4V and 6.8 years with other variants (p<0.0001), and A4V was described as "aggressive, but relatively homogeneous". [Bali2017]
- International SOD1-ALS dataset (onset data for 1122 people, duration data for 883) compared with people with ALS and no recorded SOD1 variant: mean onset 48.9 vs 61.1 years, median disease duration 27.7 vs 35.1 months, and limb onset 74% vs 63%. Without p.A5V carriers, median duration was 45 months. Of the variants with at least 10 carriers, 16 were associated with age of onset and 9 with duration. The authors conclude that onset and survival are "decoupled" in SOD1-ALS. [OpieMartin2022]
- Homozygous D90A was described in 14 people from four Swedish and Finnish families and in four apparently sporadic cases. Erythrocyte CuZn-SOD activity was "essentially normal", which pointed to gain of function rather than loss. [Andersen1995]
- Recessive D90A phenotype in 36 people: onset was always with leg weakness, spreading slowly to the arms and then the bulbar muscles. Onset age ranged from 20 to 94 (mean 44), and mean survival was 13 years in the 11 who had died, a figure the authors call "probably biased" low. [Andersen1996]
- A 2014 review describes A4V as typically leading to death within a year of symptom onset. With homozygous D90A, respiratory failure typically comes only after 10 years of illness. [Renton2014]
- Toxic gain of function, mouse evidence: mice expressing human G93A SOD1, a change with little effect on enzyme activity, developed motor neuron loss and paralysis and died by 5-6 months. [Gurney1994]
- SOD1 knockout mice develop normally with no overt motor deficits by 6 months, but they are vulnerable to motor neuron loss after axonal injury. Losing the enzyme alone therefore does not reproduce ALS. [Reaume1996]
- Removing or adding wild-type SOD1 did not change disease caused by mutant SOD1 in mice, and SOD1-containing aggregates were common to different mutants. The authors argued that toxicity comes from a property of the mutant protein, not from too little dismutase. [Bruijn1998]
- Misfolding: five ALS-linked SOD1 mutants (A4V, C6F, D90A, G93A, G93C) all showed marked destabilisation of the metal-free (apo) state. The greater the loss of stability, the shorter the mean survival of people carrying that mutation. [Lindberg2002]
- Oxidised wild-type and mutant SOD1 both dissociate from dimers into monomers before aggregating, which suggests a common aggregation-prone monomeric intermediate. [Rakhit2004]
- Pathology: all sporadic ALS, ALS-with-dementia and SOD1-negative familial ALS cases had TDP-43-positive inclusions. None of 15 SOD1-mutation cases did, although they had ubiquitin-positive inclusions. The authors suggest SOD1-ALS "may not be the familial counterpart of sporadic ALS". [Mackenzie2007]
- Tofersen, an intrathecal SOD1 antisense oligonucleotide, lowered CSF SOD1 and plasma neurofilament light over 28 weeks in the phase 3 VALOR trial but did not meet its primary clinical end point (ALSFRS-R difference 1.2 points, P=0.97). Pass E covers the drug. [Miller2022]

### Other genes
- TARDBP (TDP-43): mutations were found in a conserved region in sporadic and familial ALS, and M337V segregated with disease in a kindred linked to 1p36. [Sreedharan2008] Variants cluster in the C-terminus. A382T is frequent in Sardinia because of a founder effect. [Renton2014] The 2023 consensus puts TARDBP frequency at about 1%. [Roggenbuck2023]
- FUS: 13 mutations on chromosome 16 were specific to familial ALS, and mutant FUS accumulated in the neuronal cytoplasm. [Kwiatkowski2009] FUS cases show FUS-positive cytoplasmic inclusions without TDP-43-positive aggregates, though "some debate remains". [Renton2014]
- FUS in early-onset ALS: among 14 people with sporadic ALS beginning before age 35, 6 (43%) carried a FUS mutation. Parents were tested in 5 of those families, and in all 5 the mutation had arisen de novo. [Hubers2015]
- FUS P525L: an 11-year-old girl with sporadic juvenile ALS (onset before 25) and a de novo P525L variant needed tracheostomy 14 months after onset. The authors link P525L consistently with juvenile onset, a severe course and frequent de novo occurrence. [Conte2012]
- FUS genotype-phenotype meta-analysis: median onset 39 years (range 11-80), with 90% of mutations in the C-terminal domain. P525L and truncating/frameshift variants most often caused juvenile onset and rapid progression, often without a family history, whereas R521 variants were linked to late onset and a pure spinal phenotype. Malignancy was found in 1 of 40 patients. [Naumann2019]
- TBK1: an exome study of 2869 people with ALS and 6405 controls identified TBK1 as an ALS gene. [Cirulli2015] Loss-of-function variants were enriched in 13 familial ALS pedigrees, and haploinsufficiency was concluded to cause ALS and FTD. [Freischmidt2015] TBK1 variants occur in about 1% of familial and about 1% of sporadic ALS, with TDP-43-positive inclusions; extrapyramidal, ataxic and psychiatric features have been reported. [Chia2018]
- NEK1: exome analysis of 1,022 index familial ALS cases and 7,315 controls tied loss-of-function NEK1 variants to familial ALS risk, and p.Arg261His was found in an isolated Dutch community. NEK1 risk variants appeared in nearly 3% of ALS cases. [Kenna2016] A later review estimates up to 2%, with typical ALS and no dementia. [Chia2018] It is classed as a susceptibility gene. [ClinGen2026]
- OPTN: first described as a recessive cause of ALS in Japanese families (homozygous exon 5 deletion, homozygous Q398X), alongside a heterozygous E478G variant. OPTN is also a glaucoma gene. [Maruyama2010] Heterozygous OPTN variants rarely cause familial ALS in people of European ancestry. [Renton2014]
- VCP: R191Q was found in an Italian autosomal dominant family, and VCP was estimated to explain about 1-2% of familial ALS. VCP mutations were already known to cause inclusion body myopathy with Paget disease and FTD (IBMPFD). [Johnson2010]
- UBQLN2: mutations cause dominantly inherited X-linked juvenile and adult-onset ALS and ALS/dementia. [Deng2011] They were first found in pedigrees with no male-to-male transmission and are not a common cause of familial ALS. [Renton2014]
- KIF5A: found by a GWAS of 20,806 cases and 59,804 controls and by a rare-variant burden analysis in 1,138 familial index cases. ALS variants cluster in the C-terminal cargo-binding tail, and carriers of loss-of-function variants survived longer than in typical ALS. N-terminal motor-domain variants cause hereditary spastic paraplegia (SPG10) and CMT2 instead. [Nicolas2018]
- ANXA11: among 751 familial ALS exomes, six mutations were found in 13 people. p.D40G was absent from 70,000 control exomes, and all its carriers shared a founder haplotype. [Smith2017]
- PFN1: four mutations were found in 7 of 274 familial ALS cases, and mutant profilin 1 formed aggregates that often contained TDP-43. [Wu2012] PFN1 is not a prominent cause of ALS in larger screens. [Renton2014]
- CHCHD10: S59L was found in a family with motor neuron disease, FTD-like cognitive decline, cerebellar ataxia and mitochondrial myopathy, and again in 1 of 21 FTD-ALS families. [Bannwarth2014] CHCHD10 appears to be a rare cause of ALS that may be more frequent in FTD. [Chia2018]
- ATXN2: intermediate polyglutamine repeats (27-33 glutamines) were found in 4.7% of 915 people with ALS and were associated with ALS (OR 2.8, 95% CI 1.54-5.12). No control had more than 31, while 10 people with ALS had 32 or 33. Long expansions cause spinocerebellar ataxia type 2 instead. [Elden2010] The 2022 Seminar lists ATXN2 as a risk gene rather than a disease-causing one. [Feldman2022]

### Inheritance, penetrance, oligogenic and de novo cases
- ALS variants can be passed on as autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive or X-linked traits, but "the overwhelming majority are dominant". A person with a genetic form may have no recognised family history. [Roggenbuck2023]
- For C9orf72, "each child of an individual with C9orf72-FTD/ALS has a 50% chance of inheriting" the expansion. Almost all people with C9orf72 disease inherited it from a parent, who may be unaffected because penetrance is age-dependent and reduced. [GossyeGeneReviews]
- A child's lifetime risk of disease is the 50% chance of inheriting the variant multiplied by the variant's penetrance. With the C9orf72 figures above, that comes to about 29-33% for ALS or dementia by age 80 using the population estimate, or close to 50% using the family-based estimates. This is arithmetic from cited figures, not a published risk. [GossyeGeneReviews; Gao2025; Majounie2012]
- Penetrance is high for some SOD1 variants (the consensus cites A5V) and some FUS variants, and lower for others including C9orf72. The consensus asks counselling to convey "the variable and uncertain penetrance of most ALS variants". [Roggenbuck2023]
- Oligogenic evidence: in a Dutch series, variants were found in 48% of familial ALS families, 8% of people with sporadic ALS and 0.5% of controls. Five familial families carried several variants, more than chance would predict (P = 1.57 × 10^-7). [vanBlitterswijk2012]
- In a UK clinic series, 13% of people with ALS carried more than one variant (pathogenic or VUS), and those with two variants had earlier onset (median 56 vs 60 years). [Shepheard2021]
- Controversy, oligogenicity: a 2014 review argues that finding two "mutations" in someone from an outbred population more often means one variant is not pathogenic. It notes that many Dutch double-variant cases involved a possibly benign ANG polymorphism. [Renton2014; vanBlitterswijk2012]
- Controversy, de novo variants: an analysis of 173 parent-child trios found the de novo rate in ALS no higher than in the general population (P = 0.40), and concluded de novo mutations "in general do not impose a major burden on ALS risk". [vanDoormaal2017] De novo FUS variants are nevertheless the commonest finding in early-onset sporadic ALS. [Hubers2015; Conte2012]

### Genetic testing: the 2023 consensus
- The 2023 consensus was built from a rapid review (263 articles included), GRADE and EGAPP evidence grading, and a three-round modified Delphi process. It produced 35 recommendations: 7 on clinical testing, 19 on genetic counselling and 9 on laboratory methods. [Roggenbuck2023]
- Earlier practice: only 10-50% of clinicians offered testing to people with apparently sporadic ALS. US care guidelines (reaffirmed February 2023) did not address genetic testing, and European guidelines said testing should be offered only for familial ALS or the SOD1 D90A phenotype. [Roggenbuck2023]
- Recommendation 1: "All persons with ALS should be offered genetic testing" (GRADE A, strong). [Roggenbuck2023]
- Recommendations 2-5: the panel should include C9orf72, SOD1, FUS and TARDBP. A first-round statement making C9orf72 the first test only for people of European descent did not reach consensus, and a revised statement covering everyone with ALS reached 100% agreement. [Roggenbuck2023]
- Recommendations 6 and 34: additional genes should be those classed as definitively associated with ALS by ClinGen. Reports should clearly mark genes classed as "Limited" or "Refuted", or not curated. Recommendation 33 favours simultaneous testing (panel, exome or genome) over gene-by-gene sequencing. [Roggenbuck2023]
- Recommendation 7: when the FDA approves a therapy targeting a gene, all persons with ALS should be offered testing for that gene. [Roggenbuck2023]
- Counselling (recommendations 8-26): counselling should be offered to everyone with ALS whatever their family history, should come before the test, and should include at least a three-generation pedigree. The pedigree should record ALS, PLS, PMA, FTD, other dementias, movement disorders and psychiatric disease. Counselling should cover personalised risk, genetic heterogeneity, inheritance, penetrance, recurrence risk in relatives, and the personal, psychological and economic effects, including genetic privacy and discrimination. [Roggenbuck2023]
- Result categories: people should be told results may be "positive, negative, or uncertain". A pathogenic or likely pathogenic result usually does not predict the disease course. A negative result "does not exclude a genetic form of ALS". A VUS "may or may not be contributing". Interpretations can change over time, so periodic reevaluation may be appropriate. [Roggenbuck2023]
- After a pathogenic or likely pathogenic result, relatives' risks should be discussed. Adult relatives are candidates for presymptomatic testing, "a personal choice" made with genetic counselling. People should also be told about gene-targeted trials, observational studies and approved therapies. [Roggenbuck2023]
- The consensus says counselling is ideally given by a board-certified genetic counsellor, but neurologists and nurse practitioners may provide it when no counsellor is available. [Roggenbuck2023]

### Presymptomatic testing, reproduction and discrimination
- The Pre-fALS experience (8 years; 317 counselling sessions for 161 first-degree relatives, plus 63 people with familial ALS) produced updated presymptomatic counselling recommendations. Earlier guidelines had drawn partly on Huntington disease experience. [Benatar2016]
- Those recommendations set a minimum age of 18 for presymptomatic testing while no effective preventive therapy exists, and require "multiple (at least 2) counseling sessions". Their principles are voluntary and informed consent, psychosocial readiness, and predecision, pretest and posttest counselling. [Benatar2016]
- Uptake in Pre-fALS: 145 participants in the disclosure arm completed pretest counselling. Of 11 people who sought predecision counselling, "over half" chose the arm in which results are not disclosed. These figures come from a research study that offered a non-disclosure option, not from routine clinical uptake. [Benatar2016]
- Reproductive options as published: at-risk people may "pursue preimplantation genetic testing to avoid passing the mutation to the next generation". Prenatal testing and PGT are possible once a family's C9orf72 expansion is known. [Benatar2016; GossyeGeneReviews]
- US law, as summarised in Benatar2016: GINA bars health insurers and employers with more than 50 employees from using genetic information. No federal law covers life, disability or long-term-care insurance. [Benatar2016]

### Israel: published findings
- Ashkenazi Jews (AJ): the C9orf72 expansion was found in 80% of familial and 11% of sporadic ALS, 12.9% of all AJ-ALS, against 0.3% of AJ controls (OR 44.3). North African Jews (NAJ): 10% of familial and 9% of sporadic ALS, 9.1% of all NAJ-ALS, against 1% of controls (OR 9.9). All patients shared one risk haplotype. [Goldstein2018]
- In an Israeli neurogenetics clinic (140 people with ALS, 2018-2023), the C9orf72 expansion was found in 21% of Ashkenazi Jewish patients and 5.7% of non-Ashkenazi patients. Gene panels found disease-causing variants in 14% of non-Ashkenazi and 5.7% of Ashkenazi patients. Panel testing was positive in 12% of early-onset patients (onset before 50) against 2.9% for C9orf72. [Barel2024]
- Barel2024 proposes C9orf72 as the first test for Ashkenazi Jewish patients and a gene panel first for non-Ashkenazi and early-onset patients. This is a single-centre proposal, not a national guideline. [Barel2024]
- In 374 Israeli people with sporadic ALS, those of North African Jewish origin were younger at onset and had shorter disease duration than other ethnic groups. The authors suggest a genetic modifier but did not identify one. [Drory2007]
- No published SOD1, FUS or TARDBP founder variant specific to Israeli or Jewish populations was found in this pass.

## Step sequence
1. A normal C9orf72 gene on chromosome 9p21 carries a short GGGGCC repeat in intron 1, from 2 to about 20 units in most people. [DeJesusHernandez2011; Renton2011]
2. In an expansion carrier the repeat has grown to hundreds or thousands of units (about 700-1600 in the first patients measured). It is inherited on a shared founder haplotype, and its size varies between tissues. [DeJesusHernandez2011; Majounie2012; Roggenbuck2023]
3. Route 1, less protein: the expanded allele produces less of one C9orf72 transcript, so less C9orf72 protein is made (haploinsufficiency). [DeJesusHernandez2011; Balendra2018]
4. Route 2, RNA foci: repeat RNA from both strands (GGGGCC sense, GGCCCC antisense) gathers into nuclear foci, a toxic gain of function by RNA. [DeJesusHernandez2011; Zu2013; Balendra2018]
5. Route 3, dipeptide repeats: repeat RNA is translated without a start codon (RAN translation) into poly-GA, poly-GP and poly-GR from the sense strand and poly-PR, poly-PA and poly-GP from the antisense strand. These aggregate in neurons, with poly-GA most abundant. [Mori2013; Zu2013]
6. The three routes converge on TDP-43 pathology, motor neuron loss and frontotemporal involvement, which is why C9orf72 carriers can develop ALS, FTD or both. [DeJesusHernandez2011; Neumann2006; Byrne2012]
7. SOD1 normally works as a copper-zinc homodimer that converts superoxide into oxygen and hydrogen peroxide; the gene sits on chromosome 21q. [Rosen1993]
8. An ALS-linked SOD1 variant destabilises the immature, metal-free protein, and the dimer comes apart into misfolding-prone monomers. [Lindberg2002; Rakhit2004]
9. Misfolded SOD1 aggregates and becomes toxic through a gain of function. Enzyme activity is not the problem: knockout mice do not get ALS, and changing the amount of normal SOD1 does not alter disease in mutant mice. [Gurney1994; Reaume1996; Bruijn1998; Andersen1995]
10. SOD1-ALS neurons contain ubiquitinated inclusions without TDP-43, a pathway distinct from most ALS. That separate pathway is why SOD1 itself is a target for gene silencing. [Mackenzie2007; Renton2014; Miller2022]

### Notes on verification
- Every PMID above was fetched from the PubMed esummary API in this pass; first author, title, journal, year, volume, issue and pages match those records. Every DOI was resolved on the Crossref API, and its title matched the PubMed title (allowing for markup and capitalisation). GeneReviews has no DOI. Abstracts were read from Europe PMC (resultType=core). Full texts were read from Europe PMC (Roggenbuck2023, Murphy2017, OpieMartin2022, Iacoangeli2019, Shepheard2021) or from PMC via NCBI efetch (DeJesusHernandez2011, Renton2011, Feldman2022, Chia2018, Renton2014, Elden2010).
- The Roggenbuck2023 PMID is 37691292. Any earlier draft or seed that cites it under another PMID (for example 37736002) is wrong. The shared key Roggenbuck2023 matches this paper.
- Seeds corrected: the dossier seed "over 200 SOD1 variants" was not found in any source read. The 2022 SOD1 study says "over 180", and older papers say "more than 100". The seed "C9orf72 30-40% of familial and 5-10% of sporadic ALS in European-ancestry populations" is consistent with Zou2017 (33.7%/5.1%), Majounie2012 (39.3%/7.0%) and Renton2014 (~40%/~7%). The seed "SOD1 12-20% of familial" fits Renton2014 (~12%), Zou2017 (14.8% Europe; 30.0% Asia) and Gurney1994's "about 20 percent". The seed "C9orf72 penetrance near 50% by about 58 and near complete by 80" is confirmed from Majounie2012, but this pass found a population-based estimate (Gao2025, about 58-66% by 80) that disagrees and should appear as a controversy. The seed "heritability 40-60%" is confirmed (Al-Chalabi2010 61%; Ryan2019 52%).
- Testing-guidance seed: the 2023 consensus is a US expert panel (participants from across the United States), not an international panel, so "international consensus" in the dossier should be corrected. It recommends offering testing to all people with ALS, with a minimum gene set of C9orf72, SOD1, FUS and TARDBP. For presymptomatic testing it points to separately published recommendations (Benatar2016). Benatar2016 says earlier guidelines drew partly on Huntington disease experience; it does not describe its own protocol as Huntington-style.
- The ClinGen ALS GCEP curates ATXN2 only for spinocerebellar ataxia type 2, not ALS. The ATXN2-ALS risk association rests on association studies (Elden2010), and the 2022 Seminar lists ATXN2 as a risk gene (Feldman2022). Likewise, VCP and MATR3 are Definitive for IBMPFD and distal myopathy respectively, not for an "ALS" label.
- Variant nomenclature: Feldman2022 and Roggenbuck2023 use "A5V" for what Cudkowicz1997, Bali2017 and Renton2014 call "A4V". This is a numbering difference (initiator methionine), not a different variant (OpieMartin2022).
- Mok2012's PubMed title begins "Chromosome 9 ALS and FTD locus..." while Crossref has "The chromosome 9...". It was not used as a source here because Majounie2012 covers the founder finding.
- Not used because only a title or no abstract was available: Byrne et al. 2011 "Proposed criteria for familial ALS" (PMID 21208036), the de Boer 2022 letter on intermediate C9orf72 alleles (PMID 36131298), and the Brown & Al-Chalabi 2017 NEJM review (no abstract; full text not accessible). van Es 2017 and Hardiman 2017 abstracts held no genetics figures, so those shared keys are not cited here.
