# A. Epidemiology, phenotypes, course & history
Scope: how common ALS is and who it affects (incidence, prevalence, lifetime risk, sex and age patterns, trends and projections), the clinical phenotypes and how their survival differs, survival and prognostic factors including the ENCALS model, the ALSFRS-R and how it changes over time, King's and MiToS staging, diagnostic delay, environmental and lifestyle risk factors, and the history of the disease up to recent regulatory decisions. All journal records were checked against Europe PMC and PubMed (one batched PubMed esummary call; see Notes). Most papers were read as abstracts. Full texts were read for Roche 2012, Arthur 2016, GBD 2016, Bedlack 2016, Boddy 2025, Fang 2017, Al-Chalabi 2021, Brooks 2022 and Julian 2021 (Europe PMC XML), and for Feldman 2022, Ryan 2019 and Kumar 2011 (PMC web pages). Not accessible: the full texts of Chiò 2011 (phenotype paper; JNNP and HAL returned 403 or a bot wall), Chiò 2015 (MiToS original), Balendra 2014, Kimura 2006, Mackay 2019 (no Europe PMC full text), Rowland 2001 and Cedarbaum 1999. So the phenotype-specific median survivals in Chiò 2011, and the exact MiToS and Balendra item thresholds, could not be read in the primary papers; where secondary descriptions are used, this is marked. FDA approval dates come from openFDA Drugs@FDA records, and the Relyvrio withdrawal from the FDA Federal Register notice. Non-journal web sources (Baseball Hall of Fame, hawking.org.uk, CBS/AP, Cornell LII eCFR) are marked as such.

## Facts

### Incidence by region
- Europe (EURALS pooled registers in Ireland, UK and Italy, 1998-1999, adults aged 18 or over, reference population almost 24 million, 1028 incident cases): crude incidence was 2.16 per 100,000 person-years (95% CI 2.0-2.3), similar across registries [Logroscino2010].
- In the same EURALS data, incidence was 3.0 per 100,000 person-years in men and 2.4 in women, and occurrence fell quickly after age 80 [Logroscino2010].
- Europe (systematic review of population-based studies, 1995-2011): median incidence was 2.08 per 100,000 population (IQR 1.47-2.43), about 15,355 cases a year using 2010 populations [Chio2013].
- Worldwide meta-analysis (44 population-based studies, 45 areas, 13,146 cases, 825 million person-years, to June 2015): pooled crude incidence was 1.75 per 100,000 person-years (1.55-1.96), or 1.68 (1.50-1.85) after standardising to the 2010 US population [Marin2017].
- In the same meta-analysis, standardised incidence was 1.89 (1.46-2.32) in North Europe against 0.83 (0.42-1.24) in East Asia (China, Japan) and 0.73 (0.58-0.89) in South Asia (Iran). Europe, North America and New Zealand were homogeneous at a pooled 1.81 (1.66-1.97) [Marin2017].
- Worldwide meta-analysis (110 incidence studies to September 2018): overall crude incidence was 1.59 per 100,000 person-years (95% CI 1.39-1.81). Incidence rose with age up to 70-79 years [Xu2020].
- United States (National ALS Registry, which combines administrative databases with voluntary self-enrolment): age-adjusted incidence was 1.7 (2014), 1.5 (2015) and 1.5 (2016) per 100,000 population (5695, 6045 and 4861 cases). ALS was more common in white people, men and people aged 60-79 [Mehta2022inc].
- UK (General Practice Research Database, 1990-2005, 830 new cases of motor neuron disease): age-standardised incidence was 2.6 per 100,000 person-years in women and 3.9 in men, peaking at 75-79 years in both sexes [Alonso2009].
- Latin America, Uruguay (whole country, about 3.24 million people, 2002-2003, probable or definite El Escorial ALS): mean annual incidence was 1.37 per 100,000 (1.95 in men, 0.84 in women), or 1.42 (1.13-1.72) by capture-recapture. Incidence peaked at 65-74 in men and 55-64 in women [Vazquez2008].
- Global modelling (GBD 2016, all motor neuron diseases including ALS, SMA, hereditary spastic paraplegia, PLS, PMA and pseudobulbar palsy): all-age incidence was 0.78 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI 0.71-0.86), about 57,452 incident cases in 2016 [GBD2016MND2018].
- Africa: the only African study read in this pass (TROPALS) was a hospital-based cohort (185 people with ALS from nine centres in eight countries, 2005-2017), so it gives no incidence. No population-based African incidence study was read [Luna2019].

### Prevalence and burden
- United States 2016 (National ALS Registry): 16,424 adults met the Registry definition, an age-adjusted prevalence of 5.2 per 100,000. ALS was more common in white people, men and people aged 60-69, and men had higher prevalence than women across all data sources [Mehta2022].
- Europe (systematic review, 1995-2011): median prevalence was 5.40 per 100,000 (IQR 4.06-7.89), about 39,863 people [Chio2013].
- Worldwide (58 prevalence studies): crude prevalence was 4.42 per 100,000 (95% CI 3.92-4.96) [Xu2020].
- Uruguay: crude prevalence was 1.9 per 100,000 on 31 December 2002 [Vazquez2008].
- GBD 2016: 330,918 people (95% UI 299,522-367,254) had a motor neuron disease in 2016, an all-age prevalence of 4.5 per 100,000. There were 34,325 deaths and 926,090 DALYs, 855,924 of them years of life lost [GBD2016MND2018].
- GBD 2016 age-standardised prevalence was highest in high-income North America (16.8 per 100,000), Australasia (14.7) and western Europe (12.9). Prevalence and incidence were lower than expected from the Socio-demographic Index in high-income Asia Pacific [GBD2016MND2018].
- A high-SDI (Socio-demographic Index) clinic paper puts point prevalence at about 5 per 100,000 despite a lifetime risk of about 1 in 300, because survival is short [Fang2017].

### Lifetime risk, sex and age
- UK (GPRD, adjusted for competing causes of death): lifetime risk of motor neuron disease was 1 in 350 men (2.9 per 1000) and 1 in 472 women (2.1 per 1000). The rate in men was 54% higher than in women (95% CI 33-77%) [Alonso2009].
- Ireland (population register, mortality-adjusted current lifetime risk): 2.9 per 1000 men (1 in 347) and 2.3 per 1000 women (1 in 436). Mean age at onset was 63.9 years (SD 11.6) in the 1995-2017 cohort [Ryan2019].
- In the same Irish register, lifetime risk in first-degree relatives of people with ALS was 1.4%, against 0.3% in the general population. Mean heritability was 52.3% (95% CI 42.9-61.7%) overall and 36.9% in people with no known genetic risk [Ryan2019].
- Lancet seminar summary: lifetime risk is about 1 in 350, and the overall standardised male-to-female ratio is 1.35 [Feldman2022].
- South-east England population register: the proportion of women was 30.11% under age 51 and 43.66% at older ages. The adjusted male-to-female ratio fell from 2.5 to 1.4 (prevalence data). The authors suggest clinic over-representation of younger people may explain why reported ratios vary from 1 to 3 [Manjaly2010].
- In EURALS, spinal-onset ALS was more common in men than women, especially at ages 70-80. The authors propose this explains the higher incidence in men [Logroscino2010].
- London tertiary clinic (1459 people, 1993-2007): the male-to-female ratio was 1.9 in limb onset and 0.9 in bulbar onset [Roche2012].
- Age at onset differs by setting. Mean 57.4 years in a London tertiary clinic [Roche2012], mean 58.7 in Uruguay [Vazquez2008], mean 63.9 in the Irish population register [Ryan2019], and median 53.0 (IQR 44.5-64.0) in the African TROPALS cohort, where the male-to-female ratio was 2.9 [Luna2019].
- A comparison cited in the 2022 seminar found people in Germany had an older onset age (66.6 years), more bulbar onset (35.9%) and a male-to-female ratio of 1.33. In China the figures were 53.2 years, 22.8% bulbar and 1.51 [Feldman2022].
- Familial versus sporadic age at onset: no verified mean ages for familial against sporadic ALS were found in the sources read. Related figures: in Irish parent-child pairs, affected offspring had onset at a mean 52.0 years and their parents at 69.6 [Ryan2019]. In Ireland, people with a C9orf72 expansion had onset at a mean 56.3 years against 61.3 without it [Byrne2012].
- Proportion familial: a meta-analysis found 5.1% (95% CI 4.1-6.1%) in prospective population registers and 4.6% across all studies, "not 10% as is often stated" [Byrne2011]. Other sources give about 10% [Rosen1993] or 10-15% [Feldman2022] (see Disagreements).

### Trends and projections
- Projection (incidence applied to 2015 and 2040 populations): world ALS cases rise from 222,801 in 2015 to 376,674 in 2040 (+69%), mostly because populations age. The largest increases are in Africa (+116%), Asia (+81%) and South America (+73%). The authors call this likely an underestimate [Arthur2016].
- In the same projection, the 10 regions with incidence data rise from 80,162 to 105,693 cases (+31%), while their population over age 20 grows only 7.7%. Developing countries in the set rise 50% and developed ones 24% [Arthur2016].
- Trend evidence differs by source. One worldwide meta-analysis reports incidence rising year by year since 1957, with the trend weaker after age standardisation [Xu2020]. The UK GPRD found no rise in incidence over 1990-2005 [Alonso2009]. GBD 2016 estimated a 4.5% rise in global age-standardised prevalence between 1990 and 2016 (3.4-5.7%) [GBD2016MND2018].

### Phenotypes and onset site
- London tertiary clinic: 25.4% bulbar onset, 74.6% limb onset, none diaphragmatic onset [Roche2012].
- French incident cohort (476 people diagnosed in 2012 at ALS centres): bulbar 33.0%, spinal lumbar 28.2%, spinal cervical 23.1%, flail leg 4.4%, ALS/FTD 4.2%, possible flail arm 4.0%, respiratory 2.1%, dropped-head 1.0% [Hamidou2017].
- Bulbar-onset proportion elsewhere: 31% in the Irish register cohort with ALSFRS-R data [Rooney2017], 35.3% in an Irish population-based cognitive cohort [Elamin2011], and 22.7% in TROPALS (Africa) [Luna2019]. The 2022 seminar gives bulbar and spinal onset "each constituting about a quarter to a third of cases" [Feldman2022].
- Respiratory onset: 2.7% of people seen at a Canadian tertiary clinic (21 people). Only 14% presented acutely needing emergency intubation. Mean survival from onset to death or permanent ventilation was 27.0 months (SD 14.9), not significantly different from bulbar onset [Shoesmith2007].
- Piedmont and Valle d'Aosta register, Italy (1995-2004, phenotype assigned in 1332 of 1351 incident cases): classic and bulbar phenotypes had similar incidence. Men predominated in respiratory, flail arm, classic and pure lower motor neuron phenotypes. Onset age was lower in pyramidal, PLMN (pure lower motor neuron) and PUMN (pure upper motor neuron) phenotypes and higher in bulbar [Chio2011].
- In the same register, outcomes were best in PUMN, pyramidal, PLMN and flail arm phenotypes and worst in respiratory and bulbar phenotypes. Phenotype-specific medians are in the full text, which was not accessible [Chio2011].
- London clinic (1188 people): flail arm made up 11% and flail leg 6%. Median survival from onset was 35 months for limb onset, 27 for bulbar onset, 61 for flail arm and 69 for flail leg [Wijesekera2009].
- In the same London cohort, 5-year survival was 8.8% for bulbar onset, 20% for limb onset, 52% for flail arm and 64% for flail leg. The male-to-female ratio was 4:1 in flail arm against 2:1 in other limb onset. In Melbourne (432 people) the medians were 31 (limb), 27 (bulbar), 66 (flail arm) and 71 months (flail leg) [Wijesekera2009].
- Progressive muscular atrophy (PMA): in London, PMA not classed as flail arm or flail leg made up 4% and had a prognosis similar to typical limb-onset ALS [Wijesekera2009].
- In a New York centre (91 people with PMA, 871 with ALS), people with PMA were more often male and older, and lived longer. Upper motor neuron signs appeared in 22% within 61 months of diagnosis, and the authors conclude PMA should be considered a form of ALS [Kim2009].
- Primary lateral sclerosis (PLS): in 29 people with only upper motor neuron signs at first evaluation, 13 later developed lower motor neuron signs (by EMG at 3.17 years or examination at 3.67 years). Clinically pure PLS can be defined by isolated upper motor neuron signs 4 years after onset, and survival is longer than in ALS [Gordon2006].
- ALS with frontotemporal dementia (FTD): about 15% of people with ALS meet FTD criteria, and cognitive or behavioural changes occur in 35-50% [Feldman2022]. Other estimates: FTD in about 14% of incident cases (Ireland) [Byrne2012], and neuropsychological deficits in over 50% (ALS-FTSD consensus) [Strong2017]. ALS shares pathobiological features with FTD, and many people show features of both [vanEs2017].
- Progressive bulbar palsy as a separate phenotype: no source read in this pass gives its frequency or survival separately from bulbar-onset ALS. That data gap remains open.

### Survival and prognosis
- Systematic review: median survival from onset to death ranges from 20 to 48 months, and 10-20% of people with ALS survive more than 10 years. Older age and bulbar onset consistently predict worse outcome. Data on sex, diagnostic delay and El Escorial category conflict [Chio2009].
- Lombardy population register (1998-2002 incident cohort, 483 people, follow-up to 2013): survival from diagnosis was 76.2% at 1 year, 23.4% at 5 years and 11.8% at 10 years. The standardised mortality ratio was 9.4 at 5 years and 5.4 at 10 years [Pupillo2014].
- Lombardy register, later analysis (828 people, incident 1998-2002 and 2008-2012): median survival was 2.2 years (IQR 1.1-4.4). 7% were alive at 10 years and 3% at 15 years. Survival did not differ between the two periods [Pupillo2025].
- London tertiary clinic: median survival from onset was 42.3 months overall (range 4-274), 48.3 for limb onset and 30.8 for bulbar onset [Roche2012]. Lancet seminar: median survival of 2-4 years [Feldman2022].
- African TROPALS cohort: median survival from diagnosis was 14.0 months (95% CI 10.7-17.2). It was 19.0 in Northern Africa, 4.0 in Western and 11.0 in Southern Africa. Location and riluzole use independently affected survival [Luna2019].
- Asia (worldwide meta-analysis): the longest survival times were in Asia, from 3.74 years in South Asia to 9.23 years in West Asia [Xu2020].
- Predictors of longer survival in the Lombardy register: younger age, possible or suspected ALS at diagnosis, spinal onset, and symptoms starting more than 12 months before diagnosis. After age 75, 10-year survival in men with ALS was similar to the general population [Pupillo2014].
- Causes of death (Palermo tertiary centre, 182 people, 2000-2008): respiratory failure, including pneumonia, caused 81.3% of deaths (3.3% were people who requested terminal sedation). Sudden death caused 6.0%, death during sleep 6.6%, and heart-related causes 7.1%. 85.2% died at home [Spataro2010].
- Weight loss (Limoges, 92 people): each 5% loss from usual weight at diagnosis carried a 30% higher adjusted risk of death (RR 1.30, 1.08-1.56). During follow-up, each 5% loss carried 34% higher risk and each unit fall in BMI 24% [Marin2011].
- Forced vital capacity (FVC; Houston, 1034 people): median survival was 2.91 years with baseline FVC below 75% against 4.08 years above 75% [Czaplinski2006].
- FTD and cognition (Irish population study, 139 people): comorbid FTD was associated with shorter survival (HR 2.67, 1.04-6.85). In people without dementia, executive dysfunction was associated with shorter survival (HR 3.44, 1.45-8.18) [Elamin2011].
- C9orf72 (Irish register): the expansion was found in 41% of familial and 5% of apparently sporadic samples. Carriers had more FTD (50% against 12%), shorter age-matched survival (20 against 26 months) and a multivariable hazard ratio of 1.9 [Byrne2012].
- Trial-population predictors (PRO-ACT, over 8600 participants in 16 trials and one observational study): higher baseline uric acid and creatinine predicted slower ALSFRS-R and vital capacity decline and longer survival. Higher BMI predicted longer survival [Atassi2014].

### ENCALS personalised prediction model
- Data came from 14 ALS centres in nine European countries (patients 1992-2016). The composite endpoint was time from symptom onset to non-invasive ventilation for more than 23 h per day, tracheostomy, or death. 16 candidate predictors were tested [Westeneng2018].
- Eight predictors entered the model (univariable HRs): bulbar onset (1.71), age at onset (1.03 per year), definite against probable or possible ALS (1.47), diagnostic delay (0.52), FVC (0.99), progression rate ΔFRS (6.33), FTD (1.34) and C9orf72 repeat expansion (1.45) [Westeneng2018].
- External c statistic was 0.78 (0.77-0.80), and the calibration slope was 1.01 [Westeneng2018].
- Five risk groups had observed median times from onset to the composite endpoint of 16.5 (very short), 25.2 (short), 32.8 (intermediate) and 44.6 months (long) [Westeneng2018]. Predicted medians were 17.7, 25.3, 32.2, 43.7 and 91.0 months, from very short to very long [Feldman2022].

### ALSFRS-R: structure and what it measures
- The ALSFRS-R revised the original ALSFRS, which weighted limb and bulbar function more heavily than respiratory function. It adds assessments of dyspnoea, orthopnoea and need for ventilatory support. It showed internal consistency and construct validity, and scores correlated with quality of life (Sickness Impact Profile) [Cedarbaum1999].
- It has 12 items in four domains (bulbar, fine motor, gross motor, respiratory). Each item is scored 0-4, giving a total from 0 to 48, where 48 is normal function [Boddy2025; Brooks2022].
- The items used in published mappings are 1 speech, 2 salivation, 3 swallowing, 4 handwriting, 5a/5b cutting food (5b with gastrostomy), 6 dressing and hygiene, 8 walking, 10 dyspnoea and 12 respiratory insufficiency [AlChalabi2021]. The gross motor items are 7 turning in bed and adjusting bed clothes, 8 walking and 9 climbing stairs; orthopnoea is the remaining respiratory item [Brooks2022].
- On an item, 3 may mean a minor functional abnormality, while 2 typically means a clear disturbance or need for help. A 1-point drop in some items may be clinically meaningful [Brooks2022].
- Measurement critique (Irish register, 407 incident cases): bulbar and motor subscore slopes fitted prognostic models better than the total slope. Respiratory subscores added no prognostic information after the other two. The authors argue for domain subscores rather than one combined score [Rooney2017].
- The ALSFRS-R is analysed as an interval measure although it is categorical and non-linear. A bifactor Rasch interval transformation has been proposed [Boddy2025].

### ALSFRS-R: rate of decline, ΔFS and cut-offs
- PRO-ACT (pooled phase II/III trials): mean ALSFRS-R decline was 1.02 (±2.3) points per month, and vital capacity fell 2.24% of predicted per month [Atassi2014].
- UK and Australian clinic cohort (ProSec3, 145-455 people with MND, including some with PLS or PMA): monthly decline was -0.58 points (95% CI -0.69 to -0.48) on the summation scale. Mean ALSFRS-R was 30.8 at baseline and 27.7 at 12 months, with fast progressors more likely to drop out [Boddy2025].
- ΔFS (pre-slope) was introduced as a prognostic index by Kimura and colleagues. It uses total ALSFRS-R and symptom duration at diagnosis (82 Japanese patients), and survival differed significantly by ΔFS [Kimura2006].
- The formula: ΔFS = (48 - ALSFRS-R at diagnosis) / months from onset to diagnosis. Fast progressors were defined by ΔFS >1 and slow by <0.5 [Hamidou2017].
- In the French 2012 incident cohort, median ΔFS at diagnosis was 1.0 (IQR 0.5-2.0) [Hamidou2017].
- The three bands, slower (<0.5), intermediate (0.5 to 1.0) and faster (>1.0 points/month), are used as prognostic groups in German network studies [Meyer2026].
- Who first proposed the 0.5/1.0 cut-offs could not be established. Kimura 2006 introduced ΔFS but its abstract gives no bands. Hamidou 2017 and Meyer 2026 use the cut-offs without (in their abstracts) attributing an origin. Labra 2016 derived different data-driven cut-offs (0.47 and 1.11) [Kimura2006; Hamidou2017; Labra2016].
- Sydney cohort (203 people): ΔFS below 0.47 was associated with median survival of 2.4 years, 0.47-1.11 with 1.6 years, and above 1.11 with 0.7 years. ΔFS remained an independent predictor [Labra2016].
- An ALSFRS-R "ratio" within 100 days also correlated with survival (Hannover, 479 people). Survival from onset ranged from 4 months to 11.9 years [Kollewe2008].
- Non-linearity (Paris, 1884 people, 2002-2008): ALSFRS-R and manual muscle testing declined in a curvilinear way. A quadratic fit described the data and a linear fit did not. The early and late phases showed the fastest decline [Gordon2010].
- Plateaus (PRO-ACT): over 6 months, 25% of 3132 participants did not decline. Over 12 months, 16% of 2105 did not, and over 18 months, 7% of 1218. Fewer than 1% ever improved by 4 or more points for at least 12 months [Bedlack2016].
- In the same PRO-ACT data, 14% of 1343 participants had a 180-day interval with a positive ALSFRS-R slope. The authors caution that short-term stability should not be read as a treatment effect [Bedlack2016].
- Clinically meaningful change: 90% of surveyed NEALS clinicians and researchers (65 surveyed) rated a 20% slowing of ALSFRS-R decline as at least somewhat clinically meaningful. 93% rated a 50% slowing as very meaningful [CastrilloViguera2010].
- Patient survey (522 participants): the median minimum important slowing patients expected from a drug was 20% (IQR 10-50%). Patients progressing faster more often chose a major slowing (≥50%) [Meyer2026].
- Minimal important difference (ProSec3, anchor-based on a global rating of change over about 3 months): 3.8 points on the summation scale and 2 on the interval scale. Distribution-based estimates ranged from 2.02 to 5.43 (summation) [Boddy2025].
- MiToS validation: in a trial cohort, the best 6-month cut-offs for predicting death, tracheotomy or >23 h NIV at 12-18 months were loss of at least one MiToS domain, or an ALSFRS-R fall of 6-9 points [Tramacere2015].

### Staging: King's and MiToS
- King's staging was defined in a London tertiary clinic database (1471 people, 1993-2007). Stage 1 is symptom onset (first region involved), 2A diagnosis, 2B second region, 3 third region, 4A need for gastrostomy and 4B need for non-invasive ventilation. Regions are bulbar, upper limb, lower limb and diaphragmatic [Roche2012].
- Standardised medians (proportion of disease course to death) in people who had died: diagnosis 0.35, second region 0.38, third region 0.61, gastrostomy 0.77 and NIV 0.80 [Roche2012].
- Mean times from onset in people who had died (last recorded milestone): diagnosis 13.5 months (11.3-15.7), second region 17.7 (15.5-19.8), third region 23.3 (20.8-25.7), gastrostomy 27.7 (25.1-30.2) and NIV 30.3 (26.4-34.2) [Roche2012].
- Using every milestone instead of the last one: diagnosis 15.2, second region 14.7, third region 22.8, gastrostomy 27.3 and NIV 31.5 months [Roche2012].
- People with bulbar onset needed gastrostomy before NIV (standardised 0.71 against 0.81), and people with limb onset needed NIV first (0.73 against 0.81). The third region was reached earlier with bulbar onset (0.45 against 0.63) [Roche2012].
- Median survival from the third-region milestone was 13 months in both bulbar and limb onset. From gastrostomy it was 9 (bulbar) and 6 months (limb), and from NIV 3 and 8 months [Roche2012].
- Balendra algorithm: King's stage can be estimated from ALSFRS-R by mapping its domains to CNS regions, using gastrostomy or NIV provision as the stage-4 proxy. Agreement with clinical staging was excellent (ICC 0.92, 0.88-0.94), with no systematic bias [Balendra2014].
- The algorithm uses items 1-3 (bulbar), 4 and 5a (upper limb), 8 (lower limb), 5b (gastrostomy) and 10 and 12 (respiratory) [AlChalabi2021]. The exact thresholds are in Balendra 2014, which was not read. Secondary descriptions found in trial documents use a score below 4 on any item for regional involvement, and item 10 = 0 or item 12 ≤3 for stage 4B (unverified against the primary paper).
- MiToS (ALS Milano-Torino Staging) uses four ALSFRS-R domains: walking/self-care, swallowing, communicating and breathing. Stage 0 means functional involvement without loss of independence, stages 1-4 the number of domains with lost independence, and stage 5 death. In the Quality of Care study, 59.3% (70/118) moved to a higher stage within 12 months [Chio2015MiToS].
- MiToS mapping uses items 1 and 4 (communicating), 3 (swallowing), 6 and 8 (movement) and 10 and 12 (breathing) [AlChalabi2021]. A trial statistical analysis plan read in this pass describes walking OR dressing/hygiene, swallowing, speech AND handwriting, and dyspnoea OR respiratory insufficiency. The numeric cut-offs did not survive text extraction and the Chiò 2015 full text was not read, so exact item thresholds are unverified.
- King's and MiToS compared (LiCALS trial): King's stage 3 most often matched MiToS stage 1. The Spearman correlation was 0.54 and weighted kappa 0.21. King's gives more resolution in early and mid disease and MiToS in late disease, and the authors propose using both [Fang2017].

### Diagnostic delay
- US academic centre (304 people): median total time from first symptom to confirmed diagnosis was 11.5 months. It was longer with age over 60, sporadic disease and limb onset. 52% received an alternative diagnosis first, and people saw an average of three physicians [Paganoni2014].
- London tertiary clinic: median diagnostic delay was 11 months in people who had died, 16 in those still alive and 15 in those lost to follow-up [Roche2012].
- Meta-analysis (13 studies, 898 bulbar-onset and 2438 limb-onset patients): bulbar-onset ALS was diagnosed about 4.4 months faster (mean difference -4.42 months, -5.73 to -3.11) [Saeed2026].
- A longer diagnostic delay goes with slower disease. It is a protective predictor in ENCALS (HR 0.52) [Westeneng2018], and delay below 12 months against 18 months or more separated fast from slow progressors in the French cohort [Hamidou2017].

### Risk factors (effect sizes and strength of evidence)
- Smoking (pooled 5 prospective US cohorts, 832 cases among about 1.12 million people): relative risk was 1.44 (1.23-1.68) for former and 1.42 (1.07-1.88) for current smokers against never smokers. Dose-response did not persist after excluding never smokers. Evidence: consistent prospective data, modest effect [Wang2011].
- Military service (Cancer Prevention Study II, about 408,000 men, 1989-1998, 280 ALS deaths): ALS mortality was higher in men who served (RR 1.53, 1.12-2.09), largely regardless of branch or era [Weisskopf2005].
- US regulation since 2008 presumes service connection for ALS in veterans with 90 or more days of continuous active service (38 CFR 3.318; 73 FR 54693, 23 September 2008) [eCFR3318].
- Military service and survival (US National ALS Registry, 8643 people, 2011-2023): veterans had poorer survival after diagnosis (adjusted HR 1.25, 1.15-1.35). 5-year survival was 47.1% against 57.4% [Horton2026].
- Physical activity, positive association (Euro-MOTOR, 1557 cases and 2922 controls in the Netherlands, Ireland and Italy): odds ratios were 1.07 for leisure, 1.06 for occupational and 1.06 for all activity, per unit of metabolic-equivalent score [Visser2018].
- Physical activity, inverse association (European registries, 652 cases and 1166 controls, 2008-2012): overall physical activity was linked to lower odds of ALS (adjusted OR 0.65, 0.48-0.89), and organised sport to 0.49 [Pupillo2014PA].
- Mendelian randomisation: genetic liability to frequent strenuous leisure exercise was causally linked to ALS using a liberal instrument (p=0.01). The authors propose the effect is confined to risk genotypes, especially C9orf72 [Julian2021].
- A meta-analysis of MR analyses gave ORs of 1.22 (moderate-to-vigorous activity), 1.32 (vigorous) and 1.35 (strenuous sport) for ALS [Xu2024].
- Professional football (soccer), Italy (7325 players active 1970-2001, followed to 2006): 8 ALS cases against 1.24 expected (SMR 6.45, 2.78-12.70), with mean onset at 41.6 years. No cases occurred in 1973 basketball players or 1701 road cyclists [Chio2009soccer].
- Scotland (7676 former professional players against 23,028 matched controls): neurodegenerative disease as primary cause of death was 1.7% against 0.5% (subhazard ratio 3.45, 2.11-5.62). The abstract gives no ALS-specific estimate [Mackay2019].
- American football (3439 NFL players with 5 or more seasons, 1959-1988): ALS mortality was raised using multiple-cause-of-death rates (SMR 4.31, 1.73-8.87) [Lehman2012].
- Head injury (New England case-control study plus meta-analysis): the meta-analysis gave OR 1.7 (1.3-2.2) for prior head injury. In the case-control data, more than one head injury gave OR 3.1 (1.2-8.1) [Chen2007].
- Pesticides (meta-analysis of 6 studies in men): pooled OR 1.88 (1.36-2.61). The chemical class was mostly unspecified [Malek2012].
- Occupational lead (meta-analysis of 9 case-control studies): OR 1.81 (1.39-2.36), with an estimated attributable risk of 5% [Wang2014lead].
- GBD 2016 found no association between any of its 84 quantified risk factors and motor neuron disease incidence [GBD2016MND2018].
- Guam: from the mid-1950s, ALS and parkinsonism-dementia complex (PDC) incidence on Guam was much higher than anywhere else. A registry began in 1958 [Plato2002]. ALS incidence then declined steadily over the following 40 years to 1999, which the authors attribute to westernisation rather than genetics [Plato2003].
- First-degree relatives of people with Guam ALS/PDC had a higher risk than the Guamanian population (102 new cases among patients' relatives against 33 among controls' relatives, 1958-1999) [Plato2002].
- Cycad and BMAA hypothesis: macaques fed the cycad amino acid β-N-methylamino-L-alanine (BMAA) developed corticomotoneuronal dysfunction and parkinsonian features [Spencer1987]. Eating flying foxes, which feed on cycad seeds, was proposed as a way Chamorro people received high BMAA doses [CoxSacks2002].
- Against the BMAA hypothesis, one laboratory detected no free BMAA in brains of controls, people with Alzheimer disease or Chamorros with PDC [Montine2005]. Evidence: hypothesis, contested.
- Kii peninsula, Japan: the Koza/Kozagawa/Kushimoto area is described as a high-incidence ALS area with recent incidence reduction and later onset age, and studies of lifestyle change there are ongoing [Kihira2017].

### History and milestones
- 1850: François-Amilcar Aran published the memoir describing and naming "progressive muscular atrophy". Duchenne de Boulogne later claimed priority, but a historian credits Aran with the clinical description and the name ("Aran-Duchenne" disease) [Bonduelle1990].
- 1865-1869: Charcot and Joffroy linked lateral column lesions to progressive paralysis with contractures, and anterior horn lesions to paralysis with atrophy. Charcot offered the term "amyotrophic lateral sclerosis" in 1874, when his lectures were compiled. ALS is still called Charcot's disease in many countries [Kumar2011].
- Charcot's anatomo-clinical method used ALS to bring together primary amyotrophy and primary lateral sclerosis, formerly seen as separate disorders [Goetz2000].
- 1939: Lou Gehrig ended his streak of 2130 consecutive games on 2 May 1939, and died on 2 June 1941 (Baseball Hall of Fame).
- Film analysis found Gehrig functioned normally in January 1938 (film Rawhide), with visible signs only after May 1938. A photograph from October 1939 shows hand atrophy [Lewis2007].
- 1950s: Guam ALS/PDC was recognised as a high-incidence focus, with a registry started in 1958 [Plato2002].
- Stephen Hawking: tests soon after his 21st birthday (1963) showed motor neurone disease, and he was given about two years to live. He had a tracheostomy in 1985 after pneumonia and died at home in Cambridge on 14 March 2018 (hawking.org.uk). His roughly 55-year course is far longer than the medians above, which is consistent with the long-survivor tail [Chio2009; Pupillo2025].
- 1993: SOD1 mutations were identified in familial ALS (11 missense mutations in 13 families). The same paper states that about 10% of cases are inherited [Rosen1993].
- 1994: El Escorial World Federation of Neurology criteria were published [Brooks1994]. A riluzole trial reported 1-year survival of 74% against 58% with placebo [Bensimon1994].
- 2000 and later criteria: revised El Escorial (Airlie House) criteria in 2000 [Brooks2000], ALS-FTSD criteria in 2017 [Strong2017] and Gold Coast criteria proposed in 2020 [Shefner2020].
- 12 December 1995: the FDA approved riluzole (Rilutek, NDA 020599, priority review) (openFDA Drugs@FDA).
- 1999: the ALSFRS-R was published [Cedarbaum1999].
- 2006: TDP-43 was identified as the major disease protein in ubiquitinated inclusions of both FTLD-U and ALS [Neumann2006].
- 2011: the C9orf72 GGGGCC repeat expansion was found. It explained 23.5% of familial ALS in one series [DeJesusHernandez2011], and 46.0% of familial and 21.1% of sporadic ALS in Finland [Renton2011].
- 2014: the Ice Bucket Challenge took place. Reporting in July 2016 put the ALS Association's receipts at more than $100 million, part of which funded Project MinE (CBS/AP).
- 2016: Project MinE investigators reported NEK1 risk variants in nearly 3% of ALS cases, from exome analysis of 1022 familial index cases and replication in sporadic cases [Kenna2016].
- 5 May 2017: edaravone (Radicava, NDA 209176) was approved (openFDA Drugs@FDA).
- 29 September 2022: sodium phenylbutyrate-taurursodiol (Relyvrio, NDA 216660) was approved (openFDA Drugs@FDA).
- 25 April 2023: tofersen (Qalsody, NDA 215887) for SOD1-ALS received accelerated approval based on reduced plasma neurofilament light (openFDA Drugs@FDA; FDA page). Phase 3 trial: [Miller2022].
- Relyvrio: on 30 April 2024 Amylyx reported to FDA that the PHOENIX phase 3 trial did not meet its primary and secondary endpoints. Marketing stopped on 31 October 2024, and FDA formally withdrew approval in a Federal Register notice published 29 August 2025 (FDA FR Doc. 2025-16646).

### Disagreements and controversies (explicit)
- Incidence level. European registers give about 2.1-2.2 per 100,000 person-years [Logroscino2010; Chio2013]. Worldwide meta-analyses give 1.6-1.75 [Marin2017; Xu2020], the US Registry about 1.5-1.7 [Mehta2022inc] and GBD 0.78 [GBD2016MND2018]. The US Registry calls its count conservative [Mehta2022], and GBD is model-based.
- Prevalence level. US 2016 Registry prevalence is 5.2 per 100,000 [Mehta2022], against a GBD age-standardised 16.8 for high-income North America [GBD2016MND2018]. GBD pools all motor neuron diseases and uses a different method, so the two are not directly comparable.
- Ancestry or ascertainment. Lower incidence in East and South Asia [Marin2017] and different onset age and bulbar share in China against Germany [Feldman2022] are attributed variously to ancestry, environment or study design. Marin and colleagues call for an international consortium to test ancestry [Marin2017].
- Proportion familial. About 10% [Rosen1993] or 10-15% [Feldman2022], against 5.1% in prospective population registers [Byrne2011].
- Long survival. 10-20% surviving more than 10 years [Chio2009], against 11.8% at 10 years from diagnosis [Pupillo2014] and 7% at 10 years [Pupillo2025].
- Sex ratio. It depends on age and setting: 2.5 under 51 against 1.4 older [Manjaly2010], 1.35 standardised [Feldman2022], and 2.9 in African hospitals [Luna2019].
- Physical activity. Euro-MOTOR found a positive association [Visser2018], a European case-control study an inverse one [Pupillo2014PA], and MR a positive causal signal [Julian2021; Xu2024]. Positive sport-cohort findings may be sport-specific or linked to head impacts [Chio2009soccer; Chen2007].
- Linear slope or curve. Trials and ΔFS assume linear decline [Atassi2014; Hamidou2017], while Gordon 2010 found curvilinear decline and Bedlack 2016 frequent plateaus [Gordon2010; Bedlack2016].
- BMAA. Supported by animal and ethnographic work [Spencer1987; CoxSacks2002], and not confirmed by brain assays in one laboratory [Montine2005].
- King's against MiToS. The two capture different things, with fair agreement (kappa 0.21) [Fang2017].
- Seed corrections. The dossier seed "13.5, 17.7, 23.3, 27.3 months" mixes two Roche columns. They are means, not medians, in people who had died. The last-milestone values are 13.5, 17.7, 23.3, 27.7 (gastrostomy) and 30.3 (NIV) [Roche2012]. The seed "AMX0035 withdrawn 2024" is more exactly marketing stopped in 2024 and approval formally withdrawn on 29 August 2025.

## Step sequence
A "course over time" animation for a typical limb-onset course. Times are mean months from onset in people who had died in the London cohort, and other cohorts differ [Roche2012]. The ALSFRS-R values are an arithmetic illustration (48 minus rate times months) using the cited rate bands. They are not observed trajectories, and real decline is curvilinear with plateaus [Gordon2010; Bedlack2016].

1. Onset (King's stage 1), month 0. First weakness, wasting, spasticity, dysarthria or dysphagia in one region. ALSFRS-R starts at or near 48, and subscore decline may begin before the reported onset date [Roche2012; Cedarbaum1999; Rooney2017].
2. Seeking a diagnosis, months 0-12. Median time to confirmed diagnosis is about 11.5 months, with about three physicians seen and alternative diagnoses in about half; bulbar onset is diagnosed about 4 months sooner. Illustration at 12 months: about 42 (slow, 0.5/month), 36 (median ΔFS 1.0) or 24 (fast, 2.0/month) [Paganoni2014; Saeed2026; Hamidou2017].
3. Diagnosis (King's 2A), mean 13.5 months, about 35% of the course. ΔFS at diagnosis sorts people into slower (<0.5), intermediate (0.5-1.0) and faster (>1.0 points/month) groups; the French median is 1.0. ENCALS gives a personalised prediction from 8 variables [Roche2012; Hamidou2017; Meyer2026; Westeneng2018].
4. Second region (King's 2B), mean 17.7 months, about 38% of the course. Trial-population decline averages about 1 point per month, so a median progressor is around 30 points here (illustrative). Plateaus of 6 months occur in about a quarter of people [Roche2012; Atassi2014; Bedlack2016].
5. Third region (King's 3), mean 23.3 months, about 61% of the course. This is where King's stage 3 most often matches MiToS stage 1 (first domain of independence lost). Median survival from this point is about 13 months [Roche2012; Fang2017].
6. Gastrostomy (King's 4A), mean 27.7 months, about 77% of the course. It comes earlier, before NIV, with bulbar onset. Weight loss at diagnosis and during the course predicts shorter survival [Roche2012; Marin2011].
7. Non-invasive ventilation (King's 4B), mean 30.3 months, about 80% of the course. In people without severe bulbar dysfunction, NIV improved survival (median benefit 205 days) and quality of life in an RCT [Roche2012; Bourke2006].
8. Late stage and death (MiToS 5). Median survival from onset is about 2-4 years across cohorts (London clinic 42.3 months: 48.3 limb, 30.8 bulbar). About 20-25% are alive at 5 years and about 7-12% at 10 years. Respiratory failure causes about 80% of deaths [Roche2012; Feldman2022; Pupillo2014; Pupillo2025; Spataro2010].
