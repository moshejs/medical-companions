How much ALS runs in families?

Population registriesabout 5%

A meta-analysis of 33 studies put familial ALS at 4.6% (95% CI 3.9–5.5%) of all ALS, and 5.1% (4.1–6.1%) in prospective population-based registries. The authors concluded it is "not 10% as is often stated". Rates were lower in southern Europe. 86

Reviews10–15%

The 2022 Lancet Seminar counts ALS or related syndromes such as FTD among relatives, and says familial ALS may be underreported because of low-penetrance variants, small families and incomplete histories, perhaps "closer to 20% of cases". 180

Controversy Much of the gap is definition: whether relatives with FTD count, and how many generations are asked about. 86,180 The Seminar proposes replacing "familial versus sporadic" with genetically confirmed versus not genetically confirmed ALS: about 70% of familial cases, and about 15% of people with sporadic ALS, carry a pathogenic variant in a known gene. 180 In a UK clinic, 93% of people with a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant had no family history of ALS. 466 Twin data put the heritability of sporadic ALS at 0.61 (95% CI 0.38–0.78), and an Irish parent-offspring study at 52.3%. 8,459

One copy is enough, but it is not a certainty

Most ALS variants are dominant: one changed copy, from one parent, can cause disease. Recessive and X-linked forms exist but are rare, and a person with a genetic form may have no recognised family history. 453 The C9orf72 expansion shows why: a carrier parent may never become ill, because penetrance depends on age and is incomplete. 213 Move the age slider, or press Play to let the family age, and switch between the two kinds of estimate.

The genes side by side

By 2022 more than 40 genes had been reported in ALS. They differ in how common they are, how they are inherited (mostly dominant, rarely recessive) and how often carriers become ill; some, such as ATXN2, raise risk rather than cause disease. 180 In a meta-analysis of 111 studies, variants in the four main genes were found in 47.7% of familial and 5.2% of sporadic ALS, with a different pattern in Asia. 554

Reading a genetic result

The 2023 evidence-based consensus guidelines on ALS genetic testing and counselling came from a US expert panel, not an international one. It drew on a rapid review of 263 articles, GRADE and EGAPP evidence grading and a three-round modified Delphi process, and made 35 recommendations: 7 on clinical testing, 19 on counselling and 9 on laboratory methods. 453 Pick a result category to see how the panel describes it.

Result category

This explains published wording for each category. It does not interpret anyone's report; a genetic counsellor or clinician does that.

Recommendation 1

Offer testing to everyone with ALS

"All persons with ALS should be offered genetic testing" (GRADE A, strong). Before this, only 10–50% of clinicians offered testing in apparently sporadic ALS, US care guidelines did not address it, and European guidelines limited it to familial ALS or the SOD1 D90A phenotype. 453

Recommendations 2–7, 33, 34

Which genes

  • At minimum C9orf72, SOD1, FUS and TARDBP. A first-round statement making C9orf72 the first test only for people of European descent failed; the revised one, covering everyone with ALS, reached 100% agreement.
  • Further genes: those ClinGen rates as definitively associated with ALS, with "Limited", "Refuted" or uncurated genes clearly marked on the report.
  • Simultaneous testing (panel, exome or genome) rather than one gene at a time.
  • When the FDA approves a therapy aimed at a gene, everyone with ALS should be offered testing for that gene. 453
Recommendations 8–26

Counselling comes first

Counselling should be offered to everyone with ALS, whatever the family history, before the test. It includes at least a three-generation family tree recording ALS, PLS, PMA, FTD, other dementias, movement disorders and psychiatric illness, and covers inheritance, penetrance, risk to relatives, and psychological, economic and privacy effects, including discrimination. Ideally a board-certified genetic counsellor gives it; neurologists and nurse practitioners may when none is available. 453

Relatives

Presymptomatic testing

The consensus refers to separately published recommendations from the Pre-fALS study (8 years; 317 counselling sessions for 161 first-degree relatives). They set a minimum age of 18 while no preventive therapy exists, require at least 2 counselling sessions, and rest on voluntary informed consent, psychosocial readiness, and counselling before the decision, before the test and after it. 48,453 Of 11 people who sought counselling before deciding, "over half" chose the study arm in which results are not disclosed; that was a research setting, not routine care. 48

Family planning

Reproductive options

At-risk people may "pursue preimplantation genetic testing" (PGT) to avoid passing the variant on, and prenatal testing and PGT are possible once a family's C9orf72 expansion is known. 48,213 No published series on how often families with ALS use PGT was found for this guide.

United States law

GINA and its gaps

As summarised in 2016, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) bars health insurers and many employers from using genetic information, but no federal law covers life, disability or long-term-care insurance. 48 Laws differ between countries.

In North African Jews it was 10% of familial and 9% of sporadic ALS: 9.1% against 1% (odds ratio 9.9). All patients shared one risk haplotype. 209

One clinic, 2018–2023

Which test first?

In an Israeli neurogenetics clinic (140 people with ALS), the C9orf72 expansion was found in 21% of Ashkenazi Jewish patients and 5.7% of others. Gene panels found disease-causing variants in 14% of non-Ashkenazi and 5.7% of Ashkenazi patients, and in 12% of people with onset before 50, against 2.9% for C9orf72. 41

The authors propose C9orf72 first for Ashkenazi patients and a panel first for others and for early onset. This is a single-centre proposal, not a national guideline; the US consensus asks for the four-gene minimum in everyone. 41,453

Open question

Onset and duration by origin

Among 374 Israelis with sporadic ALS, people of North African Jewish origin were younger at onset and had a shorter illness than other groups. The authors suggest a genetic modifier but did not identify one. 148 No SOD1, FUS or TARDBP founder variant specific to Israeli or Jewish populations was found in the sources read for this guide.

Beyond one gene, one family

Controversy

More than one variant

In a Dutch series, five familial ALS families carried several variants, more often than chance would predict. 505 In a UK clinic, 13% of people carried more than one variant, and those with two had earlier onset (median 56 vs 60 years). 466 A 2014 review argues that two "mutations" in someone from an outbred population more often mean one is not pathogenic. 449

Controversy

New variants in the child

In 173 parent-child trios, the rate of de novo variants in ALS was no higher than in the general population. 509 Yet de novo FUS variants are the commonest finding in early-onset sporadic ALS: 6 of 14 people with onset before 35 carried one, and in all 5 families tested it was new. 238

Modifiers

Genes that change the odds

In UK Biobank, C9orf72 carriers homozygous for the UNC13A rs12608932 C allele had a higher risk (hazard ratio 1.81). 193 A cross-ancestry study found 15 risk loci and quoted a lifetime ALS risk of 1 in 350. 513
