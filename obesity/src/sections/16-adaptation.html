Part 1 · Understanding obesity

Why lost weight comes back

After weight loss the body pushes back: it burns a little less energy than expected and hunger signals rise, and both can last for years. [[Sumithran2011,Fothergill2016]] The Endocrine Society describes obesity as a disorder of the energy homeostasis system in which the defended level of fat mass is "reset" upward. [[Schwartz2017EndoSoc]] Regain is biology at work, not a failure of willpower.

Regain is biologyMost people who lose weight regain some or most of it, and the body's defence of its fat stores is a main reason obesity is treated as a long-term condition. [[Schwartz2017EndoSoc,Rosenbaum2010]] Weight stigma causes physical and psychological harm and poorer care; experts call for a public narrative "coherent with modern scientific knowledge". [[Rubino2020Stigma]]

The size of the slowdown depends on how much weight was lost and whether weight was stable when it was measured; appetite may be the bigger lever. How is body weight regulated? Competing models

Hypothesis

Set point

Active feedback, presumably encoded in the brain, links fat stores to intake and expenditure. It fits much of the biology but struggles to explain the rise in obesity since the 1980s. [[Speakman2011]]

Hypothesis

Settling point

Weight settles where passive feedback from the environment and expenditure balances out. It fits social and environmental effects but struggles with the genetics. Hybrid models such as the dual intervention point model try to bridge the two. [[Speakman2011]]

Hypothesis

Which fits best?

Hall and Guo judged the set-point model most consistent with current data, and note that weight loss is resisted by counterbalancing physiology, so "eat less, move more" misreads the energy-balance equation. [[Hall2017Gastro]]

Hypothesis

Energy balance or carbohydrate-insulin?

The energy balance model puts the brain at the centre, mostly below conscious awareness. [[Hall2022EBM]] The carbohydrate-insulin model proposes that hormonal responses to a high-glycaemic-load diet drive fat storage first. [[Ludwig2021CIM]] In 32 controlled feeding studies, fat loss was 16 g/day greater on lower-fat diets at equal calories. [[Hall2017Gastro]] An active debate.

Slow by nature

Half-time~1 year

Body weight responds slowly to a lasting change in intake, with a half-time of about a year; people with more body fat take longer to reach a new steady state. [[Hall2011Lancet]]

The "3500 kcal per pound" rule32.2 MJ/kg

Roughly matches the energy in lost weight only when starting body fat is above 30 kg; it overestimates the deficit needed in people with less body fat. [[Hall2008]] Hall's simulator is a population model, not a personal prescription. [[Hall2011Lancet]]

Relapse>80%

Rosenbaum and Leibel report relapse to pre-weight-loss body fat in over 80% after otherwise successful weight loss, driven by coordinated metabolic, behavioural, neuroendocrine and autonomic responses. [[Rosenbaum2010]] MacLean describes them as comprehensive, persistent and redundant. [[MacLean2011]]

If eating or weight is distressingIf eating or weight causes distress, or someone restricts, binges or purges, talk to a clinician or an eating-disorder service. The diets described here were research protocols, not advice.

Exam hookHolding a 10% lower weight lowers total energy expenditure by about 6 to 8 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day [[Leibel1995]]; one year after diet-induced loss, leptin, PYY and CCK stay low and ghrelin and hunger stay high [[Sumithran2011]]; appetite feedback is about 100 kcal/day per kg lost. [[Polidori2016]]
In plain wordsAfter weight loss, hunger hormones rise, fullness hormones fall, and the body burns a little less energy. These changes can last for years. That is why keeping weight off is hard, and why long-term care, covered in Diet, activity & behaviour and Living with obesity, matters. [[Sumithran2011,Fothergill2016]]
