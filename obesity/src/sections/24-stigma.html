Weight stigma & bias

Weight stigma is the social devaluation of people because of their body weight. It is common, it reaches into health care, and it harms health; it does not motivate change. [[Rubino2020Stigma,Puhl2010AJPH]] Scrub the loop to see how it feeds itself, and where it can be broken.

A loop that feeds itself

The 2020 joint international consensus defines weight stigma as "social devaluation and denigration of individuals because of their excess body weight", and was unanimous that it causes physical and psychological harm and keeps people from seeking and receiving good care. [[Rubino2020Stigma]] Researchers describe a cycle: stigma acts through stress, avoided care, disordered eating, low mood and less activity, which can raise weight and worsen health, which in turn draws more stigma. Words matter

People-first language says "people with obesity" rather than "obese people", so that a disease does not define a person. [[Kyle2014,Puhl2020Words]] Across 33 studies, neutral words such as "weight" or "unhealthy weight" were preferred, and "obese" and "fat" were least acceptable, especially from a clinician, though preferences vary. [[Puhl2020Words]] Tap a card to see a phrase that puts the person first, and why.

Ask the personProfessional guidance uses "people with obesity", but many young people and adults prefer "weight" or "healthy weight", and some prefer words from their own culture. Asking each person which words they want works best. [[Kyle2014,Puhl2020Words,Puhl2022Terms]]

Pictures matter too

In 371 online news videos about obesity, 65% of adults and 77% of young people with higher weight were shown in a negative, stigmatising way: headless images, isolated body parts, rear views, unhealthy eating and ill-fitting clothes. [[Puhl2013Headless]] Choose a framing to move the camera.

For clinicians and students

Implicit anti-fat bias among doctors is about as strong as in the general public, and clinician attitudes can lower the quality of care despite good intentions. [[Sabin2012,Phelan2015]] The consensus says stigma reduction should target the attitudes and behaviour of those who stigmatise, starting in health-professional training, rather than the people who experience it. [[Tomiyama2018,Rubino2020Stigma]] The modified 5As give a structure for a respectful conversation. [[Vallis2013]]

Ask

Ask permission to discuss weight, without judgement, and explore readiness. [[Vallis2013]]

Assess

BMI, waist and obesity stage, and the drivers and complications of excess weight. [[Vallis2013]] See Assessment & staging.

Advise

Health risks, the benefits of modest loss, the need for a long-term strategy, and treatment options. [[Vallis2013]]

Agree

Realistic expectations and a plan the person agrees with. [[Vallis2013]]

Assist

Barriers, resources, referral and regular follow-up. [[Vallis2013]]

Asking first

A brief, kind offer is welcome

In BWeL, 1882 adults at 137 English practices: a 30-second offer of referral to a weight-management group gave 1.43 kg more loss at 12 months than 30 seconds of advice. 81% of patients found it appropriate and helpful; fewer than 1% (four people) did not. [[Aveyard2016BWeL]]

Language

Model non-biased words

The AAP asks clinicians to model non-biased behaviour and language, use motivational interviewing, and address weight stigma and bullying at the visit. [[Pont2017AAP,Orr2026]]

The room

Equipment that fits

The consensus says suitable infrastructure for people with obesity, including severe obesity, should be a standard requirement for accrediting medical facilities (unanimous). [[Rubino2020Stigma]] A cuff that is too small overestimates blood pressure. [[Muntner2019]] More in Nursing care.

Media and policy

Fair portrayals, clear rules

The consensus calls on media for "fair, accurate, and non-stigmatizing portrayals of obesity", and for clear policies prohibiting weight-based discrimination. [[Rubino2020Stigma]]

Children and teenagers

In young people, weight stigma contributes to binge eating, social isolation, avoiding health care, less physical activity and more weight gain; it does not motivate change. Established [[Pont2017AAP,PuhlLatner2007]] In a 2021 survey of 2032 young people aged 10–17, they preferred terms such as "healthy weight" and disliked "obese", "fat" and "large", which made them feel sad, ashamed and embarrassed. [[Puhl2022Terms]] With children, talk about health and habits for the whole family rather than weight; more in Children & adolescents.

If eating or weight is distressingStigma is linked to disordered eating, low mood and anxiety. [[Wu2018]] If eating or weight causes you distress, or you restrict, binge or purge, talk to a clinician or an eating-disorder service. Help works.

Exam hookWeight discrimination is associated with about 60% higher mortality (HR 1.57 and 1.59 in two US cohorts), not explained by common risk factors. [[Sutin2015]] 19% would avoid future appointments after feeling stigmatised by their doctor. [[Puhl2013Language]]
In plain wordsBeing judged for your weight is bad for health. It raises stress and keeps people away from care. You have the right to respectful words and care: "a person with obesity" or "higher weight" puts the person first.
