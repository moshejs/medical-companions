Does it last into adulthood?

Child to teenager~55%

of children with obesity still have it in adolescence. [[Simmonds2016]]

Teenager to adult~80%

of adolescents with obesity still have it in adulthood, and around 70% over age 30. [[Simmonds2016]]

The other direction70%

of adults with obesity did not have obesity as children or adolescents, so focusing only on children would not remove most adult obesity. [[Simmonds2016]]

Health nowGradient

The more severe the obesity, the higher the risk of low HDL cholesterol, high blood pressure, high triglycerides and high HbA1c, most clearly in boys and young men. [[Skinner2015]]

Obesity in adolescence is associated with insulin resistance, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, sleep disorders, depression and musculoskeletal problems, and with higher adult deaths from cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. [[Kelly2024JAMA]] The AAP recommends checking for these at the same time: lipids, glucose and liver enzymes (ALT) from age 10 with obesity, and blood pressure at every visit from age 3 with overweight or obesity. [[Orr2026,Hampl2023AAP]] Complications are covered in Complications across the body.

Talking with children and families: health, not weight

Weight stigma in young people contributes to binge eating, social isolation, avoiding health care, less physical activity and more weight gain; it does not motivate change. Established [[Pont2017AAP,PuhlLatner2007]] In a survey of 361 adolescents at weight-loss camps, 64% reported weight-based victimisation at school, and the people doing it included peers (92%), PE teachers or coaches (42%) and parents (37%). [[Puhl2013Bullying]] The AAP stigma statement asks clinicians to model non-biased language, use motivational interviewing and address weight stigma and bullying at the visit. [[Pont2017AAP]]

Language guidance for families and clinicians
Words that hurtWhat the research foundWords that help
“obese”, “fat”, “large”In a 2021 survey of 2032 youth aged 10 to 17, these made them feel sad, ashamed and embarrassed. [[Puhl2022Terms]]“healthy weight”, “weight”, or the young person’s own words [[Puhl2022Terms,Puhl2020Words]]
“an obese child”People-first language keeps the disease from defining the person. [[Kyle2014,Puhl2020Words]]“a child with obesity”
Remarks about a body, or comparing siblingsParents were among the perpetrators of weight-based teasing in 37% of reports. [[Puhl2013Bullying]]Talk about habits the whole family shares; the AAP describes care as family-centred and non-stigmatising (KAS 9). [[Orr2026,Hampl2023AAP]]
Lecturing, warningsThe AAP recommends motivational interviewing (KAS 10). [[Orr2026,Hampl2023AAP]]Open questions: “What would you like to change, if anything?”

Ask, then listenPreferences vary between people, so asking each child or family which words they prefer works best. [[Puhl2020Words,Puhl2022Terms]] Stigma and its effects are covered in Weight stigma & bias.

If eating or weight is distressingIf eating or weight causes distress, or someone restricts, binges or purges, talk to a clinician or an eating-disorder service. In a meta-analysis of 36 studies (2589 young people), structured, professionally run treatment with a dietary component was followed by fewer eating-disorder symptoms on average; this applies to supervised programmes, not self-directed dieting, and individual risk still needs screening. [[Jebeile2019]]
