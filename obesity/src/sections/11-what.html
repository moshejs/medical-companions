Part 1 · Understanding obesity

What obesity is

Obesity is a chronic disease of excess body fat with many causes, now split into clinical and preclinical obesity. Watch how common it became between 1990 and 2022, then try the Lancet Commission's classification for yourself.

Worldwide, age-standardised prevalence for adults; 1990 and 2022 estimates only, so the fill between them is a transition, not yearly data. Population statistics. [[NCDRisC2024]]

Key numbers

World · 2022

over 1 billion

People living with obesity: 504 million women, 374 million men, 65.1 million girls and 94.2 million boys aged 5–19. The total is a sum of the four groups [[NCDRisC2024]].

World adults · 1990 → 2022

18.5% · 14.0%

Age-standardised obesity in women (from 8.8%) and men (from 4.8%). Obesity overtook underweight in 2003 for women and 2009 for men, yet 347 million adults still live with underweight, the "double burden" [[NCDRisC2024]].

World children · 5–19 years

6.9% · 9.3%

Obesity in girls (from 1.7% in 1990) and boys (from 2.1%) in 2022. Child obesity exceeded 20% for girls in 21 countries and for boys in 35 [[NCDRisC2024]].

United States · 2021–2023

40.3%

Of adults had obesity, with no significant difference between men and women; 9.4% had severe obesity (BMI ≥40) [[Emmerich2024]]. In 2011–2014, 17.0% of 2–19-year-olds had obesity [[Ogden2016Children]].

Burden · 2015

4.0 million

Deaths worldwide attributed to high BMI, more than two thirds from cardiovascular disease and nearly 40% in people whose BMI was below the obesity threshold [[Afshin2017]].

Forecast · 2050

3.8 billion

Adults forecast to have overweight or obesity if historical trends continue, over half the likely adult population (GBD 2021 reference scenario) [[GBD2021AdultBMI]].

All figures describe populations, not what will happen to any one person.

US adults over six decades

NHANES measured data: ages 20–74 to 1988–94 [[Flegal1998]], then ages 20 and over [[Flegal2002,Flegal2010,Ogden2014,Flegal2016,Emmerich2024]]; projection [[Ward2019NEJM]]. Points are survey periods, plotted at their midpoint.

The sharp rise came between the late 1970s and the early 1990s [[Flegal1998]]. From 2013–2014 to 2021–2023, age-adjusted adult obesity did not change significantly, while severe obesity rose from 7.7% to 9.7% [[Emmerich2024]]. A state-level model projected that 48.9% of US adults would have obesity by 2030, and that severe obesity would become the most common BMI category among women [[Ward2019NEJM]].

"Metabolically healthy" obesity is not risk-free: in 3.5 million UK adults, people with obesity but no diabetes, hypertension or hyperlipidaemia had more coronary heart disease (HR 1.49) and heart failure (HR 1.96) than metabolically healthy people of normal weight [[Caleyachetty2017MHO]]. The Commission's preclinical category likewise means preserved organ function with increased risk [[Rubino2025Commission]]. How organs are affected is covered in Complications across the body, and how a clinician assesses and stages them in Assessment & staging.

Why "disease"?

What the label recognises

The Commission says the causes include genetic, environmental, psychological, nutritional and metabolic factors that alter the biology maintaining fat tissue (agreement 95%) [[Rubino2025Commission]]. Supporters argue the disease label reflects biology and reduces blame, and the AMA's 2013 recognition has been linked to more access to care [[Kyle2016AMA,Bray2017WOF]]. Established

The debate

The Commission sets out the counter-argument that the label may reduce emphasis on personal responsibility, rejects it as a non-medical reason, and grounds disease status in organ dysfunction [[Rubino2025Commission]]. AACE's term "adiposity-based chronic disease" reflects a separate debate about naming and stigma [[Mechanick2017ABCD]]. Why weight bias harms care is covered in Weight stigma & bias.

Not a failure of willpowerObesity is a long-term condition shaped by genes, biology, surroundings and life circumstances [[Rubino2025Commission,Bray2017WOF]]. Weight that returns after weight loss reflects the body defending its weight, which is why it is treated as chronic; see Why lost weight comes back [[Sumithran2011]].
