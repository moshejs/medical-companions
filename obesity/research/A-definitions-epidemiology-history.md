# A · Definitions, measurement, epidemiology & history

Pass date: 2026-11-11. Modules served: what, measuring, assessment, history.

This pass covers how obesity is defined (WHO BMI classes, ethnicity-specific cut-offs, waist and waist-to-height measures, children's definitions), the 2025 Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology Commission's clinical versus preclinical obesity, staging (Edmonton), the limits of BMI, worldwide and US prevalence with trends and forecasts, deaths and disability attributable to high BMI, the BMI–mortality curve, and the history of the field (Quetelet, Keys, leptin, early surgery, drug approvals and withdrawals). All trial, cohort and survey figures are population statistics, not predictions for one person.

## Findings

### what · What obesity is: definitions and disease status
- The 2025 Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology Commission defines obesity as "a condition characterised by excess adiposity, with or without abnormal distribution or function of adipose tissue, and with causes that are multifactorial and still incompletely understood" [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- The Commission was a group of 58 experts from several medical specialties and countries, and it included people with lived experience of obesity. Its recommendations reached 90–100% agreement among commissioners, and 76 organisations worldwide endorsed it [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- The Commission's consensus statements say the causes of obesity include genetic, environmental, psychological, nutritional and metabolic factors that alter the biology maintaining normal adipose tissue mass, distribution and function (agreement 95%) [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- **Clinical obesity** is "a chronic, systemic illness characterised by alterations in the function of tissues, organs, the entire individual, or a combination thereof, due to excess adiposity". It can lead to end-organ damage such as heart attack, stroke and renal failure [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- **Preclinical obesity** is excess adiposity with preserved function of other tissues and organs. It carries a varying but generally increased risk of clinical obesity and of other non-communicable diseases (type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, some cancers, mental disorders) [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- Remission of clinical obesity is defined as partial or complete resolution of the clinical and laboratory evidence of obesity-related organ dysfunction, and "does not imply cure". Preclinical obesity can be a state of remission if resolution is sustained for at least 6 months without ongoing drug treatment (agreement 95–97%) [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- The Commission recommends that BMI be used only as a surrogate measure of health risk at a population level, for epidemiology or for screening, not as an individual measure of health [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- The Commission says public-health strategy should rest on evidence "rather than unproven assumptions that blame individual responsibility". It names weight bias and stigma as major obstacles to prevention and treatment [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- The World Obesity Federation's 2017 position statement describes obesity as "a chronic relapsing progressive disease process". It frames obesity through an epidemiological model in which environmental factors interact with genetic susceptibility, and in which enlarged fat cells and ectopic fat release products that damage organs [[Bray2017WOF]].
- In 2013 the American Medical Association recognised obesity as "a complex, chronic disease requiring medical attention". A later review argues that this has helped bring more access to care and less blame [[Kyle2016AMA]].
- AACE/ACE (2016–2017) proposed the diagnostic term "adiposity-based chronic disease" (ABCD). The term names a chronic disease with a pathophysiological basis, avoids the stigma attached to the word "obesity", and supports a complications-centric approach rather than one based on BMI alone [[Mechanick2017ABCD,Garvey2016AACE]].
- The 2016 AACE/ACE guideline has 160 specific statements, 85 (53.1%) of them Grade A. It concludes that obesity is "a complex, adiposity-based chronic disease" [[Garvey2016AACE]].
- James (2008) writes that WHO in theory recognised obesity as a disease in 1948 by adopting the International Classification of Diseases. WHO found more overweight than underweight in many developing countries only in 1995, and held its first special obesity consultation in 1997 [[James2008WHO]].

### measuring · BMI and the WHO adult classes
- Body mass index is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared (kg/m²) [[Keys1972,Eknoyan2008]].
- The WHO adult classification used in international surveillance [[WHO2000TRS894,Flegal1998]]:
  - pre-obese (overweight): BMI 25.0–29.9
  - class I obesity: 30.0–34.9
  - class II: 35.0–39.9
  - class III: ≥40.0
- NCD-RisC uses these bands, plus underweight below 18.5 kg/m², to track adults in 200 countries. It labels BMI ≥40 "morbid obesity" (the paper's term) [[NCDRisC2016]].
- In 2004 a WHO expert consultation found that a substantial share of Asian people are at high risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at BMIs below 25 kg/m² [[WHO2004Asian]].
  - The BMI at which observed risk rises ranged from 22 to 25 kg/m² across Asian populations, and high risk from 26 to 31 kg/m².
  - The consultation therefore did not set one cut-off for all Asians. It retained the WHO cut-offs as the international classification and added "public health action points" at 23.0, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5 kg/m².
- In a cohort of 1,472,819 adults in England (median follow-up 6.5 years), the BMI that gave the same age- and sex-adjusted type 2 diabetes incidence as BMI 30.0 in White adults was [[Caleyachetty2021Ethnicity]]:
  - 23.9 kg/m² in south Asian adults
  - 28.1 in Black adults
  - 26.9 in Chinese adults
  - 26.6 in Arab adults
- US drug labels state their populations in these terms:
  - Wegovy is indicated for chronic weight management in adults and pediatric patients aged 12 years and older with obesity, and in adults with overweight plus at least one weight-related comorbid condition [[FDA-Wegovy]].
  - Zepbound is indicated in adults with obesity, or with overweight plus at least one weight-related comorbid condition [[FDA-Zepbound]].

### measuring · How well BMI detects excess body fat
- In a 2010 meta-analysis (25 studies, 32 samples, 31,968 people), the usual BMI obesity cut-offs had a pooled sensitivity of 0.50 (95% CI 0.43–0.57) and a pooled specificity of 0.90 (0.86–0.94) for excess body fat measured by body-composition methods [[Okorodudu2010]].
- With a cut-off of BMI ≥30, sensitivity was 0.42 and specificity 0.97. The authors conclude that BMI "fail[s] to identify half of the people with excess BF%" [[Okorodudu2010]].
- In NHANES III (13,601 adults; fat measured by bioelectrical impedance; reference standard body fat >25% in men and >35% in women) [[RomeroCorral2008]]:
  - BMI-defined obesity was present in 19.1% of men and 24.7% of women, but fat-defined obesity in 43.9% and 52.3%.
  - BMI ≥30 had a sensitivity of 36% in men and 49% in women, and a specificity of 95% and 99%.
- BMI performs worse in the intermediate range (25–29.9), in men and in older adults. In men it correlated better with lean mass than with body-fat percentage [[RomeroCorral2008]].
- In children and adolescents (37 studies, 53,521 participants aged 4–18), BMI cut-offs had a pooled sensitivity of 0.73 (CI 0.67–0.79) and a specificity of 0.93 (0.88–0.96). They missed "over a quarter of children with excess body fat percentage" [[Javed2015]].
- Garrow and Webster (1985) found Quetelet's index (W/H²) correlated with measured fat mass per height² at r = 0.955 in women and 0.943 in men. They judged it "both a convenient and reliable indicator of obesity" at the population level [[Garrow1985]].
- Sarcopenic obesity (ESPEN/EASO 2022) is the co-existence of excess adiposity and low skeletal muscle mass and function [[Donini2022]].
  - Screening uses BMI or waist circumference plus markers of low muscle mass or function. Diagnosis rests on muscle-function testing and then body composition.
  - Stage I has no complications and stage II has complications.

### measuring · Waist circumference, waist-to-hip and waist-to-height ratio
- Waist "action levels" (Lean 1995, Glasgow; 904 men and 1,014 women) [[Lean1995]]:
  - ≥94 cm in men or ≥80 cm in women picked out people with BMI ≥25 or a high waist-to-hip ratio (sensitivity >96%, specificity >97.5%).
  - ≥102 cm in men or ≥88 cm in women picked out people with BMI ≥30 or a high waist-to-hip ratio (sensitivity >96%, specificity >98%).
- The 2009 "harmonizing" metabolic syndrome statement (IDF, NHLBI, AHA, WHF, IAS, IASO) [[Alberti2009Harmonizing]]:
  - It dropped waist as an obligatory component; any 3 of 5 abnormal findings define the syndrome.
  - Waist circumference cut-offs remain population-specific, with "national or regional cut points" used in the interim.
  - Earlier IDF criteria had made waist obligatory, with lower thresholds that differed by ethnicity.
- The IAS/ICCR consensus (2020) calls waist circumference a "vital sign". It says waist gives "both independent and additive information to BMI" for predicting morbidity and death, and recommends routine measurement and training in technique [[Ross2020IASICCR]].
- In EPIC (359,387 adults in 9 European countries, mean follow-up 9.7 years, 14,723 deaths) [[Pischon2008EPIC]]:
  - The lowest risk of death was at BMI 25.3 in men and 24.3 in women.
  - After adjusting for BMI, the highest waist quintile carried a relative risk of death of 2.05 (95% CI 1.80–2.33) in men and 1.78 (1.56–2.04) in women.
- In 15 pooled cohorts (259,388 adults, median follow-up 20 years) [[Dardari2026]]:
  - Among people with normal BMI, 5% had a high waist and 18% a high waist-to-hip ratio. Among people with overweight the figures were 39% and 40%.
  - Among people with obesity, 9% had a low waist and 45% a low waist-to-hip ratio.
  - In the normal and overweight BMI groups, a high waist or waist-to-hip ratio meant 15–50% greater risk for most cardiovascular outcomes.
- A 2010 systematic review (78 studies) reported mean areas under the ROC curve for cardiometabolic outcomes of 0.704 for waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), 0.693 for waist and 0.671 for BMI. It proposed a global WHtR boundary of 0.5: "keep your waist circumference to less than half your height" [[Browning2010]].
- A 2012 meta-analysis (31 papers, more than 300,000 adults, several ethnic groups) found that waist improved discrimination of cardiometabolic risk by 3% over BMI and WHtR by 4–5% (P < 0.01). WHtR beat waist for diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease and all outcomes [[Ashwell2012]].
- NICE and the US Department of Defense have adopted "at risk" WHtR cut-offs. A 2023 analysis (58,742 participants) argues that a fixed 0.5 or 0.55 cut-off over-classifies shorter adults, because waist scales with height to the power of about 0.42–0.5 [[Nevill2023]].

### measuring · Children and adolescents
- In the US, child obesity is defined as BMI-for-age at or above the 95th percentile of the CDC growth charts [[Ogden2025,Chaudhary2026]].
- The 2000 CDC growth charts created BMI-for-age charts for ages 2 to 20 years from five national examination surveys [[Kuczmarski2002]].
- Severe obesity in young children has been defined as BMI-for-age ≥120% of the sex-specific 95th percentile on the CDC charts, or BMI ≥35 kg/m² [[Zhao2024WIC]].
- The WHO growth reference for ages 5–19 (2007) lines up with adult cut-offs: at 19 years the +1 SD BMI is 25.4 (boys) and 25.0 (girls), and the +2 SD BMI is 29.7 for both sexes [[deOnis2007]]. NCD-RisC defines child obesity as BMI more than 2 SD above the WHO reference median [[NCDRisC2024]].
- The International Obesity Task Force cut-offs (Cole 2000) were built from six national surveys (Brazil, Great Britain, Hong Kong, the Netherlands, Singapore, USA; 97,876 males and 94,851 females). Their centile curves pass through 25 and 30 kg/m² at age 18 [[Cole2000IOTF]].
- In 2013 the AHA estimated that severe obesity affected 4–6% of US youth and was increasing [[Kelly2013AHA]].
- The Commission set 13 criteria for clinical obesity in children and adolescents (agreement 96–100%) [[Rubino2025Commission]].

### assessment · Clinical versus preclinical obesity in practice
- To confirm excess adiposity, the Commission requires one of [[Rubino2025Commission]]:
  - (a) direct body-fat measurement (DEXA, bioimpedance, etc.)
  - (b) BMI plus at least one anthropometric criterion (waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio or waist-to-height ratio)
  - (c) at least two anthropometric criteria regardless of BMI
  - Cut-offs must be validated and appropriate to age, sex and ethnicity (agreement 98%).
- When BMI is above 40 kg/m², the Commission says excess adiposity "can pragmatically be assumed" without further confirmation [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- A diagnosis of clinical obesity needs confirmed excess adiposity plus one or both of [[Rubino2025Commission]]:
  - reduced organ or tissue function due to obesity
  - substantial, age-adjusted limitations of daily activities (mobility, bathing, dressing, toileting, continence, eating)
- The Commission set 18 diagnostic criteria for clinical obesity in adults (agreement 90–100%), alongside the 13 for children [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- The Commission's minimum laboratory work-up for people with confirmed excess adiposity is full blood count, glycaemia, lipid profile, and renal and liver function. Tests for secondary causes (such as hypothyroidism or Cushing syndrome) are done when clinically indicated [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- In UK Biobank (436,751 adults), applying the Commission criteria raised estimated obesity prevalence from 23.92% (BMI-defined) to 67.58%, with clinical obesity 62.70% of the whole population (Early evidence) [[Liu2026UKB]].
  - 173,698 people with a non-obese BMI met the clinical obesity criteria.
  - Compared with people classed as non-obese by both methods, they had higher risk of incident type 2 diabetes (HR 3.18, 95% CI 3.04–3.33).
- In NHANES 1999–2018 (53,333 adults), the Commission's framework gave a total obesity prevalence that rose from 46.7% to 61.7%, and a clinical obesity prevalence that rose from 34.6% to 46.6% (Early evidence) [[Xiao2026]].
  - Clinical obesity carried higher all-cause mortality than preclinical obesity (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.29–1.66).
- In UK Biobank (232,721 adults, mean follow-up 13.4 years, 19,704 deaths), all-cause mortality was compared with people who had neither obesity nor dysfunction (Early evidence) [[Xu2025UKB]]:
  - clinical obesity at baseline: HR 2.30 (2.16–2.44)
  - preclinical obesity that stayed free of dysfunction: HR 1.15 (1.09–1.22)
- In US youth aged 5–18 (NHANES 2017–2023; 5,513 participants), the Commission's framework gave 8.3% preclinical and 12.5% clinical obesity (Early evidence) [[Chaudhary2026]].
  - The combined 20.8% was similar to the roughly 22% found with BMI percentile alone.
  - The commonest impairments were low HDL cholesterol (60.3%) and asthma (24.8%).
- Among 261,408 US adults with BMI ≥35 who had bariatric surgery or obesity medication in 2017–2023 (claims data), the framework would have classed many as preclinical obesity (Early evidence) [[Samuels2026]]:
  - 12.7% of surgery patients and 38.1% of medication users
  - The authors warn that using the labels to decide eligibility "could potentially delay intervention".
- "Metabolically healthy obesity" carried cardiovascular risk in UK primary-care records (3.5 million adults, mean follow-up 5.4 years). Compared with metabolically healthy people of normal weight, people with obesity and none of diabetes, hypertension or hyperlipidaemia had [[Caleyachetty2017MHO]]:
  - coronary heart disease: HR 1.49 (1.45–1.54)
  - heart failure: HR 1.96 (1.86–2.06)
  - cerebrovascular disease: HR 1.07 (1.04–1.11)

### assessment · Edmonton Obesity Staging System (EOSS)
- Sharma and Kushner (2009) proposed EOSS, a clinical and functional staging system used alongside anthropometric classes. It describes the morbidity and functional limitation linked to excess weight, which BMI and waist do not capture [[Sharma2009EOSS]].
- EOSS is a 5-point ordinal scale (stages 0–4) that grades comorbidity and functional status [[Padwal2011EOSS]].
- In a modified version used with cohort data, stage 0 means no risk factors or comorbidities, stage 1 mild conditions, and stages 2–3 moderate to severe conditions [[Kuk2011EOSS]].
- In NHANES III and 1999–2004 adults with overweight or obesity (mortality follow-up to 2006) [[Padwal2011EOSS]]:
  - Over 75% scored stage 1 or 2.
  - Survival curves separated by EOSS stage but not by obesity class alone.
- In NHANES 1988–1994, stage 2 (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.16–2.13) and stage 3 (HR 2.69, 1.98–3.67) carried higher mortality than stages 0–1, even after adjusting for BMI and the metabolic syndrome [[Padwal2011EOSS]].
- In the Aerobics Center Longitudinal Study (n = 29,533, mean follow-up 16.2 years), compared with normal-weight people [[Kuk2011EOSS]]:
  - People with obesity at EOSS stage 2 had higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.6, 1.3–2.0), as did those at stage 3 (HR 1.7, 1.4–2.0).
  - Stage 0/1 was not associated with higher mortality.

### what · Worldwide prevalence and trends
- NCD-RisC 2024 pooled 3,663 population-based studies (222 million participants measured for height and weight) to estimate obesity in 200 countries from 1990 to 2022 [[NCDRisC2024]].
- Global age-standardised adult obesity prevalence, 1990 to 2022 [[NCDRisC2024]]:
  - women: 8.8% (95% CrI 8.5–9.1) to 18.5% (17.9–19.1)
  - men: 4.8% (4.6–5.0) to 14.0% (13.4–14.6)
- In 2022, 504 million women and 374 million men were living with obesity, increases of 377 million and 307 million since 1990 [[NCDRisC2024]].
- In 2022, 65.1 million girls and 94.2 million boys aged 5–19 were living with obesity, increases of 51.2 million and 76.7 million since 1990. Adding all four groups gives about 1.04 billion people, i.e. over 1 billion (sum derived here) [[NCDRisC2024]].
- Obesity prevalence in school-aged children and adolescents (5–19 years), 1990 to 2022 [[NCDRisC2024]]:
  - girls: 1.7% to 6.9%
  - boys: 2.1% to 9.3%
- Global age-standardised obesity prevalence overtook underweight in 2003 for women and in 2009 for men [[NCDRisC2024]].
- In 2022, obesity was more prevalent than underweight in 177 countries (89%) for women and 145 (73%) for men [[NCDRisC2024]].
- Underweight still affected 183 million women and 164 million men in 2022, concentrated in south Asia and parts of Africa (the "double burden") [[NCDRisC2024]].
- In 2022, child obesity prevalence exceeded 20% for girls in 21 countries and for boys in 35. The highest was in Niue: 34.3% of girls and 42.9% of boys [[NCDRisC2024]].
- Over the longer view, 1975 to 2014, global age-standardised adult obesity rose from 3.2% to 10.8% in men and from 6.4% to 14.9% in women [[NCDRisC2016]].
  - Mean BMI rose from 21.7 to 24.2 kg/m² in men and from 22.1 to 24.4 kg/m² in women.
- In 2014, 2.3% of men and 5.0% of women worldwide had BMI ≥35, and 0.64% and 1.6% had BMI ≥40 [[NCDRisC2016]].
- In 2016, NCD-RisC projected that if trends continued, 2025 global obesity would reach 18% in men and exceed 21% in women. It put the probability of meeting the WHO target of halting the rise at its 2010 level at "virtually zero" [[NCDRisC2016]].
- For 5–19 year olds, obesity rose from 0.7% in 1975 to 5.6% in 2016 in girls, and from 0.9% to 7.8% in boys (WHO reference) [[NCDRisC2017]].
  - In 2016 there were 50 million girls and 74 million boys with obesity, against 75 million and 117 million with moderate or severe underweight.
- The GBD 2015 analysis (68.5 million people) estimated 107.7 million children and 603.7 million adults with obesity in 2015. Prevalence had doubled since 1980 in more than 70 countries [[Afshin2017]].
- GBD 2021 (adults 25 and older) [[GBD2021AdultBMI]]:
  - In 2021, an estimated 1.00 billion adult men and 1.11 billion adult women had overweight or obesity. China (402 million), India (180 million) and the USA (172 million) had the largest numbers.
  - Since 1990, global obesity prevalence rose by 155.1% in males and 104.9% in females.
- GBD 2021 (children and adolescents) [[GBD2021AdolescentBMI]]:
  - Between 1990 and 2021, combined overweight and obesity doubled and obesity alone tripled.
  - In 2021, 93.1 million 5–14-year-olds and 80.6 million 15–24-year-olds had obesity.

### what · Forecasts
- GBD 2021 forecasts that by 2050 about 3.80 billion adults (95% UI 3.39–4.04) will have overweight or obesity, over half the likely adult population, if historical trends continue [[GBD2021AdultBMI]].
  - The number in sub-Saharan Africa is forecast to rise by 254.8%.
  - Nigeria is forecast to reach 141 million adults with overweight or obesity.
- GBD 2021 forecasts that by 2050, 15.6% of 5–14-year-olds (186 million) and 14.2% of 15–24-year-olds (175 million) will have obesity. The steepest rise is expected between 2022 and 2030 [[GBD2021AdolescentBMI]].
- A US state-level model (BRFSS corrected with NHANES measured data) projected that by 2030 [[Ward2019NEJM]]:
  - 48.9% (95% CI 47.7–50.1) of US adults would have obesity and 24.2% (22.9–25.5) severe obesity (BMI ≥35).
  - Prevalence would exceed 50% in 29 states.
  - Severe obesity would become the most common BMI category among women (27.6%).

### what · United States: adults
- In NHANES age-adjusted data (ages 20–74), obesity (BMI ≥30) was 12.8% in 1960–62, 14.1% in 1971–74, 14.5% in 1976–80 and 22.5% in 1988–94. The sharp rise came between the late 1970s and the early 1990s [[Flegal1998]].
- US adult obesity reached 30.5% in 1999–2000, compared with 22.9% in 1988–94 (age-adjusted, adults 20+). BMI ≥40 rose from 2.9% to 4.7% over the same period [[Flegal2002]].
- In 2007–2008, age-adjusted adult obesity was 33.8% (men 32.2%, women 35.5%). Overweight and obesity combined (BMI ≥25) was 68.0% [[Flegal2010]].
- Adult obesity was 34.9% in 2011–2012 [[Ogden2014]] and 37.7% in 2013–2014 [[Flegal2016]].
  - In 2013–2014 it was 35.0% in men and 40.4% in women. Class 3 obesity (BMI ≥40) was 7.7% overall: 5.5% in men and 9.9% in women.
- In August 2021–August 2023, 40.3% of US adults had obesity, with no significant difference between men and women [[Emmerich2024]].
  - Severe obesity (BMI ≥40) affected 9.4%, and was higher in women than in men in every age group.
  - Prevalence was highest at ages 40–59, and lower in adults with a bachelor's degree or more.
- From 2013–2014 to August 2021–August 2023, age-adjusted adult obesity did not change significantly. Severe obesity rose from 7.7% to 9.7% [[Emmerich2024]].

### what · United States: children and adolescents
- Among US 12–19-year-olds, the prevalence of "overweight" as then defined rose from 10.5% (1988–94) to 15.5% (1999–2000) [[Ogden2002Children]].
- In 2011–2014, obesity affected 17.0% (95% CI 15.5–18.6) of US 2–19-year-olds and extreme obesity 5.8% [[Ogden2016Children]].
  - Adolescent (12–19) obesity rose from 10.5% (1988–94) to 20.6% (2013–14).
  - In 2–5-year-olds, obesity rose from 7.2% to 13.9% (2003–04) and then fell to 9.4% (2013–14).
- In 2011–2012, 16.9% of US 2–19-year-olds had obesity, and 8.1% of infants and toddlers had high weight-for-recumbent-length [[Ogden2014]].
- NHANES 1999–2016 showed a positive linear trend in all definitions of overweight and obesity in 2–19-year-olds. Severe obesity in 2–5-year-olds rose after 2013–2014, and there was "no evidence of a decline" at any age [[Skinner2018]].
- Severe obesity among 16.6 million children aged 2–4 enrolled in WIC fell from 2.1% (2010) to 1.8% (2016), then rose to 2.0% (2020) [[Zhao2024WIC]].
- The August 2021–August 2023 NHANES cycle had design changes and small samples for some groups. For Black boys, the authors judge the rise from 19.4% to 38.1% (2017–18 to 2021–23) "implausible" in magnitude, so subgroup estimates from this cycle need caution [[Ogden2025]].

### what · Burden: deaths, disability and the BMI–mortality curve
- High BMI accounted for 4.0 million deaths worldwide in 2015 [[Afshin2017]].
  - Nearly 40% of these deaths were in people whose BMI was below the obesity threshold.
  - More than two thirds were from cardiovascular disease.
- Between 1990 and 2017, global deaths and DALYs attributable to high BMI more than doubled. The six leading causes of high-BMI-related DALYs in 2017 were ischaemic heart disease, stroke, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, hypertensive heart disease and low back pain [[Dai2020]].
- From 2000 to 2021, the age-standardised global DALY rate attributable to high BMI rose 15.7% (95% UI 9.9–21.7), and exposure to high BMI rose by 1.8% a year [[GBD2021RiskFactors]].
- The Global BMI Mortality Collaboration pooled 239 prospective studies (10.6 million participants). Its main analysis was of 3.95 million never-smokers without chronic disease who survived 5 years, of whom 385,879 died [[GBMC2016]].
- All-cause mortality was lowest at BMI 20.0–25.0. Hazard ratios relative to BMI 22.5–<25.0 [[GBMC2016]]:

  | BMI band (kg/m²) | HR (95% CI) |
  |---|---|
  | 15.0–<18.5 | 1.51 (1.43–1.59) |
  | 18.5–<20.0 | 1.13 (1.09–1.17) |
  | 25.0–<27.5 | 1.07 (1.07–1.08) |
  | 27.5–<30.0 | 1.20 (1.18–1.22) |
  | 30.0–<35.0 | 1.45 (1.41–1.48) |
  | 35.0–<40.0 | 1.94 (1.87–2.01) |
  | 40.0–<60.0 | 2.76 (2.60–2.92) |

- Above BMI 25, mortality rose roughly log-linearly with BMI [[GBMC2016]]. The HR per 5 kg/m² higher BMI was:
  - by region: 1.39 in Europe, 1.29 in North America, 1.39 in east Asia, 1.31 in Australia and New Zealand
  - by age at measurement: 1.52 at ages 35–49 and 1.21 at 70–89
  - by sex: 1.51 in men and 1.30 in women
- The Prospective Studies Collaboration (57 studies, 894,576 adults) found mortality lowest at about 22.5–25 kg/m² [[Whitlock2009PSC]].
  - Each 5 kg/m² higher BMI meant about 30% higher overall mortality (HR 1.29, 1.27–1.32): vascular 1.41, diabetic 2.16, renal 1.59, hepatic 1.82, neoplastic 1.10.
- The same collaboration estimated that median survival is reduced by 2–4 years at BMI 30–35, and by 8–10 years at BMI 40–45 (comparable with smoking) [[Whitlock2009PSC]].
- A 2013 meta-analysis (97 studies, more than 2.88 million people) gave different all-cause mortality HRs relative to BMI 18.5–<25 [[Flegal2013JAMA]]:
  - overweight: 0.94 (0.91–0.96)
  - grade 1 obesity: 0.95 (0.88–1.01)
  - grades 2–3 obesity: 1.29 (1.18–1.41)
  - all obesity: 1.18 (1.12–1.25)
  - The open questions section covers why these results differ from GBMC 2016.

### history · Measuring fatness: from Quetelet to BMI
- In 1832 the Belgian statistician Adolphe Quetelet described the relationship now called the Quetelet index. From cross-sectional growth studies he concluded that, outside the growth spurts of infancy and puberty, "the weight increases as the square of the height" [[Eknoyan2008]].
- The search for a practical index of relative weight began after life-insurance actuaries reported higher mortality in their overweight policyholders. After World War II it came to centre on epidemiological studies of weight and cardiovascular disease [[Eknoyan2008]].
- Quetelet organised the first International Statistical Congress in 1853. It launched work on a uniform nomenclature of causes of death, a forerunner of the International Classification of Diseases [[Eknoyan2008]].
- In 1972 Ancel Keys and colleagues named weight/height² the "body mass index" [[Keys1972,Eknoyan2008]].
  - They compared indices of relative weight in 7,424 healthy men in 12 cohorts in five countries.
  - BMI was slightly better than weight/height and much better than the ponderal index, judged by low correlation with height and high correlation with body fat.
- The WHO held its first special consultation on obesity in 1997. The resulting report (Technical Report Series 894, 2000) set the BMI classification still used for international comparisons [[James2008WHO,WHO2000TRS894]].

### history · Biology and disease recognition
- In 1994 Friedman's group (Zhang et al.) cloned the mouse obese (ob) gene and its human homologue. The gene's product (leptin) was proposed as a signal from adipose tissue that helps regulate the size of the body's fat depot [[Zhang1994]]. Pass C covers leptin physiology.
- Since 2013, statements have increasingly framed obesity as a chronic disease:
  - AMA 2013 [[Kyle2016AMA]]
  - World Obesity Federation 2017 [[Bray2017WOF]]
  - AACE/ACE "ABCD" 2016–2017 [[Mechanick2017ABCD]]
  - Lancet Commission 2025 [[Rubino2025Commission]]

### history · Surgery milestones
- Mason and Ito described gastric bypass for obesity in 1967 [[Mason1967]].
- A 1991 NIH Consensus Development Conference recommended that people seeking treatment for severe obesity for the first time be considered for a nonsurgical programme first. Gastric restrictive or bypass operations could be considered for well-informed and motivated patients with acceptable operative risk [[NIH1991Consensus]].
  - Candidates should be selected by a multidisciplinary team and have lifelong medical surveillance.
- In 2022, ASMBS and IFSO published "major updates to [the] 1991 National Institutes of Health guidelines" on indications for metabolic and bariatric surgery. Pass G covers current indications [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].

### history · Anti-obesity drugs: approvals and withdrawals
- After amphetamine and amphetamine-like anorectics were approved in the 1940s and 1950s, the FDA struggled to define their efficacy and safety. Limits on duration of use and warnings about abuse and addiction followed [[Colman2005]].
- Original US approval dates from Drugs@FDA [[FDA-DrugsAtFDA-AOM]]:
  - Ionamin (phentermine resin complex): 4 May 1959
  - Meridia (sibutramine): 22 Nov 1997
  - Xenical (orlistat): 23 Apr 1999
  - Qsymia (phentermine–topiramate): 17 Jul 2012
  - Contrave (naltrexone–bupropion): 10 Sep 2014
  - Saxenda (liraglutide): 23 Dec 2014
  - Wegovy (semaglutide) injection: 4 Jun 2021
  - Zepbound (tirzepatide): 8 Nov 2023
  - Wegovy tablets: 22 Dec 2025
- In the mid-1990s, off-label "fen-phen" (fenfluramine plus phentermine) and the approval of dexfenfluramine led to widespread long-term anorectic use [[Colman2005]]. US prescriptions for fenfluramine and phentermine exceeded 18 million in 1996 [[Connolly1997]].
- Connolly et al. (1997) described valvular heart disease in 24 women (mean age 44) about 12 months after starting fenfluramine–phentermine [[Connolly1997]].
  - Valve histology was identical to carcinoid- or ergotamine-induced disease.
  - Eight women had pulmonary hypertension, and five needed cardiac surgery.
- The FDA issued a public health advisory on 8 July 1997 and wrote to 700,000 practitioners. It then requested voluntary withdrawal of fenfluramine and dexfenfluramine from the market [[CDC1997Fenfluramine]].
- Rimonabant, a CB1 receptor blocker, was marketed in Europe but never approved in the USA [[Christensen2007,Sam2011]].
  - In four RCTs (4,105 participants) it gave 4.7 kg (95% CI 4.1–5.3) more weight loss than placebo at 1 year.
  - Participants were 2.5 times more likely to stop because of depressive mood disorders and 3.0 times more likely because of anxiety.
  - The European Medicines Agency later withdrew it after postmarketing data confirmed the higher risk of depressive disorders.
- In the SCOUT trial (9,804 randomised adults aged 55 or older with cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes or both; mean 3.4 years), sibutramine raised the primary cardiovascular outcome to 11.4% versus 10.0% with placebo (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.03–1.31) [[James2010SCOUT]].
  - Nonfatal myocardial infarction: HR 1.28.
  - Nonfatal stroke: HR 1.36.
  - Cardiovascular and all-cause death were not increased.
- The European Medicines Agency withdrew sibutramine in 2010 [[Galhardo2010]]. The US product Meridia is now listed as "Discontinued" in Drugs@FDA [[FDA-DrugsAtFDA-AOM]].
- The FDA reviewed cancer risk with lorcaserin in the CAMELLIA-TIMI 61 trial (published 2020) [[Sharretts2020]]. Pass F covers current medicines and their labels.

### Exam hooks
- BMI = kg/m². WHO classes: 25–29.9 overweight, 30–34.9 class I, 35–39.9 class II, ≥40 class III [[WHO2000TRS894,Flegal1998]].
- WHO 2004 kept the international BMI cut-offs for Asian populations but added action points at 23, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5 kg/m² [[WHO2004Asian]].
- Diabetes risk at BMI 30 in White adults is reached at BMI 23.9 in south Asian, 26.6 in Arab, 26.9 in Chinese and 28.1 in Black adults (England) [[Caleyachetty2021Ethnicity]].
- BMI ≥30 is specific (about 0.97) but insensitive (about 0.42) for excess body fat, and misses about half of adults with excess fat [[Okorodudu2010]].
- Waist action levels: 94/80 cm (men/women) means gain no more weight; 102/88 cm means reduce weight [[Lean1995]].
- WHtR boundary of 0.5: "keep your waist to less than half your height" [[Browning2010,Ashwell2012]].
- Lancet Commission 2025: confirm excess adiposity by BMI plus one anthropometric measure, two anthropometric measures, or direct fat measurement. At BMI >40 it is assumed [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- Clinical obesity = excess adiposity plus organ dysfunction and/or limits on daily activities. Preclinical obesity = excess adiposity with preserved organ function. There are 18 adult and 13 child criteria [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- EOSS stages 0–4 grade comorbidity and function, not size. Stages 2 and 3 predicted mortality after adjustment for BMI [[Padwal2011EOSS,Sharma2009EOSS]].
- US child obesity is BMI-for-age ≥95th percentile. Severe obesity is ≥120% of the 95th percentile or BMI ≥35 [[Ogden2025,Zhao2024WIC]].
- In 2022 more than 1 billion people worldwide were living with obesity: 878 million adults and 159 million aged 5–19 [[NCDRisC2024]].
- US adult obesity in 2021–2023 was 40.3%, and severe obesity 9.4% [[Emmerich2024]].
- All-cause mortality is lowest at BMI 20–25. The HR is 1.45 at BMI 30–35 and 2.76 at 40–60 [[GBMC2016]].
- High BMI caused 4.0 million deaths in 2015, nearly 40% of them in people without obesity, and more than two thirds from cardiovascular disease [[Afshin2017]].
- Quetelet (1832) described the index; Keys (1972) named it the "body mass index" [[Eknoyan2008,Keys1972]].
- The ob gene (leptin) was cloned in 1994 [[Zhang1994]].
- Fenfluramine and dexfenfluramine were withdrawn in 1997 for valvulopathy. Sibutramine's withdrawal followed SCOUT (more nonfatal MI and stroke). Rimonabant was withdrawn in Europe for psychiatric harm [[CDC1997Fenfluramine,James2010SCOUT,Christensen2007]].

### In plain words
- Obesity means carrying more body fat than is healthy for you. It is a long-term health condition with many causes (genes, biology, surroundings and life circumstances), not a failure of willpower [[Rubino2025Commission,Bray2017WOF]].
- BMI is your weight divided by your height squared. It is a quick screening number, but it cannot tell fat from muscle, so on its own it is not a diagnosis [[Okorodudu2010,Rubino2025Commission]].
- Many health experts now say a waist measurement should be checked as well as BMI, because fat around the middle carries extra risk [[Ross2020IASICCR,Pischon2008EPIC]].
- A simple guide some services use is that a waist under half your height is a lower-risk range. It is a rough guide, and it may be less fair to shorter people [[Browning2010,Nevill2023]].
- Health risks can start at a lower BMI in people of south Asian, Chinese, Arab or Black background, so some services use lower cut-off points [[WHO2004Asian,Caleyachetty2021Ethnicity]].
- Doctors now separate obesity that is already affecting how organs work or how you manage daily life ("clinical obesity") from obesity that is not yet doing so ("preclinical obesity") [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- Obesity has become much more common worldwide since 1990. More than 1 billion people now live with it, including children [[NCDRisC2024]].
- In the US, about 4 in 10 adults have obesity [[Emmerich2024]].
- On average, across very large groups of people, the risk of dying early rises as BMI goes up beyond about 25. These are group averages and do not predict what will happen to any one person [[GBMC2016]].
- For children, the focus should be on health and healthy habits for the whole family, not on a number on the scale. A child's weight is judged against growth charts for their age and sex [[Kuczmarski2002,Ogden2025]].
- Some past weight-loss medicines were taken off the market when safety problems appeared. This is one reason today's medicines are studied in large safety trials [[CDC1997Fenfluramine,James2010SCOUT]].
- Callout: if eating or weight causes you distress, or you find yourself restricting food, bingeing or purging, please talk to a clinician or an eating-disorder service.
- Do not start, stop or change any medicine without talking to your clinician.

## Open questions and controversies
- **Is BMI fit for individual diagnosis?**
  - The Lancet Commission says BMI should be a population or screening measure only, with adiposity confirmed by other measures [[Rubino2025Commission]].
  - Meta-analyses show low sensitivity for excess fat [[Okorodudu2010,Javed2015]].
  - Garrow and Keys found BMI to be the best simple index of fatness at the population level [[Garrow1985,Keys1972]].
  - Status: BMI is kept for screening and surveillance; confirmation with waist or body-composition measures is increasingly recommended.
- **Does overweight raise or lower mortality (the "obesity paradox")?**
  - Flegal 2013 reported a lower all-cause HR for overweight (0.94) and no excess for grade 1 obesity (0.95) relative to BMI 18.5–<25 [[Flegal2013JAMA]].
  - GBMC 2016 restricted analyses to never-smokers without baseline disease, excluded the first 5 years of follow-up, and found higher risk across the overweight range (HR 1.07–1.20) [[GBMC2016]].
  - Status: the difference is largely attributed to confounding by smoking and illness-related weight loss (reverse causality). Both are population averages.
- **Will the clinical/preclinical framework widen or narrow care?**
  - Applying it to UK Biobank raised estimated obesity prevalence from about 24% to about 68% (Early evidence) [[Liu2026UKB]].
  - In US claims data it would class 38.1% of medication users and 12.7% of surgery patients as preclinical. The authors warn this could delay treatment if used for eligibility (Early evidence) [[Samuels2026]].
  - Status: validation studies are ongoing, and how it is used in practice will depend on payers and guidelines.
- **Which waist measure and which cut-offs?**
  - WHtR 0.5 performs better than waist or BMI in meta-analyses [[Ashwell2012,Browning2010]].
  - A fixed WHtR cut-off may over-classify shorter adults [[Nevill2023]].
  - Waist cut-offs remain population-specific, and international harmonisation is unfinished [[Alberti2009Harmonizing,Ross2020IASICCR]].
  - Status: unresolved.
- **Ethnicity-specific BMI thresholds.**
  - WHO 2004 declined to set a single Asian cut-off and kept the international classes, adding action points [[WHO2004Asian]].
  - English cohort data support lower, group-specific thresholds (23.9–28.1 kg/m²) [[Caleyachetty2021Ethnicity]].
  - Status: adoption varies by country. Ethnicity categories are coarse, and the thresholds are based on diabetes risk only.
- **Is "metabolically healthy obesity" benign?**
  - Large record-linkage data show higher risk of coronary heart disease and heart failure even without metabolic abnormalities (Established for association) [[Caleyachetty2017MHO]].
  - The Commission's preclinical category recognises preserved organ function but increased risk [[Rubino2025Commission]].
- **Has US prevalence plateaued?**
  - Age-adjusted adult obesity did not change significantly from 2013–14 to 2021–23, while severe obesity rose [[Emmerich2024]].
  - Models project close to 49% by 2030 [[Ward2019NEJM]].
  - The 2021–23 child estimates have sampling limitations [[Ogden2025]].
  - Status: watch the post-2023 NHANES cycles, and whether wider use of GLP-1-based medicines changes the trend. That last question is (Hypothesis) and not yet measured in national data here.
- **Calling obesity a disease.**
  - Supporters argue that it reflects biology and reduces blame [[Kyle2016AMA,Bray2017WOF]].
  - The Commission sets out the counter-argument that the label may reduce emphasis on personal responsibility, rejects it as a non-medical reason, and grounds disease status in organ dysfunction [[Rubino2025Commission]].
  - Status: the major medical bodies cited here treat obesity as a chronic disease. AACE's "ABCD" term reflects a separate debate about naming and stigma [[Mechanick2017ABCD]].
