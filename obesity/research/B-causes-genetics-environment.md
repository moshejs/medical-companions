# B · Causes: genetics, environment & risk factors

Pass date: 2026-11-11. Modules served: causes, appetite.

This pass covers why obesity develops: how much of the variation in body weight between people is inherited (twin, adoption and family studies), common and rare genetic variants, the food, sleep, social and economic environment, early-life and maternal factors, the microbiome, medicines that cause weight gain, endocrine causes, stopping smoking, and the arithmetic of energy balance. Every number below is a population average or an association from a named study, not a prediction for one person.

## Findings

### causes · Framing: obesity has many causes
- An international consensus statement (modified Delphi process) states that "the idea that obesity is a 'choice' is a misconception". It says the evidence shows obesity "results primarily from a combination of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors" (Established) [[Rubino2020Stigma]].
- The same statement says body-weight regulation "is not entirely under volitional control". Biological, genetic and environmental factors "critically contribute" [[Rubino2020Stigma]].
- It also says voluntary weight-loss efforts "activate potent compensatory biologic responses" (more appetite, lower metabolic rate) that "typically promote long-term weight regain" [[Rubino2020Stigma]].
- A Lancet series paper describes rising obesity in almost all countries at once. It says this is driven mainly by changes in the global food system, "which is producing more processed, affordable, and effectively marketed food than ever before". Within a population, environmental and individual factors (including genes) interact to explain who is affected [[Swinburn2011Lancet]].
- A major genetics review describes common (polygenic) obesity and rare, severe, early-onset monogenic obesity as sharing genetic and biological roots. Both point to "a key role for the brain in the control of body weight" (Established) [[Loos2022]].

### causes · Heritability: twin, adoption and family studies
- **Adoption (Denmark, 1986):** 540 adult adoptees were studied. Their weight class tracked strongly with the BMI of their biological parents (mothers P<0.0001; fathers P<0.02) and not with the BMI of their adoptive parents. The link held across the whole range "from very thin to very fat" [[Stunkard1986]].
- The adoption study concluded that genes have an important role in adult fatness and "the family environment alone has no apparent effect" [[Stunkard1986]].
- **Twins reared apart (Sweden, 1990):** there were 93 pairs of identical twins reared apart. The correlation in their BMI was 0.70 in men and 0.66 in women. Model-fitted heritability was 0.74 (men) and 0.69 (women) [[Stunkard1990]].
- In the same twin study, environmental influences unique to each person explained about 30% of BMI variance. A shared childhood environment did not add to twins' similarity in later life [[Stunkard1990]].
- **Meta-regression of 88 twin estimates (140,525 twins):** BMI heritability ranged from 0.47 to 0.90. The 5th, 50th and 95th centiles were 0.58, 0.75 and 0.87 [[Elks2012]].
- Family studies (27 estimates, 42,968 people) gave lower heritability: range 0.24 to 0.81, with 5th, 50th and 95th centiles of 0.25, 0.46 and 0.68 [[Elks2012]].
- Twin heritability was 0.07 higher in children than in adults. It rose by 0.012 per year of age through childhood and fell by 0.002 per year in adults [[Elks2012]].
- **Children growing up during the obesity rise:** in 5,092 UK twin pairs aged 8 to 11, heritability was 77% for both BMI and waist circumference. Shared-environment effects were "very modest" [[Wardle2008]].
- **CODATwins (87,782 twin pairs from 45 cohorts, ages 0.5 to 19.5):** additive genetic variance was lowest at age 4 (0.42 in boys, 0.41 in girls). It rose to 0.75 in both sexes by age 19. The shared-environment effect seen in mid-childhood was no longer detectable after age 15 [[Silventoinen2016]].
- The relative share of genetic variation was roughly similar in North America and Australia, Europe and East Asia, even though BMI variance differed between these regions [[Silventoinen2016]].
- **Overfeeding identical twins:** 12 pairs of young men were overfed by 1000 kcal a day, 6 days a week, for 84 days (84,000 kcal in total). Mean weight gain was 8.1 kg, with a range of 4.3 to 13.3 kg [[Bouchard1990]].
- In that study, variance in weight gain was about three times larger between twin pairs than within pairs. For abdominal visceral fat it was about six times larger. This suggests genes shape how the body stores extra energy [[Bouchard1990]].
- Heritability is a population statistic. It describes how much of the variation in a given environment tracks with genes. It does not mean weight is fixed, and it can change when the environment changes (see Pima and FTO birth-cohort findings below) [[Elks2012,Rosenquist2015]].

### causes · Common genetic variants (polygenic obesity)
- **FTO (2007):** the first common obesity variant was replicated in 13 cohorts (38,759 people). The 16% of adults who carry two copies of the risk allele weighed about 3 kg more and had 1.67-fold higher odds of obesity than people with no copies [[Frayling2007]].
- The FTO association appears from age 7 years upward and reflects an increase in fat mass specifically [[Frayling2007]].
- **FTO mechanism:** the rs1421085 risk variant switches off a repressor (ARID5B) binding site. This doubles IRX3 and IRX5 expression in early fat-cell development and shifts cells from energy-burning beige fat to energy-storing white fat. Mitochondrial heat production falls by a factor of 5 (Early evidence) [[Claussnitzer2015]].
- In the same work, CRISPR-Cas9 repair of the variant in fat-cell precursors from a risk-allele carrier restored heat production by a factor of 7 (laboratory evidence; Early evidence) [[Claussnitzer2015]].
- **Physical activity and FTO:** a meta-analysis covered 218,166 adults. Each FTO risk allele raised the odds of obesity 1.23-fold overall: 1.30-fold in inactive adults and 1.22-fold in active adults, about 27% less. No such interaction was found in 19,268 children [[Kilpelainen2011]].
- **Birth cohort changes FTO's effect:** in Framingham Heart Study data over 30 years, the FTO–BMI association changed at an inflection point for people born after 1942. This suggests the environment changes how strongly a gene variant shows itself (Early evidence) [[Rosenquist2015]].
- **97 loci (2015):** a meta-analysis of up to 339,224 people found 97 BMI-associated regions of the genome, 56 of them new. Together they explain about 2.7% of BMI variation. All common variants together are estimated to explain more than 20% [[Locke2015]].
- Pathway analysis of those loci "strongly" supports a role for the central nervous system in obesity susceptibility. This includes synaptic function and glutamate signalling [[Locke2015]].
- **About 700,000 people (2018):** combining UK Biobank with earlier studies found 941 near-independent BMI-associated variants. Together they explained about 6.0% of BMI variance in an independent cohort. The polygenic score correlated with BMI at about 0.22 [[Yengo2018]].
- **Polygenic score (2019):** a score built from 2.1 million variants was tested in 288,016 UK Biobank adults (mean age 57). People in the top decile weighed 13.0 kg more than people in the bottom decile (85.3 vs 72.2 kg). Their mean BMI was 30.0 vs 25.2 kg/m² [[Khera2019]].
- Severe obesity (BMI ≥40) affected 5.6% of the top polygenic decile and 0.2% of the bottom decile, a 25-fold gradient. Obesity (BMI ≥30) affected 43.2% vs 9.5% [[Khera2019]].
- In the ALSPAC birth cohort, top-decile and bottom-decile babies differed by only 0.06 kg at birth (3.47 vs 3.41 kg). The gap was 3.5 kg at age 8 and 12.3 kg at age 18 [[Khera2019]].
- The top 1.6% of the polygenic score distribution had a mean BMI 4.1 kg/m² higher than everyone else. That matches the BMI excess of carriers of a disease-causing MC4R mutation in the same dataset [[Khera2019]].
- **Thrifty or drifty genes:** the "thrifty gene" idea is that fat-storing genes were selected because they helped people survive famine. One critique calls it "attractive but flawed" and proposes a "drifty gene" alternative, in which some obesity-risk variants spread by random genetic drift rather than selection (Hypothesis) [[Speakman2008]].

### causes · Monogenic obesity (single-gene causes)
- **Leptin deficiency (1997):** two severely affected children from one consanguineous family had very low leptin despite high fat mass. Both had a homozygous frameshift mutation in the leptin gene. This was the first genetic evidence that leptin regulates energy balance in humans [[Montague1997]].
- In 1999, the first treatment of a child with congenital leptin deficiency using recombinant leptin was reported [[Farooqi1999Leptin]].
- Three children with congenital leptin deficiency were treated with daily leptin injections for up to 4 years. They had sustained improvements in appetite, fat mass, high insulin and high blood lipids. Thyroid hormone rose, and puberty developed at an appropriate time [[Farooqi2002Leptin]].
- **Leptin receptor (1998):** a homozygous mutation that truncates the leptin receptor caused early-onset severe obesity with no pubertal development and reduced growth hormone and thyrotropin secretion [[Clement1998]].
- **POMC (1998):** loss-of-function POMC mutations cause a recognisable triad: early-onset obesity, adrenal insufficiency (no ACTH) and red hair pigmentation (no α-MSH) [[Krude1998]].
- **PCSK1 / prohormone convertase 1 (1997):** a woman with extreme childhood obesity, abnormal glucose handling, hypogonadism and low cortisol had compound heterozygous PC1 mutations. Her prohormone processing was impaired [[Jackson1997]].
- **MC4R in severe childhood obesity:** sequencing of 500 people with severe childhood obesity found MC4R mutations in 29 (5.8%). 23 had one copy and 6 had two copies [[Farooqi2003MC4R]].
- MC4R deficiency causes hyperphagia (intense, persistent hunger), increased lean mass, faster linear growth and severe hyperinsulinaemia. Two-copy carriers are more severely affected (codominant inheritance). Mutations that leave some receptor signalling have milder effects [[Farooqi2003MC4R]].
- **MC4R in the general population:** in 5,724 people from a UK birth cohort, about 1 in 337 (0.30%) carried one copy of a loss-of-function MC4R mutation. This is "considerably higher than previous estimates" [[Wade2021MC4R]].
- At age 18, these carriers weighed 17.76 kg more on average (95% CI 9.41 to 26.10). Their BMI was 4.84 kg/m² higher (2.19 to 7.49) and their fat mass 14.78 kg higher (8.56 to 20.99) [[Wade2021MC4R]].
- In UK Biobank exome data, 9 of 6,547 people (0.14%) carried a disease-causing MC4R variant. Their mean BMI was 4.1 kg/m² higher, but only 1 of the 9 had severe obesity. Penetrance in adults is incomplete [[Khera2019]].
- **Protective MC4R variants:** 61 MC4R variants found among 0.5 million UK Biobank participants were tested. A subset that increases receptor function was linked to lower BMI and lower odds of obesity, type 2 diabetes and coronary artery disease [[Lotta2019]].
- β-arrestin recruitment explained 88% of the variance in how MC4R variants relate to BMI. Canonical cAMP signalling explained less. This is a potential drug-design lead (Early evidence) [[Lotta2019]].
- **GPR75 (2021):** exome sequencing of 645,626 people found protein-truncating GPR75 variants in about 4 in 10,000 people. Carriers of one copy had a BMI 1.8 kg/m² lower and 54% lower odds of obesity (Early evidence as a drug target) [[Akbari2021]].
- **Syndromic forms (link only):** Bardet-Biedl syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive ciliopathy. Its features include retinal dystrophy, obesity, extra fingers or toes, kidney problems, learning difficulties and hypogonadism. Gene sequencing confirms the diagnosis in 80% [[Forsythe2013]].
- Prader-Willi syndrome causes severe infant hypotonia with poor feeding, followed by early-childhood obesity and hyperphagia. It results from loss of paternally expressed genes at 15q11.2-q13: paternal deletion (65–75%), maternal uniparental disomy (20–30%) or an imprinting defect (1–3%) [[Cassidy2012]].

### causes · Genes and environment together
- **Pima Indians:** Pima people in Mexico had a type 2 diabetes prevalence of 6.9%, less than one fifth of the 38% seen in Pima people in Arizona. Obesity was much lower in the Mexican group (7% of men, 20% of women), despite considerable genetic similarity. Physical activity was much higher in Mexico [[Schulz2006]].
- The Pima authors conclude that even in genetically susceptible populations, whether these conditions develop "is determined mostly by environmental circumstances" [[Schulz2006]].
- Gene discovery and environment are not opposites. The FTO effect is about 27% smaller in active adults and changed for people born after 1942. Both findings show genes acting through the environment [[Kilpelainen2011,Rosenquist2015]].

### causes · Food environment and diet quality
- **Ultra-processed food RCT (NIH, 2019):** 20 weight-stable adults (mean BMI 27) lived on a metabolic ward for 4 weeks. They had 2 weeks on an ultra-processed diet and 2 weeks on an unprocessed diet, in random order. Meals were matched for calories offered, energy density, macronutrients, sugar, sodium and fibre, and people ate as much or as little as they wanted [[Hall2019UPF]].
- On the ultra-processed diet, people ate 508 ± 106 kcal more a day (p=0.0001). The extra came from carbohydrate (+280 kcal a day) and fat (+230 kcal a day), not protein (−2 kcal a day) [[Hall2019UPF]].
- Weight rose by 0.9 ± 0.3 kg over 2 weeks on the ultra-processed diet and fell by 0.9 ± 0.3 kg on the unprocessed diet. Body fat rose by 0.4 kg and fell by 0.3 kg, respectively [[Hall2019UPF]].
- People ate faster on the ultra-processed diet: 17 kcal per minute more (p<0.0001). Their ratings of hunger and appetite did not differ between the diets [[Hall2019UPF]].
- **Sugar-sweetened drinks, children (RCT, 2012):** 641 mostly normal-weight children aged about 5 to 12 got one drink a day (250 mL) for 18 months, either sugar-free or sugar-sweetened (104 kcal). Weight rose 6.35 kg in the sugar-free group and 7.37 kg in the sugar group (95% CI for the difference −1.54 to −0.48 kg) [[deRuyter2012]].
- In that trial the BMI z score rose by 0.02 SD with sugar-free drinks and by 0.15 SD with sugar-sweetened drinks [[deRuyter2012]].
- **Sugar-sweetened drinks meta-analysis:** in adult cohorts, each extra daily serving was linked to 0.22 kg more weight gain per year (random effects; 95% CI 0.09 to 0.34). In adult RCTs, adding sugar-sweetened drinks raised weight by 0.85 kg (0.50 to 1.20) [[Malik2013SSB]].
- **Long-term weight gain in US cohorts:** 120,877 adults gained an average of 3.35 lb in each 4-year period. Each extra daily serving was linked to more gain for potato chips (+1.69 lb), potatoes (+1.28 lb) and sugar-sweetened drinks (+1.00 lb), and less gain for yogurt (−0.82 lb), nuts (−0.57 lb), fruit (−0.49 lb) and vegetables (−0.22 lb) (observational associations) [[Mozaffarian2011]].
- The same cohorts linked 0.31 lb more gain per 4 years to each hour a day of television. Sleeping less than 6 or more than 8 hours was also linked to more gain [[Mozaffarian2011]].
- **Portion size (crossover trial):** when every food and drink was served at 150% of the usual amount for 2 days, daily intake rose by 16%. At 200% it rose by 26% (women +530 kcal a day, men +812 kcal a day). Fullness ratings did not differ between 150% and 200% [[Rolls2006]].
- **Food supply across countries:** of 69 countries studied, 56 showed a rising national food energy supply alongside rising average body weight. In 45 countries the supply rose more than the model-predicted rise in intake. The authors conclude that more food energy is enough to explain rising population weight, especially in high-income countries [[Vandevijvere2015]].
- **US food supply, 1970s to 2000s:** the rise in food energy predicted a weight gain of 4.0 kg in children, matching the measured 4.0 kg. In adults it predicted 10.8 kg against the measured 8.6 kg [[Swinburn2009AJCN]].

### causes · Physical activity and energy expenditure
- **Work activity:** in the early 1960s almost half of US private-industry jobs required at least moderate physical activity. Now fewer than 20% do. Estimated work-related energy use fell by more than 100 kcal a day (142 kcal a day in men) [[Church2011]].
- **Counter-evidence:** doubly labelled water data from Europe and North America since the 1980s show no fall in physical activity energy expenditure. The authors conclude that lower expenditure is unlikely to have driven the obesity epidemic [[Westerterp2008]].
- **Hadza hunter-gatherers:** they are more active than Western adults, but their average daily energy expenditure was no different once body size was taken into account [[Pontzer2012]].
- **Constrained total energy expenditure:** in 332 adults from five populations, total energy expenditure rose with physical activity at low activity levels and then levelled off at higher levels. This fits a "constrained" model in which the body adapts to keep total expenditure within a narrow range (Early evidence) [[Pontzer2016]].
- **Across the lifespan:** in a doubly labelled water database spanning ages 8 days to 95 years, size-adjusted expenditure peaks at about 50% above adult values around age 1. It stays stable from about 20 to 60 years, including during pregnancy, and then declines [[Pontzer2021]].
- **Across economies:** in 4,213 adults from 34 populations, size-adjusted total energy expenditure was about 6 to 11% lower in more economically developed populations. This explained only about one tenth of the extra body fat and BMI. Higher energy intake and more ultra-processed food played "a far greater role" (Early evidence) [[McGrosky2025]].
- **Screen time (school RCT):** an 18-lesson, 6-month curriculum to cut television, videotape and video-game use in 8- to 9-year-olds reduced BMI gain by 0.45 kg/m² (95% CI −0.73 to −0.17) and waist gain by 2.30 cm compared with a control school [[Robinson1999]].

### causes · Sleep
- **Meta-analysis (634,511 people):** short sleep was linked to obesity in children (pooled OR 1.89, 95% CI 1.46 to 2.43) and adults (OR 1.55, 1.43 to 1.68) [[Cappuccio2008]].
- In adults, each extra hour of sleep was linked to 0.35 lower BMI units (95% CI −0.57 to −0.12). The authors caution that causal inference is difficult because these studies were mostly cross-sectional [[Cappuccio2008]].
- **Sleep restriction (crossover):** 2 days of restricted sleep in 12 healthy young men lowered leptin by 18%, raised ghrelin by 28% and raised hunger by 24%. Appetite for calorie-dense, high-carbohydrate foods rose by 33 to 45% [[Spiegel2004]].
- **5 days of insufficient sleep (inpatient):** energy use went up by about 5%, but people ate more than that, especially after dinner. Weight rose by 0.82 ± 0.47 kg [[Markwald2013]].
- **Sleep extension RCT (real life):** 80 adults with overweight who usually slept less than 6.5 hours got sleep-hygiene counselling. They slept about 1.2 hours more a night and ate 270 kcal a day less than controls (95% CI −393 to −147). Intake was measured objectively with doubly labelled water [[Tasali2022]].

### causes · Social and economic context
- **Social networks (Framingham, 12,067 people, 1971–2003):** a person's chance of developing obesity rose by 57% (95% CI 6 to 123) if a friend developed obesity in the same period, 40% for a sibling and 37% for a spouse. No effect was seen among geographic neighbours [[Christakis2007]].
- Those clusters reached up to three degrees of separation. They did not appear to be explained only by people choosing similar friends (an observational design; mechanisms are debated) [[Christakis2007]].
- **Socioeconomic status:** a review of 333 studies (1,914 associations) found the pattern depends on a country's level of development. Positive links (higher status, larger body size) were more common in less-developed countries. Negative links were more common in highly developed countries, for women especially with education and occupation [[McLaren2007]].
- The Lancet series notes that in low-income countries obesity mostly affects middle-aged adults, especially women, in wealthy urban settings. In high-income countries it affects all ages and both sexes but is greater in disadvantaged groups [[Swinburn2011Lancet]].
- **Neighbourhood poverty (randomised, Moving to Opportunity):** 4,498 women with children in high-poverty public housing were randomised. Being offered a voucher to move to a low-poverty area lowered the prevalence of BMI ≥40 by 3.38 percentage points (95% CI −6.39 to −0.36) and HbA1c ≥6.5% by 4.31 points at follow-up in 2008–2010 [[Ludwig2011MTO]].

### causes · Pregnancy and early life
- **Maternal BMI before pregnancy (162,129 mother–child pairs, 37 cohorts):** maternal obesity was linked to higher odds of overweight or obesity in the child. The odds ratio was 2.43 in early childhood, 3.12 in mid-childhood and 4.47 in late childhood (10–18 years). For maternal overweight the ORs were 1.66, 1.91 and 2.28 [[Voerman2019]].
- Excess weight gain in pregnancy was linked to ORs of 1.39, 1.55 and 1.72 across the same ages. Maternal overweight, maternal obesity and excess pregnancy weight gain each accounted for 10.2% to 21.6% of childhood overweight or obesity prevalence [[Voerman2019]].
- **Gestational diabetes (HAPO follow-up, 4,832 children aged 10–14):** obesity affected 19.1% of children of mothers with gestational diabetes vs 9.9% of the others. After adjusting for maternal BMI, the OR for childhood obesity was 1.58 (95% CI 1.24 to 2.01). The OR for overweight or obesity combined was 1.21 (1.00 to 1.46), which was not significant [[Lowe2018HAPO]].
- **Smoking in pregnancy (39 studies, 236,687 children):** adjusted OR 1.37 (1.28 to 1.46) for childhood overweight and 1.55 (1.40 to 1.73) for childhood obesity (observational) [[Rayfield2017]].
- **Rapid infant weight gain (47,661 people, 10 cohorts):** each +1 SD rise in weight score between birth and age 1 doubled the odds of childhood obesity (OR 1.97, 1.83 to 2.12). It was also linked to 23% higher odds of adult obesity (OR 1.23, 1.16 to 1.30) [[Druet2012]].
- **Dutch famine 1944–45 (300,000 19-year-old men):** exposure in the first half of pregnancy was linked to *higher* obesity rates. Exposure in the last trimester and early infancy was linked to *lower* rates. This is an early clue that timing of nutrition in development matters (Early evidence for mechanism) [[Ravelli1976]].

### causes · Microbiome and antibiotics
- **Mice:** the gut microbiome of genetically obese mice harvests more energy from food. Germ-free mice given an "obese microbiota" gained more body fat than those given a "lean microbiota" (Established in mice; Hypothesis for humans) [[Turnbaugh2006]].
- **Twin-to-mouse transfer:** gut microbes from human twin pairs discordant for obesity were moved into germ-free mice. Mice receiving the microbes of the twin with obesity gained more fat. Housing them with mice carrying the lean twin's microbes prevented this, depending on diet (Early evidence) [[Ridaura2013]].
- **Low-dose antibiotics in young mice** increased adiposity and changed the microbiome and short-chain fatty acid metabolism. This mirrors the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in farming (Established in mice) [[Cho2012]].
- **Early-life antibiotics in children (15 cohorts, 445,880 children):** RR 1.21 (1.13 to 1.30) for childhood obesity. Each additional course was linked to a 6% higher risk (RR 1.06). All the cohorts were from high-income countries (observational; Early evidence for causation) [[Shao2017]].
- **Microbial richness:** 23% of 292 Danish adults had low gut bacterial gene richness. Compared with high-richness individuals, they had more adiposity, insulin resistance and dyslipidaemia (association) [[LeChatelier2013]].
- **Faecal transplant, small trial:** in men with metabolic syndrome, microbiota from lean donors raised insulin sensitivity at 6 weeks. Median glucose disposal went from 26.2 to 45.3 µmol/kg/min (Early evidence) [[Vrieze2012]].
- **Faecal transplant, placebo-controlled pilot (FMT-TRIM, 24 adults with obesity):** capsules from lean donors engrafted, but did not significantly improve insulin sensitivity (mean difference 9%, 95% CI −5% to 28%) or body composition over 12 weeks [[Yu2020FMT]].

### causes · Medicines that can cause weight gain
- **Drug meta-analysis (257 RCTs, 84,696 patients):** average weight gain compared with placebo was 2.4 kg for olanzapine, 2.2 kg for gabapentin, 1.8 kg for amitriptyline, 1.5 kg for mirtazapine, 1.1 kg for quetiapine and 0.8 kg for risperidone [[Domecq2015]].
- In the same analysis, diabetes medicines linked to weight gain included pioglitazone (2.6 kg), glyburide (2.6 kg), glipizide (2.2 kg), glimepiride (2.1 kg) and gliclazide (1.8 kg). Metformin (−1.1 kg) and the GLP-1 receptor agonists liraglutide (−1.7 kg) and exenatide (−1.2 kg) were linked to weight loss [[Domecq2015]].
- **Antipsychotics at 10 weeks (81 studies):** estimated mean change was +4.45 kg for clozapine, +4.15 kg for olanzapine, +2.10 kg for risperidone and +0.04 kg for ziprasidone, compared with −0.74 kg for placebo [[Allison1999]].
- **Network meta-analysis of 15 antipsychotics (212 RCTs, 43,049 people):** for weight gain, the standardised mean difference against placebo ranged from −0.09 (haloperidol, least) to −0.74 (olanzapine, most) [[Leucht2013]].
- **Olanzapine label, adults:** across 13 placebo-controlled trials (median 6 weeks), people taking olanzapine gained 2.6 kg on average, against a 0.3 kg loss on placebo. 22.2% gained at least 7% of their body weight, against 3% on placebo [[FDA-Zyprexa]].
- With long-term use (at least 48 weeks; median 573 days; N=2021), mean weight gain was 5.6 kg. 64%, 32% and 12% gained at least 7%, 15% and 25% of their starting weight [[FDA-Zyprexa]].
- **Olanzapine label, adolescents:** mean gain was 4.6 kg over a median of 3 weeks (placebo 0.3 kg). With at least 24 weeks of use it was 11.2 kg, and 89% gained at least 7% of their starting weight [[FDA-Zyprexa]].
- **Children and teenagers starting antipsychotics (SATIETY cohort, ages 4–19):** after a median of 10.8 weeks, weight rose by 8.5 kg on olanzapine, 6.1 kg on quetiapine, 5.3 kg on risperidone and 4.4 kg on aripiprazole. Untreated comparison patients gained 0.2 kg [[Correll2009]].
- **Mirtazapine label:** in US controlled trials, 17% reported increased appetite (placebo 2%), and 7.5% gained at least 7% of body weight (placebo 0%). In an 8-week paediatric trial, 49% gained at least 7% (placebo 5.7%) [[FDA-Remeron]].
- **Antidepressants in UK primary care (294,719 adults):** new episodes of ≥5% weight gain occurred at 11.2 per 100 person-years with antidepressants and 8.1 without (adjusted rate ratio 1.21, 1.19 to 1.22). The higher risk lasted at least 6 years. The authors say the association may not be causal [[Gafoor2018]].
- **Insulin and sulfonylureas (UKPDS 33):** over 10 years, the intensive-control group gained 2.9 kg more than the conventional group. Weight gain was greatest with insulin (4.0 kg), followed by chlorpropamide (2.6 kg) and glibenclamide (1.7 kg) [[UKPDS33]].
- **Glucocorticoids:** in 1,066 people with rheumatoid arthritis, taking 5 mg/day or more of prednisone equivalent for over 6 months was linked to more self-reported weight gain. A "cushingoid" appearance rose steadily with dose [[Huscher2009]].
- The Endocrine Society guideline notes that many medicines for diabetes, depression and other chronic diseases affect weight. It advises choosing, where possible, options with favourable weight profiles (quoted as published, not as instructions) [[Apovian2015]].

> **Medicines callout (for builders):** the medicines in this section treat serious conditions. Do not start, stop or change any medicine without a clinician. Weight effects can be discussed, and alternatives may exist.

### causes · Endocrine causes (minor contributors)
- **Thyroid function within the normal range (Framingham, 2,407 adults):** mean weight rose from 64.5 to 70.2 kg across TSH quartiles in women and from 82.8 to 85.6 kg in men. A rise in TSH over 3.5 years was linked to modest weight gain of 0.5–2.3 kg in women and 0.4–1.3 kg in men across quartiles [[Fox2008Thyroid]].
- Overt hypothyroidism may be associated with weight gain, but the within-range effects measured in Framingham were small, at a few kilograms at most [[Fox2008Thyroid]].
- Cushing's syndrome is the result of chronic exposure to excess glucocorticoids, from adrenal tumours or ACTH-secreting tumours. It is rare compared with common obesity [[Lacroix2015]].

### causes · Stopping smoking
- **Meta-analysis of 62 studies:** people who quit without treatment gained 1.12 kg on average at 1 month, 2.26 kg at 2 months, 2.85 kg at 3 months, 4.23 kg at 6 months and 4.67 kg (95% CI 3.96 to 5.38) at 12 months [[Aubin2012]].
- At 12 months, 16% of quitters had lost weight, 37% had gained less than 5 kg, 34% had gained 5–10 kg and 13% had gained more than 10 kg. Most of the gain came within the first 3 months [[Aubin2012]].
- In US cohorts, people who had recently quit gained 5.17 lb more over 4 years [[Mozaffarian2011]].
- Weight change after quitting is similar whether or not people use medicines to help them quit. It is also similar in people who are especially worried about weight gain [[Aubin2012]].

### appetite · Energy balance arithmetic
- **The 3500 kcal rule is wrong:** Hall and colleagues write that official advice "erroneously" states that eating about 2 MJ (about 500 kcal) less per day causes a steady 0.5 kg loss per week. The "3500 kcal per pound rule" ignores how energy expenditure adapts as weight changes [[Hall2011Lancet]].
- **Rule of thumb:** for an average adult with overweight, each lasting change in intake of 100 kJ a day leads to an eventual weight change of about 1 kg ("10 kcal per day per pound"). About half of the change happens in 1 year and 95% in about 3 years [[Hall2011Lancet]].
- **Worked model:** a sedentary 100 kg man who eats 2 MJ (480 kcal) less per day is predicted to level off at about 75 kg. He reaches half of the loss in about 1 year and 95% after about 3 years, with ±4 kg spread from uncertainty about baseline needs [[Hall2011Lancet]].
- **Energy gap of the US epidemic:** average adult weight gain from 1978 to 2005 corresponds to a daily imbalance of only about 30 kJ (about 7 kcal). But the "maintenance energy gap", the extra intake needed to sustain the higher weight, is about 0.9 MJ (about 220 kcal) a day [[Hall2011Lancet]].
- The energy content of 1 kg of body fat is 39.5 MJ, compared with 7.6 MJ for 1 kg of lean tissue [[Hall2011Lancet]].
- **Policy example:** a static 3500 kcal calculation predicted about 10 kg of average weight loss over 5 years from a US sugar-sweetened beverage tax. The dynamic model predicted about 1.8 kg [[Hall2011Lancet]].
- **Reversing the US trend** would need average intake to fall by about 2000 kJ (500 kcal) a day for adults and 1500 kJ (350 kcal) a day for children. The alternative is large increases in activity, such as 110 to 150 minutes of walking a day (population-level model, not an individual target) [[Swinburn2009AJCN]].
- **Two models of cause:** the carbohydrate-insulin model proposes that high-glycaemic-load diets drive fat storage, which then drives eating (Hypothesis) [[Ludwig2021CIM]].
- The energy balance model describes the brain as the main regulator of body weight, mostly below conscious awareness, through endocrine, metabolic and nervous-system signals responding to the body's needs and to the food environment (Established framework) [[Hall2022EBM]].

### Exam hooks
- Twin heritability of BMI is 0.47–0.90, with a median of 0.75. Family studies give lower estimates (median 0.46) [[Elks2012]].
- Identical twins reared apart: BMI correlation 0.70 (men) and 0.66 (women). Shared childhood environment adds little in adult life [[Stunkard1990]].
- In adoptees, BMI tracks with biological parents, not adoptive parents [[Stunkard1986]].
- FTO was the first common obesity variant. Two copies of the risk allele mean about 3 kg more weight and OR 1.67 for obesity [[Frayling2007]].
- 97 loci explain about 2.7% of BMI variance. All common variants together explain more than 20% [[Locke2015]].
- Top vs bottom polygenic-score decile: 13 kg weight difference and 25-fold risk of severe obesity in adults. Only 0.06 kg difference at birth [[Khera2019]].
- MC4R mutations are the most common monogenic cause: 5.8% of severe childhood-onset obesity, with codominant inheritance and hyperphagia [[Farooqi2003MC4R]].
- POMC deficiency triad: early obesity, adrenal insufficiency, red hair [[Krude1998]].
- Leptin receptor deficiency: obesity plus no puberty and reduced GH and TSH [[Clement1998]].
- Congenital leptin deficiency responds to recombinant leptin therapy [[Farooqi2002Leptin]].
- Prader-Willi syndrome: loss of paternal 15q11.2-q13, most often by paternal deletion (65–75%) [[Cassidy2012]].
- Ultra-processed vs unprocessed diet (matched nutrients): +508 kcal a day and +0.9 kg in 2 weeks [[Hall2019UPF]].
- Short sleep and obesity: OR 1.89 in children and 1.55 in adults [[Cappuccio2008]].
- Weight gain after quitting smoking: about 4–5 kg at 12 months, mostly in the first 3 months [[Aubin2012]].
- Of the antipsychotics, clozapine and olanzapine cause the most weight gain. In the studies compared, the least came with ziprasidone (Allison), haloperidol (Leucht) and aripiprazole (Correll, still 4.4 kg in youth) [[Allison1999,Leucht2013,Correll2009]].
- Hall rule: 100 kJ a day per kg of eventual weight change. Half the change comes in about 1 year and 95% in about 3 years. The 3500 kcal per pound rule overestimates weight loss [[Hall2011Lancet]].
- In a friend network, OR rises 57% if a friend develops obesity, but not when a neighbour does [[Christakis2007]].
- FTO's effect is about 27% smaller in physically active adults [[Kilpelainen2011]].

### In plain words
- Obesity is not a choice or a lack of willpower. Genes, biology and the world around us all shape body weight [[Rubino2020Stigma]].
- Body size runs in families mostly because of shared genes, not shared kitchens. Adopted children resemble their birth parents more than the parents who raised them [[Stunkard1986]].
- Hundreds of small gene differences each nudge weight a little. Added together, they can make a big difference [[Locke2015,Khera2019]].
- A few children have one powerful gene change, such as in MC4R or leptin, that makes them feel hungry all the time. A genetic test can find this [[Farooqi2003MC4R,Montague1997]].
- Genes load the dice, but the environment rolls them. The same genes led to much less obesity in a traditional, active setting [[Schulz2006]].
- Highly processed foods make it easy to eat more without noticing. In one study people ate about 500 calories a day more on them [[Hall2019UPF]].
- Short sleep raises hunger hormones. Sleeping longer helped people eat less in one trial [[Spiegel2004,Tasali2022]].
- Some medicines, such as certain mental-health, diabetes and steroid medicines, can cause weight gain. Never stop a medicine on your own: ask your clinician if there are other options [[Domecq2015,Apovian2015]].
- After quitting smoking, people gain about 4 to 5 kg in a year on average. Some lose weight and some gain more [[Aubin2012]].
- Weight changes slowly. A small, lasting change in eating takes years to show its full effect [[Hall2011Lancet]].
- Where you live and how much money you have can affect weight. Moving to a less poor neighbourhood lowered severe obesity in a randomised study [[Ludwig2011MTO]].
- Weight coming back after weight loss is the body defending its weight. It is biology, not failure [[Rubino2020Stigma]].
- If eating or weight causes you distress, or you restrict, binge or purge, talk to a clinician or an eating-disorder service.

## Open questions and controversies
- **What drove the epidemic: more food or less movement?** One side argues that occupational energy use fell by more than 100 kcal a day and explains much of US weight gain [[Church2011]]. The other side finds that doubly labelled water data show no fall in activity expenditure since the 1980s [[Westerterp2008]], that the rise in food energy supply is enough to explain the weight gain [[Swinburn2009AJCN,Vandevijvere2015]], and that lower expenditure explains only about one tenth of development-linked adiposity [[McGrosky2025]]. Status: most doubly labelled water evidence favours intake as the larger driver. The occupational analysis relies on modelled, not measured, expenditure.
- **Carbohydrate-insulin model vs energy balance model:** one view is that a high-glycaemic-load diet raises insulin, which drives fat storage and then hunger [[Ludwig2021CIM]]. The other is that the brain regulates intake against energy needs and environmental cues, and that the CIM is a narrower special case [[Hall2022EBM]]. Status: an open scientific debate. The ultra-processed food trial found extra intake from both carbohydrate and fat [[Hall2019UPF]].
- **Does physical activity add to total energy expenditure without limit?** The additive model and the constrained model disagree. Doubly labelled water data show total expenditure levelling off at high activity [[Pontzer2016]], and Hadza foragers spend similar size-adjusted energy to Western adults [[Pontzer2012]]. Status: Early evidence. It does not mean activity lacks health benefits.
- **Why are ultra-processed foods eaten in excess?** Proposed reasons are faster eating rate, energy density, and taste or texture [[Hall2019UPF]]. The trial was small (n=20), short (2 weeks per diet) and inpatient. Status: mechanism unresolved.
- **Is the social spread of obesity causal?** Network clustering has been reported up to three degrees of separation [[Christakis2007]]. The design is observational, so shared environments and people choosing similar friends cannot be fully excluded. Status: debated.
- **Do microbes cause human obesity?** Microbe transfer causes weight gain in mice [[Turnbaugh2006,Ridaura2013]]. In humans, early antibiotics are associated with childhood obesity (RR 1.21) [[Shao2017]], but a placebo-controlled faecal transplant changed neither insulin sensitivity nor body composition [[Yu2020FMT]]. Status: Hypothesis in humans.
- **How heritable is BMI really?** Twin studies give 0.47–0.90, family studies 0.24–0.81 [[Elks2012]], and all common variants together more than 20% [[Locke2015]]. Study design explains nearly half of the variation between twin estimates (the "missing heritability" gap) [[Elks2012]].
- **How penetrant are MC4R mutations?** In a clinic series of severe childhood obesity they are common (5.8%) [[Farooqi2003MC4R]]. In the general population carriers are 1 in 337, with a large average weight excess at 18 years [[Wade2021MC4R]]. Yet only 1 of 9 adult carriers in UK Biobank had severe obesity [[Khera2019]]. Status: penetrance varies with age and setting.
- **Thrifty or drifty genes?** One position is that famine selected for fat-storing genes. The other is that relaxed selection and random drift better explain the spread of risk variants [[Speakman2008]]. Status: Hypothesis on both sides.
- **Gestational diabetes and child weight:** obesity is associated with gestational diabetes after adjusting for maternal BMI (OR 1.58), but overweight or obesity combined is not (OR 1.21, CI to 1.46) [[Lowe2018HAPO]]. Maternal pre-pregnancy BMI shows stronger associations [[Voerman2019]]. Status: separating intrauterine effects from shared genes and environment is unresolved.

## Provenance
- All 77 journal sources were checked against PubMed E-utilities esummary records in a single batch. Author lists, titles, journals, years, volume, issue, pages, PMIDs, PMCIDs and DOIs in the Sources lines were generated directly from those records. Every number was read in the PubMed or Europe PMC abstract, or in PubMed efetch text where the Europe PMC abstract was cut short.

- Farooqi1999Leptin has no PubMed abstract and was not read in full. It is cited only for what its title states, and the treatment details come from Farooqi2002Leptin. Lacroix2015 is cited only for the definition in its abstract.
- Nothing is marked (unverified). Dropped for lack of a checkable source: valproate weight-gain magnitude (not in the Domecq meta-analysis; no separate source validated), the Ong 2000 BMJ catch-up growth paper (not found by search, replaced by Druet 2012), and any claim about chemical "obesogens".
