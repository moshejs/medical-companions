# G · Metabolic & bariatric surgery

Pass date: 2026-11-11. Modules served: surgery, aftersurgery, pregnancy.

This pass covers the operations and endoscopic procedures used to treat obesity, how they work beyond "making the stomach smaller", who they are offered to (ASMBS/IFSO 2022 versus the 1991 NIH criteria), what the long-term studies show (SOS, LABS, STAMPEDE, SLEEVEPASS, SM-BOSS, Teen-LABS, large matched cohorts and meta-analyses), the risks (early surgical complications, reflux and Barrett's oesophagus after sleeve, dumping and post-bariatric hypoglycaemia, internal hernia, gallstones, micronutrient deficiencies, bone, alcohol use disorder, self-harm, weight recurrence) and pregnancy after surgery. Every number comes from the PubMed abstract of the cited paper, or, where named, from the open full text in Europe PMC. All results are population averages from trials and cohorts. They are not predictions for any one person, and none of the doses or thresholds below are instructions.

## Findings

### surgery · How many operations, and which
- About 280,000 metabolic and bariatric operations were done in the United States in 2022, up from about 262,893 in 2021 (+6.5%) and +41% from 2020; the ASMBS estimates that about 1% of people who qualify have surgery [[Clapp2024ASMBS]].
- Worldwide, IFSO national societies reported 507,806 surgical and endoluminal procedures in 2020 and 598,834 in 2021; sleeve gastrectomy remained the most performed operation and adjustable gastric banding continued to decline [[Angrisani2024IFSO]].
- Sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass together account for about 90% of operations worldwide; vertical banded gastroplasty is no longer performed and the adjustable band "has diminished significantly over the past decade" [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- A 2020 JAMA review counted about 252,000 US procedures in 2018, of which an estimated 15% were revisions [[Arterburn2020JAMA]].
- Contemporary operations are done laparoscopically in the vast majority of cases, with faster recovery, less pain and fewer wound complications than open surgery; a 2017 review puts mortality at three in 1,000 patients [[Nguyen2017NatRev]].

### surgery · The procedures and their anatomy
- Sleeve gastrectomy removes the greater curvature of the stomach. One review describes resection of "approximately 80%" of the stomach [[Kim2026Complications]]; another describes a vertical division that reduces stomach size "by 75%" while preserving the pylorus [[Lupoli2017]].
- In one sleeve series the resection started 6 cm from the pylorus and was calibrated on a 48 Fr bougie, with the staple line oversewn [[Genco2017]].
- Resecting the greater curvature during a sleeve lowers plasma ghrelin, a hunger-promoting hormone [[Kim2026Complications,Karamanakos2008]].
- Roux-en-Y gastric bypass creates a small gastric pouch joined to a jejunal Roux limb, bypassing most of the stomach, the duodenum and a variable length of proximal small bowel [[Kim2026Complications]].
- In the YOMEGA trial the bypass used a 150 cm alimentary (Roux) limb and a 50 cm biliary limb; the one-anastomosis gastric bypass used a single gastrojejunal anastomosis with a 200 cm biliopancreatic limb [[Robert2019YOMEGA]].
- A longer biliopancreatic limb has been associated with greater weight loss and better glucose control than a longer Roux limb (Early evidence) [[Kim2026Complications]].
- Adjustable gastric banding places a silicone band a few centimetres below the cardia; the outlet is adjusted by adding or removing saline through a port under the skin [[Lupoli2017]].
- Biliopancreatic diversion leaves 200-250 mL of upper stomach joined to the distal 250 cm of small bowel, with a 50 cm common channel where food and digestive juices mix, producing marked fat and protein malabsorption [[Lupoli2017]].
- Biliopancreatic diversion and its duodenal-switch variant are used in under 2% of operations because of their high risk of nutritional complications [[Lupoli2017]].
- Single-anastomosis duodeno-ileal bypass with sleeve gastrectomy (SADI-S) appears in the IFSO/ASMBS evidence review among reported procedures, for example in one study of Asian patients with BMI below 30 [[DeLuca2024IFSO]].
- Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty (ESG) is an "endolumenal, organ-sparing" procedure done through the mouth with an endoscope [[AbuDayyeh2022MERIT]].
- Intragastric balloons are placed endoscopically; in a randomised Orbera trial they were removed after 6 months [[Courcoulas2017IGB]].

### surgery · Mechanisms beyond restriction
- After gastric bypass, postprandial PYY and GLP-1 rose and insulin responses were early and exaggerated compared with lean controls and controls with obesity; none of these changes occurred in people who lost equivalent weight with a gastric band (Established) [[leRoux2006]].
- In rats with a jejuno-intestinal bypass, giving PYY reduced food intake and blocking endogenous PYY increased it, supporting a causal role for PYY (Early evidence) [[leRoux2006]].
- In a randomised double-blind study (16 bypass, 16 sleeve), fasting ghrelin fell markedly after sleeve gastrectomy but did not change significantly after bypass; fasting PYY rose after both [[Karamanakos2008]].
- In the same study, excess weight loss at 12 months was 69.7% after sleeve versus 60.5% after bypass (P = 0.05), with greater appetite suppression after sleeve [[Karamanakos2008]].
- Diet-induced weight loss of 17% raised the 24-hour ghrelin area under the curve by 24%; after a 36% weight loss with gastric bypass, the ghrelin area under the curve was 77% lower than in normal-weight controls and meal-related ghrelin rhythms were absent [[Cummings2002]].
- In mice with diet-induced obesity, the benefits of vertical sleeve gastrectomy did not come from mechanical restriction; sleeve raised circulating bile acids and changed gut microbes, and without the bile-acid receptor FXR the weight and glucose benefits were "substantially reduced" (Early evidence; animal data) [[Ryan2014Nature]].
- Gastric bypass and vertical banded gastroplasty produced durable gut-microbiome changes in people that did not depend on BMI; germ-free mice given these patients' stool deposited less fat (Hypothesis for humans; mouse transfer data) [[Tremaroli2015]].
- Diabetes-surgery consensus: the gastrointestinal tract "constitutes a meaningful target" for managing type 2 diabetes because of its role in metabolic regulation [[Rubino2016DSS]].

### surgery · Who is offered surgery: indications
- 1991 NIH consensus: people seeking treatment for severe obesity should first be considered for a non-surgical programme; gastric restrictive or bypass procedures could be considered for well-informed patients with acceptable operative risk, selected by a multidisciplinary team, with lifelong medical surveillance after surgery [[NIH1991Consensus]].
- The 1991 thresholds that became the universal standard were BMI >40 kg/m², or BMI >35 kg/m² with comorbidities [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS,Arterburn2020JAMA]].
- ASMBS/IFSO 2022 (as published): metabolic and bariatric surgery "is recommended for individuals with a body mass index (BMI) >35 kg/m², regardless of presence, absence, or severity of co-morbidities" [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- ASMBS/IFSO 2022 (as published): surgery "should be considered for individuals with metabolic disease and BMI of 30-34.9 kg/m²" [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- ASMBS/IFSO 2022 (as published): in Asian populations a BMI >25 kg/m² suggests clinical obesity, and individuals with BMI >27.5 kg/m² should be offered surgery [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- ASMBS/IFSO 2022: "Appropriately selected children and adolescents should be considered" for surgery [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- ASMBS/IFSO 2022: there is "no evidence to support an age limit"; surgery has been done in people over 70, with slightly higher complication rates in septuagenarians, and frailty assessment is recommended [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- ASMBS/IFSO 2022: there are "no data to support" insurance-mandated preoperative weight loss, which the statement calls "discriminatory, arbitrary, and scientifically unfounded" [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- Diabetes Surgery Summit 2016 (endorsed by 45 societies): surgery should be recommended for type 2 diabetes with BMI ≥40, and with BMI 35.0-39.9 when glucose is inadequately controlled; it should be considered at BMI 30.0-34.9 if glucose is inadequately controlled despite optimal treatment; thresholds are lowered by 2.5 kg/m² for Asian patients [[Rubino2016DSS]].
- ASMBS pediatric guideline 2018: adolescents with class II obesity (≥120% of the 95th percentile) plus a listed comorbidity, or class III obesity (≥140% of the 95th percentile), "should be considered" for surgery [[Pratt2018ASMBS]].
- ASMBS pediatric guideline 2018: adolescents with cognitive disabilities, treated mental illness or eating disorders, immature bone growth or low Tanner stage "should not be denied treatment" [[Pratt2018ASMBS]].
- ASMBS/IFSO 2022: surgery does not harm pubertal development or linear growth, so a specific Tanner stage or bone age should not be required [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- Shared decision-making: a JAMA review advises that all patients with severe obesity, especially those with type 2 diabetes, be offered a conversation about surgery versus continuing usual care, driven by informed patient preference [[Arterburn2020JAMA]].
- The 2019 AACE/TOS/ASMBS/OMA/ASA perioperative guideline has 85 numbered recommendations and states that a team approach to perioperative care "is mandatory", with attention to nutritional and metabolic issues [[Mechanick2020SOARD]].

### surgery · Weight outcomes over time
- SOS (2010 surgery, 2037 matched controls): maximum weight loss came at 1-2 years: gastric bypass 32%, vertical banded gastroplasty 25%, banding 20%; at 10 years losses stabilised at 25%, 16% and 14%; controls changed by less than ±2% [[Sjostrom2007SOS]].
- SOS mean weight change in the surgery group was -23% at 2 years, -17% at 10, -16% at 15 and -18% at 20 years, versus 0%, +1%, -1% and -1% in controls [[Sjostrom2013JIM]].
- SOS surgery mix was mostly vertical banded gastroplasty (68%), with banding 19% and gastric bypass 13%, so SOS results describe older operations [[Sjostrom2013JIM]].
- LABS-2 (US, 10 hospitals): 3 years after bypass the median weight loss was 31.5% of baseline weight; after adjustable band it was 15.9%; most of the loss was evident by 1 year [[Courcoulas2013LABS]].
- LABS-2 at 7 years: mean weight loss was 28.4% after bypass (38.2 kg) and 14.9% after band (18.8 kg); between years 3 and 7, mean regain was 3.9% of baseline weight after bypass [[Courcoulas2018LABS]].
- LABS-2: reoperations by 7 years occurred in 14 bypass and 160 band participants [[Courcoulas2018LABS]].
- SLEEVEPASS (240 randomised, Finland): mean excess weight loss at 5 years was 49% after sleeve and 57% after bypass (difference 8.2 percentage points, 95% CI 3.2-13.2); the procedures did not meet the equivalence criteria [[Salminen2018SLEEVEPASS]].
- SLEEVEPASS at 10 years: median excess weight loss was 43.5% after sleeve and 50.7% after bypass; bypass was 8.4 points higher (95% CI 3.1-13.6) [[Salminen2022SLEEVEPASS]].
- SM-BOSS (217 randomised, Switzerland): excess BMI loss at 5 years was 61.1% after sleeve and 68.3% after bypass, not significantly different (P = .22 after adjustment) [[Peterli2018SMBOSS]].
- YOMEGA (253 randomised, France): excess BMI loss at 2 years was 87.9% after one-anastomosis bypass and 85.8% after Roux-en-Y bypass, confirming non-inferiority [[Robert2019YOMEGA]].
- In people with BMI 50-60, BMI fell by 22.1 units after duodenal switch versus 13.6 after gastric bypass at 5 years (difference 8.5) [[Risstad2015JAMASurg]].
- A 2004 meta-analysis (22,094 patients) found mean excess weight loss of 47.5% for banding, 61.6% for gastric bypass, 68.2% for gastroplasty and 70.1% for biliopancreatic diversion or duodenal switch [[Buchwald2004JAMA]].
- In a matched US cohort, the surgery group weighed 24.8 kg (19.2%) less than controls at 10 years [[Aminian2022SPLENDID]].

### surgery · Endoscopic options
- MERIT (209 randomised, class 1-2 obesity): at 52 weeks, total body weight loss was 13.6% after ESG plus lifestyle versus 0.8% with lifestyle alone; 68% of ESG participants kept at least 25% excess weight loss at 104 weeks [[AbuDayyeh2022MERIT]].
- MERIT: ESG-related serious adverse events occurred in 2% (3 of 131), without deaths, intensive care or surgery [[AbuDayyeh2022MERIT]].
- Orbera balloon RCT (255 adults, BMI 30-40): weight loss was 10.2% with balloon plus lifestyle versus 3.3% with lifestyle at 6 months, and 7.6% versus 3.1% at 12 months (6 months after removal) [[Courcoulas2017IGB]].
- In that trial, 86.9% of balloon recipients had nausea, 75.6% vomiting, and 18.8% had the balloon removed early [[Courcoulas2017IGB]].
- An ASGE meta-analysis (17 studies, 1683 patients) found 11.27% total body weight loss 12 months after Orbera placement [[AbuDayyeh2015ASGE]].

### surgery · Diabetes, blood pressure, sleep apnoea
- STAMPEDE (150 people with type 2 diabetes, BMI 27-43): HbA1c ≤6.0% at 5 years was reached by 5% with intensive medical therapy alone, 29% after gastric bypass and 23% after sleeve [[Schauer2017STAMPEDE]].
- STAMPEDE at 5 years: weight changed by -23% (bypass), -19% (sleeve) and -5% (medical therapy); insulin use fell by 35%, 34% and 13% [[Schauer2017STAMPEDE]].
- Rome RCT (60 people with type 2 diabetes, BMI ≥35): 10-year remission was 5.5% with medical therapy, 50.0% after biliopancreatic diversion and 25.0% after gastric bypass [[Mingrone2021Lancet]].
- In that trial, 58.8% of those in remission at 2 years relapsed by 10 years, but all kept adequate control (mean HbA1c 6.7%), and both surgical groups had fewer diabetes complications (relative risk 0.07) [[Mingrone2021Lancet]].
- ARMMS-T2D (pooled 4 US RCTs, 262 people, median follow-up 11 years): HbA1c fell by 1.6% after surgery versus 0.2% with medical/lifestyle care at 7 years; remission was 18.2% versus 6.2% at 7 years and 12.7% versus 0.0% at 12 years [[Courcoulas2024ARMMS]].
- SOS: diabetes remission at 2 years was 72.3% after surgery versus 16.4% in controls; at 15 years 30.4% versus 6.5% [[Sjostrom2014JAMA]].
- SOS: microvascular complications occurred at 20.6 versus 41.8 per 1000 person-years (HR 0.44), and macrovascular at 31.7 versus 44.2 (HR 0.68) [[Sjostrom2014JAMA]].
- SOS: in people without diabetes at baseline, new type 2 diabetes occurred at 6.8 versus 28.4 per 1000 person-years (adjusted HR 0.17) [[Carlsson2012NEJM]].
- LABS-2: diabetes remission after bypass was 71.2% at 1 year, 69.4% at 3, 64.6% at 5 and 60.2% at 7 years; after band 30.7%, 29.3%, 29.2% and 20.3% [[Courcoulas2018LABS]].
- SLEEVEPASS at 10 years: type 2 diabetes remission was 26% after sleeve and 33% after bypass (not significant); hypertension remission was 8% versus 24% (P = .04) [[Salminen2022SLEEVEPASS]].
- GATEWAY (100 people with hypertension, BMI 30-39.9): at 12 months, 83.7% after gastric bypass versus 12.8% with medical therapy cut antihypertensive drugs by ≥30% with controlled blood pressure; 51% of the bypass group were off all blood pressure medicines [[Schiavon2018GATEWAY]].
- 2004 meta-analysis: diabetes resolved in 76.8%, hypertension in 61.7% and obstructive sleep apnoea in 85.7% of patients [[Buchwald2004JAMA]].
- SLEEVEPASS at 10 years: obstructive sleep apnoea remission was 16% after sleeve and 31% after bypass (not significant), much lower than older pooled estimates [[Salminen2022SLEEVEPASS]].

### surgery · Survival, heart disease and cancer
- SOS (mean 10.9 years): 101 deaths in the surgery group versus 129 in controls; adjusted hazard ratio 0.71 (P = 0.01) [[Sjostrom2007SOS]].
- SOS: the commonest causes of death were myocardial infarction (13 surgery versus 25 control) and cancer (29 versus 47) [[Sjostrom2007SOS]].
- SOS life expectancy (median follow-up 24 years): adjusted median life expectancy was 3.0 years (95% CI 1.8-4.2) longer after surgery than with usual care, but 5.5 years shorter than in the general population [[Carlsson2020NEJM]].
- SOS: overall mortality hazard ratio 0.77 (95% CI 0.68-0.87); cardiovascular death 0.70; cancer death 0.77; 90-day postoperative mortality was 0.2% and 2.9% had repeat surgery [[Carlsson2020NEJM]].
- SOS: cardiovascular deaths were 28 versus 49 (adjusted HR 0.47), and first-time myocardial infarction or stroke 199 versus 234 (adjusted HR 0.67) [[Sjostrom2012JAMA]].
- SOS: first-time cancers were 117 versus 169 (HR 0.67); the reduction was seen in women (HR 0.58) but not in men (HR 0.97) [[Sjostrom2009LancetOncol]].
- Utah cohort (7925 matched pairs, mean 7.1 years): all-cause mortality was 40% lower after gastric bypass (37.6 versus 57.1 per 10,000 person-years); coronary deaths fell 56%, diabetes deaths 92% and cancer deaths 60% [[Adams2007NEJM]].
- Utah cohort: deaths not caused by disease, such as accidents and suicide, were 58% higher after surgery (11.1 versus 6.4 per 10,000 person-years) [[Adams2007NEJM]].
- US veterans (2500 surgical, 74% men): mortality was not lower in the first year (HR 1.28) but was lower at 1-5 years (HR 0.45) and 5-14 years (HR 0.47); Kaplan-Meier mortality at 10 years was 13.8% versus 23.9% [[Arterburn2015JAMA]].
- Meta-analysis of 174,772 participants: surgery was associated with 49.2% lower hazard of death and 6.1 years longer median life expectancy; the gain was 9.3 years with diabetes and 5.1 years without [[Syn2021Lancet]].
- Same meta-analysis: the number needed to treat to prevent one death over 10 years was 8.4 with diabetes and 29.8 without; effects did not differ between bypass, banding and sleeve [[Syn2021Lancet]].
- Cleveland Clinic cohort (2287 surgical, 11,435 matched controls with type 2 diabetes): 8-year incidence of extended MACE was 30.8% versus 47.7% (adjusted HR 0.61); all-cause mortality 10.0% versus 17.8% (adjusted HR 0.59) [[Aminian2019JAMA]].
- SPLENDID (5053 surgical, 25,265 controls): 10-year incidence of obesity-associated cancer was 2.9% versus 4.9% (adjusted HR 0.68), and cancer death 0.8% versus 1.4% (adjusted HR 0.52) [[Aminian2022SPLENDID]].
- The MACE analysis is observational, and its authors state the findings must be confirmed in randomised trials [[Aminian2019JAMA]].

### surgery · Adolescents
- Teen-LABS 3 years (242 adolescents, mean BMI 53): mean weight fell 27%; type 2 diabetes remitted in 95% and elevated blood pressure in 74%, but 57% had low ferritin and 13% had further abdominal procedures [[Inge2016NEJM]].
- Teen-LABS versus adult LABS 5 years after bypass: weight change was -26% in adolescents and -29% in adults (P = 0.08); diabetes remitted in 86% versus 53% and hypertension in 68% versus 41% [[Inge2019NEJM]].
- In the same comparison, 1.9% of adolescents and 1.8% of adults died within 5 years, abdominal reoperations were more frequent in adolescents (19 versus 10 per 500 person-years), and low ferritin was more common (48% versus 29%) [[Inge2019NEJM]].
- AMOS (Sweden, 81 adolescents): BMI fell by 13.1 units at 5 years after bypass while it rose by 3.3 in adolescent controls; 25% needed further abdominal surgery and 72% had some nutritional deficiency [[Olbers2017AMOS]].

### aftersurgery · Early surgical risk
- LABS (4776 first-time operations, 2005-2007): 30-day mortality after bypass or band was 0.3% and 4.3% had at least one major adverse outcome [[Flum2009LABS]].
- In LABS, prior deep-vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, obstructive sleep apnoea, impaired functional status and extreme BMI predicted the composite adverse outcome; age, sex and race did not [[Flum2009LABS]].
- Current perioperative mortality is reported at 0.03% to 0.2%, substantially improved since the early 2000s [[Arterburn2020JAMA,Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- Operative mortality (≤30 days) in the 2004 meta-analysis was 0.1% for purely restrictive procedures, 0.5% for gastric bypass and 1.1% for biliopancreatic diversion or duodenal switch [[Buchwald2004JAMA]].
- A 2026 review gives early bleeding at 0.6-2.5% after sleeve and 1-1.5% after bypass, leak about 1% after sleeve and 0.6% after bypass, and venous thromboembolism 0.3-2.4% [[Kim2026Complications]].
- About 70-80% of venous thromboembolism after bariatric surgery is diagnosed after hospital discharge [[Kim2026Complications]].
- Signs of a leak can be nonspecific; sustained tachycardia, fever, abdominal or shoulder pain and breathing changes are warning features [[Kim2026Complications]].

### aftersurgery · Late complications
- Reported late complication ranges: reflux 23-28%, sleeve stenosis or twist 0.5-3.5%, gastrojejunal stricture about 6%, marginal ulcer 6-16%, internal hernia 0.2-9%, gallstones up to 50% [[Kim2026Complications]].
- In 350 bypass patients, 6.6% developed a symptomatic marginal ulcer and 1.1% a perforation; smoking, corticosteroids and NSAIDs were associated risk factors [[Coblijn2015]].
- Swedish RCT (2507 bypass patients): closing the mesenteric defects cut 3-year reoperation for small bowel obstruction from 10.2% to 5.5% (HR 0.56), at the cost of more early severe complications (4.3% versus 2.8%), mainly kinking [[Stenberg2016Lancet]].
- Gallstones form in about 10-50% after surgery, driven by rapid weight loss, typically in the first 6-12 months [[Kim2026Complications]].
- UPGRADE (985 randomised): ursodeoxycholic acid 900 mg daily for 6 months did not significantly reduce symptomatic gallstone disease overall (6.5% versus 9.7%, RR 0.67, P = 0.071), but did in people without gallstones before bypass (RR 0.37) [[Haal2021UPGRADE]].
- Reoperation or intervention by 5 years was 15.8% after sleeve and 22.1% after bypass in SM-BOSS [[Peterli2018SMBOSS]], and 15.7% versus 18.5% by 10 years in SLEEVEPASS [[Salminen2022SLEEVEPASS]].
- Duodenal switch caused more surgical, nutritional and gastrointestinal adverse effects than bypass: 44.8% versus 9.7% had further procedures related to the first operation [[Risstad2015JAMASurg]].
- One-anastomosis bypass with a 200 cm biliopancreatic limb had more serious adverse events than Roux-en-Y bypass (42 versus 24), including nine nutritional complications versus none [[Robert2019YOMEGA]].

### aftersurgery · Reflux and Barrett's oesophagus after sleeve
- SM-BOSS: reflux worsened in 31.8% after sleeve versus 6.3% after bypass, and remitted in 25.0% versus 60.4% [[Peterli2018SMBOSS]].
- SLEEVEPASS at 10 years: endoscopic oesophagitis in 31% after sleeve versus 7% after bypass; Barrett's oesophagus 4% versus 4% [[Salminen2022SLEEVEPASS]].
- Meta-analysis (10 studies, 680 patients scoped 6 months to 10 years after sleeve): pooled Barrett's prevalence was 11.6% (95% CI 8.1-16.4) and was not correlated with reflux symptoms [[Qumseya2021]].
- An Italian series found new non-dysplastic Barrett's in 17.2% at a mean of 58 months after sleeve, unrelated to symptoms [[Genco2017]].
- The size of the Barrett's risk after sleeve is disputed: 4% in the 10-year RCT [[Salminen2022SLEEVEPASS]] versus 11.6% pooled across mostly observational studies [[Qumseya2021]].

### aftersurgery · Dumping and post-bariatric hypoglycaemia
- Dumping consensus: diagnostic criteria include a rise in haematocrit >3% or pulse >10 bpm 30 minutes into a glucose test for early dumping, and glucose nadir <50 mg/dL for late dumping [[Scarpellini2020]].
- Dumping consensus: dietary adjustment is the first treatment step; acarbose helps late dumping; somatostatin analogues are preferred if diet and acarbose fail [[Scarpellini2020]].
- Early dumping is reported in up to 40% and late dumping in up to 25% of patients after surgery [[Kim2026Complications]].
- Swedish registry (5040 operated): after gastric bypass the adjusted hazard ratio for hospitalisation with hypoglycaemia was 2.7, but only 0.2% of bypass patients were affected versus 0.04% of referents; no excess was seen after gastroplasty or banding [[Marsk2010]].
- Hyperinsulinaemic hypoglycaemia with nesidioblastosis after gastric bypass was first described in six patients in 2005 [[Service2005NEJM]].
- Post-bariatric hypoglycaemia typically presents more than 1 year after surgery, with neuroglycopenic symptoms such as confusion or seizures [[Kim2026Complications]].

### aftersurgery · Nutrition and supplements
- Many people already have at least one vitamin or mineral deficiency before surgery, and the ASMBS 2016 guideline covers screening, prevention and repletion of B1, B12, folate, iron, vitamins A, E, K and D, calcium, copper and zinc [[Parrott2017ASMBS]].
- The ASMBS micronutrient guideline notes deficiencies are becoming more common while follow-up monitoring is declining [[Parrott2017ASMBS]].
- Anaemia affects 33-49% within 2 years of surgery, about 17% after sleeve and 45-50% after bypass or biliopancreatic diversion; iron deficiency (low ferritin) occurs in more than 30% at 5 years [[Lupoli2017]].
- Vitamin B12 deficiency affects 19-35% at 5 years after biliopancreatic diversion and bypass, because the duodenum is bypassed and gastric acid and intrinsic factor fall [[Lupoli2017]].
- Thiamine (B1) deficiency can occur in up to 49% after surgery and, untreated, can lead to Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome; persistent vomiting is a trigger [[Lupoli2017]].
- Vitamin D deficiency (<20 ng/mL) is seen in about 60% of operated patients across studies [[Lupoli2017]].
- Vitamin A deficiency was found in 61-69% at 2-4 years after biliopancreatic diversion [[Lupoli2017]].
- Supplement content as cited by a 2026 review from ASMBS guidance (not instructions): a bariatric multivitamin with at least 350-1000 μg vitamin B12 and 400-800 μg folic acid; 1200-1500 mg/day elemental calcium, preferably citrate in divided doses; at least 3000 IU/day vitamin D titrated to 25-OH vitamin D above 30 ng/mL [[Kim2026Complications]].
- Iron and calcium supplements should be taken apart because calcium interferes with iron, zinc and copper absorption [[Lupoli2017,Kim2026Complications]].
- When iron deficiency is established after surgery, oral iron may not be enough and intravenous iron may be needed [[Lupoli2017]].

### aftersurgery · Bone
- Quebec (12,676 operated): after surgery, fracture risk was higher than in matched controls with obesity (RR 1.38) and without (RR 1.44); the pattern shifted from lower-limb fractures to upper-limb, spine, pelvis, hip and femur [[Rousseau2016BMJ]].
- In that study the fracture increase reached significance only for biliopancreatic diversion; results for bypass and sleeve were inconclusive [[Rousseau2016BMJ]].
- ARMMS-T2D also found anaemia, fractures and gastrointestinal adverse events more often after surgery than with medical care [[Courcoulas2024ARMMS]].

### aftersurgery · Alcohol and mental health
- LABS-2: alcohol use disorder symptoms were 7.6% the year before surgery, 7.3% at 1 year and 9.6% in the second year after; bypass versus band doubled the odds (AOR 2.07) [[King2012JAMA]].
- LABS-2 at 5 years: cumulative incidence of new alcohol use disorder symptoms was 20.8% after bypass versus 11.3% after band (AHR 2.08) [[King2017SOARD]].
- SOS: compared with controls, gastric bypass raised the risk of alcohol abuse diagnoses (adjusted HR 4.97); banding did not [[Svensson2013SOS]].
- A systematic review estimated a suicide rate of 4.1 per 10,000 person-years after bariatric surgery, higher than in the general population [[Peterhansel2013]].
- Ontario (8815 adults): self-harm emergencies rose from 2.33 to 3.63 per 1000 patient-years after surgery (RR 1.54); 93% of events were in people with a mental health diagnosis in the 5 years before surgery [[Bhatti2016]].
- Meta-analysis (148,643 people): self-harm or suicide attempt odds were 1.9 times higher after surgery than before, and 3.8 times higher than in matched controls [[Castaneda2019]].
- These findings support psychological screening and follow-up before and after surgery, and alcohol education and screening [[Peterhansel2013,King2017SOARD]].

### aftersurgery · Weight recurrence
- LABS-2 bypass (1406 people): median maximum weight loss was 37.4% at a median 2.0 years; regain rose from a median 9.5% of the maximum lost 1 year after nadir to 26.8% at 5 years [[King2018JAMA]].
- Five years after nadir, 67.3% had regained ≥20% of the maximum weight lost; regain measured as percentage of maximum weight lost best tracked the return of diabetes, high blood pressure and quality-of-life decline [[King2018JAMA]].
- Most LABS-2 participants regained only a small share of their year-3 loss by year 7, but trajectories varied widely [[Courcoulas2018LABS]].
- Revisional surgery achieves further weight loss in selected patients but carries more complications than a first operation [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].

### pregnancy · Pregnancy after surgery
- Swedish register (670 post-surgery pregnancies, matched controls): gestational diabetes 1.9% versus 6.8% (OR 0.25) and large-for-gestational-age babies 8.6% versus 22.4% (OR 0.33) [[Johansson2015NEJM]].
- Same study: small-for-gestational-age babies 15.6% versus 7.6% (OR 2.20), gestation 4.5 days shorter, and stillbirth or neonatal death 1.7% versus 0.7% (OR 2.39, 95% CI 0.98-5.85, P = 0.06) [[Johansson2015NEJM]].
- Meta-analysis (33 studies, 14,880 post-surgery pregnancies): higher odds of perinatal death (OR 1.38), congenital anomalies (1.29), preterm birth (1.57) and NICU admission (1.41); after gastric bypass, SGA rose (OR 2.72) and LGA fell (OR 0.24) [[Akhter2019PLoSMed]].
- Babies born after maternal bariatric surgery weighed on average 242 g less [[Akhter2019PLoSMed]].
- International consensus 2019: postpone pregnancy until weight is stable, typically about 1 year after sleeve or bypass and 2 years after a band [[Shawe2019ObesRev]].
- The same consensus advises contraceptive counselling before surgery, avoiding combined oral contraceptives, and encouraging long-acting reversible contraception [[Shawe2019ObesRev]].
- The consensus lists a minimum preconception multivitamin content (as published) including folic acid 5 mg, iron 45-60 mg, thiamine >12 mg and vitamin A as beta-carotene 5000 IU, because the retinol form is teratogenic [[Shawe2019ObesRev]].
- The consensus advises checking blood count, ferritin, folate, B12 and vitamin A every trimester [[Shawe2019ObesRev]].
- Breastfeeding can be recommended after bariatric surgery, with micronutrients monitored during lactation [[Shawe2019ObesRev]].
- Because of dumping and accuracy concerns, the consensus advises an oral glucose tolerance test at 24-28 weeks only after a band; after bypass, sleeve or biliopancreatic diversion it suggests a seven-point capillary glucose profile or 1 week of continuous glucose monitoring instead [[Shawe2019ObesRev]].

### Exam hooks
- ASMBS/IFSO 2022: surgery recommended at BMI >35 regardless of comorbidity; considered at 30-34.9 with metabolic disease; Asian threshold >27.5 [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- The 1991 NIH thresholds were BMI >40, or >35 with comorbidities [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]].
- SOS was a prospective matched (not randomised) study; adjusted mortality HR 0.71 [[Sjostrom2007SOS]].
- SOS life expectancy: +3.0 years versus usual care but -5.5 years versus the general population [[Carlsson2020NEJM]].
- STAMPEDE 5-year HbA1c ≤6%: 29% bypass, 23% sleeve, 5% medical [[Schauer2017STAMPEDE]].
- Sleeve lowers ghrelin; bypass raises postprandial GLP-1 and PYY [[Karamanakos2008,leRoux2006]].
- Bypass gives more excess weight loss than sleeve at 5 and 10 years in SLEEVEPASS (about 8 points) [[Salminen2018SLEEVEPASS,Salminen2022SLEEVEPASS]].
- Reflux is the signature late problem of sleeve; oesophagitis 31% versus 7% at 10 years [[Salminen2022SLEEVEPASS]].
- Marginal ulcer after bypass: think smoking, NSAIDs, steroids [[Coblijn2015]].
- Internal hernia after bypass is reduced by closing mesenteric defects (HR 0.56) [[Stenberg2016Lancet]].
- LABS 30-day mortality 0.3%; modern perioperative mortality 0.03-0.2% [[Flum2009LABS,Arterburn2020JAMA]].
- Thiamine deficiency with vomiting after surgery risks Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome [[Lupoli2017]].
- Late dumping diagnostic nadir: glucose <50 mg/dL [[Scarpellini2020]].
- Alcohol use disorder risk is higher after bypass than band (about twofold) [[King2012JAMA,King2017SOARD]].
- Pregnancy after surgery: less gestational diabetes and LGA, more SGA [[Johansson2015NEJM]].
- Avoid retinol-form vitamin A in pregnancy after surgery; use beta-carotene [[Shawe2019ObesRev]].
- Teen-LABS: adolescents lose about as much weight as adults and remit diabetes more often (86% versus 53%) [[Inge2019NEJM]].
- Biliopancreatic diversion/duodenal switch: most weight loss, most malabsorption [[Risstad2015JAMASurg,Buchwald2004JAMA]].

### In plain words
- Weight-loss surgery changes the stomach and gut. It also changes the hormones that control hunger and fullness, so it is more than "a smaller stomach".
- The two most common operations are the sleeve (part of the stomach is removed) and the gastric bypass (food skips most of the stomach and the first part of the gut).
- People who have surgery lose more weight on average, and keep more of it off, than with diet and exercise alone. Results vary a lot from person to person.
- Many people with type 2 diabetes need fewer medicines after surgery, and some go into remission. Diabetes can come back over the years.
- Large studies link surgery to a longer life on average. These are averages for groups, not a promise for any one person.
- Surgery is safe for most people, but it has risks: leaks, bleeding, clots, ulcers, reflux, gallstones and blocked bowel.
- After surgery you need vitamins and minerals for life, and regular blood tests. Your team will tell you which ones.
- Some weight often comes back after the first two years. This is the body's biology, not a personal failure, and there are treatments that can help.
- Alcohol can hit harder after a bypass, and the risk of drinking problems goes up. Tell your team if drinking worries you.
- If you feel low, have thoughts of harming yourself, or feel unsafe, contact your care team or an emergency service straight away.
- If eating or weight causes you distress, or you restrict, binge or purge, talk to a clinician or an eating-disorder service.
- If you might become pregnant, talk to your team about timing and contraception, and about the special vitamins you will need.
- Do not start, stop or change any medicine or supplement without your clinician.

## Open questions and controversies
- Sleeve or bypass? Bypass gives about 8 points more excess weight loss at 5 and 10 years and better reflux control [[Salminen2018SLEEVEPASS,Salminen2022SLEEVEPASS,Peterli2018SMBOSS]]; sleeve has fewer reoperations in some data and similar diabetes outcomes [[Arterburn2020JAMA]]; procedure choice is individualised.
- How common is Barrett's oesophagus after sleeve? 4% in the 10-year SLEEVEPASS RCT [[Salminen2022SLEEVEPASS]] versus 11.6% pooled from mostly observational series [[Qumseya2021]] and 17.2% in one centre [[Genco2017]]; routine endoscopic surveillance after sleeve is advocated by some authors [[Genco2017,Qumseya2021]] but not settled.
- Does surgery lower cardiovascular events and cancer? Large matched cohorts say yes [[Aminian2019JAMA,Aminian2022SPLENDID,Sjostrom2012JAMA]]; the authors call for randomised confirmation [[Aminian2019JAMA]]. SOS found a cancer reduction in women only [[Sjostrom2009LancetOncol]].
- How much do mechanisms other than restriction matter? Hormone changes are established in people [[leRoux2006,Karamanakos2008]]; bile-acid/FXR and microbiome roles come mainly from mouse experiments (Early evidence/Hypothesis) [[Ryan2014Nature,Tremaroli2015]].
- BMI-only thresholds: ASMBS/IFSO 2022 lowered them and removed comorbidity requirements at BMI >35 [[Eisenberg2022ASMBS]]; SOS found baseline glucose and insulin, not baseline BMI, predicted benefit, suggesting selection criteria should be revised [[Sjostrom2013JIM]].
- Self-harm and suicide: overall mortality falls after surgery, but suicide and self-harm rates are higher than in comparison groups [[Adams2007NEJM,Peterhansel2013,Castaneda2019]]; whether surgery causes this or reflects pre-existing risk is unresolved [[Bhatti2016]].
- Pregnancy timing: the 2019 consensus recommends waiting until weight is stable (typically 1 year after sleeve or bypass) but notes that studies found no clear difference in outcomes by surgery-to-conception interval and that many recommendations lack high-quality evidence [[Shawe2019ObesRev]].
- Possible higher perinatal death after surgery: borderline in Sweden (OR 2.39, P = 0.06) [[Johansson2015NEJM]]; significant in pooled data (OR 1.38) [[Akhter2019PLoSMed]]; both observational.
- Endoscopic therapies: ESG and balloons give smaller, shorter-tested weight loss than surgery [[AbuDayyeh2022MERIT,Courcoulas2017IGB]]; their place relative to anti-obesity medicines is not yet defined by head-to-head trials.
- Adolescents: outcomes resemble or exceed adults' for comorbidity remission [[Inge2019NEJM]], with more reoperations and iron deficiency [[Inge2019NEJM,Olbers2017AMOS]]; long-term data beyond 5-10 years remain limited.
