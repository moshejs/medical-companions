# E · Lifestyle & behavioural treatment

Pass date: 2026-11-11. Modules served: lifestyle, adaptation, diabetes, living.

This pass covers nutrition, activity and behavioural treatment of obesity in adults. It includes head-to-head diet trials, meal-timing trials (time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting), total diet replacement and very-low-energy diets, and intensive behavioural programmes (DPP, Look AHEAD, USPSTF evidence, primary-care and commercial programmes, digital delivery). It also covers physical activity and lean mass, maintenance of lost weight and the biology of regain, psychological approaches, and binge-eating disorder. Every trial number comes from the PubMed abstract. The guideline wording is from the full text of the 2013 AHA/ACC/TOS guideline (PMC5819889). The lisdexamfetamine label wording is from openFDA.

## Findings

### lifestyle · What "comprehensive lifestyle intervention" means
- The 2013 AHA/ACC/TOS guideline lists three core components of an effective high-intensity, on-site comprehensive lifestyle intervention: a moderately reduced-calorie diet, a programme of increased physical activity, and behavioural strategies to help people stick to both [[Jensen2014Guideline]].
- The guideline recommends (A, Strong) on-site, high-intensity interventions, defined as "≥14 sessions in 6 mo", delivered individually or in groups by a trained interventionist [[Jensen2014Guideline]].
- The guideline states that comprehensive lifestyle intervention "produces average weight losses of approximately 8 kg in a 6-month period of frequent, in-person treatment", about 5–10% of starting weight. It adds that individual weight loss cannot be predicted accurately [[Jensen2014Guideline]].
- As published, the guideline's initial goal is loss of 5%–10% of baseline weight within 6 months. It notes that sustained loss of as little as 3%–5% may give clinically meaningful falls in some cardiovascular risk factors, and that larger losses give greater benefit [[Jensen2014Guideline]].
- Diet prescriptions quoted from the guideline: an energy deficit of ≥500 kcal/d, "often" sought with 1200–1500 kcal/d for women and 1500–1800 kcal/d for men. These are a population-level research convention, not individual advice [[Jensen2014Guideline]].
- Activity quoted from the guideline: programmes "typically prescribe" ≥150 min/wk of aerobic activity such as brisk walking. About 200–300 min/wk is recommended to keep weight off over the long term (>1 year) [[Jensen2014Guideline]].
- For maintenance, the guideline recommends (A, Strong) programmes with contact at least monthly for ≥1 year that support high activity (200–300 min/wk), weighing at least weekly and a reduced-calorie diet [[Jensen2014Guideline]].
- The guideline reports that programmes that keep contact at least every two months after year 1 still see gradual regain of 1–2 kg per year on average, but long-term losses stay larger than with usual care [[Jensen2014Guideline]].
- A 2020 review estimated that about 95 million US adults qualify for intensive behavioural treatment. In face-to-face programmes, people lose up to 8 kg (8%) in 6 months [[Wadden2020AmPsychol]].
- In a Cochrane review (36 trials, 3495 participants), behaviour therapy alone gave 2.5 kg more weight loss than placebo. Higher behavioural intensity added 2.3 kg. CBT combined with diet and exercise added 4.9 kg (95% CI 2.4–7.3) over diet and exercise alone (Established) [[Shaw2005Psych]].
- A systematic review found self-monitoring (food diaries, activity logs, self-weighing) consistently linked with weight loss. The evidence was weak because samples were mostly white women and relied on self-report [[Burke2011JADA]].

### lifestyle · USPSTF and the size of the behavioural effect
- USPSTF 2018 recommends that clinicians offer or refer adults with BMI ≥30 to intensive, multicomponent behavioural interventions (grade B). It judged their harms "small to none" [[Curry2018USPSTF]].
- In the USPSTF evidence review (122 RCTs, n = 62,533), behaviour-based interventions gave 2.39 kg more weight loss than control at 12–18 months (95% CI 1.93–2.86; 67 studies). Weight regain in maintenance trials was 1.59 kg lower [[LeBlanc2018]].
- In the same review, people with prediabetes in weight-loss interventions had a lower risk of developing diabetes (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.51–0.89). Mortality and cardiovascular outcomes were rarely reported [[LeBlanc2018]].
- A meta-analysis of 80 trials with ≥1 year of follow-up found mean losses of 5–8.5 kg (5–9%) at 6 months from reduced-energy diets and/or medicines, with a plateau at about 6 months. At 48 months, 3–6 kg (3–6%) was maintained and no group had regained to baseline [[Franz2007]].
- In the same meta-analysis, "advice-only" and "exercise-alone" groups lost very little weight at any time point [[Franz2007]].

### lifestyle · Diet composition head-to-heads
- POUNDS LOST randomised 811 adults to four reduced-calorie diets: fat/protein/carbohydrate 20/15/65, 20/25/55, 40/15/45 or 40/25/35% of energy. Every group lost an average of 6 kg (7%) by 6 months and began to regain after 12 months [[Sacks2009POUNDS]].
- At 2 years in POUNDS LOST, loss was similar between protein levels (3.0 vs 3.6 kg), fat levels (3.3 vs 3.3 kg) and carbohydrate levels (2.9 vs 3.4 kg), with P>0.20 for all comparisons. 14–15% of participants lost ≥10% of starting weight [[Sacks2009POUNDS]].
- POUNDS LOST found that attending group sessions predicted weight loss: about 0.2 kg more per session attended. Satiety, hunger and satisfaction did not differ between diets [[Sacks2009POUNDS]].
- DIETFITS randomised 609 adults (BMI 28–40) to a healthy low-fat or a healthy low-carbohydrate diet for 12 months. Weight change was −5.3 kg vs −6.0 kg (difference 0.7 kg, 95% CI −0.2 to 1.6) [[Gardner2018DIETFITS]].
- In DIETFITS, neither a 3-SNP "genotype pattern" (P = .20) nor baseline insulin secretion (P = .47) predicted which diet worked better for whom [[Gardner2018DIETFITS]].
- DIRECT (Israel, 322 adults, 86% men) compared three diets over 2 years. Mean weight loss was 2.9 kg on low-fat, 4.4 kg on Mediterranean and 4.7 kg on low-carbohydrate [[Shai2008DIRECT]].
- In DIRECT, the low-carbohydrate diet cut the total:HDL cholesterol ratio by 20% vs 12% on low-fat. The Mediterranean diet improved fasting glucose more than low-fat among the 36 participants with diabetes [[Shai2008DIRECT]].
- A network meta-analysis (48 trials, 7286 people) found low-carbohydrate (8.73 kg at 6 months) and low-fat (7.99 kg) diets both beat no diet. Differences between named diets were small, which "supports the practice of recommending any diet that a patient will adhere to" [[Johnston2014]].
- A 2020 BMJ network meta-analysis (121 trials, 21,942 people) found low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets similar at 6 months vs usual diet (4.63 vs 4.37 kg). Weight loss diminished by 12 months and most cardiovascular risk-factor benefits disappeared, except with the Mediterranean diet [[Ge2020BMJ]].
- A meta-analysis of 53 RCTs (68,128 participants) found that low-carbohydrate diets gave 1.15 kg (95% CI 0.52–1.79) more loss than low-fat diets. Low-fat diets beat other diets only when compared with a usual diet [[Tobias2015]].
- In a meta-analysis of 13 RCTs with ≥12 months of follow-up, very-low-carbohydrate ketogenic diets (≤50 g/d) gave 0.91 kg more loss than low-fat diets (95% CI 0.17–1.65). They raised LDL cholesterol by 0.12 mmol/L and HDL by 0.09 mmol/L [[Bueno2013]].
- In a metabolic-ward crossover of 19 adults, isocaloric fat restriction caused more body-fat loss than carbohydrate restriction (89 vs 53 g/day over 6 days). Modelling predicted that the body minimises these differences over longer periods [[Hall2015CellMetab]].

### lifestyle · Meal timing: time-restricted eating and alternate-day fasting
- In a 12-month RCT of 139 adults with obesity, all on the same calorie-restricted diet, adding time-restricted eating (8 am–4 pm window) did not give significantly more weight loss: −8.0 kg vs −6.3 kg (net −1.8 kg, 95% CI −4.0 to 0.4; P = 0.11) [[Liu2022TRE]].
- In the 12-week TREAT trial, 16:8 time-restricted eating (eating noon–8 pm) without other changes was no better than three structured meals: between-group difference −0.26 kg (95% CI −1.30 to 0.78). Appendicular lean mass fell more with TRE [[Lowe2020TREAT]].
- In a 14-week RCT of 90 adults with matched calorie restriction, early TRE (7 am–3 pm) gave 2.3 kg more weight loss (95% CI 0.9–3.7) but no significant difference in body fat. The authors equated it to about 214 kcal/d of extra restriction (Early evidence) [[Jamshed2022]].
- In a 1-year RCT of 100 adults with obesity, alternate-day fasting (25% of energy needs on fast days, 125% on feast days) and daily 75% calorie restriction gave the same loss vs control: −6.8% vs −6.8% at 6 months and −6.0% vs −5.3% at 12 months [[Trepanowski2017ADF]].
- Dropout in that trial was highest with alternate-day fasting (38%), vs 29% with daily restriction and 26% with control. LDL cholesterol was 11.5 mg/dL higher at 12 months with alternate-day fasting than with daily restriction [[Trepanowski2017ADF]].

### lifestyle · Meal replacements, total diet replacement and very-low-energy diets
- A meta-analysis and pooling of 6 RCTs found that partial meal replacement (1–2 meals replaced in a 800–1600 kcal/d plan) gave 2.43–2.63 kg more loss at 1 year than a conventional reduced-calorie diet. About 7–8% vs 3–7% of body weight was lost, and dropout was lower at 1 year [[Heymsfield2003]].
- In a meta-analysis of 6 RCTs, very-low-calorie diets (<800 kcal/d) gave greater short-term loss than low-calorie diets (16.1% vs 9.7%) but similar long-term loss (6.3% vs 5.0%, P>0.2) [[Tsai2006VLCD]].
- The AHA/ACC/TOS guideline evidence statement says medically supervised very-low-calorie diets (often <800 kcal/d) in high-intensity programmes produce about 14.2–21.0 kg loss over 11–14 weeks [[Jensen2014Guideline]].
- DROPLET (278 adults, Oxfordshire primary care) used an 810 kcal/day formula-only diet for 8 weeks with behavioural support. At 12 months, loss was −10.7 kg vs −3.1 kg with nurse-led usual care (adjusted difference −7.2 kg, 95% CI −9.4 to −4.9) [[Astbury2018DROPLET]].
- In DROPLET, 45% of the total-diet-replacement group vs 15% of usual care lost ≥10%. Moderate-or-worse adverse events were similar (11% vs 12%) [[Astbury2018DROPLET]].
- At 3 years in DROPLET, loss was −6.2 kg vs −2.7 kg (difference −3.3 kg). Regain from programme end to 3 years was larger in the total-diet-replacement group (+8.9 vs +1.2 kg), and only the blood-pressure benefit persisted [[Astbury2021DROPLET]].
- In a meta-analysis of US studies, people treated with very-low-energy diets kept off 7.1 kg at 4–5 years, vs 2.0 kg after hypoenergetic balanced diets [[Anderson2001]].

### diabetes · DiRECT and diabetes remission with total diet replacement
- DiRECT (49 UK practices, 306 adults with type 2 diabetes for <6 years, BMI 27–45, not on insulin) used: withdrawal of diabetes and blood-pressure drugs, 825–853 kcal/day formula total diet replacement for 3–5 months, stepped food reintroduction over 2–8 weeks, then structured maintenance support [[Lean2018DiRECT]].
- At 12 months in DiRECT, 46% (68/149) of the intervention group vs 4% (6/149) of controls were in diabetes remission (OR 19.7, 95% CI 7.8–49.8). Remission was defined as HbA1c <6.5% after ≥2 months off all glucose-lowering drugs [[Lean2018DiRECT]].
- Mean weight change in DiRECT at 12 months was −10.0 kg vs −1.0 kg (adjusted difference −8.8 kg). 24% of the intervention group lost ≥15 kg vs none of the controls [[Lean2018DiRECT]].
- In the whole DiRECT population, remission rose with weight loss. It was 0% (0/76) in people who gained weight, 7% at 0–5 kg loss, 34% at 5–10 kg, 57% at 10–15 kg and 86% (31/36) at ≥15 kg [[Lean2018DiRECT]].
- At 24 months in DiRECT, 36% (53/149) of the intervention group vs 3% of controls were in remission. In a post-hoc analysis, 64% of those who kept off ≥10 kg were in remission [[Lean2019DiRECT]].
- In the 5-year DiRECT extension, participants given low-intensity support averaged 6.1 kg below baseline, and 13% (11/85) were in remission. Original controls averaged 4.6 kg lost, with 5% in remission. Of those in remission at year 2, 26% were still in remission at 5 years [[Lean2024DiRECT]].
- Over 5 years, the original DiRECT intervention group had fewer serious adverse events than controls: 4.8 vs 10.2 per 100 patient-years [[Lean2024DiRECT]].
- DIADEM-I (Qatar, 158 adults aged 18–50 with type 2 diabetes for ≤3 years) used total diet replacement plus activity support. At 12 months, loss was 11.98 kg vs 3.98 kg with usual care, and remission was 61% vs 12% (OR 12.03) [[Taheri2020DIADEM]].
- In Look AHEAD (no formula-only phase), any partial or complete remission was 11.5% in year 1 and 7.3% at year 4 with the intensive intervention, vs 2.0% with diabetes support and education. Only 3.5% had remission sustained for 4 years [[Gregg2012Remission]].

### diabetes · Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP)
- The DPP lifestyle arm had two goals: at least 7% weight loss (then maintenance) and at least 150 min/week of activity similar in intensity to brisk walking. It used a 16-session core curriculum with lifestyle coaches [[DPPRG2002Lifestyle,Knowler2002DPP]].
- The DPP randomised 3234 adults with raised fasting and post-load glucose (mean BMI 34.0; 45% from minority groups). Over 2.8 years, diabetes incidence was 11.0, 7.8 and 4.8 per 100 person-years with placebo, metformin and lifestyle [[Knowler2002DPP]].
- Lifestyle cut diabetes incidence by 58% (95% CI 48–66) and metformin by 31% vs placebo. To prevent one case over 3 years, 6.9 people would need the lifestyle programme and 13.9 would need metformin [[Knowler2002DPP]].
- In the DPP lifestyle arm, weight loss was the main predictor of lower diabetes risk: 16% lower risk per kilogram lost, and HR 0.42 per 5 kg [[Hamman2006]].
- In the same analysis, among people who did not reach the weight goal at year 1, those who met the activity goal still had 44% lower diabetes incidence [[Hamman2006]].
- At 10 years, the DPP lifestyle group had lost and then partly regained weight. Diabetes incidence was still 34% lower (95% CI 24–42) than with placebo, and 18% lower with metformin [[DPPRG2009]].
- At 15 years, the reduction was 27% with lifestyle (HR 0.73) and 18% with metformin. Cumulative diabetes incidence was 55%, 56% and 62% in the lifestyle, metformin and placebo groups [[DPPRG2015]].
- At 15 years, the aggregate microvascular outcome did not differ between arms overall. In women, it was 21% lower with lifestyle than with placebo [[DPPRG2015]].
- In the first 4 years of the CDC National Diabetes Prevention Program (14,747 participants), 35.5% met the 5% weight-loss goal (mean 4.2%). Each extra session attended went with 0.3% more weight loss [[Ely2017]].

### diabetes · Look AHEAD: intensive lifestyle in type 2 diabetes
- Look AHEAD randomised 5145 adults with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity at 16 US centres to an intensive lifestyle intervention or to diabetes support and education [[LookAHEAD2013]].
- At 1 year, the intensive arm lost 8.6% of starting weight vs 0.7% in the control arm. Mean HbA1c fell from 7.3% to 6.6%, vs 7.3% to 7.2% [[LookAHEAD2007]].
- Averaged over 4 years, weight loss was 6.15% vs 0.88%, with greater gains in fitness, HbA1c, systolic blood pressure and HDL cholesterol [[LookAHEAD2010]].
- At year 4, 46% of intensive-arm participants vs 25% of controls had kept off ≥5%. Of 887 who lost ≥10% at year 1, 42.2% still had ≥10% off at year 4 [[Wadden2011LookAHEAD]].
- At year 8, the intensive arm was 4.7% below starting weight vs 2.1% for controls. 50.3% vs 35.7% had kept off ≥5%, and 26.9% vs 17.2% had kept off ≥10% [[LookAHEAD2014Obesity]].
- Look AHEAD was stopped for futility at a median 9.6 years. Weight loss was 6.0% vs 3.5% at study end. The primary cardiovascular outcome rate was 1.83 vs 1.92 per 100 person-years (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.83–1.09; P = 0.51) [[LookAHEAD2013]].
- In a post-hoc analysis of the whole Look AHEAD cohort, people who lost ≥10% in year 1 had a 21% lower risk of the primary cardiovascular outcome than those whose weight stayed stable or rose (adjusted HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.64–0.98). The study was observational, not randomised (Early evidence) [[LookAHEAD2016LDE]].
- In Look AHEAD at 1 year, losing 5 to <10% vs staying weight-stable gave an OR of 3.52 for a 0.5-point HbA1c drop and 2.20 for a 40 mg/dL fall in triglycerides. LDL cholesterol was not associated with weight loss [[Wing2011DiabetesCare]].
- Early response predicted long-term response: losing >6% by month 2 gave an OR of 3.85 for keeping off ≥5% at year 4 [[Unick2015]].
- In a small RCT, 5% weight loss improved insulin sensitivity in adipose tissue, liver and muscle, and improved β-cell function. Further loss (11%, 16%) brought dose-dependent extra changes [[Magkos2016]].

### lifestyle · Primary-care, commercial and brief programmes
- POWER-UP (390 adults, 6 primary-care practices) reported 2-year weight loss of 1.7 kg with usual care, 2.9 kg with brief lifestyle counselling, and 4.6 kg with enhanced counselling that added meal replacements or medicine. ≥5% loss was reached by 21.5%, 26.0% and 34.9% [[Wadden2011POWERUP]].
- In the Hopkins POWER trial (415 adults), 24-month loss was −0.8 kg with self-directed control, −4.6 kg with remote support only (telephone, web, email) and −5.1 kg with in-person support. Remote and in-person support did not differ [[Appel2011POWER]].
- In BWeL (1882 adults, 137 English GPs), a 30-second opportunistic offer of referral to a 12-week weight-management group gave 1.43 kg more loss at 12 months than 30 seconds of advice (95% CI 0.89–1.97) [[Aveyard2016BWeL]].
- In BWeL, 81% of patients found the GP's brief intervention appropriate and helpful. Fewer than 1% (four people) found it inappropriate and unhelpful [[Aveyard2016BWeL]].
- In a systematic review of commercial programmes, Weight Watchers gave at least 2.6% more loss than control/education at 12 months and Jenny Craig at least 4.9% more. Very-low-calorie programmes gave at least 4.0% more short-term loss than counselling, with attenuation after 6 months [[Gudzune2015]].
- In a three-country primary-care RCT (772 adults), referral to a commercial programme for 12 months gave −5.06 kg vs −2.25 kg with standard care (adjusted difference −2.77 kg). The trial was funded by the provider [[Jebb2011]].
- In WRAP (1267 adults in England), 1-year weight change was −3.26 kg with brief advice, −4.75 kg with a 12-week commercial programme and −6.76 kg with a 52-week programme (52 vs 12 weeks: −2.14 kg) [[Ahern2017WRAP]].

### lifestyle · Digital and remote delivery
- A meta-analysis of 11 RCTs found web-based programmes gave more short-term loss than offline ones (<6 months: −2.13 kg) but no long-term difference (−0.17 kg, 95% CI −2.10 to 1.76). High attrition suggested engagement is a major problem [[Beleigoli2019]].
- In the IDEA trial (471 young adults), adding a wearable activity device at 6 months gave less weight loss at 24 months than standard web self-monitoring: 3.5 kg vs 5.9 kg (difference 2.4 kg) [[Jakicic2016IDEA]].

### lifestyle · Physical activity
- The 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans recommend that adults do 150–300 min/week of moderate-intensity or 75–150 min/week of vigorous aerobic activity, plus muscle-strengthening on ≥2 days/week. The least active benefit most from even modest increases [[Piercy2018]].
- The ACSM 2009 position stand says moderate activity of 150–250 min/week helps prevent weight gain but "will provide only modest weight loss". Greater amounts (>250 min/week) are associated with clinically significant weight loss [[Donnelly2009ACSM]].
- ACSM: after weight loss, weight maintenance is better with >250 min/week of activity in cross-sectional and prospective studies, but no well-designed RCT evidence exists for preventing regain [[Donnelly2009ACSM]].
- ACSM: resistance training does not enhance weight loss but may increase fat-free mass and fat loss. Endurance or resistance exercise without weight loss still improves health risk [[Donnelly2009ACSM]].
- In a meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (1847 patients), aerobic exercise alone reduced weight by only 1.6 kg at 6 months and 1.7 kg at 12 months. The authors called it "not an effective weight loss therapy" on its own [[Thorogood2011]].
- In a Cochrane review (43 RCTs), exercise plus diet gave 1.1 kg more loss than diet alone. Exercise without weight loss still lowered diastolic blood pressure, triglycerides and fasting glucose [[Shaw2006Exercise]].
- A review estimated that aerobic exercise at public-health levels gives on average up to about 2 kg loss, with wide individual variation, and stressed that activity has many health benefits even without weight loss [[Swift2014]].
- In a 24-month trial in women, people who kept off ≥10% reported about 275 min/week (1835 kcal/week) of activity, vs less among those who kept off less [[Jakicic2008]].
- In LITOE (160 older adults with obesity, 6 months, about 9% weight loss in all exercise arms), lean mass fell 5% with aerobic training vs 2–3% with resistance or combined training. Physical function improved most with combined training (+21%) [[Villareal2017]].
- In an RCT of 120 adults on tirzepatide (40 weeks), adding thrice-weekly resistance exercise did not change total weight loss (−22.3 vs −22.6 kg). It reduced lean soft-tissue loss by 1.09 kg (95% CI 0.43–1.75) and increased strength (Early evidence) [[Green2026Tirzepatide]].
- NEAT: in 16 people without obesity overfed by 1000 kcal/day for 8 weeks, two-thirds of the rise in daily energy expenditure came from non-exercise activity thermogenesis. Changes in NEAT explained a 10-fold range in fat gain [[Levine1999]].
- In 20 sedentary adults, those with mild obesity sat about 2 hours a day longer than lean participants. Posture allocation did not change with weight loss or gain, which suggests it is partly biologically set (Early evidence) [[Levine2005]].

### adaptation · Why lost weight tends to return (biology, not willpower)
- Keeping body weight ≥10% below usual lowered total energy expenditure by 6 ± 3 kcal (people who had never had obesity) and 8 ± 5 kcal (people with obesity) per kg fat-free mass per day. These compensatory changes resist a new weight [[Leibel1995]].
- One year after an average loss of 13.5 kg on a very-low-energy diet, leptin, peptide YY, cholecystokinin and insulin were still lower and ghrelin was still higher than at baseline. Hunger was also still higher [[Sumithran2011]].
- In a quantitative model built on trial data, appetite rose by about 100 kcal/day above baseline for every kilogram of weight lost. That is more than three times the energy-expenditure adaptation [[Polidori2016]].
- In 14 former "Biggest Loser" contestants, mean loss was 58.3 kg after 30 weeks. 41.0 kg was regained by 6 years, yet resting metabolic rate was still 704 kcal/day below baseline, and metabolic adaptation was −499 kcal/day [[Fothergill2016]].
- Mathematical modelling shows that body weight responds slowly to a lasting change in intake, with a half-time of about 1 year. People with more body fat lose more for the same change, and take longer to reach a new steady state [[Hall2011Lancet]].
- Reviews describe the near-universal trajectory as early loss, a plateau at about 6 months, then gradual regain. They attribute it to biological, behavioural and environmental factors, and say long-term clinical care is needed [[Hall2018Maintenance,Franz2007]].
- Experts in an international consensus state that weight stigma causes physical and psychological harm and leads to poorer care. They call for a public narrative "coherent with modern scientific knowledge" [[Rubino2020Stigma]].

### living · Maintaining weight loss
- About 20% of people with overweight succeed at long-term maintenance when it is defined as losing ≥10% and keeping it off for ≥1 year [[Wing2005AJCN]].
- National Weight Control Registry members lost an average of 33 kg and kept it off for >5 years. They reported about 1 hour/day of activity, regular breakfast, self-weighing and a consistent eating pattern across the week. This is a self-selected registry, not a trial [[Wing2005AJCN]].
- In the first registry report (784 members), 42% said maintaining weight loss was less difficult than losing it, and 77% reported a triggering event [[Klem1997]].
- Over 10 years of follow-up of 2886 registry members, mean loss was 31.3 kg at entry, 23.8 kg at 5 years and 23.1 kg at 10 years. More than 87% were estimated to still be ≥10% below their maximum weight [[Thomas2014NWCR]].
- Five years after structured programmes, the average person kept off >3 kg and >3% of starting weight. Maintenance was 2.0 kg after balanced low-energy diets and 7.1 kg after very-low-energy diets [[Anderson2001]].
- In a meta-analysis of 45 maintenance trials (7788 adults), behavioural interventions targeting both diet and activity reduced regain by 1.56 kg at 12 months vs control (95% CI 0.86–2.27) [[Dombrowski2014]].
- In the Weight Loss Maintenance trial (1032 adults who lost a mean 8.5 kg), regain at 30 months was 4.0 kg with monthly personal contact vs 5.5 kg self-directed. 71% stayed below their entry weight [[Svetkey2008WLM]].
- In STOP Regain (314 people who had lost a mean 19.3 kg), 18-month regain was 2.5 kg face-to-face, 4.7 kg online and 4.9 kg with newsletters only. Regain of ≥2.3 kg occurred in 45.7%, 54.8% and 72.4% [[Wing2006STOPRegain]].
- In STOP Regain, more frequent or daily self-weighing was not linked to adverse psychological effects in that population [[Wing2007STOPRegain]]. This finding should not be generalised to people with eating disorders.
- A systematic review of 67 prospective studies found that demographic factors did not predict maintenance. Behaviours that lower intake, raise expenditure and monitor the balance did [[Varkevisser2019]].
- A meta-analysis of 124 trials found that behavioural programmes still lowered cardiometabolic risk factors 5 years after programme end despite regain: total:HDL cholesterol ratio 1.5 points lower and HbA1c 0.38% lower. Benefits dwindled as weight returned [[HartmannBoyce2023]].

### lifestyle · Psychological approaches
- In an RCT of 190 adults, acceptance-based behavioural treatment gave more 12-month weight loss than standard behavioural treatment (13.3% vs 9.8%; P = 0.005). 64.0% vs 48.9% kept off ≥10% at 12 months (Early evidence) [[Forman2016]].
- A new CBT for obesity, designed specifically to prevent regain, produced about 10% loss. Over 3 years of follow-up, most participants regained almost all of it, and it was no better than behaviour therapy [[Cooper2010]].

### living · Binge-eating disorder
- A meta-analysis of 81 RCTs (7515 people with binge-eating disorder) found large effects of psychotherapy, mostly CBT, on binge episodes and abstinence. Pharmacotherapy (including lisdexamfetamine) had small effects vs placebo. Evidence quality was generally very low [[Hilbert2019]].
- In 125 adults with obesity and binge-eating disorder, 12-month binge-eating remission was 51% with CBT, 36% with behavioural weight loss and 40% with the sequential approach. Behavioural weight loss gave modestly more BMI loss during treatment [[Grilo2011]].
- In a phase 3 RCT (n = 255 ITT), lisdexamfetamine 50 and 70 mg/d reduced binge days, but 30 mg/d did not. 4-week binge cessation was 42.2% and 50.0% vs 21.3% with placebo [[McElroy2015]].
- In a randomised-withdrawal trial, relapse over 6 months was 3.7% in responders who continued lisdexamfetamine vs 32.1% with placebo (HR 0.09) [[Hudson2017]].
- The US label lists lisdexamfetamine for moderate to severe binge-eating disorder in adults. It states the drug "is not indicated or recommended for weight loss" and that its safety and effectiveness for treating obesity have not been established. It carries a boxed warning for abuse, misuse and addiction [[FDA-Vyvanse]].

### Exam hooks
- DPP lifestyle goals: ≥7% weight loss and ≥150 min/week of activity. They cut diabetes incidence by 58% vs placebo; metformin cut it by 31% [[Knowler2002DPP,DPPRG2002Lifestyle]].
- DPP number needed to treat over 3 years: 6.9 for lifestyle and 13.9 for metformin [[Knowler2002DPP]].
- Each kilogram lost in the DPP lifestyle arm lowered diabetes risk by 16% [[Hamman2006]].
- Look AHEAD weight loss: 8.6% vs 0.7% at 1 year, and 6.0% vs 3.5% at study end [[LookAHEAD2007,LookAHEAD2013]].
- Look AHEAD primary cardiovascular outcome: HR 0.95 (0.83–1.09). It was stopped for futility at a median 9.6 years [[LookAHEAD2013]].
- DiRECT remission at 12 months: 46% vs 4%. It reached 86% in people who lost ≥15 kg [[Lean2018DiRECT]].
- DiRECT remission fell from 46% at 1 year to 36% at 2 years, tracking regain [[Lean2019DiRECT]].
- DIETFITS: −5.3 kg (low-fat) vs −6.0 kg (low-carbohydrate) at 12 months, a non-significant difference. Genotype did not predict response [[Gardner2018DIETFITS]].
- POUNDS LOST: four macronutrient mixes gave the same loss at 2 years. Attending sessions mattered more [[Sacks2009POUNDS]].
- Time-restricted eating plus calorie restriction was no better than calorie restriction alone at 12 months (net −1.8 kg, P = 0.11) [[Liu2022TRE]].
- Alternate-day fasting matched daily calorie restriction but had higher dropout (38%) [[Trepanowski2017ADF]].
- High-intensity lifestyle intervention is defined as ≥14 sessions in 6 months, with about 8 kg average loss [[Jensen2014Guideline]].
- USPSTF 2018 gives a grade B recommendation to offer or refer adults with BMI ≥30 to intensive behavioural interventions. The pooled effect was about 2.4 kg vs control at 12–18 months [[Curry2018USPSTF,LeBlanc2018]].
- Aerobic exercise alone gives about 1.6–1.7 kg loss. Activity matters more for maintenance and health than for initial loss [[Thorogood2011,Donnelly2009ACSM]].
- Appetite rises by about 100 kcal/day per kilogram lost, roughly three times the fall in energy expenditure [[Polidori2016]].
- Hormonal changes that favour regain (leptin down, ghrelin up) persist 1 year after weight loss [[Sumithran2011]].
- Lisdexamfetamine is labelled for moderate to severe binge-eating disorder, not for weight loss [[FDA-Vyvanse]].

### In plain words
- No single "best diet" exists. In big trials, low-fat, low-carbohydrate and Mediterranean diets gave similar average results. The one you can keep up matters most [[Sacks2009POUNDS,Gardner2018DIETFITS,Johnston2014]].
- Eating in a shorter daily window is not magic. When total food is the same, it did about as well as ordinary calorie reduction [[Liu2022TRE]].
- Programmes with regular support work better than advice alone, and support can be in person, by phone or online [[Appel2011POWER,Jensen2014Guideline]].
- Even a short, kind offer of help from a family doctor led to a little more weight loss, and most people found it helpful, not offensive [[Aveyard2016BWeL]].
- Exercise on its own usually changes weight only a little. It still helps the heart, blood sugar, mood and strength, and it helps people keep weight off [[Thorogood2011,Shaw2006Exercise]].
- Strength training during weight loss helps protect muscle [[Villareal2017,Green2026Tirzepatide]].
- After weight loss, the body pushes back: hunger hormones rise and the body burns a little less. Regain is biology, not a lack of willpower [[Sumithran2011,Polidori2016,Leibel1995]].
- Health gains from a programme can last for years even when some weight comes back [[HartmannBoyce2023]].
- For some people with type 2 diabetes, a supervised, structured meal-replacement programme put diabetes into remission. This must be done with their care team, because diabetes and blood-pressure medicines may need to change [[Lean2018DiRECT]].
- For people at high risk of diabetes, a lifestyle programme lowered the chance of getting diabetes for at least 15 years [[DPPRG2015]].
- Binge-eating disorder is a treatable health condition. Talking therapies such as CBT help most, and there is an approved medicine for it [[Hilbert2019,FDA-Vyvanse]].
- If food or weight is causing you distress, or you restrict, binge or purge, talk to a clinician or an eating-disorder service before starting any diet.
- All the numbers here are averages from studies. Your own result may be different, and weight is only one part of health [[Jensen2014Guideline]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Is any macronutrient pattern better? Large head-to-head trials and network meta-analyses find small or no differences [[Sacks2009POUNDS,Gardner2018DIETFITS,Johnston2014]]. Pooled analyses find a slight edge for low-carbohydrate over low-fat (about 1 kg) [[Tobias2015,Bueno2013]]. Status: the differences are clinically small, and adherence dominates.
- Carbohydrate-insulin model vs energy-balance model: Ebbeling reports that a low-carbohydrate diet raised energy expenditure by 209 kcal/d during weight maintenance [[Ebbeling2018BMJ]], and Ludwig argues hormonal fat storage drives intake (Hypothesis) [[Ludwig2021CIM]]. Hall reports more fat loss with fat restriction on a metabolic ward [[Hall2015CellMetab]] and argues the brain-centred energy-balance model is more robust [[Hall2022EBM]]. Status: unresolved and actively debated.
- Does meal timing matter beyond calories? Liu and TREAT found no added benefit [[Liu2022TRE,Lowe2020TREAT]]. Early TRE showed 2.3 kg extra loss at 14 weeks (Early evidence) [[Jamshed2022]]. Status: any timing effect appears small. Long-term trials are few.
- Did Look AHEAD show that lifestyle cannot prevent cardiovascular events? The randomised result was null (HR 0.95) [[LookAHEAD2013]], but people who lost ≥10% had fewer events in post-hoc observational analysis [[LookAHEAD2016LDE]]. The control group also lost weight (3.5% at the end) [[LookAHEAD2013]] and used more lipid-lowering medication at 4 years [[LookAHEAD2010]]. Status: the trial does not settle whether larger, sustained loss reduces events.
- How durable is diabetes remission? DiRECT remission fell from 46% to 36% at 2 years and to 13% in the 5-year extension, tracking regain [[Lean2018DiRECT,Lean2019DiRECT,Lean2024DiRECT]]. Status: remission is real but needs long-term support. The DiRECT extension was non-randomised.
- Do psychological treatments prevent regain? Acceptance-based therapy improved 12-month results [[Forman2016]], but a CBT designed for maintenance did not prevent regain at 3 years [[Cooper2010]]. Status: long-term evidence is weak.
- Does regain cancel the benefit? Pooled trial data suggest risk-factor benefits last ≥5 years but dwindle with regain, and evidence on events is imprecise [[HartmannBoyce2023]].
- Digital tools: early web-based benefit faded by the long term [[Beleigoli2019]], and a wearable reduced weight loss in one trial [[Jakicic2016IDEA]]. Remote coaching with human feedback matched in-person support [[Appel2011POWER]]. Status: human contact seems to be the active ingredient. The evidence base is changing fast.
