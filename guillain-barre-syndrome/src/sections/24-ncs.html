Part 2 · Diagnosis

Nerve conduction studies

A nerve conduction study sends small electric pulses along a nerve and times the response. Run one on the median nerve, switch between published group values for axonal GBS and AIDP, then try two sets of criteria on the same numbers.

Electrodiagnostic studies are not required to diagnose Guillain-Barré syndrome, but a 2019 consensus review recommends them where possible, particularly when the presentation is atypical. Typical findings are reduced conduction velocities, reduced amplitudes, abnormal temporal dispersion and partial motor block [[Leonhard2019]]. The 2023 EAN/PNS guideline advises electrodiagnostic testing to support the diagnosis (good practice point) [[vanDoorn2023]].

In 494 Dutch people, nerve studies were compatible with a neuropathy in 99%, but only 59% met criteria for a distinct subtype [[Fokke2014]]. About one-third of people meet no subtype criteria, and there is no international consensus on which criteria to use [[Leonhard2019]].

Same studies, different answers

The IGOS team classed 1,137 studies with both rule sets. They agreed in 68.3%; in 13.6% a study was demyelinating by Hadden and axonal by Rajabally [[Arends2024]]. Using amyotrophic lateral sclerosis criteria as a marker of "pure axonal loss", red flags against it were met in 68.6% of Rajabally-axonal studies, mostly through conduction block, and in 1.8% of Hadden-axonal studies [[Arends2024]].

A modified single study

Rajabally's modified criteria on one study in 365 people gave AIDP 56.2%, axonal 35.1% and equivocal 7.7%, close to rates from serial work; existing criteria gave 71.5%, 17.5% and 9.9% [[Rajabally2015]].

Misclassified at first study

Against serial studies plus antibodies as the reference, first-study error was 15.3% for a statistical model, 30% for Uncini's criteria, 45% for Rajabally's and 48% for Hadden's [[Uncini2017]]. The reference itself was built by that group.

Timing: early studies and repeats

Normal studies in the first week do not exclude GBS, especially with proximal weakness, mild disease, slow progression or a variant; a repeat 2–3 weeks later can help [[Leonhard2019]]. Because the lesion often starts in the roots and spinal nerves [[Berciano2017Proximal]], late responses tend to go first. Abnormal findings in 31 people studied in the first week [[Gordon2001]]:

Debated

Is subtyping worth it?

Serial studies moved 24% of people in an Italian series, mostly towards axonal [[Uncini2010]], and very early studies allowed subtyping in only 3 of 15 [[Berciano2020]]. The IGOS authors conclude that early studies support the diagnosis and may be repeated if non-diagnostic, but that "electrodiagnostic subtyping offers no additional value" [[Arends2026]]. One retrospective study called serial studies unhelpful [[Ibrahim2018]].

What the guideline does not pick

The 2023 EAN/PNS guideline deliberately does not endorse specific electrodiagnostic criteria for AIDP, AMAN or AMSAN, because there is no gold standard and subtype does not currently change management [[vanDoorn2023]].

Sensory responses and late responses

Sural sparing

An abnormal median or ulnar sensory response with a normal sural one. Against 38 mimics it was the most specific feature, but insensitive [[Derksen2014]]; the guideline gives sensitivity 16–100% and specificity 91–98% [[vanDoorn2023]]. It is not exclusive to AIDP: it appeared in 5 of 13 with AMAN or AMSAN and 7 of 20 with MFS in one series [[Umapathi2015]].

Common is not specific

Against the same mimics, prolonged distal latency had sensitivity 0.71 and specificity 0.74, and abnormal F waves 0.80 and 0.57, so the common abnormalities do not rule out rarer diagnoses [[Derksen2014]].

H reflex

Absent H reflexes have 95–100% sensitivity but 33% specificity for AIDP, and present H reflexes make GBS less likely [[vanDoorn2023]].

A waves

Late responses of identical shape. Present in 59% of 105 nerves within 10 days, more often in nerves that were otherwise normal, so they may come before CMAP changes; their shape did not separate AIDP from AMAN [[Veltsista2023]]. Linked to weaker strength at 6 weeks in one study [[Badry2019]].

Sensory amplitude separates the subtypes

In the Korean first studies, median SNAP was 18.3 against 5.3 µV and ulnar 14.0 against 1.8 µV (axonal against AIDP), while CMAP amplitude and block did not differ. An ulnar SNAP of 7.5 µV or more favoured axonal GBS in this small group, which needs validation [[Koo2016]].

Amplitude and outcome

The strongest predictor in 1988

In the North American plasmapheresis study (210 people), distal CMAP amplitude was the strongest outcome predictor: a mean of 0–20% of the lower limit carried a markedly raised probability of poor outcome, which plasmapheresis still improved [[Cornblath1988]]. In 60 severely affected people, a mean CMAP under 10% of the lower limit was the strongest predictor [[Miller1988]].

Inexcitable nerves

In the 1990s trial cohort, 50% (6 of 12) with inexcitable nerves were dead or unable to walk unaided at 48 weeks, against 15% of everyone else [[Hadden1998]]. These are population statistics, not forecasts for one person. In IGOS, inexcitable studies appeared only after day 3 [[Arends2026]].

In plain wordsA nerve test sends small electric pulses along a nerve and measures the response. A normal test in the first days does not rule out GBS, and it may be repeated. The label for your type can change on a later test; this does not always mean you are getting worse. An MRI scan or ultrasound can show swollen nerve roots, but these are extra tests, used mainly when the picture is unclear.
