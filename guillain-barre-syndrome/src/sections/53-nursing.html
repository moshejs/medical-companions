Part 5 · Special situations & care

Nursing care

Much of what keeps a person with GBS safe happens at the bedside: repeated checks of strength, breathing, swallowing, heart rhythm, blood pressure, bladder and bowels, care of skin and legs, and talking to someone who may hear everything but cannot answer. Walk one round of checks, then try the single breath count.

EmergencyWeakness that gets worse quickly, or breathing or swallowing that becomes hard, is an emergency and needs urgent care [[Leonhard2019]]. GBS can bring on breathing failure within hours to days, and missing it can cause death or brain injury from lack of oxygen [[vanDoorn2023]].

The ten-steps guideline names a team of nurses, physiotherapists, rehabilitation specialists, occupational therapists, speech therapists and dietitians [[Leonhard2019]]. The research for this guide found no trial of specific nursing interventions in GBS, such as positioning, swallow screens or communication aids; the guidance here comes from guidelines, cohorts and expert consensus [[Leonhard2019,Hughes2005]]. Breathing at the bedside: the single breath count

The person breathes in fully and counts aloud, at a steady pace, as far as one breath allows. In Kalita's study people counted at 2 per second to an audio prompt, and single breath count correlated well with spirometry in an Indian cohort [[Kalita2020SBC,KannanKanikannan2014]]. Which bedside test should nurses use? Guideline single breath count thresholds (15 and 19) differ from cohort cut-offs (13 and 5) [[Leonhard2019,Kalita2020SBC]], and the 20/30/40 rule needs spirometry [[Lawn2001]]. No head-to-head comparison was found. The count is simple, but its thresholds are not interchangeable.

Heart, blood pressure and medicines

Care that can slow the heart

Minor stimulation such as suction, intubation, mouth care or bathing can provoke severe bradycardia; some teams keep 1 mg IV atropine ready, and a pacemaker is rarely needed. This is an expert review, quoted as published, not as an instruction [[Weiss2025]]. Serious arrhythmias were often preceded by transient asystole after tracheal suction [[Winer1988]].

Succinylcholine

Warnings date from 1981 and 1991 [[Fergusson1981,Reilly1991]], with a fatal case after re-intubation in a tetraplegic person [[Dalman1994]]. The US label contraindicates it after extensive denervation of skeletal muscle because of severe hyperkalaemia and cardiac arrest [[FDA-Quelicin]]; an anaesthesia review advises special caution in GBS [[Radkowski2024]].

Other cautions

With significant dysautonomia, an ICU review favours non-hypotensive induction agents and treats antihypertensives and antiarrhythmics as risky unless truly necessary [[Weiss2025]]. Tricyclics need care in autonomic failure [[vanDoorn2023]]. In ileus, the same ICU review advises keeping potassium normal and avoiding sedatives [[Weiss2025]].

What is recommended

Standard preventive measures used for any bed-bound person are recommended against pressure ulcers, compression neuropathy, contractures and ossification [[Leonhard2019]]. A 2005 consensus group recommended heparin and graduated pressure stockings for bed-bound adults in the acute phase, from observational data and expert opinion [[Hughes2005]].

Still open. There is no randomised trial of clot prevention in GBS, and the 2023 EAN/PNS guideline makes no statement on it [[vanDoorn2023]]. DVT still occurred in anticoagulated people clinically [[Gaber2002]] and on ultrasound screening [[Ryabinkina2016]].

Pain, sleep, hallucinations and mood

Pain is common and real

In 156 Dutch people, pain came before weakness in 36%, was present in the acute phase in 66% and at 1 year in 38%, mostly moderate to severe [[Ruts2010Pain]]. One person's account describes very painful physiotherapy with no change in heart rate or blood pressure: monitors can miss pain [[Kumta2022]].

Medicines for pain

EAN/PNS weakly recommends gabapentin or carbamazepine, advises gabapentinoids or tricyclics before carbamazepine, and says opioids may be used with care for constipation, ileus, confusion, breathing suppression and addiction [[vanDoorn2023]]. Evidence is insufficient to support any single drug [[Liu2015]]; consensus urges cautious use of narcotics [[Hughes2005]].

Dreams and hallucinations

Of 139 people, 31% had mental status changes (16% of ICU controls). Among them, vivid dreams 19%, illusions 30%, hallucinations 60% (mainly visual) and delusions 70% (mostly paranoid); often hypnagogic, starting a median 9 days after onset and lasting a median 8 days; 16% began before ICU [[Cochen2005]]. Abnormal REM sleep reversed as they resolved Early evidence for a sleep-dream mechanism [[Cochen2005]].

Fear and low mood

In 49 severely affected people in a neuro-ICU, anxiety (82%), depressive episodes (67%) and brief reactive psychosis (25%) were seen; psychosis was linked to severe weakness, ventilation and cranial nerve palsies [[Weiss2002]]. Top fears at admission were staying paralysed, uncertainty and intubation [[Sharshar2012]]. Early recognition matters, and a psychologist or psychiatrist may help some [[Leonhard2019]].

For the person in the bedPain, strange dreams and fear are part of this illness, not a sign of weakness or of losing your mind. The dreams and hallucinations usually pass [[Cochen2005]]. Saying what you feel, in any way you can, helps the team ask about it directly [[Leonhard2019,Ruts2010Pain]].

When someone cannot speak

In 49 severely affected people, losing the ability to communicate was the hardest thing to cope with; 90% named contact with relatives as most helpful and 55% felt reassured by the ICU environment [[Weiss2002]]. A small 2026 qualitative study also named communication barriers among four lived-experience themes [[Susanti2026]]. Pain, hallucinations, anxiety and depression should be asked about directly, especially when communication is limited [[Leonhard2019]].

Three common misunderstandings. Choose one to see what the sources say.

"They are paralysed, so they cannot hear us."

Not so. Even with complete paralysis, consciousness, vision and hearing are usually intact, so staff should mind what is said at the bedside and explain procedures [[Leonhard2019]].

"Seeing things means they are going mad."

Hallucinations in GBS behave like wakeful dreams, often starting as the eyes close, and they resolve; they are linked to autonomic dysfunction, ventilation and abnormal REM sleep, and differ from ICU delirium [[Cochen2005]].

"They have recovered, so any anaesthetic is safe."

A normal examination did not rule out succinylcholine-induced hyperkalaemia: one woman had a cardiac arrest within a month of recovery, and no safe interval could be recommended (single case, unconfirmed diagnosis) [[Feldman1990]].

Families and teaching

Relatives

72%

of 86 Dutch close relatives reported one or more problems in daily living; distress was highest in the first months, and a family approach and support groups were recommended [[Bernsen2006Relatives]].

A long stay

82 days

average inpatient stay in 42 Swedish people; most were satisfied, but dissatisfaction centred on information and finances, and spouses took on more at home [[Forsberg2006Burden]].

What helps fear

2–5%

recurrence risk. Accurate information about the relatively good chance of recovery and this low recurrence risk can help reduce fear [[Leonhard2019]].

What people valued

In a UK survey of 291 people after GBS, positive social contact, physical activity, changes at home and immunoglobulin treatment were reported as important for recovery; information provided was one of the questionnaire's four scales [[Siriwardena2022]].

Planning for discharge

Before going home

Possible long-term effects (incomplete motor and sensory recovery, fatigue, pain, psychological distress) should be considered, and a rehabilitation programme arranged with a rehabilitation specialist, physiotherapist and occupational therapist [[Leonhard2019]].

Coming back in

Of 2,587 US adults, 14.1% were readmitted within 90 days, 46.7% of those within 15 days; GBS itself was the main diagnosis in 46.7% of readmissions, then septicaemia (9.6%) and cardiovascular or thromboembolic conditions (6.9%) [[Voth2026]].

Home from rehabilitation

Of 1,304 people in US inpatient rehabilitation, 81.8% went home or to the community; gains in bed, chair and wheelchair transfers were the strongest predictor [[Kushner2023]].

After discharge

In a UK survey of 216 people, over a quarter did not receive community physiotherapy after hospital discharge, and age and body pain predicted falls [[Davidson2022Falls]].

Exam hookInability to count to 15 on one breath signals imminent respiratory insufficiency [[Leonhard2019]]; 20/30/40 is VC below 20 ml/kg, MIP below 30 cmH2O, MEP below 40 cmH2O [[Lawn2001]]. Suction can trigger asystole in severe dysautonomia [[Winer1988,Weiss2025]]. A paralysed person with GBS is usually fully conscious and can hear [[Leonhard2019]]. Up to two-thirds of GBS deaths happen in the recovery phase, often after ICU discharge [[Leonhard2019]].
In plain wordsNurses check breathing often, sometimes by asking you to count out loud on one breath [[Leonhard2019]]. Even someone who cannot move or speak can usually hear and understand everything, so talk to them and explain what you are doing [[Leonhard2019]]. Being unable to talk is often the hardest part, and visits from family help most [[Weiss2002]]. Families are affected too, and support groups can help [[Bernsen2006Relatives]].
