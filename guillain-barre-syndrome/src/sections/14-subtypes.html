Part 1 · Understanding GBS

Subtypes: AIDP, AMAN and AMSAN

The immune attack in Guillain-Barré syndrome can land on the myelin sheath or on the nerve fibre itself. Watch both happen on one motor fibre, see the two ways an axonal attack can end, then compare how common each subtype is around the world.

GBS is not one disease. When 12 people who died with GBS in Hebei Province, China, were examined at autopsy, 3 had acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP), with lymphocytes in the nerve and macrophages stripping myelin; 6 had mainly axonal damage; and 3 had only very mild changes in roots and sciatic nerves although they had been paralysed [[Griffin1995]]. Among the 6 axonal cases, 3 had degeneration of sensory as well as motor fibres (acute motor-sensory axonal neuropathy, AMSAN) and 3 almost only of motor fibres (acute motor axonal neuropathy, AMAN) [[Griffin1995]].

An axonal form was first described in 1986, in 5 people whose motor nerves could not be stimulated at all [[Feasby1986]]. AMAN was named in northern China, where 88 of 90 people with acute flaccid paralysis had normal motor latencies and velocities, small muscle responses and normal sensory responses [[McKhann1993]]. Where each subtype is common

Pick a cohort. Each block is 100 people; the dots fill in that cohort's split between demyelinating, axonal and everything else. Studies used different criteria, one or several nerve studies, and different years, so they are not strictly comparable.

Is axonal GBS really commoner in Asia? Bangladesh (67% axonal among 100 people in Dhaka) and historic northern China say yes [[Islam2010,Ho1995]]. IGOS found only 6% axonal in Asia outside Bangladesh, but from just 65 studies [[Doets2018]], and figures differ inside one region too: 67% AIDP in northeast China in 2006–10, yet 54% AMAN in a water-linked epidemic there in 2007 [[Ye2013China,Ye2010Epidemic]]. A 2014 review calls the question largely unresolved, partly because a single nerve study misses axonal cases [[Bae2014]].

Antibodies and triggers

Established

Molecular mimicry

The sugar coat (lipo-oligosaccharide) of Campylobacter jejuni mimics the gangliosides GM1 and GD1a. Rabbits sensitised with it made anti-GM1 IgG and developed flaccid weakness with GBS-like nerve changes [[Yuki2004,Yuki2007]].

GD1a separates the subtypes

In 138 Chinese people, IgG anti-GD1a above 1:100 was found in 60% with AMAN and 4% with AIDP. Anti-GM1 above 1:100 was 57% against 35%, a difference that was not significant [[Ho1999]].

Debated

Is anti-GM1 an axonal marker?

Serial studies tie it to AMAN and reversible conduction failure [[Kuwabara1998GM1,Shahrizaila2014]]. The largest profile so far, 1,413 people, found 92.6% positive for at least one antibody on a combinatorial array, and anti-GM1 in every subtype [[Thomma2025Brain]].

After Campylobacter

In IGOS (768 people with serology), 30% had recent C. jejuni, 10% Mycoplasma pneumoniae, 4% cytomegalovirus, 3% hepatitis E and 1% Epstein-Barr virus. Axonal and pure motor forms were commoner in Asian than in American or European C. jejuni-positive people [[Leonhard2022IGOS]].

An AIDP label that changed

With weekly studies, all 5 people with strict C. jejuni evidence first classed as AIDP normalised their distal latency within 2 weeks and ended as AMAN. After cytomegalovirus or Epstein-Barr virus, AIDP latencies kept rising for 8 weeks [[Kuwabara2004Cj]].

Testing, per the guideline

The 2023 EAN/PNS guideline says anti-ganglioside testing is of limited clinical value in most people with typical motor-sensory GBS; it advises anti-GQ1b testing when Miller Fisher syndrome is suspected, and nodal-paranodal antibodies when autoimmune nodopathy is suspected (good practice points) [[vanDoorn2023]].

Reversible conduction failure

A conduction block does not prove demyelination. In 34 people studied repeatedly, those with IgG anti-GM1 had slowing and block that resolved within days, and distal responses that grew back without the slow, dispersed components that new myelin produces; the authors place the problem in conduction at the node [[Kuwabara1998GM1]]. Definite block was much commoner across the ulnar elbow segment (39%) than in the forearm (6%), but the elbow is an entrapment site, and IGOS left that segment out [[Kokubun2010,Arends2024]].

Proposed criteria for reversible failure in one nerve: a 50% rise in distal amplitude, or a proximal block that resolves with a fall in distal latency or response duration, or a rise in velocity. In 82 people, 45% met them [[Chan2017]]. Sensory fibres can show it too: serial recordings found sensory involvement in 69% of people with AMAN [[Capasso2011]]. It can recover without immunotherapy [[Kokubun2013JNS]], and a non-randomised series of 72 people found more early reversible block after IVIg or plasma exchange than in the natural course [[Kalita2024]].

Hypothesis

"Nodo-paranodopathy"

Uncini and colleagues proposed one category spanning transient conduction failure through to axonal degeneration, to explain axonal neuropathy with fast recovery [[Uncini2013,Uncini2018,Uncini2023]], and later a classification by fibre domain and target antigen [[Uncini2022]]. It is a proposal from one group, not an agreed classification; the 2023 guideline uses only the term autoimmune nodopathy [[vanDoorn2023]].

Reflexes can be brisk

Hyperreflexia during early recovery was seen in 13% of 54 people, more often in AMAN (6 of 23) than AIDP (1 of 18) and with anti-GM1 (7 of 26 against 0 of 28) [[Kuwabara1999]]. In a Dutch cohort of 494, 2% kept normal reflexes in weak arms [[Fokke2014]].

Exam hookWorldwide, demyelinating is still the most frequent subtype in all three IGOS regions, including Bangladesh (40% against 36% axonal) [[Doets2018]]. A conduction block can be axonal in mechanism, so it does not prove AIDP [[Kokubun2010,Uncini2010]].
In plain wordsWhich type is common depends on where you live. People in Europe and the Americas rarely have the axonal type; in Bangladesh it is much more common. The label for your type can change on a later test, and that does not always mean you are getting worse.
