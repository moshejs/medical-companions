Autonomic storms

GBS can also damage the automatic nerves that steady blood pressure, heart rate, the gut and the bladder. Scrub through one schematic day on a monitor, then explore where autonomic failure shows and what the studies counted.

The autonomic nervous system runs the body's background work through two sets of fibres: sympathetic ("speed up, squeeze") and parasympathetic, largely through the vagus nerve ("slow down"). In GBS both can be affected, and the problems show in the heart and blood vessels, sweating and the gut [[Zochodne1994]]. A 2019 review quotes "up to two-thirds" of people with GBS as having some autonomic dysfunction and argues that deaths from presumed autonomic causes now outnumber those from breathing failure [[Zaeem2019]]. That claim is debated, and there is no agreed definition or screening standard [[Zaeem2019,Weiss2025]].

Dysautonomia travels with severity: it was linked to weakness of all four limbs, bulbar and neck flexor weakness, and mechanical ventilation [[Chakraborty2020]]. Blood pressure swings and the heart

Pfeiffer and colleagues compared 36 ventilated people with GBS with 11 ventilated people with myasthenia and set tolerance limits from the controls. The daily systolic range exceeded 85 mmHg in 27 of the 36. All 16 whose mean systolic pressure was above 165 mmHg also had persistent tachycardia (mean over 125 beats a minute) or were on beta-blockers; 6 of them also had bradycardia below 48 [[Pfeiffer1999]].

Except in one person, every bradycardia episode came after a day with systolic variation above 85 mmHg, so the swing worked as a warning signal [[Pfeiffer1999]]. In one carefully studied man, falls in pressure behaved like a vasodepressor reflex with a "vagotomised" heart and rises like sympathetic overactivity; failure of the baroreflex buffer may explain the speed of the swings [[Ropper1990]].

Among 100 people in an older series, 11 of 33 ventilated people had serious arrhythmias and 7 of those died; serious arrhythmias happened only in ventilated people. They were often preceded by wide swings in pulse or pressure, and by brief asystole after tracheal suction [[Winer1988]].

A 2025 intensive care review notes that minor stimulation (suction, intubation, mouth care, bathing) can provoke severe bradycardia, that some teams keep 1 mg of intravenous atropine ready, and that a pacemaker is rarely needed; this is quoted as published, not as an instruction [[Weiss2025]]. Eyeball pressure testing found vagal over-reactivity in 4 of 13 people, but it is a small, older study and not a guideline test [[Flachenecker1996]].

Other causes of low pressure or arrhythmia, such as sepsis, bleeding or pulmonary embolism, have to be excluded before blaming dysautonomia [[Weiss2025]].

The ten-steps guideline lists monitoring of the ECG, heart rate, blood pressure, and bowel and bladder function [[Leonhard2019]]. Autonomic failure also complicates the ventilator: failed extubation was associated with autonomic dysfunction (73% vs 27%) [[Nguyen2006]].

The dangerous time is not only the worst time

Cardiovascular and respiratory problems cause most deaths in GBS, and up to two-thirds of deaths occur in the recovery phase, so the guideline stresses watching people who have recently left intensive care [[Leonhard2019]]. In 527 Dutch patients, 10 of 15 deaths (67%) came after neurological improvement had begun [[vanDenBerg2013]]. The EAN/PNS guideline advises frequent, careful monitoring for late respiratory or cardiovascular complications after intensive care discharge [[vanDoorn2023]].

Gut and bladder

Ileus: autonomic or not?

At Mayo, ileus was the commonest autonomic feature (42%) [[Chakraborty2020]]. But in an earlier Mayo series, adynamic ileus affected 17 of 114 (15%) with severe GBS, and cardiovascular dysautonomia coincided in only 5; ventilation and immobility (4) or previous abdominal surgery or rising opioid doses (8) were implicated in the rest, and medical management worked in all [[Burns2001]]. A 2025 review advises keeping potassium normal and avoiding sedatives in ileus [[Weiss2025]].

Bladder

Urinary dysfunction affected 27.7% of 65 consecutive patients and retention 9.2%; it was commoner in AIDP (39%) than AMAN (19%) [[Sakakibara2009]]. In an earlier series, 7 of 28 (25%) had trouble passing urine, appearing after the weakness and improving with it [[Sakakibara1997]]. Bladder or bowel trouble at the very start, or persisting throughout, is a red flag that suggests another diagnosis [[Leonhard2019]].

Rarer storms: PRES and takotsubo

PRES

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (brain swelling with headache, confusion or seizures) is reported in 7% of people with dysautonomia (review figure); extreme pressure swings are the proposed driver, and IVIg may add risk [[Weiss2025]]. It was commoner with dysautonomia at Mayo [[Chakraborty2020]]. Altered consciousness in GBS should prompt thought of low sodium or PRES [[Weiss2025]].

PRES in children

3 of 75 children with GBS (31 with dysautonomia) developed PRES with high blood pressure, seizures and low sodium, all ventilated; all left hospital walking after a mean of 104 days of care [[Surve2024]].

Takotsubo

A stress-related, usually reversible weakness of the heart muscle. In 8 Japanese cases vs 62 controls, it began a mean 6.5 days after GBS onset; those affected were older (76.5 vs 52 years), all had lower cranial nerve deficits (vs 41.9%) and 62.5% were ventilated (vs 11.3%) [[Terayama2024]]. One case mimicked a heart attack and recovered within two weeks [[Martins2010]]; a 2021 review counted 14 cases [[Puentes2021]].

Medicines that need care

Warning

Succinylcholine (suxamethonium)

Warnings date from 1981 and 1991 [[Fergusson1981,Reilly1991]], and a fatal cardiac arrest followed its use for re-intubation in a tetraplegic person [[Dalman1994]]. Cardiac arrest from high potassium also occurred within 1 month of apparent recovery, and no safe interval could be recommended (a single case) [[Feldman1990]]. The US label contraindicates it after the acute phase of injury following extensive denervation of skeletal muscle [[FDA-Quelicin]]. An anaesthesia review advises special caution in GBS [[Radkowski2024]].

Blood pressure and rhythm drugs

A 2025 review: with significant dysautonomia, induction drugs that do not drop blood pressure are favoured, antihypertensives and antiarrhythmics are risky unless truly needed, and conservative management is preferred outside severe bradycardia [[Weiss2025]]. The EAN/PNS guideline notes that tricyclic antidepressants need care in autonomic failure [[vanDoorn2023]].
