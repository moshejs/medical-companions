Part 3 · Acute care

Steroids, repeats & relapses

What did not work in Guillain-Barré syndrome, what to make of no early improvement, and what it means when someone gets better and then worse again: a treatment-related fluctuation, acute-onset CIDP or, rarely, GBS a second time.

Steroid medicines help some similar nerve conditions, but they have not helped GBS in trials 154,200. Treatment also does not always bring a smooth recovery. Follow six schematic courses over the first 16 weeks; the curves are drawn shapes, and only the time marks come from the studies. This is not medical advice.

Treatment speeds recovery; it does not switch GBS off

IVIg or plasma exchange speeds recovery but does not cure everyone: about 20% of treated people cannot walk at 6 months and about 5% die as a consequence of GBS (population statistics) 268. 60 to 80% can walk independently 6 months after onset, with or without treatment 266. Neither treatment has been shown to reduce mortality 75,198.

In plain wordsThese treatments help you recover faster. They do not switch the illness off straight away 75,199.

Fluctuation or A-CIDP?

The Dutch prospective study proposed a simple rule for when worsening after GBS treatment should raise the question of acute-onset chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (A-CIDP) 391. Choose how many times someone got worse again and when the last episode came.

Episodes
Last one

Worsening that comes within 8 weeks of onset and fewer than three times fits a treatment-related fluctuation of GBS. The guideline suggests considering re-treatment with IVIg or plasma exchange; there is no randomised trial, only observational data 266,473.

How often is GBS relabelled CIDP?

In a US claims database, 32.0% of people first coded GBS were later coded CIDP 428. Prospective clinical data and guidelines give about 5% 391,473. Claims coding is the less reliable of the two; that judgement is the research team's for this guide.

Who fluctuates?

Fluctuation rates were 6 of 72 after plasma exchange and 8 of 74 after IVIg in the Dutch trial 228, 16 of 172 in a Dutch cohort 486 and 53 of 1,023 in IGOS 483. Risk did not depend on the treatment given, and those who fluctuated tended to have a protracted course 486.

Position B: possible benefit

The adjusted odds ratio of 1.89 suggested a possible benefit, and the authors called for further investigation 474.

Status: no confirmatory trial was identified. The ten-steps guideline cites eight steroid trials and Cochrane six with outcome data, probably a counting difference 200,266.

A second IVIg course

A small rise in blood IgG after IVIg is linked to poor outcome (module 3.2), so a second dose seemed logical 246. Before the SID-GBS trial, around 25% of severely affected people worldwide received one 499. The trial included 327 people, of whom 112 had a poor prognosis; it found no benefit and more serious adverse events, and the authors concluded that a second course "should not be considered" for a poor prognosis 499.

That conclusion is about re-dosing because of a poor prognosis. Re-treating a fluctuation is a separate question, which the guideline handles on its own 473. Practice still varies: 39.7% of 2,325 people in a US database received repeat IVIg 428, one US centre re-treated 10 of 68 people between 2019 and 2025 286, and in IGOS 35% of those not improving got a second immunomodulatory treatment 483.

Mild GBS, late arrivals and Miller Fisher syndrome

Treatment evidence comes mainly from adults with typical, severe GBS. Mild forms, variants and people who arrive late remain clinical dilemmas 481. IVIg trials started within 2 weeks and plasma exchange trials within 4 weeks; beyond that, evidence on efficacy is lacking 266.

Mild GBS in IGOS

Of 188 people able to walk, IVIg (148) was not associated with lower 4-week disability than supportive care (40): adjusted OR 1.62, 95% CI 0.63 to 4.13. Time to full strength was shorter (28 against 56 days) and pain at 26 weeks less common (22% against 40%). At 1 year, 40% still had symptoms 485.

Established

Two exchanges for mild GBS

Plasma exchange has randomised support in mild disease: two exchanges beat none for time to motor recovery 142.

Treated anyway

In IGOS, 75% of people with mild GBS and 76% with Miller Fisher syndrome received immunotherapy, against 97% with severe GBS 483.

Miller Fisher syndrome

In 92 people, neither IVIg nor plasmapheresis changed outcome compared with no immune treatment, presumably because natural recovery is good 302. The ten-steps guideline does not generally recommend treatment for pure Miller Fisher syndrome but advises close monitoring, as some develop limb, bulbar or breathing involvement 266.

Pregnancy

Neither IVIg nor plasma exchange is contraindicated in pregnancy; IVIg might be preferred because plasma exchange needs additional monitoring 266. IVIg labels state that there are no human data on drug-associated risk and that immune globulins cross the placenta 117,118. A 2026 review of 101 published cases, biased toward severe or unusual ones, reported ventilation in 45%, maternal mortality of 5.2% and neonatal survival of 85.6% 518.

In plain wordsSteroid medicines help some similar nerve conditions, but they have not helped GBS in trials 154,200. If you get better and then worse again within a couple of months, tell your team; a repeat treatment may be considered 266,473. If you get worse after 8 weeks, doctors may check for a related long-term condition called CIDP 473. Getting GBS a second time is uncommon, at about 2 to 5 in 100 people 266.
