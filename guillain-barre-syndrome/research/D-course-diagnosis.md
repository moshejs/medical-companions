## Findings

### course · Time to nadir
- GBS typically reaches maximum disability within 2 weeks of onset; maximum disability within 24 h or after 4 weeks should prompt consideration of other diagnoses [[Leonhard2019]].
- In 494 adults with GBS (therapeutic and observational study cohorts), nadir was reached within 2 weeks in 80%, within 4 weeks in 97% and within 6 weeks in all [[Fokke2014]].
- In the International GBS Outcome Study (IGOS, first 1000 inclusions), nadir was reached within 2 weeks in 824 patients (96%) and within 4 weeks in 858 (99.8%). IGOS defined nadir as the lowest MRC sum score during the first 4 weeks from study entry, so its clock starts at entry and is capped at 4 weeks; do not compare these figures directly with onset-based figures [[Doets2018]].
- Median time from onset of weakness to hospital admission in IGOS was 3 days (IQR 2–6): 3 days (2–6) in Europe/Americas, 4 (2–6) in Asia and 4 (2–8) in Bangladesh [[Doets2018]].
- In 297 Italian patients followed prospectively, mean times to nadir, first improvement and clinical recovery were 12, 28 and 200 days [[Italian1996]].
- In the same Italian cohort, the cumulative probability of reaching the plateau was 73% by 1 week and 98% by 4 weeks [[Italian1996]].
- In 67 Dutch children (median age 5.0 years), the progressive phase lasted a median 6 days (IQR 3–8) and under 4 weeks in all [[Roodbol2017]].
- In 156 children with GBS, the median interval from weakness onset to nadir was 8 days, against 3 days in 26 children with acute flaccid myelitis [[Helfferich2022]].
- The EAN/PNS guideline requires progressive worsening for no more than 4 weeks, applied only if the duration of progression is known, and notes that progression does not exceed 2 weeks in most patients and almost never exceeds 4 weeks [[vanDoorn2023]].
- The guideline lists no further worsening after 24 h as making GBS less likely, and also relatively slow worsening (2–4 weeks) with mild weakness [[vanDoorn2023]].

### course · Plateau and start of recovery
- Plateau duration is thinly reported. The best direct numbers come from a Zika-era outbreak of 42 patients in French Polynesia: median installation phase 6 days (IQR 4–9) and median plateau 4 days (3–10) [[CaoLormeau2016]].
- A clinical report of the same 42 patients gives mean progressive and plateau phases of 7 and 9 days. This is a mean in a different analysis of one outbreak, so it does not contradict the medians [[Watrin2016]].
- In the Italian cohort, improvement began during the first week in 36% and within 4 weeks in 85% [[Italian1996]].
- The Italian cohort's clinical recovery rates at 1 week, 4 weeks, 6, 12 and 24 months were 4, 24, 57, 70 and 82% (population statistics from a 1996 report, so they may not reflect current care) [[Italian1996]].
- About 80% of people with GBS regain independent walking at 6 months, and improvement is usually most extensive in the first year and can continue for more than 5 years (population statistics) [[Leonhard2019]].

### course · Severity at nadir
- In IGOS at nadir (n=815), GBS disability score was 0 in 0.1%, 1 in 3%, 2 in 18%, 3 in 20%, 4 in 44% and 5 in 15% [[Doets2018]].
- Summing scores 3–5, 643 of 815 (79%) could not walk independently at nadir. This overall percentage is derived here from the table rows; IGOS is not population-based [[Doets2018]].
- Inability to walk independently at nadir by region: Europe/Americas 478/626 (76%), Asia 50/66 (76%), Bangladesh 100/107 (93%) [[Doets2018]].
- In the French Polynesian outbreak, 55% could walk unaided at admission and 38% at nadir [[Watrin2016]].
- Ventilator dependency in IGOS was 176/925 (19%): Europe/Americas 121/715 (17%), Asia 17/69 (25%), Bangladesh 36/125 (29%) [[Doets2018]].
- Sources differ on ventilation: about 20% develop respiratory failure and up to 22% are ventilated within the first week of admission [[Leonhard2019]]; about 25% develop respiratory insufficiency [[vanDenBerg2014]]; respiratory failure affects 20–30% [[Willison2016]].
- Up to two-thirds of deaths in GBS occur in the recovery phase, mostly from cardiovascular and respiratory dysfunction, so monitoring continues after the nadir [[Leonhard2019]].
- GBS is usually monophasic: 95% of the Fokke cohort, of whom 10% had a treatment-related fluctuation [[Fokke2014]]; 97% of Dutch children, including 5 with a treatment-related fluctuation [[Roodbol2017]].

### signs · Typical presentation
- Classic GBS begins with distal paraesthesias or sensory loss, accompanied or followed by weakness that starts in the legs and progresses to the arms and cranial muscles [[Leonhard2019]].
- All 494 adults in the Dutch cohort had bilateral limb weakness; it stayed restricted to the legs in 6% and to the arms in 1% [[Fokke2014]].
- In 67 children, bilateral limb weakness was present at admission in 93% and at nadir in all; 70% presented with tetraparesis and 23% with paraparesis [[Roodbol2017]].
- Weakness and sensory signs are always bilateral but can be asymmetrical, predominantly proximal or distal, and can start in the legs, the arms or all limbs at once [[Leonhard2019]].
- Severe diffuse pain or isolated cranial nerve dysfunction can precede weakness [[Leonhard2019]].
- Children under 6 can present with poorly localised pain, refusal to bear weight, irritability, meningism or unsteady gait, which can delay diagnosis [[Leonhard2019]].
- About two-thirds report an infection in the 6 weeks before onset, and about one-third report none of the queried antecedent events, so absence does not exclude GBS [[Leonhard2019,vanDoorn2023]].

### signs · Frequencies at study entry and nadir
- Sensory deficits in IGOS at entry: 543/890 (59%) overall; Europe/Americas 65%, Asia 54%, Bangladesh 28%. At nadir: 69%, 59% and 29% [[Doets2018]].
- Cranial nerve involvement in IGOS at entry: 464/922 (50%) overall; Europe/Americas 46%, Asia 64%, Bangladesh 67%. At nadir: 49%, 68% and 68% [[Doets2018]].
- Autonomic dysfunction in IGOS at entry: 228/924 (25%) overall; Europe/Americas 27%, Asia 10%, Bangladesh 22%. At nadir: 29%, 17% and 28% [[Doets2018]].
- Pain in IGOS at entry: 506/923 (55%) overall; Europe/Americas 58%, Asia 12%, Bangladesh 62%. At nadir: 57%, 17% and 63% [[Doets2018]].
- The predominant clinical variant was sensorimotor in Europe/Americas (387/562, 69%) and Asia (27/63, 43%), and pure motor in Bangladesh (74/107, 69%) [[Doets2018]].

### signs · Pain
- In 156 prospectively followed patients (18 with Miller Fisher syndrome), pain was reported in the 2 weeks before weakness by 36%, in the acute phase (first 3 weeks after inclusion) by 66%, and at 1 year by 38%; intensity was mostly moderate to severe [[Ruts2010Pain]].
- Pain occurs across the spectrum, including Miller Fisher syndrome, mild disease and pure motor forms [[Ruts2010Pain]].
- In 32 patients, 43.7% had neuropathic pain in the acute phase, and lower distal-leg intraepidermal nerve fibre density correlated with pain intensity (rs=-0.51) [[Ruts2012]].
- The sites of pain can be muscular, radicular or neuropathic [[Leonhard2019]].

### signs · Reflexes, including preserved reflexes
- Reflexes in paretic limbs were decreased at first assessment in 91% of adults in the Fokke cohort and in all during follow-up, yet 10 patients (2%) kept normal reflexes in paretic arms [[Fokke2014]].
- Reflexes at IGOS entry (n=920, both paretic and normal limbs): upper limbs areflexia 59%, hyporeflexia 28%, normal 12%, hyperreflexia 1%; lower limbs 77%, 20%, 2%, 2% [[Doets2018]].
- In 213 patients from three hospital cohorts, reflexes stayed normal or exaggerated throughout in 23 (about 10%) [[Yuki2012JNeurol]].
- Compared with 190 patients with reduced reflexes, those 23 more often had pure motor weakness (87 vs 47%), walked 5 m independently at nadir (70 vs 33%), had antibodies to GM1, GM1b, GD1a or GalNAc-GD1a (74 vs 47%) and had AMAN (65 vs 34%) [[Yuki2012JNeurol]].
- In 54 Japanese patients, 7 (13%) developed hyperreflexia in early recovery, 6 of them with AMAN and all 7 with anti-GM1 antibodies; AMAN 6/23 vs AIDP 1/18 [[Kuwabara1999]].
- Reflexes can remain present or even appear brisk in up to 10% of people with GBS [[Wakerley2015]].
- The guideline states that initial hyperreflexia does not exclude GBS, though hyperreflexia still makes it less likely [[vanDoorn2023]].

### signs · Autonomic involvement
- Dysautonomia can include blood pressure or heart rate instability, pupillary dysfunction, and bowel or bladder dysfunction [[Leonhard2019]].
- Frequencies differ by definition and setting: 25% at entry in IGOS [[Doets2018]]; 71/187 (38%) in a retrospective Mayo Clinic series [[Chakraborty2020]]; up to two-thirds in a literature review [[Zaeem2019]].
- A Mexican series reports "51 (31%)" of 214 with dysautonomia; 51/214 computes to 24%, so this abstract's percentage is internally inconsistent and should not be used as a headline figure [[BazanRodriguez2023]].
- In the Mayo series, the commonest features were ileus 42%, hypertension 39%, hypotension 37%, fever 29%, tachycardia or bradycardia 27% and urinary retention 24% [[Chakraborty2020]].
- Mortality in that series was 6% with dysautonomia against 2% in the whole cohort (P=0.02; retrospective, single centre) [[Chakraborty2020]].

### signs · Red flags
- Leonhard2019 Box 1 lists features that cast doubt on the diagnosis: CSF mononuclear or polymorphonuclear cells above 50×10^6/l; marked, persistent asymmetry of weakness; bladder or bowel dysfunction at onset or persistent; severe respiratory dysfunction with limited limb weakness at onset [[Leonhard2019]].
- The same box continues: sensory signs with limited weakness at onset; fever at onset; nadir under 24 h; sharp sensory level; hyper-reflexia or clonus; extensor plantar responses; abdominal pain [[Leonhard2019]].
- The same box concludes: slow progression with limited weakness without respiratory involvement; continued progression for more than 4 weeks; altered consciousness (except in Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis) [[Leonhard2019]].
- The EAN/PNS Table 1 lists findings that make GBS less likely, adding nystagmus, vomiting and abnormal routine blood tests (hyponatraemia may occur in GBS), and specifying that bladder or bowel dysfunction does not exclude GBS [[vanDoorn2023]].
- The EAN/PNS Table 1 also lists continued worsening beyond 4 weeks or at least 3 treatment-related fluctuations as a prompt to consider A-CIDP [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Red flags are prompts, not exclusions: mild pleocytosis and even rare counts of 50 or more are compatible with GBS after a thorough search for other causes [[AlHakem2023]].

### criteria · NINDS criteria (revised 1990)
- Asbury and Cornblath reaffirmed the earlier criteria and expanded the electrodiagnostic criteria; the original tables could not be read for this pass, so the wording below is Leonhard's labelled adaptation [[Asbury1990,Leonhard2019]].
- Required (as adapted): progressive bilateral weakness of arms and legs (initially only legs may be involved), and absent or decreased tendon reflexes in affected limbs at some point in the clinical course [[Leonhard2019]].
- Strongly supportive (as adapted): progressive phase days to 4 weeks (usually under 2 weeks); relative symmetry; relatively mild sensory symptoms and signs; cranial nerve involvement, especially bilateral facial palsy; autonomic dysfunction [[Leonhard2019]].
- Further supportive items added by Leonhard and colleagues, not in the original NINDS text: muscular or radicular back or limb pain; raised CSF protein (normal protein does not exclude); electrodiagnostic features of motor or sensorimotor neuropathy (normal early studies do not exclude) [[Leonhard2019]].
- NINDS and Brighton were both designed to study epidemiological links between GBS and vaccination; the Leonhard panel judged NINDS better suited to clinicians and Brighton useful for grading certainty [[Leonhard2019]].
- The criteria do not apply to some variants: bilateral facial palsy with paraesthesias, pure sensory GBS and Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis do not fulfil commonly used criteria [[Leonhard2019]].

### criteria · EAN/PNS 2023 criteria
- Required for motor-sensory or motor GBS: progressive weakness of arms and legs (may start in the legs or elsewhere in regional variants); tendon reflexes absent or decreased in affected limbs; progressive worsening for no more than 4 weeks [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Clinical support: relative symmetry; relatively mild or absent sensory symptoms and signs; cranial nerve involvement; autonomic dysfunction; respiratory insufficiency from muscle weakness; pain; recent infection (under 6 weeks) [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Laboratory support: raised CSF protein with white cells usually under 5×10^6/l; anti-GQ1b usually present in Miller Fisher syndrome; nerve conduction consistent with polyneuropathy, which may be normal in the first days [[vanDoorn2023]].
- The guideline deliberately does not endorse specific electrodiagnostic criteria for AIDP, AMAN or AMSAN, because no gold standard exists and subtype does not currently alter management [[vanDoorn2023]].

### criteria · Brighton levels of diagnostic certainty
- Sejvar and colleagues published case definitions for GBS and Fisher syndrome for immunisation safety data [[Sejvar2011Brighton]]. The original could not be read, so the grid below is as reproduced in two secondary tables [[LopezHernandez2022,Ghazanfar2020]].
- Levels 1, 2 and 3 all require bilateral flaccid limb weakness, reduced or absent reflexes in weak limbs, a monophasic course with 12 hours to 28 days from onset to nadir, and no alternative diagnosis [[LopezHernandez2022,Ghazanfar2020]].
- Level 1 adds CSF cell count under 50/µl, CSF protein above normal, and nerve conduction consistent with a GBS subtype [[LopezHernandez2022,Ghazanfar2020]].
- Level 2 has CSF cell count under 50/µl with raised protein optional, and if CSF was not collected or is unavailable, neurophysiology must be consistent with GBS [[LopezHernandez2022]].
- Level 3 requires the clinical features only, with CSF and nerve conduction not contributing [[LopezHernandez2022,Ghazanfar2020]].
- Level 4 requires absence of an alternative diagnosis, with any other item present or absent [[LopezHernandez2022,Ghazanfar2020]].
- The secondary tables mark level 3 CSF and nerve conduction as "absent", which reads as not contributing, not as a required negative result; check the original before quoting (unverified).

### criteria · How people distribute across Brighton levels
- Adults in the Fokke cohort with complete data (n=335): level 1 61%, level 2 33%, level 3 none, level 4 6% [[Fokke2014]].
- Dutch children with complete data (n=46): cumulative sensitivity level 1 72% (95% CI 57–84), level 2 96% (85–99), level 3 98% (88–100) [[Roodbol2017]].
- Malaysian GBS (n=84): level 1 67%, level 2 25%, level 3 4%, level 4 5%. Malaysian Miller Fisher syndrome (n=25): level 1 20%, level 2 56%, level 3 8%, level 4 16% [[Tan2020]].
- Single-hospital classic GBS (n=61): level 1 60.66%, level 2 34.42%, level 3 4.92%, level 4 0% [[Zeng2019]].
- Mexican GBS with complete data (n=173): level 1 54.6%, level 2 45%, level 4 0.6%. Across all 248 including incomplete work-up: level 1 38%, level 2 57%, level 3 4.8% [[LopezHernandez2022]].
- Iranian cohort: level 1 41.4%, level 2 51.6%, level 4 7.0% [[Boostani2019]].
- Timing shapes the level: Mexican patients reaching level 1 had later lumbar puncture (median 8 vs 4 days) and later nerve conduction (9 vs 6 days) than others. A true day-by-day Brighton curve was not found, because Fokke's abstract does not give one [[LopezHernandez2022]].
- Patients in different Brighton levels did not differ in preceding events, initial manifestations or outcome in the Fokke cohort, so level reflects certainty of data rather than severity [[Fokke2014]].
- Brighton criteria are designed for classical symmetric, ascending GBS and Fisher syndrome and could miss very rare regional variants such as pharyngeal-cervical-brachial weakness [[Korinthenberg2020]].
- Specificity is imperfect: 8% of children with acute flaccid myelitis met Brighton criteria for GBS when the "no alternative diagnosis" item was omitted [[Helfferich2022]].

### criteria · GBS Classification Group (Wakerley 2014)
- The classification splits GBS into classic and localised forms (for example pharyngeal-cervical-brachial weakness, bifacial weakness with paraesthesias) and Miller Fisher syndrome into incomplete forms (for example acute ophthalmoparesis, acute ataxic neuropathy) and a CNS subtype (Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis), with diagnostic criteria based on clinical characteristics [[Wakerley2014]]. The criteria text itself could not be read (unverified).
- Applied to 103 Japanese patients: classic GBS 71%, classic Miller Fisher syndrome 17%, Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis 3%, pharyngeal-cervical-brachial weakness 2% [[Wakerley2016]].
- Applied to 61 consecutive Malaysian patients: classic GBS 67%, classic Miller Fisher syndrome 10%, paraparetic 7%, pharyngeal-cervical-brachial 5%, bifacial 5%, overlap 5% [[Hiew2017]].

### csf · Albuminocytological dissociation and timing
- The classic finding is raised CSF protein with a normal cell count, but protein is normal in 30–50% in the first week and 10–30% in the second week [[Leonhard2019]].
- Raised protein was found in 64% overall in the Fokke cohort, rising from 49% on the first day after weakness onset to 88% after 2 weeks [[Fokke2014]].
- IGOS: CSF was examined in 823 patients a median 4 days (IQR 2–8) from onset of neurological symptoms; 68% had raised protein (over 0.45 g/l) [[Doets2018]].
- IGOS by timing: 50% raised within 3 days (median 0.45 g/l, IQR 0.33–0.73) against 84% after 7 days (median 0.98 g/l, IQR 0.59–1.84) [[Doets2018]].
- The larger IGOS CSF analysis (1,231 patients) found albuminocytological dissociation in 846 (70%): 57% at 4 days or fewer from weakness onset and 84% beyond 4 days [[AlHakem2023]].
- In Ottawa (138 Brighton level 1–2 cases, mean interval to puncture 7.9 days, mean protein 1.23 g/l), first-week sensitivity was 70% with a 0.45 g/l limit and 45% with age-adjusted limits, falling to 32% in the first 3 days with age-adjusted limits [[Bourque2019]].
- All limits reached statistically similar sensitivities of 74–87% in the second week in that series [[Bourque2019]].
- Children: 77% had raised protein [[Roodbol2017]]; dissociation in 80% of a retrospective and 75% of a prospective cohort [[Korinthenberg2020]].
- Lower protein is more likely with Miller Fisher syndrome, distal-predominant weakness and normal or equivocal nerve conduction; higher protein goes with demyelinating subtype, proximal or global weakness and lower odds of running at week 2 (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.25–0.70) [[AlHakem2023]].
- Mean protein was 1.56 g/l with a demyelinating pattern against 0.68 g/l axonal and 0.65 g/l normal or equivocal in 173 North American cases from one institution [[Bourque2020]].
- Miller Fisher syndrome: dissociation in 76% of 466 Japanese patients, 46% of 53 with Bickerstaff encephalitis [[Ito2008]]; 40% of 25 Malaysian patients with Miller Fisher syndrome [[Tan2020]].
- Albuminocytological dissociation remains the only consistent CSF biomarker that supports the diagnosis, among many candidates studied (Established) [[Illes2017]].
- The guideline advises CSF examination particularly when diagnosis is uncertain or another cause must be excluded, while Leonhard advises it in the initial evaluation, mainly to rule out other causes [[vanDoorn2023,Leonhard2019]].

### csf · Cell count
- IGOS first 1000: under 5 cells/µl in 80%, 5–50 in 19%, over 50 in 2% (range 53–232) [[Doets2018]].
- IGOS 1,500 analysis: under 5 cells/µl in 1,005 (83%), 5–49 in 200 (16%), 50 or more in 13 (1%) [[AlHakem2023]].
- Fokke cohort: 5–50 cells/µl in 15%, and none above 50 [[Fokke2014]].
- Marked pleocytosis above 50/µl suggests leptomeningeal malignancy or infectious or inflammatory disease of the cord or roots; mild pleocytosis of 10–50/µl is compatible but should still prompt consideration of infectious polyradiculitis [[Leonhard2019]].
- Guideline wording: "usually <5 cells/μL, rarely 5–50 cells/μL and very rarely >50 cells/μL" [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Both CSF protein and cell count may be artefactually raised after IVIg, which matters when puncture follows treatment [[vanDoorn2023]].

### csf · Age-dependent protein limits
- A systematic review (20 studies in quantitative analysis) found a weighted upper reference limit of 0.55 g/l in high-quality studies, with limits exceeding 0.60 g/l after age 50 [[Breiner2019JNeurol]].
- Ottawa's age-adjusted limits are 0.54 g/l at 30, 0.57 at 40, 0.60 at 50, 0.63 at 60 and 0.67 g/l at 70 [[Bourque2019]].
- Over 85% of clinical centres surveyed use the fixed 0.45 g/l limit [[Bourque2019]].
- Age adjustment trades early sensitivity for specificity in GBS: yield fell from 66% at the fixed 0.45 g/l limit to 49% with age-adjusted limits in 110 patients [[Rath2021]].
- CSF/serum albumin quotient correlated closely with total protein (r=0.95) with similar yield, so it adds little [[Rath2021]].
- Raised protein is non-specific: among 2,627 samples with raised protein and white cells of 5×10^9/l or fewer, a diagnosis explaining it was found in 57%; 75% with protein above age-adjusted norms against 51% below them [[Brooks2019]].
- Higher, age-dependent limits raised CSF specificity for CIDP from 39% to 57–64%, and sensitivity fell from 95% to 84–86% [[Breiner2019MuscleNerve]].

### csf · Neurofilament light chain (research biomarker, not for diagnosis)
- Median serum neurofilament light was 85.5 pg/ml in 27 people with GBS at admission against 9.1 pg/ml in 22 controls (Early evidence) [[Altmann2020]].
- In Spanish IGOS patients (98 serum samples), serum levels were 55.49 pg/ml against 9.83 pg/ml in healthy controls; AMAN 190.55 pg/ml against 46.79 pg/ml for other variants (Early evidence) [[MartinAguilar2020]].
- That study proposed high-specificity baseline cut-offs: over 319 pg/ml for inability to walk independently and over 248 pg/ml for inability to run at 1 year, and under 34 pg/ml for ability to run. These are unvalidated research values (Early evidence) [[MartinAguilar2020]].
- In 281 trial patients, high levels at week 2 were associated with inability to walk unaided at week 4 (OR 1.74, 95% CI 1.27–2.45), and high levels at week 4 with inability at week 26 (OR 2.79, 1.72–4.90) (Early evidence) [[vanTilburg2024]].
- In 293 trial patients, neurofilament improved models for inability to walk (C-statistic 0.88 for one new model at 26 weeks) but was not associated with respiratory failure (Early evidence) [[Thomma2025]].
- Low CSF/serum neurofilament ratios (mean 4.4) in 18 axonal or mixed cases, against 26.7 in controls, suggest a peripheral origin (Early evidence) [[Kortvelyessy2020]].
- For diagnosis, AUC was 0.86 for neurofilament index in 96 Swedish patients against healthy controls, multiple sclerosis and ALS; this needs validation against true mimics (Hypothesis) [[Hafsteinsdottir2024]].

### csf · Serum antibodies
- Antibody testing has limited, assay-dependent value; a positive result can help when diagnosis is in doubt, a negative result does not exclude GBS, and treatment should not wait for results [[Leonhard2019]].
- The guideline does not advise routine anti-ganglioside testing in most motor-sensory GBS, because of low to moderate sensitivity and assay variability, and does not advise testing to subtype AIDP, AMAN or AMSAN [[vanDoorn2023]].
- It suggests testing could help in some suspected motor GBS or variants or other uncertainty, and advises anti-GQ1b testing in suspected Miller Fisher syndrome [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Anti-GM1 IgG sensitivity for all GBS ranges 20–69%, varying by region, with specificity 94–98%; anti-ganglioside panel sensitivity ranges 32–64% [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Anti-GQ1b sensitivity in Miller Fisher syndrome is reported as 88–100% with very high specificity [[vanDoorn2023]]; "up to 90%" in the consensus guideline [[Leonhard2019]]; 83% of 466 with Fisher syndrome and 68% of 53 with Bickerstaff encephalitis in a large Japanese series [[Ito2008]].
- Anti-GQ1b was present in 18 of 19 with typical Miller Fisher syndrome and in 5 of 6 with GBS with ophthalmoplegia, and in none of 23 with GBS without ophthalmoplegia [[Chiba1993]].
- Anti-GM1 or anti-GD1a IgG was found in 50% of those with GBS and anti-GQ1b, 16% with Miller Fisher syndrome and 8% with Bickerstaff encephalitis, illustrating overlap [[Odaka2001]].
- High IgG anti-GM1 titres on a standardised ELISA occurred only in GBS and other inflammatory polyneuropathies among 1,232 samples [[Kuijf2005]].
- Consider nodal-paranodal antibodies (NF155, Caspr1, CNTN1) for poor treatment response, continuing worsening or relapse, using cell-based assays with a second technique [[vanDoorn2023]].

### csf · Electrodiagnosis and imaging as diagnostic support
- Nerve conduction was compatible with neuropathy in 99% of the Fokke cohort, yet only 59% met criteria for a distinct subtype [[Fokke2014]].
- The sural-sparing pattern is abnormal median or ulnar sensory responses with a normal sural response; sensitivity 16–100%, specificity 91–98% [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Absent H-reflexes have 95–100% sensitivity and 33% specificity for AIDP, and present H-reflexes make GBS less likely [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Normal studies in the first week do not exclude GBS, and a second study later can help [[vanDoorn2023]].
- In IGOS studies done within 3 days, a subtype could be shown in 58.4% (Hadden) and 52.1% (Rajabally) [[Arends2026]].
- Electrodiagnostic subtype changed on serial studies in 37.8% (Hadden) and 44.7% (Rajabally) [[Arends2026]].
- Routine MRI or nerve ultrasound is not advised for typical GBS. Consider them in atypical cases. Whole-spine MRI with contrast can help exclude compression, transverse myelitis or tumour [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Nerve MRI sensitivity in small uncontrolled studies was 82–95% with unknown specificity; nerve ultrasound sensitivity was 47–95% and specificity 36–91%, with no objective cut-offs [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Nerve enlargement was seen on ultrasound on days 1–3 in 18 patients against 21 controls, including vagal nerve and C6 root (Early evidence) [[Grimm2014]].

### ddx · How often the first diagnosis is wrong
- No large study specifically measuring GBS misdiagnosis rates was found. The figures below are indirect, come from different settings and should not be merged.
- In a tertiary database, 12 of 141 patients labelled GBS (8.5%, percentage derived here) received another diagnosis during follow-up [[Tham2018]].
- IGOS excluded 62 patients (6%) from the first 1000 for alternative diagnoses: A-CIDP 37, other peripheral neuropathy 8, CNS disorder 12, functional disorder 2, unspecified 3 [[Doets2018]].
- In a 2002 Italian validation of hospital discharge coding for 1996, GBS was not confirmed in 103 of 228 patients; positive predictive value was 54.8% overall and 76.4% in neurology departments [[Bogliun2002]].
- In Ottawa, 152 of 392 cases coded as GBS were excluded for inconclusive documentation, coding error or a more likely non-GBS diagnosis, which bundles coding errors with diagnostic error [[Bourque2019]].
- Missed diagnosis also occurs: in 119 consecutive UK patients, 27 (22.7%) had a diagnostic delay of at least 5 days from first presentation, associated independently with at least one prior visit (OR 10.15) and pre-existing cardiac or respiratory disease (OR 3.98) [[Bose2022]].

### ddx · Differential by site (matrix rows)
- Framing: mimics divide into those with symmetrical limb weakness and those with brainstem signs; Miller Fisher syndrome and pharyngeal-cervical-brachial GBS are often mistaken for brainstem stroke, botulism or myasthenia, and Bickerstaff encephalitis for Wernicke's encephalopathy [[Wakerley2015]].
- Transverse myelitis: focal inflammatory cord disorder causing motor, sensory and autonomic dysfunction. Discriminators are a sensory level, extensor plantars, hyperreflexia and cord lesion on MRI [[TMCWG2002,vanDoorn2023]].
- Cord compression (metastatic): new or worsening back pain may come before motor, sensory, bowel or bladder deficits. Whole-spine MRI is recommended [[Lawton2019]].
- Spinal epidural abscess: the triad of fever, back pain and neurological symptoms is seen in only about 10%, though most report severe localised back pain; gadolinium MRI is the most useful test [[Tetsuka2020]].
- Acute myasthenia gravis: antibody-mediated postsynaptic junction disease with localised or general weakness. Crisis is worsening weakness causing respiratory failure needing ventilation, so severe breathing failure with mild limb weakness points away from GBS [[Gilhus2019,Wendell2011,vanDoorn2023]].
- Lambert-Eaton syndrome: a 41-year-old labelled GBS had a static 3-week course, ptosis, proximal weakness and normal sensation; reflexes improved with exercise and short exercise produced a CMAP increment [[Tham2018]].
- Botulism: cranial nerve palsies then bilateral, symmetric, descending flaccid paralysis, proximal before distal, evolving over hours to a few days; patients are alert, with rare sensory deficits and pain [[Rao2021]].
- Botulism frequencies in 332 confirmed cases: afebrile 99%, descending paralysis 93%, ptosis 81%, diplopia 75%, dilated pupils 37%, sensory deficits or paraesthesias 17%, altered mental status 8% [[Rao2021]].
- Botulism tests: routine CSF is typically normal, mild protein rises are infrequent, and high-rate (30–50 Hz) repetitive nerve stimulation classically shows an increment, though early studies can be normal [[Rao2021]].
- Tick paralysis: prodrome, unsteady gait, then ascending symmetric flaccid paralysis; early ophthalmoplegia is a feature in Australian cases. Conduction velocity and sensory studies are normal, with low-amplitude motor responses [[GrattanSmith1997]].
- Tick paralysis action point: removal leads to rapid improvement, and one expert advises never definitively diagnosing GBS without searching the whole body for a tick [[Edlow2010]]; Australian Ixodes holocyclus cases can worsen for 24–48 h after removal [[GrattanSmith1997]].
- Tick paralysis delay: in 9 Louisiana children, 4 had a delayed diagnosis, and all recovered fully after removal [[Pontiff2021]].
- Acute porphyria: can resemble GBS in symptoms, signs and CSF. Clues are abdominal pain, psychological features, proximal asymmetric weakness and axonal electrodiagnosis [[Albers2004]].
- Acute porphyria test: markedly raised urinary porphobilinogen on a single-void sample confirms rapidly [[Anderson2005]]. Tham's case had abdominal pain, tea-coloured urine, encephalopathy and seizure before flaccid tetraparesis [[Tham2018]].
- Lyme neuroborreliosis: definite diagnosis requires neurological symptoms, CSF pleocytosis and intrathecal Borrelia antibodies, so pleocytosis with radicular symptoms in an exposed person should prompt testing [[Mygland2010]].
- HIV: in nine people with inflammatory demyelinating neuropathy and HIV (3 with GBS), mean CSF protein was 193 mg/dl and most had pleocytosis (mean 23 cells/mm³); authors advised testing all such patients [[Cornblath1987]]. Tham's case had 10 cells/µl and a relapsing course [[Tham2018]].
- Critical illness polyneuropathy and myopathy: weakness and failure to wean arising during critical illness, with axonal degeneration and muscle myosin loss. It is diagnosed clinically, electrophysiologically and sometimes by biopsy [[Latronico2011,Stevens2009]].
- Hypokalaemia and periodic paralysis: episodic flaccid weakness often triggered by diet or rest after exercise, with documented potassium change during attacks [[Statland2018]].
- Thyrotoxic periodic paralysis: adult male with no family history, systolic hypertension, tachycardia, hypokalaemia with low urinary potassium excretion; potassium can rapidly restore strength, with rebound hyperkalaemia risk [[Lin2005]].
- Poliomyelitis and acute flaccid myelitis: anterior horn cell disease with prodromal fever or respiratory symptoms and flaccid limb weakness, with spinal cord grey-matter abnormality on MRI [[Murphy2020,Murphy2021]].
- Acute flaccid myelitis against GBS in children: time to nadir 3 vs 8 days, asymmetric limb weakness 58 vs 0%, sensory deficits 0 vs 40%, more CSF cells and less protein; cord lesions on MRI only in myelitis [[Helfferich2022]].
- Nerve root enhancement is possible in acute flaccid myelitis as well, so it does not settle the question alone [[Leonhard2019]].
- West Nile virus: in 32 Colorado patients with paralysis, 84% had an asymmetric poliomyelitis-like syndrome and 13% a Guillain-Barré-like syndrome [[Sejvar2005]].
- West Nile discriminators: fever in 91%, tremor in 66%, altered mental status in 50%; median CSF leukocytes 108/mm³ (range 0–740), protein 98 mg/dl; median weakness-to-nadir 1 day (poliomyelitis-like) vs 4 days (Guillain-Barré-like) [[Sejvar2005]].
- Distinguishing direct viral causes of acute flaccid paralysis from GBS matters for public health and to avoid inappropriate treatment [[Solomon2003]].
- Functional neurological disorder: diagnosed from positive signs. Validated signs had low sensitivity (8–100%) but high specificity (92–100%), in class III studies [[Daum2014,Espay2018]].
- Acute-onset CIDP: see next section. Tham's case had over 4 weeks of progression, demyelinating conduction, 9 cells/µl and protein above 3.00 g/l [[Tham2018]].
- Vasculitis: sensory or sensorimotor, asymmetric or multifocal, lower-limb and distal predominant, painful, with non-demyelinating electrodiagnosis [[Collins2010]]. Tham's case had a cryoglobulin-positive serum and ESR 134 mm/h [[Tham2018]].
- Drug and toxin causes listed: nitrous oxide, alcohol, vitamin B6, lead, thallium, arsenic, organophosphates, glycol, methanol, N-hexane, glue; colchicine, chloroquine, emetine and statin-related myopathy or rhabdomyolysis [[vanDoorn2023,Leonhard2019]].
- Immune checkpoint inhibitors: GBS and peripheral neuropathy are recognised toxicities, seemingly commoner with melanoma, and severe neurological events occur in 1–3% [[Vogrig2022]]. The guideline notes GBS risk may rise after these agents and anti-TNF drugs [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Diphtheria: first neuropathy symptoms at 3–5 weeks after infection; all 32 severe cases had cranial nerve and autonomic involvement and sensory signs, with limb weakness increasing as cranial nerves recover in month 2 [[Piradov2001]].
- Wernicke's encephalopathy with beriberi neuropathy: encephalopathy and nystagmus were the atypical clues in several mimics of Miller Fisher syndrome with GBS features [[Tham2018]].
- Malignancy: one mimic had 79 white cells/µl and lymphoma cells in CSF, showing why cytology matters when cell count is raised [[Tham2018]].
- Mimic lists depend on setting: improved laboratory access and imaging removed spinal shock and hypokalaemic periodic paralysis from common mimics at one centre [[Tham2018]].

### ddx · GBS with fluctuations against acute-onset CIDP
- About 5% of people first diagnosed with GBS turn out to have A-CIDP [[vanDoorn2023,Leonhard2019]].
- In 170 prospectively followed patients, 16 (9%) had treatment-related fluctuations and 8 (5%) were later diagnosed with A-CIDP [[Ruts2010]].
- First fluctuation in GBS always occurred within 8 weeks (median 18 days, range 10–54); 5 (31%) had a second and none had more [[Ruts2010]].
- Consider A-CIDP when deterioration occurs again after 8 weeks from onset or 3 or more times; especially if the person can still walk, lacks cranial nerve dysfunction and has CIDP-like electrophysiology [[Ruts2010]].
- An earlier study of 13 A-CIDP and 11 GBS with fluctuations proposed a threshold of 9 weeks with the same 3-event rule [[Ruts2005]].
- Accuracy: further worsening after 8 weeks has sensitivity 100% and specificity 92%; 3 or more fluctuations have sensitivity 52% and specificity 96% [[vanDoorn2023]].
- A-CIDP cannot be confirmed unless worsening continues at least 8 weeks after onset [[vanDoorn2023]].
- A-CIDP is possibly more likely with marked sensory abnormality including sensory ataxia, no facial, bulbar or respiratory weakness, onset to nadir possibly over 2 weeks, widespread nerve enlargement on ultrasound, or early large fall in motor conduction velocity [[vanDoorn2023]].
- In a chart review of 30 AIDP and 15 A-CIDP, A-CIDP more often had prominent sensory signs and less often autonomic involvement, facial weakness, preceding infection or ventilation; sural sparing did not distinguish them. Up to 16% of CIDP may present acutely [[Dionne2010]].
- Absence of IgG anti-ganglioside antibodies has 96% sensitivity but 35% specificity for A-CIDP against GBS [[vanDoorn2023]].
- A-CIDP should not be over-diagnosed in severely weak patients with slow or no improvement; muscle wasting and denervation point to axonal GBS instead [[vanDoorn2023]].
- In a fluctuation, the guideline suggests considering re-treatment with IVIg or plasma exchange (observational evidence), and A-CIDP should be treated as CIDP (as published, not instructions) [[vanDoorn2023,VandenBergh2021]].
- In 67 children, two had later relapse at 9 and 19 weeks after onset [[Roodbol2017]].

### ddx · Stepwise diagnostic pathway (synthesis of published guidance; not instructions)
- Step 1, suspect: rapidly progressive bilateral leg or arm weakness without CNS involvement or another obvious cause, plus reduced reflexes, pain or sensory symptoms; check for atypical and paediatric forms [[Leonhard2019]].
- Step 2, history: time course, antecedent illness in prior 6 weeks, drugs and toxins, tick or outbreak exposure; search the whole body for a tick [[vanDoorn2023,Leonhard2019,Edlow2010]].
- Step 3, red flags: go through the features that cast doubt, including sensory level, sphincter onset, fever and consciousness; if a cord cause is possible, whole-spine MRI [[Leonhard2019,vanDoorn2023]].
- Step 4, blood tests: complete blood count, glucose, electrolytes, kidney function and liver enzymes for everyone suspected; further tests guided by the differential [[Leonhard2019]].
- Step 5, lumbar puncture: at initial evaluation, mainly to exclude other causes; a normal protein in week 1 does not exclude GBS, and over 50 cells/µl prompts a search [[Leonhard2019,vanDoorn2023]].
- Step 6, electrodiagnosis: not required but recommended where possible, especially in atypical cases; normal first-week studies do not exclude GBS, and repeat later if needed [[Leonhard2019,vanDoorn2023]].
- Step 7, targeted tests: anti-GQ1b in suspected Miller Fisher syndrome, MRI or ultrasound in atypical cases; do not delay treatment for antibody results [[vanDoorn2023,Leonhard2019]].
- Step 8, safety alongside diagnosis: assess respiratory, swallowing and autonomic risk throughout; respiratory failure can occur without dyspnoea [[Leonhard2019]].
- Step 9, treatment threshold: published guidance is to start immunotherapy if unable to walk 10 m independently; trials support IVIg within 2 weeks of weakness onset and plasma exchange within 4 weeks [[Leonhard2019]].
- Step 10, reclassify: review the diagnosis if recovery does not follow the expected course; at 3 or more fluctuations or worsening beyond 8 weeks, consider A-CIDP; consider nodal-paranodal antibodies when response is poor [[vanDoorn2023,Ruts2010]].

### Exam hooks
- Required NINDS features as adapted: progressive bilateral limb weakness plus reduced or absent reflexes in affected limbs [[Leonhard2019]].
- A normal CSF protein in week 1 does not rule out GBS: about 50% are raised within 3 days and 84% after 7 days [[Doets2018]].
- CSF over 50 cells/µl should send you looking for another diagnosis [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Albuminocytological dissociation is not specific: a diagnosis explained raised protein in only 57% of 2,627 samples [[Brooks2019]].
- Nadir under 24 h or progression beyond 4 weeks makes GBS doubtful [[Leonhard2019]].
- About 80% of adults in one large cohort reached nadir within 2 weeks and 97% within 4 weeks [[Fokke2014]].
- Preserved or brisk reflexes occur in about 10%, particularly with pure motor AMAN [[Yuki2012JNeurol]].
- Pain precedes weakness in about one-third (36%) [[Ruts2010Pain]].
- Brighton level 1 needs both CSF and nerve conduction support; level 3 is clinical only [[LopezHernandez2022]].
- Do not wait for anti-ganglioside results before treating [[Leonhard2019]].
- Anti-GQ1b is the antibody to request in suspected Miller Fisher syndrome, with sensitivity of 88–100% [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Sural sparing: normal sural with abnormal median or ulnar sensory responses [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Present H-reflexes make GBS less likely [[vanDoorn2023]].
- A-CIDP: 3 or more fluctuations, or further worsening 8 weeks or more after onset [[Ruts2010,vanDoorn2023]].
- First treatment-related fluctuation in GBS occurs within 8 weeks, median 18 days [[Ruts2010]].
- Botulism descends from the cranial nerves, without fever, in a person who stays alert [[Rao2021]].
- Search for a tick before finalising a GBS diagnosis [[Edlow2010]].
- Abdominal pain, psychological features and neuropathy: request urinary porphobilinogen [[Albers2004,Anderson2005]].
- Asymmetric flaccid weakness with fever and marked pleocytosis suggests anterior horn cell infection, such as West Nile virus [[Sejvar2005]].

### In plain words
- GBS usually gets worse over days, and most people reach their weakest point within about 2 weeks.
- After that comes a flat period, then slow recovery. Recovery can go on for more than a year.
- Many people feel pain, sometimes before any weakness. Pain is a real part of GBS, so tell your care team about it.
- Doctors check your reflexes, because they are usually weak or missing in GBS. A few people keep normal reflexes.
- A spinal fluid test looks for high protein with few cells. In the first days it can be normal, and you can still have GBS.
- Nerve tests can also be normal early on, so doctors may repeat them.
- No single test proves GBS. Doctors put together your story, your exam and your test results.
- Doctors also look for other illnesses that can look like GBS, such as spinal cord problems, botulism, a hidden tick or low potassium.
- Tell your team if you have a fever, cannot pass urine, or notice numbness below a line on your body, because these can point to a different problem.
- Treatment should not wait for antibody blood test results.
- Some people get a bit worse again after treatment. This can happen in GBS and does not always mean something new.
- If you keep getting worse after about 8 weeks, or worse again three or more times, your doctor may look at a long-term condition called CIDP.
- These numbers come from groups of people, so they cannot say exactly what will happen to you.

## Open questions and controversies
- Is CSF needed in every suspected case? Leonhard advises it during initial evaluation [[Leonhard2019]]; EAN/PNS advises it particularly when diagnosis is uncertain [[vanDoorn2023]]; status: both agree a normal early result does not exclude GBS.
- Which CSF protein limit should be used? Fixed 0.45 g/l maximises early sensitivity (70% in week 1) [[Bourque2019]]; age-adjusted limits are data-driven but drop first-week sensitivity to 45% [[Bourque2019,Breiner2019JNeurol]]; status: unresolved, with specificity data missing.
- Should repeat nerve conduction be routine? One study calls serial studies unhelpful [[Ibrahim2018]]; a larger IGOS analysis suggests repeating if early studies are non-diagnostic [[Arends2026]]; EAN/PNS says a second study can help [[vanDoorn2023]]; status: repeat for diagnosis, not for subtype.
- Should preserved reflexes be added to criteria? Yuki and colleagues argue yes [[Yuki2012JNeurol]]; EAN/PNS keeps reduced or absent reflexes as required while noting initially normal or brisk reflexes can occur [[vanDoorn2023]]; status: open.
- Do current criteria miss variants? Existing criteria can miss variants [[Shahrizaila2021]], and the Wakerley classification tries to be more inclusive [[Wakerley2014]]; EAN/PNS used Leonhard's consensus for clinical diagnosis [[vanDoorn2023]]; status: no single accepted standard.
- Does recurrent GBS exist apart from A-CIDP? A 2024 review questions the distinction and notes that definitive criteria remain elusive [[Inan2024]]; status: open.
- Where should the A-CIDP clock start? Ruts 2005 used 9 weeks [[Ruts2005]], Ruts 2010 used 8 weeks [[Ruts2010]], and EAN/PNS Table 1 uses continued worsening over 4 weeks as a prompt, with confirmation only at 8 weeks or later [[vanDoorn2023]].
- How often is GBS wrongly diagnosed today? No modern, purpose-designed estimate was found; available data span 6% to 45% and measure different things [[Doets2018,Tham2018,Bogliun2002]]; status: evidence gap.
- Can neurofilament enter practice? Prognostic signal is consistent but cut-offs lack external validation and it does not predict respiratory failure [[MartinAguilar2020,Thomma2025]]; status: research use (Early evidence).
- Can nerve ultrasound aid early diagnosis? Small studies show enlargement, but specificity and cut-offs are unsettled [[vanDoorn2023,Grimm2014]]; status: selected atypical cases only.
