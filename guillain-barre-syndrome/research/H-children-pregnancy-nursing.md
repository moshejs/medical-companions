# H · Children, pregnancy, nursing care & living with GBS

Pass date: 2026-11-10. Modules served: children, nursing, after (living parts).

Access notes:
- Pregnancy and newborn findings sit under the `children` module id (mother and baby), except delivery and anaesthesia care, which sit under `nursing`.
- All proportions below are population statistics from the cohort named in each bullet. None predicts what will happen to one person, child or pregnancy.
- Numbers come from PubMed abstracts unless the bullet says "full text". Full text was read for Leonhard2019 (Europe PMC XML), Krief2023 and Patra2022 (PMC XML).
- Pregnancy data are thin: two Swedish register studies, one US administrative database, small hospital series and a systematic review of case reports. Case-report pools over-represent severe and unusual cases.
- Doses are quoted as published in trials or guidelines. They are not instructions.

## Findings

### children · How common GBS is in children
- Ten studies of children aged 0 to 15 years gave an annual incidence of 0.34 to 1.34 per 100,000 [[McGrogan2009]].
- Sejvar's meta-analytic model puts the rate at 0.62 per 100,000 person-years at 0 to 9 years, against 2.66 at 80 to 89 years; incidence rises 20% for each 10 years of age [[Sejvar2011]].
- An Australian review gives an incidence of 0.8 per 100,000 children per year and calls GBS the most common cause of acute flaccid paralysis in childhood [[Ryan2005]].
- In a Northeast China series of 750 people, paediatric GBS peaked in summer, especially July and August (p < 0.01) [[Wu2016]].

### children · Acute flaccid paralysis surveillance
- Acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) surveillance was built for polio eradication and covers children under 15; studies from China and Bangladesh show GBS is now the predominant cause of AFP in that age group [[Papri2021]].
- In Spain, 678 AFP cases were notified in 1998 to 2015, a mean rate of 0.58 per 100,000 under 15 years; 76.9% (504/655) were clinically diagnosed as GBS, and no wild poliovirus case was found [[MasaCalles2018]].
- In a South African tertiary children's hospital, 99 of 119 children with AFP had GBS, and 47 (39.5%) of the 119 needed mechanical ventilation [[vanderPijl2018]].
- AFP surveillance remains key to detecting poliovirus reintroduction, and the authors argue for enhanced testing for non-polio enteroviruses (Established) [[MasaCalles2018]].

### children · How GBS presents in children
- In a prospective German-speaking multicentre study of 95 children (median age 6.2 years), the first symptom was usually a gait disturbance or neuropathic pain; 70 had an infection and 8 a vaccination in the previous 6 weeks [[Korinthenberg2007]].
- In that study, symptoms progressed for a median of 7 days; at the worst point 60% could not walk, 24% could not use their arms, 46% had cranial nerve involvement and 51% autonomic dysfunction [[Korinthenberg2007]].
- 79% of those children had neuropathic pain, half of them severe; the authors call severe pain a frequent therapeutic challenge in the acute phase [[Korinthenberg2007]].
- In 29 children under 6 years seen over 15 years in Montreal, pain was a symptom in all and present on admission in 79%; back and lower limb pain was the commonest pattern (83%) [[Nguyen1999]].
- Pain led to misdiagnosis in 20 of those 29 children (69%), and in 11 the correct diagnosis took more than a week [[Nguyen1999]].
- In a Chinese comparison of 750 children and adults, pain was more frequent in children (17.2% vs 9.6%), time to nadir was shorter (6.3 vs 7.3 days) and bulbar dysfunction was more common (22.0% vs 14.8%); overall severity was similar [[Wu2016]].
- Pain frequency differs widely between cohorts: 79% in the prospective German study [[Korinthenberg2007]], 100% in a Canadian under-6 series [[Nguyen1999]] and 17.2% in a retrospective Chinese series [[Wu2016]]. Prospective scoring of pain and younger age probably explain part of the gap.
- In 35 US children over 20 years, the commonest presenting symptoms were paraesthesias (54%), weakness (49%) and myalgias (49%); hyporeflexia or areflexia was present in 94% [[Hicks2010]].
- Children under 6 can present with poorly localised pain, refusal to bear weight, irritability, meningism or unsteady gait, and failure to recognise these signs may delay diagnosis (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- In 67 Dutch children (median age 5.0 years), 70% presented with tetraparesis and 23% with paraparesis, and the progressive phase lasted a median 6 days [[Roodbol2017]].

### children · Delay in diagnosis at a young age
- In Rotterdam, 15 of 23 preschool children (68%) were first misdiagnosed, against 6 of 32 older children (21%) (p = 0.001) [[Roodbol2011]].
- Patient delay to see a paediatrician was the same in both age groups (median 5.0 days), but doctor's delay to suspect GBS was longer in preschool children (median 3 vs 0 days); in a quarter it exceeded 1 week, up to 22 days [[Roodbol2011]].
- Refusal to walk and leg pain were the most frequent presenting symptoms in preschool children (65%); first diagnoses included myopathy, tonsillitis, meningitis, rheumatoid disorders, coxitis and discitis [[Roodbol2011]].
- GBS is correctly diagnosed at admission in only about one-third of affected preschool children [[Papri2021]].
- MRI nerve-root enhancement is non-specific but sensitive and can support the diagnosis especially in young children, in whom clinical and electrophysiological assessment can be difficult (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].

### children · Subtype mix
- In the prospective German study, electrophysiology showed demyelination in 74% (26% of these with very low motor amplitudes) and purely axonal change in 11% [[Korinthenberg2007]].
- In 110 French children, 70% had AIDP and 16% AMAN [[Estrade2019]].
- In 224 critically ill children in North India (2010 to 2022), AMAN was the commonest subtype (61.6%), followed by AIDP (20.1%) and AMSAN (8%) [[Bansal2026]].
- In 52 Indian children followed for a year, AMAN was the commonest variant (50%) [[Kumar2026]].
- Subtype proportions in a Chinese child and adult comparison were "overall comparable" between age groups [[Wu2016]].
- The subtype mix in children therefore depends on region and setting, as in adults: mostly demyelinating in European cohorts, mostly axonal in Indian intensive care cohorts (Established for the regional pattern) [[Korinthenberg2007,Estrade2019,Bansal2026]].

### children · Breathing failure and ventilation
- In 421 children from German-speaking countries and the Netherlands, 79 (19%) needed mechanical ventilation and one died [[Roodbol2023]].
- In the prospective German study 13% needed artificial ventilation [[Korinthenberg2007]]; in 142 Chinese children 12.68% did [[Qinrong2023]].
- In 224 children admitted to one Indian paediatric ICU, 54.9% were ventilated and 24.2% had a tracheostomy, a selected critically ill group [[Bansal2026]].
- Roodbol and colleagues found the adult EGRIS inaccurate in children and built EGRIS-Kids from age, cranial nerve involvement and GBS disability score at admission: a 9-point score with predicted risk of respiratory failure from 4 to 50% (AUC 0.71) (Early evidence) [[Roodbol2023]].
- A Chinese cohort reported the adult EGRIS as helpful in children, with a median of 6 in ventilated vs 3 in non-ventilated children (p < 0.001), and called an EGRIS of 5 or more high risk [[Qinrong2023]].
- In 103 Chinese children, facial or bulbar weakness (OR 7.936) and axonal type (OR 4.582) independently predicted ventilation [[Luo2020]].
- In the 750-person Chinese comparison, autonomic dysfunction was more frequent in ventilated than non-ventilated children (39.1% vs 8.8%) and predicted ventilation in children (OR 70.415, 95% CI 9.265 to 535.158), unlike in adults [[Wu2016]].
- In French children, need for intubation correlated with peripheral facial paralysis and dysautonomia (both p < 0.01) [[Estrade2019]].
- In the South African AFP cohort, bulbar dysfunction, autonomic dysfunction and upper limb paralysis predicted ventilation [[vanderPijl2018]].

### children · Autonomic problems in children
- Autonomic dysfunction was reported in 51% of children in the prospective German study [[Korinthenberg2007]] and 46% of a US series, including blood pressure and pulse changes, bowel or bladder control and priapism [[Hicks2010]].
- In the US series, autonomic dysfunction, cranial nerve involvement and CSF albuminocytological dissociation were each linked to a shorter time to nadir [[Hicks2010]].
- In critically ill Indian children, autonomic instability, particularly hypertension (P = 0.04), was more frequent with AIDP than with axonal subtypes [[Bansal2026]].

### children · Treatment in children
- The ten-steps guideline sees no indication to deviate from standard adult treatment in children; IVIg is usually first line because plasma exchange is available only in experienced centres and seems to cause more discomfort and complications in children (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- The German-language paediatric guideline found few controlled paediatric studies and none with low risk of bias, so its recommendations largely rest on adult data and a Delphi consensus [[Korinthenberg2020Guideline]].
- That guideline recommends IVIg for severe or persistently progressive GBS, and plasma exchange where IVIg is not tolerated or not effective; corticosteroids are called ineffective for GBS [[Korinthenberg2020Guideline]].
- Three trials with 75 children in total suggested IVIg hastens recovery compared with supportive care (low-quality evidence) [[Hughes2014]].
- In the German paediatric trial, children unable to walk 5 m were randomised to IVIg 1 g/kg over 2 days or 0.4 g/kg over 5 days (as published); median time to unaided walking was 19 vs 13 days, not significantly different [[Korinthenberg2005]].
- Secondary transient deterioration was more frequent with the 2-day regimen (5 of 23 vs 0 of 23) [[Korinthenberg2005]].
- In the early-treatment arm, 11 of 21 children still walking at entry lost unassisted walking and 6 became bedridden, with no significant difference between IVIg and no treatment; recovery was somewhat faster after early IVIg, and power was low [[Korinthenberg2005]].
- Disease severity at nadir was the only prognostic factor for recovery in that trial [[Korinthenberg2005]].
- In 41 ventilated Egyptian children randomised to plasma exchange or IVIg (0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days, as published), ventilation was shorter with plasma exchange (median 11 vs 13 days, p = 0.037); walking unaided within 4 weeks of PICU discharge was similar (95.2% vs 90%) [[ElBayoumi2011]].

### children · Course and outcome in children
- In the prospective German study, improvement began at a median of day 13, unaided walking returned after 27 days, and children followed long term were symptom-free after 118 days [[Korinthenberg2007]].
- At the end of observation (288 days) 75% were symptom-free, 21% had residual symptoms with no effect on daily functioning, and the 4% with more severe disability had CIDP or concurrent myelitis [[Korinthenberg2007]].
- In 142 Chinese children, 94.37% walked independently and 85.21% managed without assistance at 6 months [[Qinrong2023]].
- In the same children, mEGOS and EGOS were judged valid predictors of 6-month outcome for those admitted within 2 weeks of onset (Early evidence) [[Qinrong2023]].
- In 421 European children, a lower GBS disability score at nadir was the strongest predictor of independent walking (at one month OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.25 to 0.74) [[Roodbol2023]].
- In 110 French children, 77% were cured at follow-up and 9% had sequelae, linked to an axonal form and a short interval from onset to hospital admission [[Estrade2019]].
- In 52 Indian children (half AMAN), all had a GBS disability score of 2 or more at discharge, 51.9% at 6 months and 19.2% at 1 year [[Kumar2026]].
- Fine motor problems lasted in some: an ONLS arm score above 2 fell from 46.2% at discharge to 26.9% at 6 months and 7.7% at 1 year [[Kumar2026]].
- In 47 children followed at least 2 years, 23% had mild persisting weakness in at least one muscle group, all with perfect functional independence scores; young age and rapid progression predicted it [[Vajsar2003]].
- In 80 children with severe GBS treated in a PICU and seen a median 3.0 years later, 95% had a favourable neurological outcome (Hughes 0 to 1), 97.5% attended school and 57.7% had satisfactory school performance [[Devi2023]].
- Quadriparesis at admission, ventilation, tracheostomy and longer PICU and hospital stay predicted unfavourable long-term outcome in that cohort [[Devi2023]].
- Long-term sequelae are common even after good recovery: 67% (95% CI 53 to 78) of 51 French children at a median 6 years 4 months, mostly minor; paraesthesia 43%, pain 35%, fatigue 31%, symptoms of depression 34% [[Estublier2024]].
- In 37 people with childhood GBS (median follow-up 11 years), 65% had residual complaints; 26% reported a negative effect on their school career, and 2 had a recurrence [[Roodbol2014Kids]].
- Children seem to fare better than adults: about 80% of adults walk independently at 6 months [[Leonhard2019]], against 94.37% of Chinese children at 6 months [[Qinrong2023]] and unaided walking at a median of 27 days in German children [[Korinthenberg2007]]. Cohorts differ in setting and definitions, so this is an indirect comparison.

### children · Pregnancy and the risk period after birth
- In Swedish women aged 15 to 49 (1978 to 1993), the age-adjusted rate ratio for GBS was 0.89 (95% CI 0.52 to 1.53) in pregnancy, 1.37 (0.64 to 2.91) in the first 90 days after delivery and 2.93 (1.20 to 7.11) in the first 30 days [[Cheng1998]].
- The authors conclude the risk rises after delivery, particularly in the first 2 weeks postpartum (Early evidence) [[Cheng1998]].
- An earlier Swedish register cohort (1973 to 1983) gave relative risks of 0.86 (0.40 to 1.84) in pregnancy, 1.47 (0.54 to 3.99) in the 3 months and 2.21 (0.55 to 8.94) in the 30 days after delivery: the same direction, but not significant [[Jiang1996]].
- In the US National Inpatient Sample (1999 to 2015), 291 of 13,792,544 births were to women with GBS, 2.1 per 100,000 births, rising from 1.26 to 3.8 per 100,000 over the period (p = 0.02) [[Taylor2024]].
- Trimester of onset: 31%, 31% and 38% in the first, second and third trimesters in 16 French women [[Krief2023]]; 52.5% in the third trimester in 101 published case reports [[Yakubu2026]].
- In 36 peripartum women in Assam, 28 had onset before and 8 after delivery; 6 of the 8 postpartum cases developed weakness on the day of delivery (full text) [[Patra2022]].

### children · Outcomes for mother and baby
- Compared with women without GBS, women with GBS in the US sample had more pre-eclampsia (OR 1.69), sepsis (OR 9.30), postpartum haemorrhage (OR 1.83), transfusion (OR 4.39) and caesarean delivery (OR 2.07); their newborns were more often growth restricted (OR 2.50) [[Taylor2024]].
- In 16 French women with GBS in pregnancy, 25% needed respiratory assistance, 94% received IVIg and neurological recovery was complete in all [[Krief2023]].
- Compared with 18 non-pregnant women with GBS, the pregnant women more often had CMV infection (31% vs 11%), admission more than 7 days after onset (57% vs 12%), ICU admission (56% vs 33%), respiratory assistance (25% vs 11%) and treatment-related fluctuations (37% vs 0%) [[Krief2023]].
- Two of 16 fetuses (12.5%) died in that series, one with CMV infection and one with HELLP syndrome; the authors call GBS in pregnancy a severe maternal condition with significant fetal mortality [[Krief2023]].
- Among the 10 children with follow-up data in that series, Apgar scores were normal in all, 9 had normal long-term psychomotor development and 1 had delay at age 4 (full text) [[Krief2023]].
- In 101 published case reports, ICU admission was 64% and ventilation 45%; bulbar weakness (OR 5.29) and autonomic dysfunction (OR 7.17) predicted ICU need; maternal mortality was 5.2% and recovery complete in 35%, partial in 59.4% [[Yakubu2026]].
- In the same review, caesarean delivery was 54.8%, preterm birth 36%, one-third of neonates had low birthweight and neonatal survival was 85.6%; third-trimester onset was linked to more preterm delivery and maternal respiratory compromise [[Yakubu2026]].
- In 36 peripartum women in Assam, 11.1% needed ventilation and 1 (2.8%) died; preterm birth was 35.7% and stillbirth 8.33%; 72.2% had complete recovery at 3 months [[Patra2022]].
- In a retrospective series of 47 pregnant women in North East India, most had good maternal and fetal outcomes; 2 needed ventilation, and 1 had an intrauterine death and died of respiratory failure [[Sharma2015]].
- Series disagree on severity: case-report pools give 45% ventilated [[Yakubu2026]], a French multicentre series 25% [[Krief2023]] and Indian hospital series 11.1% [[Patra2022]] or 2 of 47 [[Sharma2015]]. Reporting bias in case reports probably inflates the first figure.

### children · GBS in the newborn
- A neonate born to a mother with ongoing GBS developed GBS 12 days after birth; serum from mother and infant blocked neuromuscular transmission in animal preparations, and the blocking activity disappeared after both recovered [[Buchwald1999]].
- The authors propose that transplacentally transferred antibodies target the mature but not the fetal neuromuscular junction, which could explain the delayed onset (Hypothesis) [[Buchwald1999]].
- A floppy preterm infant born at 34 weeks to a mother with acute ulcerative colitis had areflexia, autonomic dysfunction, respiratory failure and nerve conduction velocity of 3 m/s; congenital GBS was diagnosed and the infant improved within 48 hours of IVIg (single case) [[Bamford2002]].

### nursing · Pregnancy, delivery and anaesthesia
- Neither IVIg nor plasma exchange is contraindicated in pregnancy, but as plasma exchange needs additional considerations and monitoring, IVIg might be preferred (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- An obstetric review states that treatment in pregnancy should follow the same principles as outside pregnancy, with venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, physical therapy, pressure ulcer prevention, enteral nutrition, respiratory support and close monitoring for respiratory and autonomic failure [[Pacheco2016]].
- The EAN/PNS guideline makes no pregnancy-specific treatment recommendation in the sections pass E reviewed [[vanDoorn2023]].
- In 16 French women, epidural anaesthesia was used in 8 (50%) and general anaesthesia in 3 (19%); 10 (62%) delivered vaginally and 5 (31%) had an unscheduled caesarean (full text) [[Krief2023]].
- In Assam, 66.67% had spontaneous vaginal delivery and 27.78% caesarean section; the authors conclude GBS is not in itself an indication for caesarean section [[Patra2022]].
- Patra and colleagues note that uterine contraction is not affected despite extensive peripheral nerve involvement, so many women deliver vaginally (full text, discussion) [[Patra2022]].
- A case report chose combined spinal epidural over general anaesthesia for caesarean section in GBS, to avoid prolonged postoperative intubation and allow careful titration; the woman later deteriorated and needed a second IVIg course (single case) [[Okahara2024]].
- A woman in labour who had recovered from a neurological deficit thought to be GBS had a cardiac arrest from succinylcholine-induced hyperkalaemia within 1 month of recovery; a normal examination did not rule out the risk, and no safe interval could be recommended (single case, unconfirmed diagnosis) [[Feldman1990]].

### nursing · Neurological and breathing observations
- The ten-steps guideline advises routine measurement of respiratory function, because not all people heading for respiratory insufficiency show dyspnoea (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- It lists signs of imminent respiratory insufficiency: breathlessness at rest or while talking, inability to count to 15 in a single breath, accessory muscle use, rising respiratory or heart rate, vital capacity below 15 to 20 ml/kg or below 1 l, or abnormal blood gases or oximetry (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- Up to 22% of people with GBS need ventilation within the first week of admission (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- The guideline also names a single breath count of 19 or less as predicting the need for ventilation, alongside vital capacity and maximum inspiratory and expiratory pressures (full text) [[Leonhard2019,KannanKanikannan2014]].
- The 20/30/40 rule: risk of respiratory failure with vital capacity below 20 ml/kg, maximal inspiratory pressure below 30 cmH2O, maximal expiratory pressure below 40 cmH2O, or a fall of more than 30% in any of these [[Lawn2001]].
- Serial pulmonary function tests detected those at risk even though no clinical feature predicted the pattern of respiratory decline [[Lawn2001]].
- In a prospective Indian study, single breath count, forced vital capacity, neck weakness and bulbar weakness independently predicted ventilation, and single breath count correlated well with spirometry [[KannanKanikannan2014]].
- In 94 people counting at 2 Hz to an audio prompt, all kept acceptable blood gases without respiratory distress down to a count of 7; at a count of 5, need for ventilation was predicted with 90.6% sensitivity and 95.2% specificity (Early evidence) [[Kalita2020SBC]].
- An admission single breath count cut-off of 13 had a negative predictive value of 88.5% for ruling out ventilation (Early evidence) [[Kalita2020SBC]].
- Different studies attach different meanings to single breath count numbers: 15 marks imminent insufficiency and 19 or less predicts ventilation in the guideline [[Leonhard2019]], while 5 marked the point of blood-gas failure in Kalita's cohort [[Kalita2020SBC]]. The tool is simple, but thresholds are not interchangeable.
- Monitoring in the ten steps covers muscle strength and disability, swallowing and coughing, ECG, heart rate, blood pressure, and bowel and bladder function; the frequency depends on the rate of deterioration, autonomic involvement, disease phase and setting (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- Up to two-thirds of deaths occur in the recovery phase, mostly from cardiovascular and respiratory dysfunction, so watch for arrhythmias, blood pressure shifts or mucus-plug distress, especially soon after ICU discharge (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- A consensus group recommended monitoring blood pressure, pulse, autonomic disturbance and respiratory failure, with timely ventilation and tracheostomy (observational data and expert opinion) [[Hughes2005Supportive]].

### nursing · Swallowing and aspiration
- The guideline links choking, nutrition problems and hospital-acquired pneumonia to bulbar and facial palsy (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- Swallowing impairment was found in over 80% early after ICU admission, and only about half of those with piecemeal swallowing had clinically apparent swallowing problems [[Ogna2017]].
- In 88 people, 39.7% needed a tracheostomy; immediate decannulation after weaning was possible in 40% of them, and dysphagia (especially laryngeal and pharyngeal loss of sensation) was the main risk factor for staying cannulated [[Schroder2019]].
- Respiratory muscle weakness was the main reason for intubation in that series, while neurogenic dysphagia drove prolonged cannulation [[Schroder2019]].

### nursing · Communication for people who cannot speak
- People with GBS, even with complete paralysis, usually have intact consciousness, vision and hearing; staff should be mindful of what is said at the bedside and explain procedures to reduce anxiety (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- In 49 severely affected people in a neuro-ICU, loss of communication was the condition they found hardest to cope with; 90% named contact with relatives as most helpful and 55% felt reassured by the ICU environment [[Weiss2002]].
- Pain, hallucinations, anxiety and depression should be asked about directly, especially when communication is limited or the person is in the ICU (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- The guideline names a multidisciplinary team including nurses, physiotherapists, rehabilitation specialists, occupational therapists, speech therapists and dietitians (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].

### nursing · Positioning, pressure areas and venous thrombosis
- The guideline links pressure ulcers and compression neuropathy to immobility, and contractures and ossification to prolonged severe weakness; standard preventive measures used for any bed-bound person are recommended (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- In 16,280 US Medicare beneficiaries with GBS (2005 to 2020), 96% avoided hospital-acquired pressure ulcers and 91% avoided deep vein thrombosis [[Wright2026]].
- A 2005 consensus group recommended heparin and graduated pressure stockings for bed-bound adults in the acute phase, based on observational data and expert opinion, not trials [[Hughes2005Supportive]].
- In 73 people at one UK hospital (1995 to 1999), 68% received prophylactic anticoagulation; 5 (7%) developed clinical DVT, 3 with pulmonary emboli, and one person on enoxaparin died of pulmonary embolism [[Gaber2002]].
- In 65 Russian ICU patients screened with duplex ultrasound, half developed venous thromboembolism despite low-molecular-weight heparin (DVT 52%, pulmonary embolism 15%); ventilation, bed rest beyond 3 days, infection, central venous catheter and age over 40 were risk factors [[Ryabinkina2016]].
- These VTE rates differ by method: clinical diagnosis gave 7% [[Gaber2002]], routine ultrasound screening in ICU gave 52% [[Ryabinkina2016]] and coded claims data 9% [[Wright2026]].

### nursing · Bowel and bladder
- Urinary dysfunction occurred in 27.7% of 65 consecutive people, including retention in 9.2%; it was more common in AIDP (39%) than AMAN (19%) and was linked to motor grade, bowel dysfunction and age [[Sakakibara2009]].
- Adynamic ileus occurred in 17 of 114 people with severe GBS (15%); it coincided with cardiovascular dysautonomia in only 5, while ventilation, immobilisation, prior abdominal surgery or rising opioid doses were implicated in others, and medical management worked in all [[Burns2001]].
- The guideline lists urinary retention as a complication to watch for in all patients and constipation with immobility (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].

### nursing · Pain, sleep, hallucinations and mood
- In 156 Dutch people, pain preceded weakness in 36%, was present in the acute phase in 66% and was still present at 1 year in 38%, mostly moderate to severe [[Ruts2010Pain]].
- A consensus group noted that carbamazepine or gabapentin may help pain and narcotic analgesics may need cautious use [[Hughes2005Supportive]].
- Mental status changes occurred in 31% of 139 people with GBS vs 16% of ICU controls (OR 2.3): vivid dreams 19%, illusions 30%, hallucinations 60% (mainly visual) and delusions 70% (mostly paranoid) among those affected [[Cochen2005]].
- They began a median 9 days after onset, lasted a median 8 days, were often hypnagogic (starting when eyes closed), and 16% started before ICU admission [[Cochen2005]].
- Sleep was short and fragmented in all monitored groups; those with hallucinations had very abnormal REM sleep, which reversed as the symptoms resolved (Early evidence for a sleep-dream mechanism) [[Cochen2005]].
- In the neuro-ICU study, anxiety (82%), depressive episodes (67%) and brief reactive psychosis (25%) were observed; psychosis was strongly linked to severe tetraparesis, ventilation and multiple cranial nerve palsies [[Weiss2002]].
- Psychological distress can be severely traumatic after a rapid loss of function; early recognition matters because mental state and physical recovery influence each other, and referral to a psychologist or psychiatrist may help some (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].

### nursing · Family support and patient education
- In 86 Dutch close relatives, 72% reported one or more problems in daily living; psychological morbidity was highest in the first months, and a family approach and patient support groups were recommended [[Bernsen2006Relatives]].
- In 42 Swedish people followed for 2 years, inpatient stay averaged 82 days; most were satisfied, but dissatisfaction centred on information and finances, and spouses reported more concern and responsibility for household and family [[Forsberg2006Burden]].
- Accurate information about the relatively good chance of recovery and the low recurrence risk (2 to 5%) can help reduce fear (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- In a UK online survey of 291 people after GBS, positive social interactions, physical activity, changes at home and immunoglobulin treatment were reported as important for recovery; information provided formed one of four questionnaire scales [[Siriwardena2022]].
- Common misunderstanding: paralysis does not mean the person cannot hear or understand (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- Common misunderstanding: hallucinations in GBS are not a sign of madness; they behave like wakeful dreams and resolve [[Cochen2005]].
- Common misunderstanding: a normal examination after recovery does not prove that succinylcholine is safe [[Feldman1990]].

### nursing · Discharge planning
- Before discharge, possible long-term effects (incomplete motor and sensory recovery, fatigue, pain, psychological distress) should be considered, and a rehabilitation programme arranged with a rehabilitation specialist, physiotherapist and occupational therapist (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- Among 2,587 US adults, 14.1% were readmitted within 90 days; 46.7% of readmissions came within 15 days, and discharge to a facility rather than home raised the odds (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.27 to 2.28) [[Voth2026]].
- GBS itself was the principal diagnosis in 46.7% of readmissions, followed by septicaemia (9.6%) and cardiovascular or thromboembolic conditions (6.9%) [[Voth2026]].
- Of 1,304 people in US inpatient rehabilitation, 81.8% went home or to the community; gain in bed, chair and wheelchair transfers was the strongest predictor of community discharge [[Kushner2023]].
- In a UK survey of 216 people after GBS, over a quarter did not receive community physiotherapy after hospital discharge; age and body pain predicted falls [[Davidson2022Falls]].

### after · Support organisations
- The ten-steps guideline describes GBS|CIDP Foundation International as the international patient association for GBS, a non-profit offering support, education, research funding and advocacy, which reviewed the guideline (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- Connecting people with others who have had GBS can help guide them through rehabilitation, and the Foundation and national organisations can help build these networks (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- Patient support groups may also help relatives in the first year [[Bernsen2006Relatives]].

### after · Work, school and daily life
- 3 to 6 years after GBS, 38% of those with a job had to change it, 44% altered leisure activities, 37% functioned less well at home and 39% reported a change in their partner's life [[Bernsen2002Work]].
- All Swedish people working before onset were unable to work at 2 weeks, and 17% were still unable to work at 2 years [[Forsberg2005Disability]].
- 26% of Swedish people still depended on informal help 2 years after onset [[Forsberg2006Burden]].
- In a qualitative study of 5 people returning to work, themes were the value of work, losing and regaining a familiar identity, and dilemmas about using support and adaptations; limited public awareness of GBS was a hindrance [[Royal2009]].
- In childhood GBS, 26% reported a negative effect on their school career [[Roodbol2014Kids]], while 97.5% of children seen after PICU care attended school [[Devi2023]].
- No published study on returning to driving after GBS was found in this pass (see Provenance).

### after · Fatigue and its management
- Severe fatigue (Fatigue Severity Scale 5.0 or more) was present in 60% of 100 people after GBS, more often in women and those over 50, and unrelated to disability at nadir or antecedent infection [[Garssen2006Fatigue]].
- In a 12-week bicycle training study (20 people, 16 after GBS), self-reported fatigue fell 20% (p = 0.001) and fitness, function and quality of life improved (Early evidence; uncontrolled) [[Garssen2004Training]].
- A consensus group states that persistent fatigue is common and may be helped by an exercise programme [[Hughes2005Supportive]].
- In children, fatigue persisted in 31% at a median 6 years [[Estublier2024]] and severe fatigue in 22% at a median 11 years [[Roodbol2014Kids]].

### after · Recurrence counselling
- Recurrence affects 2 to 5% (full text) [[Leonhard2019]].
- In a Dutch series, 32 people had 81 episodes; they were younger than non-recurrent people (mean 34.2 vs 46.9 years) and more often had Miller Fisher syndrome or milder symptoms [[Kuitwaard2009Recurrent]].
- Symptoms in later episodes resembled the first episode in each person, but severity and preceding infection varied [[Kuitwaard2009Recurrent]].
- Because the increase in recurrence risk after immunisation is very small and possibly only theoretical, the need for immunisation should be reviewed individually [[Hughes2005Supportive]].

### Exam hooks
- Children under 6 with GBS often present with pain, refusal to walk or irritability rather than weakness; 68% of preschool children were first misdiagnosed [[Leonhard2019,Roodbol2011]].
- In the German paediatric IVIg trial, the 2-day regimen caused more early transient deterioration than the 5-day regimen (5/23 vs 0/23) [[Korinthenberg2005]].
- About 1 in 5 children with GBS in European cohorts needs ventilation (19% of 421) [[Roodbol2023]].
- Autonomic dysfunction is a strong predictor of ventilation in children (OR 70.4 in one Chinese cohort) [[Wu2016]].
- GBS risk is not raised during pregnancy but rises after delivery: rate ratio 2.93 in the first 30 days postpartum [[Cheng1998]].
- In pregnancy, neither IVIg nor plasma exchange is contraindicated; IVIg might be preferred [[Leonhard2019]].
- 20/30/40 rule: VC below 20 ml/kg, MIP below 30 cmH2O, MEP below 40 cmH2O [[Lawn2001]].
- Inability to count to 15 in one breath signals imminent respiratory insufficiency [[Leonhard2019]].
- Hallucinations in GBS are often hypnagogic, linked to autonomic dysfunction and ventilation, and differ from ICU delirium [[Cochen2005]].
- A paralysed person with GBS is usually fully conscious and can hear: speak accordingly at the bedside [[Leonhard2019]].
- Up to two-thirds of GBS deaths happen in the recovery phase, often after ICU discharge [[Leonhard2019]].

### In plain words
- Children can get GBS too, but it is rare: roughly 1 child in 100,000 each year [[McGrogan2009,Ryan2005]].
- In young children, the first sign is often pain in the back or legs, or not wanting to walk, so it can be missed at first [[Roodbol2011,Nguyen1999]].
- Most children recover well, and usually faster than adults. Some still have tingling, pain or tiredness years later [[Korinthenberg2007,Estublier2024]].
- Children are usually treated with immunoglobulin (IVIg) through a drip [[Leonhard2019,Korinthenberg2020Guideline]].
- GBS in pregnancy is uncommon. The risk seems highest in the first weeks after the baby is born [[Cheng1998]].
- Treatment can be given during pregnancy. Most babies do well, but early birth is more common [[Leonhard2019,Yakubu2026]].
- Even someone who cannot move or speak can usually hear and understand everything. Talk to them, and explain what you are doing [[Leonhard2019]].
- Being unable to talk is often the hardest part. Visits and contact with family help most [[Weiss2002]].
- Strange dreams or seeing things that are not there can happen in GBS. They pass, and they do not mean you are losing your mind [[Cochen2005]].
- Nurses check breathing often, sometimes by asking you to count out loud on one breath. Getting breathless when talking is a warning sign [[Leonhard2019]].
- If weakness gets worse quickly, or breathing or swallowing becomes hard, this is an emergency and needs urgent care [[Leonhard2019]].
- Families are affected too. Most relatives have some problems in daily life in the first months, and support groups can help [[Bernsen2006Relatives]].
- Many people feel very tired for a long time. Gentle, steady exercise helped some people feel less tired [[Garssen2006Fatigue,Garssen2004Training]].
- GBS rarely comes back. About 2 to 5 people in 100 have it again [[Leonhard2019]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Does the adult respiratory score work in children? Roodbol and colleagues found EGRIS inaccurate and built EGRIS-Kids (AUC 0.71) [[Roodbol2023]]; a Chinese cohort reported EGRIS useful in children with a threshold of 5 [[Qinrong2023]]. Status: both retrospective; EGRIS-Kids not yet externally validated in this literature.
- Two-day or five-day IVIg in children? The 2-day regimen brought more early deterioration [[Korinthenberg2005]], yet some paediatric centres still use it (full text) [[Leonhard2019]]. Status: one underpowered trial.
- IVIg or plasma exchange in ventilated children? Plasma exchange shortened ventilation by 2 days in one small randomised trial [[ElBayoumi2011]], but guidelines favour IVIg on grounds of availability and comfort [[Leonhard2019,Korinthenberg2020Guideline]]. Status: unresolved.
- How dangerous is GBS in pregnancy? Case-report reviews show 45% ventilation and 5.2% maternal mortality [[Yakubu2026]]; hospital series show 11.1% ventilation [[Patra2022]] and mostly good outcomes [[Sharma2015,Krief2023]]. Status: publication bias likely; no prospective cohort.
- Is the postpartum risk increase real? Significant at 30 days in one Swedish register [[Cheng1998]], same direction but not significant in another [[Jiang1996]]. Status: (Early evidence).
- What is the right VTE prophylaxis? Consensus recommends heparin and stockings [[Hughes2005Supportive]], but DVT still occurred in 6% of anticoagulated people clinically [[Gaber2002]] and 52% on ultrasound screening [[Ryabinkina2016]]. Status: no RCT; no EAN/PNS statement [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Which bedside breathing test should nurses use? Guideline thresholds for single breath count (15, 19) [[Leonhard2019]] differ from cohort cut-offs (13, 5) [[Kalita2020SBC]]; the 20/30/40 rule needs spirometry [[Lawn2001]]. Status: no head-to-head comparison found.
- How long do children's problems last? Function is usually normal [[Vajsar2003,Devi2023]], yet two-thirds report sequelae years later, including depressive symptoms [[Estublier2024,Roodbol2014Kids]]. Status: both may be true; outcome measures differ.
- Return to driving: no GBS-specific evidence found. Status: gap.
