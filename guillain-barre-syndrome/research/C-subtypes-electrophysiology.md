# C · Subtypes, nerve pathology and electrophysiology

Pass date: 2026-11-09. Modules served: subtypes, mfs, ncs.

This pass covers the pathology of the acute inflammatory demyelinating (AIDP), acute motor axonal (AMAN) and acute motor-sensory axonal (AMSAN) patterns, how common each is by region, antibody links, reversible conduction failure, the Miller Fisher spectrum and other variants, nerve conduction studies (NCS), and nerve ultrasound and MRI.

## Findings

### subtypes · The three pathological patterns
- Autopsy of 12 people from Hebei Province, China, clinically diagnosed with GBS (11 died early in the disease): 3 had AIDP, with lymphocytic infiltration and macrophage-mediated demyelination; 6 had predominantly axonal involvement; and 3 had only very mild changes in roots and sciatic nerves despite being paralysed at death [[Griffin1995]].
- Within those 6 axonal cases, 3 had extensive Wallerian-like degeneration of sensory as well as motor fibres (AMSAN), and 3 had degeneration almost exclusively in motor fibres (AMAN) [[Griffin1995]].
- In both AMAN and AMSAN, a prominent finding was macrophages inside the periaxonal space, surrounding or displacing the axon, with the myelin sheath intact around them [[Griffin1995]].
- The 3 paralysed cases with very mild changes suggest severe paralysis can occur early without prominent structural change along the nerve, pointing to physiological block or nerve-terminal changes [[Griffin1995]].
- The earliest AMAN change on electron microscopy (7 cases) was lengthening of the node of Ranvier with distorted paranodal myelin. Macrophages then entered at the node, moved under the myelin terminal loops and advanced into the internodal periaxonal space, while internodal myelin stayed normal [[Griffin1996Nodal]].
- In 7 fatal AMAN cases, IgG and the complement product C3d were bound to the motor-fibre axolemma. The node was the most frequently involved site, and in more severe cases the deposits extended into the periaxonal space of the internodes [[HaferMacko1996AMAN]].
- AMAN has little demyelination or lymphocytic inflammation, and sensory fibres are spared [[HaferMacko1996AMAN]].
- In 3 people with AIDP autopsied 3 to 9 days after symptom onset, C3d and C5b-9 lay along the outer Schwann cell surface. Vesicular myelin change was seen before macrophages invaded the myelin, and C3d was not on the myelin membranes; the authors speculate that antibody binds the Schwann cell surface (Early evidence) [[HaferMacko1996AIDP]].
- AMSAN autopsies of 4 people who died 7, 7, 18 and 60 days after onset showed Wallerian-like degeneration in ventral and dorsal roots and peripheral nerves, with minimal demyelination or lymphocytic infiltration in the 3 early cases. The 60-day case showed extensive large-fibre loss with little remyelination [[Griffin1996AMSAN]].
- The authors read AMSAN as the most severe end of a spectrum of immune attack on axonal epitopes [[Griffin1996AMSAN]].
- The first description of an axonal form was 5 people with electrically inexcitable motor nerves, all quadriplegic. One died, with severe axonal degeneration and no inflammation or demyelination at autopsy, and 3 of the 4 survivors recovered poorly [[Feasby1986]].
- AMAN was named from northern China: of 90 people with acute flaccid paralysis, 88 had normal motor distal latencies and limb conduction velocities, reduced compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes and normal sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs). Autopsies showed Wallerian-like degeneration of motor fibres [[McKhann1993]].
- Those northern China epidemics occurred mostly in summer, in children and young adults in rural areas (historical analysis of more than 3,200 people) [[McKhann1993]].
- Motor-point biopsy from a person with AMAN showed denervated neuromuscular junctions and fewer fibres in intramuscular nerves, with normal sural nerve and skin innervation, suggesting the distal motor terminal can be the site of damage (Early evidence: single case) [[Ho1997Terminal]].
- Proximal nerves carry much of the early lesion: pathology predominates in roots, spinal nerves and plexuses, and endoneurial inflammatory oedema is the outstanding feature in very early disease [[Berciano2017Proximal]].
- In an AMAN disease model, sodium channel clusters were disrupted or lost at abnormally lengthened nodes, together with IgG and complement deposits, and paranodal axoglial junctions were also disrupted. Channels redistributed during recovery (Established in the animal model) [[Susuki2007]].
- Rabbits sensitised with *Campylobacter jejuni* lipo-oligosaccharide developed anti-GM1 IgG and flaccid limb weakness, with nerve changes identical to those in GBS. Anti-GM1 IgG from people with GBS blocked muscle action potentials in a muscle-spinal cord coculture (Established) [[Yuki2004]].
- Detached paranodal myelin terminal loops in that rabbit model would reduce the safety factor for impulse transmission and might cause the rapidly reversible conduction block seen in human AMAN (Early evidence) [[Yuki2007]].

### subtypes · How common each subtype is
- IGOS first 1,000 inclusions, electrophysiological subtype: demyelinating 55% (312/573) in Europe/Americas, 45% (29/65) in Asia without Bangladesh, and 40% (38/94) in Bangladesh. Demyelinating was the most common subtype in all three regions [[Doets2018]].
- IGOS axonal subtype: 36% (34/94) in Bangladesh, 6% (33/573) in Europe/Americas and 6% (4/65) in other Asian countries (P < 0.001) [[Doets2018]].
- IGOS clinical variant: sensorimotor was most common in Europe/Americas (69%, 387/562) and Asia (43%, 27/63), and pure motor in Bangladesh (69%, 74/107) [[Doets2018]].
- In all IGOS regions, people with the axonal subtype were younger, had fewer sensory deficits and showed a trend towards poorer recovery than people with the demyelinating subtype [[Doets2018]].
- Plasma Exchange/Sandoglobulin trial (369 people tested within 15 days of onset, Hadden criteria): 69% demyelinating, 3% axonal, 3% inexcitable, 2% normal and 23% equivocal [[Hadden1998]].
- Northern China, 129 people (1991–92): AMAN 65%, AIDP 24%, unclassifiable 11% [[Ho1995]].
- Dhaka, Bangladesh, 100 people (2006–07): 67% axonal and 92% pure motor; 57% had positive *C. jejuni* serology, against 8% of family controls [[Islam2010]].
- Japan, 97 consecutive people: AMAN 45%, AIDP 34%, unclassified 21% [[Hiraga2005]].
- Northeast China, 99 people (2006–10): 67% AIDP [[Ye2013China]]; the 2007 water-linked epidemic in the same region (26 people): 54% AMAN [[Ye2010Epidemic]]. Figures can differ within one region between settings.
- Spain, 56 consecutive people (serial studies): 62.5% demyelinating, 28.5% axonal, 1.8% inexcitable, 1.8% equivocal and 5.4% normal [[Sedano2019]].
- Italy, 55 people with at least two studies: AIDP 65–67% falling to 58% at follow-up, axonal 18% rising to 38%, equivocal 14–16% falling to 4% [[Uncini2010]].
- AMAN is reported as the major subtype in Asia and Central and South America, in 30–65% of people [[Kuwabara2013]]. That range is higher than the 6% for non-Bangladesh Asia in IGOS, where only 65 Asian studies were analysed [[Doets2018]].
- Whether axonal GBS is truly more common in Asia than in Europe or North America is described as largely unresolved, partly because single-study criteria miss cases [[Bae2014]].
- Sensory study abnormalities were found in 85% of AIDP and only 6% of AMAN (59 people) [[Kuwabara2004Sensory]].
- In Hadden's trial cohort, 16% (53/342) had "pure motor GBS", with normal SNAPs and clinical sensory examination. They more often had IgG anti-GM1 and preceding diarrhoea, with a similar outcome [[Hadden1998]].

### subtypes · Antibodies and triggers by subtype
- Molecular mimicry between GM1 and GD1a gangliosides and *C. jejuni* lipo-oligosaccharide is a cause of AMAN (Established) [[Yuki2004,Yuki2007]].
- IgG anti-GD1a in 138 Chinese people at titre >1:100: 60% of AMAN against 4% of AIDP. At >1:1,000: 24% against 0% [[Ho1999]].
- IgG anti-GM1 in the same cohort at >1:100: 57% of AMAN against 35% of AIDP (not significant). At >1:1,000: 24% against 8% (not significant) [[Ho1999]].
- Northern China: IgG anti-GM1 in 42% of people with GBS against 6% of village controls (P < 0.01), without a significant link to subtype in that series of 38 [[Ho1995]].
- IgG to GM1, GM1b and GD1a were significantly more frequent in 21 people with AMAN than in 19 with AIDP, and were also present in AMSAN, so AMAN and AMSAN share an immunological profile [[Yuki1999AMSAN]].
- Anti-GM1b and anti-GalNAc-GD1a in 55 Chinese people: 32% and 21% in AMAN against 11% and 0% in AIDP, not significant in this small sample [[Yuki1999China]].
- In 199 people with serial NCS, AMAN was significantly linked to IgG against GM1, GalNAc-GD1a, GA1 or the LM1/GA1 complex. Reversible conduction failure was linked to GM1, GalNAc-GD1a, GD1b or LM1/GA1, and AIDP to none [[Shahrizaila2014]].
- Of 25 people with antibodies to GM1, GM1b, GD1a or GalNAc-GD1a, 48% had the AMAN pattern, 20% the AIDP pattern and 12% isolated F-wave absence at first examination. Three of the 5 with an AIDP pattern later showed AMAN or rapid normalisation [[Hiraga2005Neurology]].
- The largest profile to date (1,413 people, 15 glycolipids plus one phospholipid and their complexes, IgM, IgG and IgA): 92.6% were positive for at least one antibody on the combinatorial array, and complexes outperformed single glycolipids [[Thomma2025Brain]].
- That study challenges the idea that anti-GM1 antibodies mainly mean axonal GBS: they occurred in every electrophysiological subtype. Axonal proportion differed by GM1:phosphatidylserine interaction (enhanced 21.8%, attenuated 6.6%, independent 9.6%; P = 0.001) [[Thomma2025Brain]].
- Antibodies to GM1/GalNAc-GD1a complexes are linked to pure motor GBS, GD1a/GD1b and/or GD1b/GT1b complexes to severe GBS needing ventilation, and complexes containing GQ1b or GT1a to Fisher syndrome [[Kusunoki2011]].
- IGOS serology (768 people with samples): recent *C. jejuni* 30%, *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* 10%, cytomegalovirus 4%, hepatitis E 3%, Epstein-Barr virus 1%. Proportions were similar across continents, but the axonal subtype and pure motor variant were more frequent in Asian than in American or European *C. jejuni*-positive people [[Leonhard2022IGOS]].
- With weekly studies, 22 people with strict evidence of *C. jejuni* were first classed as AMAN (16), AIDP (5) or unclassified (1). All 5 with an AIDP pattern normalised distal latency within 2 weeks and eventually showed AMAN, whereas 13 people with cytomegalovirus- or Epstein-Barr-related AIDP showed distal latencies rising progressively over 8 weeks [[Kuwabara2004Cj]].
- A later serial study reached the same conclusion: in 23 people with recent *C. jejuni*, all eventually showed the AMAN pattern or reversible conduction failure [[Kokubun2013CNP]].
- EAN/PNS: anti-ganglioside testing is of limited clinical value in most people with typical motor-sensory GBS; anti-GQ1b testing should be considered when MFS is suspected; nodal-paranodal antibodies should be tested when autoimmune nodopathy is suspected (good practice points) [[vanDoorn2023]].

### subtypes · Reversible conduction failure and the node of Ranvier
- Sequential studies of 345 nerves in 34 people: IgG anti-GM1-positive people showed rapid resolution of conduction slowing and block, and rapid distal CMAP rises without prolonged duration or polyphasia, so no remyelinating slow components appeared. The authors attribute this to impaired physiological conduction at the nodes [[Kuwabara1998GM1]].
- In 3 of those people first diagnosed with AIDP, conduction slowing resolved within days [[Kuwabara1998GM1]].
- Of 18 people with AMAN studied serially, 12 (67%) had definite (7) or probable (5) conduction block. It reversed in 7 of 12 and was followed by axonal degeneration in 6; one person had both [[Kokubun2010]].
- All those conduction blocks had disappeared or begun to resolve within 3 weeks, without electrophysiological evidence of remyelination [[Kokubun2010]].
- Definite conduction block was much commoner across the ulnar elbow segment (39%) than in forearm segments (6%). The elbow is an entrapment site, and IGOS excluded that segment from its analysis [[Kokubun2010,Arends2024]].
- Reversible block, block followed by degeneration, and degeneration without block form one continuum, as clinical and antibody profiles did not differ between them [[Kokubun2010]].
- At second study, 46.6% of 45 people with axonal GBS had reversible conduction failure in two motor nerves and 8.8% in two sensory nerves [[Uncini2017]].
- Proposed criteria for early reversible conduction failure in a nerve: a 50% rise in distal CMAP or SNAP amplitude, or resolution of proximal motor block with a fall in distal latency or CMAP duration, or a rise in velocity. In 82 people, 45% met these, 26% had a contrasting pattern and 10% had both [[Chan2017]].
- Sensory fibres can show it too: serial recordings revealed sensory involvement in 69% of people with AMAN and 49% of their nerves. Rapid rises suggested reversible failure, and rises over months suggested regeneration [[Capasso2011]].
- Least significant test-retest change in SNAP amplitude in 20 controls was 44% (median), 47% (ulnar) and 58% (sural), which sets how large a change must be to count as real [[Capasso2011]].
- Reversible failure is reported in the pharyngeal-cervical-brachial variant (2 people, normalised within 4 weeks) [[Capasso2010]] and paraparetic GBS (1 person, followed over 5 months) [[Kimachi2017]].
- It can recover without immunotherapy, and it differs from mild AIDP, in which latency and slowing progress over 2 weeks (2 case reports) [[Kokubun2013JNS]].
- Reversible failure and axonal degeneration together explain the "paradox" of axonal neuropathy with fast recovery; Uncini proposed "nodo-paranodopathy" as a category spanning transitory conduction failure through to axonal degeneration (Hypothesis, widely discussed; a proposal, not agreed classification) [[Uncini2013,Uncini2018,Uncini2023]].
- A further proposal classifies autoimmune neuropathies by domain of the myelinated fibre and target antigen rather than as demyelinating or axonal (Hypothesis) [[Uncini2022]].
- Autoimmune nodo-paranodopathy includes IgG4 antibodies to paranodal proteins, with paranodal dismantling and no segmental de-remyelination; those people had been classified as chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy [[Uncini2018]].

### subtypes · Recovery: early and slow
- In northern China, median time to walk 5 metres with assistance was 31 days in AMAN (n = 32) and 32 days in AIDP (n = 8), too fast for severe Wallerian degeneration of the whole nerve [[Ho1997Recovery]].
- Proposed mechanisms for fast recovery are reversal of changes at the node, and degeneration and regrowth of intramuscular motor terminals, or both (Early evidence) [[Ho1997Recovery,Griffin1996Nodal]].
- Motor unit number estimates in 7 people with AMAN fell markedly at peak (mean 11). Strength began to recover during week 4 with rising CMAP, without a significant rise in motor unit number, suggesting early collateral reinnervation and later nerve regeneration [[Kuwabara2001]].
- Slow recovery (unable to walk independently at 6 months): 14% (6/44) of AMAN against 6% (2/33) of AIDP. Of the 6 with AMAN, 4 walked by one year and 2 at 28 and 57 months [[Hiraga2005]].
- At 48 weeks in Hadden's cohort, dead or unable to walk unaided: 50% (6/12) with inexcitable nerves against 15% (52/357) in the rest; axonal 10% (1/10) did not differ significantly from demyelinating 17% (44/254). These are population statistics, not individual forecasts [[Hadden1998]].
- Treatment outcome did not differ by neurophysiological category in the same trial [[Hadden1998]].
- In a non-randomised series of 72 people, IVIg or plasmapheresis groups showed more reduction in axonal burden and more early reversible block than the natural-course group [[Kalita2024]].
- Hyperreflexia in early recovery occurred in 13% of 54 people, more often in AMAN than AIDP (6/23 against 1/18) and with anti-GM1 (7/26 against 0/28) [[Kuwabara1999]].
- Up to 10% of people with GBS can have preserved or brisk reflexes [[Wakerley2015]]; in a Dutch cohort of 494, 2% had persistently normal reflexes in paretic arms [[Fokke2014]].

### mfs · Miller Fisher syndrome
- MFS is defined by ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and areflexia. Pupillary abnormalities, ptosis and facial palsy are frequent; sensory loss is unusual despite profound ataxia (50 consecutive people) [[Mori2001MFS]].
- Estimated share of the GBS spectrum: 5–25%, and it overlaps with classic sensorimotor GBS in an estimated 15% [[Leonhard2019]].
- IGOS: MFS plus MFS-GBS overlap in 22% (14/63) in Asia, 11% (64/562) in Europe/Americas and 1% (1/107) in Bangladesh (P < 0.001) [[Doets2018]].
- Anti-GQ1b discovery: IgG raised in the early phase in 6 of 6 people with typical MFS, falling with time, and absent in controls including GBS [[Chiba1992]].
- Anti-GQ1b positivity differs by series: 18/19 typical MFS (95%) [[Chiba1993]], 13/16 (81%) [[Yuki1993GQ1b]] and 83% in 466 people with Fisher syndrome [[Ito2008]].
- Anti-GQ1b is closely tied to ophthalmoplegia: 5 of 5 atypical MFS without ataxia and 5 of 6 GBS with ophthalmoplegia were positive, against none of 23 with GBS without ophthalmoplegia [[Chiba1993]].
- All anti-GQ1b sera also reacted with GT1a in that study. A monoclonal antibody stained the paranodal regions of human oculomotor, trochlear and abducens nerves, and oculomotor nerve contained more GQ1b than spinal roots [[Chiba1993]].
- Anti-GQ1b-positive people had more severe cerebellar-type ataxia than antibody-negative people (16 people) [[Yuki1993GQ1b]].
- Antibody titres peak at clinical presentation and can be undetectable as early as about one month after onset, so a late sample may miss them [[Overell2007]].
- Antibodies to ganglioside complexes containing GQ1b or GT1a were found in 47% of MFS, 47% of GBS with ophthalmoplegia and none of GBS without it (64, 53 and 53 people) [[Kanzaki2008]].
- The lesion sites are the oculomotor nerves and group Ia neurons in the dorsal root ganglion; ataxia and areflexia reflect an impaired proprioceptive afferent system [[Kuwabara2017]].
- Electrophysiology in Fisher syndrome: soleus H-reflex typically absent, a 1-Hz body-sway peak on posturography, SNAP and somatosensory-evoked potential abnormalities in about 30%, and an axonal GBS overlap in about 15% [[Kuwabara2017]].
- In IGOS, 25 of 27 people with MFS had normal or equivocal first NCS by Hadden criteria (17 equivocal, 8 normal; share derived here as 93%) [[Arends2026]].
- CSF albuminocytological dissociation was found in 76% of 466 with Fisher syndrome and 46% of 53 with Bickerstaff [[Ito2008]]. MFS is linked to lower CSF protein in IGOS [[AlHakem2023]].
- Course (Mori series as summarised by Cochrane; 28 untreated): ataxia gone at a median of 32 days (range 8–271) and ophthalmoplegia at 88 days (29–165) [[Overell2007]].
- Later Mori cohort of 92 people, median days to disappearance of ophthalmoplegia/ataxia: IVIg 82/39, plasma exchange 92/21, supportive care 76/31, with no statistically significant difference. Symptom-free at 6 months: 25/28 (89%), 22/23 (96%) and 39/41 (95%) [[Overell2007]].
- In that cohort 89 of 92 (96%) had complete remission of ophthalmoplegia and ataxia at one year, with no deaths recorded [[Overell2007]].
- The Cochrane review found no randomised or non-randomised prospective controlled trials of immunotherapy in Fisher syndrome or related disorders [[Overell2007]].
- Most people with pure MFS recover completely without treatment within 6 months, so treatment is generally not recommended, but close monitoring is advised [[Leonhard2019]].

### mfs · Overlap and progression
- All 60 consecutive people with Fisher syndrome started with the triad alone: 50% stayed pure and 50% overlapped, with pharyngeal-cervical-brachial GBS in 23%, conventional GBS in 15% and Bickerstaff in 12% [[Sekiguchi2016]].
- Median (range) days from Fisher syndrome onset to overlap: 5 (1–7) for pharyngeal-cervical-brachial GBS, 3 (1–4) for conventional GBS, and 3 (1–5) for Bickerstaff. Every overlap occurred within 7 days [[Sekiguchi2016]].
- In a Dutch cohort, 10 of 23 (43%) with MFS developed limb weakness, all in the first week, and no early predictor was found, so all people with MFS need careful monitoring for at least a week [[Verboon2017MFS]].
- Fisher/GBS overlap needed ventilation in 24% against 10% for GBS, and none with Fisher syndrome alone needed it in that study. Titubation as first symptom (55% against 18%) and descending tetraparesis (64% against 21%) marked ventilated people [[Funakoshi2009]].
- Clinical frequencies from 103 Japanese people classified by the 2014 criteria: classic GBS 71%, classic MFS 17%, Bickerstaff 3%, and 1–2% each for pharyngeal-cervical-brachial (2%), paraparetic, bifacial weakness with paraesthesias, acute ophthalmoparesis, acute ataxic neuropathy and each overlap [[Wakerley2016]].
- Ophthalmoplegic GBS had worse severity at nadir and outcome than MFS-GBS overlap (12 people) [[Verboon2017MFS]].

### mfs · Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis
- Bickerstaff: 62 people defined by progressive, relatively symmetrical ophthalmoplegia and ataxia by 4 weeks, plus disturbed consciousness or hyperreflexia [[Odaka2003]].
- Features in those 62: antecedent illness 92%, disturbed consciousness 74%, flaccid tetraparesis 60%, facial diplegia 45%, Babinski's sign 40%, pupillary abnormality and bulbar palsy 34%, anti-GQ1b IgG 66%, brain MRI abnormality 30% [[Odaka2003]].
- An autopsy showed perivascular lymphocytic infiltration with oedema and glial nodules in the brainstem. Limb weakness was attributed to overlap with axonal GBS [[Odaka2003]].
- Bickerstaff against Fisher syndrome in 581 people (53 and 466): anti-GQ1b 68% against 83%, *C. jejuni* 23% against 21%, CSF dissociation 46% against 76%, brain MRI abnormality 11% against 2%, abnormal EEG 57% against 25%; the authors conclude they form one spectrum [[Ito2008]].
- Fisher syndrome and Bickerstaff are grouped with acute ophthalmoparesis and acute ataxic neuropathy (limited end) and pharyngeal-cervical-brachial weakness and GBS overlap (extensive end) as "anti-GQ1b antibody syndrome" [[Shahrizaila2013JNNP]].
- Estimated share below 5%, and it does not meet commonly used GBS criteria [[Leonhard2019]]. The severity of the course justifies IVIg or plasma exchange, though evidence of efficacy is limited [[Leonhard2019]].

### mfs · Other localised variants and classification
- The 2014 classification divides GBS into classic and localised forms (such as pharyngeal-cervical-brachial weakness and bifacial weakness with paraesthesias), and MFS into incomplete forms (acute ophthalmoparesis, acute ataxic neuropathy) and a CNS subtype (Bickerstaff) [[Wakerley2014]].
- Estimated frequencies (to the nearest 5%, nine mainly adult cohorts): sensorimotor 30–85%, pure motor 5–70%, paraparetic 5–10%, pharyngeal-cervical-brachial <5%, bilateral facial palsy with paraesthesias <5%, pure sensory <1% [[Leonhard2019]].
- Pure motor runs from 5–15% in most studies to about 70% in Bangladesh [[Leonhard2019]].
- Bilateral facial palsy with paraesthesias, pure sensory and Bickerstaff do not meet commonly used diagnostic criteria but are often included in the spectrum, and this inclusion is debated [[Leonhard2019]].
- Pharyngeal-cervical-brachial, 100 people: pure 13, preserved reflexes 8, GBS overlap 48, Fisher overlap 26, Bickerstaff overlap 5 [[Nagashima2007]].
- In those 100: anti-GT1a IgG 51%, anti-GQ1b IgG 39%, axonal-pattern antibodies 27%, and 31% of antecedent infections were *C. jejuni* [[Nagashima2007]].
- Among 220 consecutive people with GBS, anti-GT1a and anti-GQ1b IgG were positive in 10% and 9%. All 5 with anti-GT1a not cross-reacting with GQ1b had bulbar palsy, neck weakness, no sensory disturbance and positive *C. jejuni* serology [[Koga2002]].
- Among 140 people with anti-GT1a IgG: Fisher syndrome 46%, GBS 16%, Bickerstaff 10%, pharyngeal-cervical-brachial 4%, plus overlaps [[Nagashima2004]].
- Polyneuritis cranialis (15 historical case reports): mean 6 cranial nerves (range 3–10), ocular signs 93%, bulbar 87%, facial 73%; anti-ganglioside antibodies in half of those tested [[Wakerley2015Polyneuritis]].
- Facial diplegia and paraesthesias, 22 people from about 8,600 referred: 86% had infectious symptoms, all had CSF dissociation, and 64% showed limb demyelination [[Susuki2009]].
- In those 22, anti-cytomegalovirus IgM was the most common serology (35%), and 4 had anti-GM2 IgM alongside it [[Susuki2009]].
- Acute facial diplegia with hyperreflexia (2 people) followed *C. jejuni* with antiganglioside IgG, so hyperreflexia does not exclude a GBS variant [[Susuki2004]].
- Paraparetic GBS: leg weakness and areflexia without arm, cranial or respiratory involvement, with onset often in the lower back, buttock or leg pain [[Kimachi2017]].
- Frequency of leg-only weakness: 6% (and arm-only 1%) of 494 Dutch people [[Fokke2014]]; 7 of 56 in Spain [[Sedano2019]]; 1 of 103 in Japan [[Wakerley2016]].
- Pure sensory GBS: 22 of 1,056 (about 2%, derived here) in southern China with normal CMAP. They had milder symptoms at admission and shorter stays, with no difference in CSF or treatment from sensory-only presentations with abnormal CMAP (retrospective) [[Liu2025Sensory]].
- MFS and the pharyngeal-cervical-brachial variant are frequently mistaken for brainstem stroke, botulism or myasthenia gravis, and Bickerstaff for Wernicke's encephalopathy [[Wakerley2015]].

### ncs · What the study measures and the criteria
- Electrodiagnostic studies are not required to diagnose GBS but are recommended where possible, particularly in atypical presentations. Typical findings are reduced velocities, reduced amplitudes, abnormal temporal dispersion and/or partial motor block [[Leonhard2019]].
- EAN/PNS advises electrodiagnostic testing to support the diagnosis (good practice point) [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Brighton level 1 diagnostic certainty requires NCS consistent with GBS [[Arends2024]]; in 494 Dutch people, nerve electrophysiology was compatible with neuropathy in 99%, but only 59% met criteria for a distinct subtype [[Fokke2014]].
- About one-third of people meet no subtype criteria and are labelled equivocal or inexcitable; international consensus on criteria has not been reached [[Leonhard2019]].
- Hadden demyelinating, needing the same feature in at least two nerves: distal motor latency (DML) >110% upper limit of normal (ULN) if distal CMAP (dCMAP) ≥50% lower limit of normal (LLN), or >120% ULN if dCMAP <50% LLN [[Arends2024]].
- Hadden, same rule: motor conduction velocity (MCV) <90% LLN if dCMAP ≥50% LLN, or <85% LLN if dCMAP <50% LLN [[Arends2024]].
- Hadden, same rule: proximal-to-distal CMAP ratio <0.5 with dCMAP ≥20% LLN; or F-wave latency >120% ULN [[Arends2024]].
- Hadden axonal: dCMAP <80% LLN in at least two nerves without demyelinating features (one demyelinating feature in one nerve allowed if dCMAP <10% LLN) [[Arends2024]].
- Rajabally demyelinating is stricter: DML >150% ULN, MCV <70% LLN, or F-wave latency >120% ULN (or >150% with dCMAP below 50% LLN, as the table's wording is read here), each in at least two nerves [[Arends2024]].
- Rajabally also counts ratio <0.7, or F-wave absence with dCMAP ≥20% LLN, in at least two nerves only with an additional demyelinating parameter in another nerve. Without demyelinating features, these same findings count towards axonal [[Arends2024]].
- Rajabally's modified criteria applied to 365 people gave AIDP 56.2%, axonal 35.1% and equivocal 7.7% on a single study, matching rates from serial work, against 71.5%, 17.5% and 9.9% with existing criteria. Classification change correlated with testing ≤7 days after onset [[Rajabally2015]].
- Against the reference of serial studies plus antibodies (53 demyelinating, 45 axonal), first-study misclassification was 15.3% for sparse linear discriminant analysis, 30% for Uncini's criteria, 45% for Rajabally's and 48% for Hadden's [[Uncini2017]].
- Uncini's criteria additionally evaluate duration of motor responses and the sural sparing pattern, and define reversible conduction failure at second study [[Uncini2017]].
- Criteria choice changes the answer: 1,137 IGOS studies were classed by Hadden as 56.6% demyelinating, 9.8% axonal, 24.3% equivocal, 3.1% inexcitable and 6.3% normal; by Rajabally as 32.5%, 30.0%, 28.1%, 3.1% and 6.3% [[Arends2024]].
- The two sets agreed in 68.3%. In disagreement, 13.6% were demyelinating by Hadden and axonal by Rajabally [[Arends2024]].
- Applying amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) electrodiagnostic criteria as a marker for "pure axonal loss", red flags were fulfilled in 68.6% of Rajabally axonal studies and 1.8% of Hadden axonal studies, mainly through conduction block (62.5%) and prolonged DML (23.5%) [[Arends2024]].
- Earlier, six published criteria sets applied to 43 people labelled from 21% to 72% as AIDP [[Alam1998]].
- In IGOS by nerve, unobtainable responses (<10% LLN) were found in 8.8% of median, 10.8% of ulnar, 13.4% of peroneal and 16.0% of tibial nerves. dCMAP was normal in 46.4%, 45.4%, 48.5% and 40.1% respectively [[Arends2024]].
- Most common demyelinating feature by person: prolonged DML (55.6%), reduced MCV (42.8%), lowered proximal-to-distal ratio (31.8%), prolonged F-wave latency (22.0%) [[Arends2024]].
- A proximal-to-distal ratio should be read with caution when dCMAP is below 1 mV [[Berciano2020]].
- Published reference limits vary by age and sex for the median nerve, and by age and height for others: median motor amplitude lower limit is 5.9 mV at 19–39 years and 3.8 mV at 60–79 years [[Chen2016NCSRef]].

### ncs · Direct comparison at first study
- First study within 10 days, 25 seropositive axonal against 19 seronegative AIDP (Korea, selected for certainty): CMAP amplitude did **not** differ between the groups, and conduction block was similar (32% against 37%) [[Koo2016]].
- Distal latency, velocity and CMAP duration did differ: median motor DL 4.0 against 5.7 ms, peroneal DL 4.6 against 10.6 ms, median MCV 51.2 against 43.8 m/s, peroneal proximal duration 6.8 against 13.7 ms (medians) [[Koo2016]].
- SNAP was the strongest discriminator: median 18.3 against 5.3 µV, ulnar 14.0 against 1.8 µV, while sural was similar at 13.5 against 15.6 µV [[Koo2016]].
- Ulnar SNAP ≥7.5 µV favoured axonal GBS (sensitivity 0.789, specificity 0.920), and median SNAP ≥12.5 µV likewise (0.944, 0.783) in this small selected group, which needs validation [[Koo2016]].

### ncs · Sural sparing, F waves, H reflex and A waves
- The sural sparing pattern is an abnormal upper-limb SNAP with a normal sural SNAP [[Derksen2014,Leonhard2019]].
- In 73 people with AIDP against 38 mimics, sural sparing was the most specific electrodiagnostic feature: at the orthodromic centre, ulnar-sural specificity 0.95 and sensitivity 0.41. Overall table values were 0.93 and 0.35 (ulnar-sural) and 0.91 and 0.26 (median-sural) [[Derksen2014]].
- Against the same mimics, prolonged DML had sensitivity 0.71 and specificity 0.74, abnormal F waves 0.80 and 0.57, and slow velocity 0.49 and 0.66, so common abnormalities do not exclude rarer diagnoses [[Derksen2014]].
- The pattern is not exclusive to AIDP: 4 of 8 AIDP, 5 of 13 AMAN/AMSAN, 3 of 9 unclassified and 7 of 20 MFS showed it in another series [[Umapathi2015]].
- In AIDP, sensory abnormalities were present in 85% of median, 85% of ulnar and 38% of sural nerves [[Kuwabara2004Sensory]].
- In the first week (31 people, Cleveland Clinic), abnormal findings included absent H reflex 97%, abnormal F wave 84%, reduced CMAP 71%, prolonged distal latency 65%, low or absent upper-limb SNAP 61%, temporal dispersion 58%, slowed velocity 52% and conduction block 13% [[Gordon2001]].
- Definite diagnosis was possible in 55% of those people, but not commonly until day 5. The abnormal upper-limb with normal sural SNAP pattern appeared in 48% overall [[Gordon2001]].
- Within 4 days of onset (18 people): motor abnormality 83%, abnormal late responses 77%, prolonged DML 55%, and no strictly normal study; 72% were still equivocal [[Alberti2011]].
- A waves are late motor responses of identical latency and shape. In the first week, multiple A waves were seen in 13 of 14 people, averaging 8 ± 2 per tibial and 4 ± 1 per peroneal nerve, and about half were below 50 µV [[Kornhuber1999]].
- A waves were present in 59% of 105 nerves within 10 days in 26 people, and were more frequent in nerves with otherwise normal NCS, so they may precede CMAP changes. Their shape did not distinguish AIDP from AMAN [[Veltsista2023]].
- A waves were seen in 58% of 50 people and linked to poorer short-term strength at 6 weeks (single study) [[Badry2019]].

### ncs · Timing and repeat studies
- NCS may be normal when done early (within 1 week of onset), or in people with proximal weakness, mild disease, slow progression or variants. A repeat 2–3 weeks later can help [[Leonhard2019]].
- Repeat study 3–8 weeks after onset may classify or reclassify cases, though this practice is controversial [[Leonhard2019]].
- Two studies, within 2 weeks and at 3–8 weeks, are likely to suffice (21 people): first study 17 AIDP, 2 AMAN, 2 unclassified; final 12 AIDP, 7 AMAN, 2 unclassified [[Shahrizaila2013]].
- IGOS serial data (469 people, first NCS at median day 7, IQR 4–10): first study confirmed polyneuropathy in 62.0% (Hadden) and 58.2% (Rajabally); equivocal 29.6% and 33.7%; normal 8.3% and 8.1% [[Arends2026]].
- Within 3 days of onset, IGOS could already class 58.4% (Hadden) or 52.1% (Rajabally) as axonal or demyelinating, with no significant effect of timing on subtype [[Arends2026]].
- On a second study (median 24 days later), subtype changed in 37.8% (Hadden) and 44.7% (Rajabally) of 331 people with motor-sensory or motor GBS, in multiple opposite directions, so overall proportions stayed stable [[Arends2026]].
- Change was commoner in motor GBS (55.1% by both sets, n = 78) than motor-sensory GBS (32.4% and 41.5%, n = 253) [[Arends2026]].
- Of those with a normal or equivocal first study, 37.0% (Hadden) and 36.9% (Rajabally) showed polyneuropathy on the second; in 19.0% and 21.1% both studies stayed normal or equivocal [[Arends2026]].
- IGOS authors conclude that early NCS supports the diagnosis and may be repeated if non-diagnostic, but "electrodiagnostic subtyping offers no additional value" [[Arends2026]].
- Inexcitable studies appeared only after day 3 in IGOS: 1.1% at days 4–7 and 2.5% at days 8–14 [[Arends2026]].
- Serial studies moved 24% of people in an Italian series, mostly from AIDP and equivocal to axonal, because they exposed reversible failure and length-dependent CMAP reduction [[Uncini2010]].
- Very early (≤4 days) study in 15 people allowed subtyping in only 3 (20%), all axonal; 40% were mixed, 33.3% equivocal and 6.7% normal; after serial studies, 46.7% were AIDP, 46.7% axonal and 6.6% unclassified [[Berciano2020]].
- A similar series of 7 reached accurate subtype in only 43% even after serial studies [[Nedkova2021]].
- Serial studies show that AIDP distal latencies rise gradually during the acute phase [[Shahrizaila2011]].
- In C. jejuni-related GBS, prolonged latencies and slowing in 11 people normalised within 3 weeks in 7 and were minor throughout in 4 [[Kokubun2013CNP]].

### ncs · Prognostic value of amplitude
- Distal CMAP amplitude was the strongest outcome predictor in the North American plasmapheresis study (210 people). A mean distal CMAP of 0–20% LLN carried a markedly raised probability of poor outcome, which plasmapheresis still improved [[Cornblath1988]].
- In 60 severely affected people, the strongest predictor of poor outcome was mean CMAP below 10% LLN [[Miller1988]].
- Six months after onset, CMAP amplitude abnormalities persisted while F-wave abnormalities improved (27 people) [[Grimm2016]].

### ncs · Ultrasound and MRI
- At days 2–3 (27 people, 31 controls), ultrasound showed significant enlargement of all measured nerves except sural (P = 0.086): vagus median 3.0 against 2.0 mm², cervical spinal nerves 3.5/4.0 against 2.6/3.2 mm [[Grimm2016]].
- Six months later, spinal nerves returned to 2.6/3.2 mm and vagus to 2.0 mm², except in those with persistent autonomic dysregulation (4.0 mm²). Peripheral nerves did not change significantly [[Grimm2016]].
- Serial ultrasound in 17 people against 17 controls showed enlarged median, ulnar and sural nerves within 3 weeks, significantly improving in ulnar and sural by 12 weeks, with no subtype difference or correlation with strength [[Razali2016]].
- In 38 people within 3 weeks, cervical roots enlarged most and tended to be larger in AIDP; C5 7.0 against 5.9 mm² and C6 12.0 against 10.5 mm² (the abstract's text does not assign the order of values explicitly) [[Liu2021Ultrasound]].
- Vagus was larger in those with autonomic dysfunction, 2.3 against 1.4 mm² [[Liu2021Ultrasound]].
- High-frequency ultrasound found enlargement at 16 of 20 sites around a median of 11 days, with variable regression and no consistent correlation with clinical severity (21 people) [[Storgaard2025]].
- In very early GBS, ultrasound lesions mainly involved ventral rami of cervical nerves [[Berciano2020,Berciano2017Proximal]].
- Ultrasound needs further validation before it can establish a diagnosis early [[Leonhard2019]]. EAN/PNS suggests MRI or ultrasound imaging be considered in atypical cases [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Gadolinium cauda equina enhancement in 24 people prospectively: 83% overall (mild 6, prominent 14), 18 of 19 typical (95%) and 2 of 5 variants, MRI at mean 13 days (range 2–42) [[Gorson1996]].
- Prominent enhancement went with severe back or leg pain (60% against 10%) and fewer walking independently by 2 months [[Gorson1996]].
- In children: 92% (22/24) on first MRI, 95% CI 0.745–0.978, reaching 100% after repeat in 2 [[Mulkey2010]]; 38 of 40 in another series, with anterior roots mean 2.19 mm and posterior 1.80 mm [[Yikilmaz2010]].
- Eight people studied: all had thickened, enhancing roots; 6 had anterior roots only. Anterior-only enhancement is strongly suggestive of GBS, whereas general enhancement also occurs in neoplasia and other inflammatory processes [[Byun1998]].
- Enhancement selectively involves anterior roots in AMAN [[Berciano2017Proximal]].
- Enhancement is sensitive but nonspecific; MRI is not routine, and is mainly for excluding other diagnoses or supporting diagnosis in young children [[Leonhard2019]].

### Exam hooks
- AIDP targets the Schwann cell surface and myelin; AMAN targets the axolemma at the node of Ranvier [[HaferMacko1996AIDP,HaferMacko1996AMAN]].
- AMAN earliest pathology: nodal lengthening, then periaxonal macrophages under an intact myelin sheath [[Griffin1996Nodal]].
- Worldwide, demyelinating is still the most frequent subtype in all three IGOS regions, including Bangladesh (40% against 36% axonal) [[Doets2018]].
- Axonal share: 6% in Europe/Americas against 36% in Bangladesh in IGOS [[Doets2018]].
- AMAN classic antibodies: IgG anti-GM1, GD1a, GalNAc-GD1a, GM1b [[Yuki1999AMSAN,Shahrizaila2014,Ho1999]].
- Fisher syndrome antibody: anti-GQ1b, cross-reacting with GT1a [[Chiba1993]].
- Pharyngeal-cervical-brachial antibody: anti-GT1a IgG, 51% [[Nagashima2007]].
- Triad of MFS: ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia [[Mori2001MFS]].
- Bickerstaff has impaired consciousness or hyperreflexia on top of ophthalmoplegia and ataxia [[Odaka2003]].
- Fisher overlap occurs in the first week: monitor at least 7 days [[Sekiguchi2016,Verboon2017MFS]].
- Reversible conduction failure: block or low CMAP that recovers fast with no remyelination signature [[Kuwabara1998GM1,Kokubun2010]].
- A conduction block can be axonal in mechanism, so it does not prove AIDP [[Kokubun2010,Uncini2010]].
- Earliest sensitive signs are in late responses: absent H reflex 97%, abnormal F wave 84% [[Gordon2001]].
- Sural sparing is specific (about 0.9) but insensitive (about 0.3–0.4) [[Derksen2014]].
- Normal NCS in the first week do not exclude GBS [[Leonhard2019]].
- Hadden block cut-off: proximal-to-distal ratio <0.5, with dCMAP ≥20% LLN [[Arends2024]].
- Low distal CMAP (0–20% LLN) predicts poor outcome [[Cornblath1988]].
- AMAN can recover as fast as AIDP: median 31 against 32 days to walk 5 m with help [[Ho1997Recovery]].
- GBS MRI sign: enhancing cauda equina roots, especially anterior; sensitive but not specific [[Gorson1996,Byun1998,Leonhard2019]].
- Early ultrasound sign: enlarged cervical spinal nerves and vagus [[Grimm2016]].

### In plain words
- GBS is not one disease. In some people the immune system damages the insulation around a nerve. In others it attacks the nerve fibre itself.
- A nerve is like an electric cable. The nodes of Ranvier are small gaps in the insulation, where the signal jumps along.
- Sometimes antibodies only stop the signal at those gaps for a while. When that clears up, strength can come back quickly.
- If the nerve fibre itself is badly damaged, it has to regrow, and that can take many months. Some people keep improving for years.
- Which type is common depends on where you live. People in Europe and the Americas rarely have the axonal type. In Bangladesh it is much more common.
- Miller Fisher syndrome affects the eyes, balance and reflexes. Most people recover fully, usually within 6 months.
- Miller Fisher syndrome can spread to the arms, legs, swallowing or breathing in the first week. That is why doctors watch closely at first.
- A nerve test sends small electric pulses along a nerve and measures the response.
- A normal nerve test in the first days does not rule out GBS. The test may need to be repeated.
- The label for your type can change on a later test. This does not always mean you are getting worse.
- Blood tests for antibodies help in some forms, such as Miller Fisher syndrome. Most people with typical GBS do not need them to be diagnosed.
- An MRI scan or ultrasound can show swollen nerve roots. These are extra tests, mainly used when the picture is unclear.
- The numbers here describe groups of people. They cannot tell you exactly what will happen to you.

## Open questions and controversies
- Is subtyping useful at all? Serial studies are needed to get it right [[Uncini2010,Bae2014]]; a single study with modified criteria may suffice [[Rajabally2015]]; IGOS finds that subtype changes in 38–45% and concludes it adds no value [[Arends2026]]. Status: unresolved. EAN/PNS advises testing to support the diagnosis [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Which criteria? Hadden and Rajabally agree in 68.3% of studies [[Arends2024]]; Uncini reports lowest first-study error for sparse linear discriminant analysis (15.3%), but against a reference built from serial study and antibodies [[Uncini2017]]. Rajabally "axonal" often fulfils red flags against pure axonal loss [[Arends2024]]. Status: no consensus [[Leonhard2019]].
- Does *C. jejuni* elicit true AIDP? Serial studies say no [[Kuwabara2004Cj,Kokubun2013CNP]]; IGOS finds demyelinating classification most frequent even after *C. jejuni*, though less so than after cytomegalovirus, [[Leonhard2022IGOS]]. Status: the gap may partly reflect single against serial testing.
- Is anti-GM1 an axonal marker? Traditional association [[Kuwabara1998GM1,Shahrizaila2014]]; large array data show it in all subtypes [[Thomma2025Brain]]. Status: assay method and antigen complexes matter.
- Is axonal GBS commoner in Asia? Bangladesh and historic northern China say yes [[Islam2010,Ho1995]]; IGOS Asia outside Bangladesh shows 6%, from just 65 studies [[Doets2018]]; a 2014 review calls it unresolved [[Bae2014]].
- Do axonal forms recover worse? Trend to worse in IGOS [[Doets2018]]; slow recovery in 14% against 6% in Japan [[Hiraga2005]]; similar median recovery in China [[Ho1997Recovery]]; no significant difference at 48 weeks in Hadden, with few axonal cases [[Hadden1998]].
- Is "nodo-paranodopathy" a better category? Proposed and developed by one group [[Uncini2013,Uncini2018,Uncini2022,Uncini2023]]. (Hypothesis). Not a formal diagnostic category in the guideline abstract, other than the term autoimmune nodopathy [[vanDoorn2023]].
- Does reversible conduction failure depend on immunotherapy? Some cases recover without it [[Kokubun2013JNS]]; a non-randomised series suggests treatment increases it [[Kalita2024]]. Status: no controlled data found.
- Is Bickerstaff part of GBS? Shared antibodies and overlap support one spectrum [[Ito2008,Shahrizaila2013JNNP]]; it does not fulfil commonly used GBS criteria [[Leonhard2019]].

- vanDoorn2023 is the Eur J Neurol record; the same guideline appeared in J Peripher Nerv Syst (PMID 37814551).
- Every source line was confirmed against a PubMed esummary record on 2026-11-09 (authors, title, journal, year, PMID, PMCID and DOI generated from the record). Abstracts were read through PubMed efetch for each cited paper.
- Full text read through Europe PMC: Arends 2024, Arends 2026, Leonhard 2019, Berciano 2020, Koo 2016, Thomma 2025 and Liu 2025. Read through the PMC site: Derksen 2014, Overell 2007 and Shahrizaila 2014 (its per-antibody percentages sit in an image table and were not extracted, so none are quoted).
- Reference values: Chen 2016 is cited for the paper; the values were read from the AANEM chart summarising it (aanem.org "NDTF Chart", charts dated 4/20/2020) and from the AANEM-hosted PDF of the paper. Sural and sensory velocity limits were read in the paper text.
- Hadden and Rajabally cut-offs are reproduced from Arends 2024 Tables 2 and 3, not the original papers, whose full texts were paywalled.
- Paywalled and not read in full: Doets 2018, Hadden 1998, Rajabally 2015, Uncini 2017, Wakerley 2014, van Doorn 2023, Shahrizaila 2021, Kokubun 2010, Odaka 2003 and Nagashima 2007. Their numbers are from abstracts.
- Mori's day counts are as reported by the Cochrane review (Overell 2007), because the abstract of Mori 2001 gives none. The review's 28 untreated and later 92-person groups come from the same group's reports and are not directly comparable.
- Gordon 2001's abstract gives "25 patients [84%]" for abnormal F wave, though 25 of 31 is 81%. The published 84% is quoted as printed. Its sural-sparing sentence is also garbled, so the 48% overall figure is used.
- Derksen 2014's text and table differ for the ulnar-sural likelihood ratio, so no likelihood ratio is quoted. Sensitivity and specificity are given with their centre or overall label.
- Liu 2021's abstract prints the C5 and C6 values in the order 5.9 against 7.0 and 10.5 against 12.0 after saying AIDP was larger, so the assignment of values to subtype is an inference from that sentence.
- Dropped because they could not be traced: anti-GQ1b "85–90%" as a stated figure, absolute F-wave reference latencies, Uncini 2017's numeric cut-offs and per-antibody subtype percentages from Shahrizaila 2014. Yuki 2012 and Sejvar 2011 (Brighton) are not listed because no numbers were taken from them.
- Secondary reports not used as evidence: web-search mirrors stating 89% for anti-GQ1b in Mori 2001.
