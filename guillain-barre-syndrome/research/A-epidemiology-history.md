# A · Epidemiology, burden, presentation and history

Pass date: 2026-11-09. Modules served: what, history.

This pass covers what Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is, how it presents in outline, how common it is by age, sex, region and season, its outbreaks, its death and disability figures, its costs and burden, the International GBS Outcome Study (IGOS) cohort, and the history of the syndrome from 1859 to now. Triggers and mimicry (B), subtypes and variants (C), criteria and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) detail (D), treatment trials (E), intensive care (F) and prognosis tools (G) belong to other passes and appear here only as context.

All outcome and survival figures below are population statistics from the named cohorts. They do not predict what will happen to any one person.

## Findings

### what · What GBS is
- The EAN/PNS 2023 guideline defines GBS as "an acute polyradiculoneuropathy"; besides weakness and sensory disturbance, people may have cranial nerve involvement, respiratory insufficiency, autonomic dysfunction and pain [[vanDoorn2023]].
- GBS is a rare but potentially fatal immune-mediated disease of the peripheral nerves and nerve roots, usually triggered by infections [[Leonhard2019]].
- GBS is described as the most common cause of acute flaccid paralysis worldwide [[Shahrizaila2021]] and as "the most common and most severe acute paralytic neuropathy", with about 100,000 people developing it each year worldwide [[Willison2016]].
- The acute flaccid paralysis surveillance programme was built for poliomyelitis, and it covers children up to 15 years. Studies from China and Bangladesh show GBS is now the predominant cause of acute flaccid paralysis in that age group [[Papri2021]].
- Classic GBS is an acute-onset ascending sensorimotor neuropathy, but it can present atypically or as a clinical variant [[Leonhard2019]].
- Classic sensorimotor GBS is rapidly progressive symmetrical weakness with sensory signs and absent or reduced tendon reflexes, usually reaching its worst point (nadir) within 2 weeks [[Leonhard2019]].
- GBS is defined clinically as a peripheral neuropathy causing limb weakness that progresses for up to 4 weeks before reaching a plateau [[Hughes1997]].
- GBS has a monophasic course. Treatment-related fluctuations and relapses occur in a minority [[Leonhard2019]]. In 494 Dutch adults, 95% had a monophasic course, and 10% of those had a treatment-related fluctuation [[Fokke2014]].
- Changing the diagnosis to acute-onset chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy should be considered if progression continues after 8 weeks from onset, which occurs in around 5% of people first diagnosed with GBS [[vanDoorn2023]].
- After the progressive phase comes a plateau lasting days to weeks or months, followed by recovery [[Leonhard2019]].

### what · How it presents, in outline
- All 494 adults in the Dutch cohort developed bilateral limb weakness, generally in arms and legs. It stayed in the legs in 6% and in the arms in 1% [[Fokke2014]].
- Reflexes were reduced in paretic limbs in 91% at first and in all patients during follow-up, although 2% kept normal reflexes in paretic arms [[Fokke2014]].
- Weakness is always bilateral but can be asymmetrical, mainly proximal or mainly distal, and it can start in the legs, the arms or all limbs at once [[Leonhard2019]].
- A minority, particularly those with a pure motor variant and the acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) subtype, may keep normal or even exaggerated reflexes [[Leonhard2019]].
- Severe diffuse pain or isolated cranial nerve dysfunction can come before the weakness [[Leonhard2019]].
- Dysautonomia is common and can include blood pressure or heart rate instability, pupil changes, and bowel or bladder dysfunction [[Leonhard2019]].
- Children under 6 can present with poorly localised pain, refusal to bear weight, irritability, meningism or an unsteady gait [[Leonhard2019]]. GBS is correctly diagnosed at admission in only about one-third of affected preschool-aged children [[Papri2021]].
- Maximum disability within 24 hours of onset, or after 4 weeks, should prompt a search for another diagnosis [[Leonhard2019]].
- Time to nadir varies by source: within 2 weeks in 80%, 4 weeks in 97% and 6 weeks in all (494 Dutch adults) [[Fokke2014]]; within 2 weeks in 96% and 4 weeks in 99.8% (IGOS, n=858 with data) [[Doets2018]].
- In the 2013-14 French Polynesian Zika series, progression was especially fast: the median installation phase was 6 days (IQR 4-9) and the median plateau 4 days (3-10) [[CaoLormeau2016]].
- In IGOS, 76% (649/857) reported an antecedent event [[Doets2018]]. Serology in the first 1,000 IGOS patients found recent *Campylobacter jejuni* in 30%, *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* in 10%, cytomegalovirus in 4%, hepatitis E virus in 3% and Epstein-Barr virus in 1% (n=768 with biosamples) [[Leonhard2022IGOS]].

### what · Incidence worldwide
- The best meta-analytic estimate for Western countries pooled 16 North American and European studies, 1,643 cases and 152.7 million person-years. It gave a crude incidence range of 0.81 to 1.89 per 100,000 person-years, median 1.11 (13 studies) [[Sejvar2011]].
- Sejvar's model: rate per 100,000 person-years = exp[-12.0771 + 0.01813 × age in years] × 100,000. This suits Western background-rate calculations, so a calculator can be built directly on it [[Sejvar2011]].
- Incidence rises 20% for every 10-year increase in age, from a model estimate of 0.62 at 0-9 years to 2.66 at 80-89 years per 100,000 person-years [[Sejvar2011]].
- Male to female rate ratio: 1.78 (95% CI 1.36-2.33) in the model [[Sejvar2011]].
- A 2024 global meta-analysis of 122 articles put incidence at 1.12 (95% CI 0.98-1.27) per 100,000 person-years, with males 1.38 (1.14-1.64) and females 0.99 (0.81-1.18) [[Xu2024]].
- An earlier systematic review of 63 papers gave a best overall estimate of 1.1 to 1.8 per 100,000 per year, rising after 50 years of age from 1.7 to 3.3 per 100,000 per year [[McGrogan2009]].
- Children (0-15 years): 0.34 to 1.34 per 100,000 per year in ten studies [[McGrogan2009]].
- A 1997 review gave a median incidence of 1.3 per 100,000 (range 0.4-4.0) and described peaks in young adults and the elderly [[Hughes1997]].
- Lifetime risk of GBS in the general population is about 0.1% [[Leonhard2019]].

### what · Incidence by region
- Reported incidence ranges from 0.16 to 3.0 per 100,000 persons per year. That includes about 0.40 in Brazil, 0.84-1.91 in Europe and North America, and 2.1-3.0 in Iran, Curaçao and Bangladesh [[Papri2021]].
- The true incidence in many low-income and middle-income countries remains unknown, and publications from Africa are especially scarce [[Papri2021]].
- United States: 1.65-1.79 per 100,000 in adults in the Nationwide Inpatient Sample, 2000-2004 (n=4,954) [[Alshekhlee2008]]; about 1.72 per 100,000 person-years, age-standardised, in the Vaccine Safety Datalink, 2000-2009, after correcting for a 55% positive predictive value of the diagnosis code [[Shui2012]].
- United Kingdom: 1.65-1.88 per 100,000 per year treated in hospital, 2016-2019 [[Keddie2021]].
- New Zealand: an average of 2.32 first hospitalisations per 100,000 per year, 1988-2010 (n=2,056) [[Baker2012NZ]].
- Mainland China: 0.698 per 100,000 person-years (95% CI 0.691-0.705), 0.233 in children and 0.829 in adults, from 38,861 people at all 1,665 tertiary hospitals, 2016-2019. Only tertiary hospitals were covered, so this may undercount (my inference) [[Zheng2022]].
- Urban China: 0.41 (95% CI 0.27-0.58) to 0.58 (0.38-0.82) per 100,000 person-years, 2013-2017, from 1.44 billion insured person-years and 3,534 cases [[Xu2024]].
- Peru, before the 2019 outbreak: 0.62 (2015), 0.92 (2016) and 0.91 (2017) per 100,000, from hospital discharge data [[Munayco2019]].
- Across countries, enteric infection incidence was positively associated with GBS incidence (coefficient 0.0000185, P=0.007) [[Xu2024]].

### what · Age and sex
- Almost all reports document more GBS in men than women, about 1.5:1, including in low-income and middle-income countries [[Papri2021]].
- Ratios in individual cohorts: 1.48 male to female in IGOS (552/373) [[Doets2018]]; 1.49 in China [[Zheng2022]]; males 40% higher in the US Vaccine Safety Datalink [[Shui2012]]; 56% male in the Dutch cohort [[Fokke2014]].
- Where cases fall by age depends on who lives there. In ageing populations in Europe and North America, GBS is most frequent at 50-80 years (2.0-4.0 per 100,000 per year); studies from Bangladesh, China, India, Brazil, Ethiopia and Tanzania suggest most cases occur at 21-35 years [[Papri2021]].
- China's incidence peak was at 70-74 years: 1.806 per 100,000 (95% CI 1.741-1.870) [[Zheng2022]].
- Median age in IGOS: 51 years (IQR 33-64) overall, 55 in Europe/Americas, 50 in Asia and 28 in Bangladesh [[Doets2018]].
- Mean age was 41.7 years in the 2019 Peruvian outbreak (58.7% male) [[ReyesVega2023]].
- The GBD analysis found the age-standardised burden increased with age and was higher in males in all age groups [[Bragazzi2021]].

### what · Seasons
- A meta-analysis of 42 studies and 9,836 patients found a 14% higher risk in winter than summer (IRR 1.14, 95% CI 1.02-1.27), with marked heterogeneity (I²=77%) [[Webb2015]].
- Winter to summer IRR by region: Western countries 1.28, Far East 1.20, Middle East 1.12, Indian subcontinent 0.86, Latin America 0.75 [[Webb2015]].
- Seasonality was greater with a respiratory prodrome (IRR 3.06, 1.84-5.11) than a diarrhoeal one (IRR 1.10, 0.60-2.00) [[Webb2015]].
- In Oxford, 2001-2012 (n=140), the winter to summer IRR was 1.92 (1.18-3.11) [[Webb2015]].
- In the US, rates were 15% higher in winter and spring than in summer [[Shui2012]].
- Northern China and Bangladesh show summer rises, thought to reflect more preceding diarrhoea when heat and humidity favour bacterial growth [[Papri2021]]. China's nationwide data put the south-eastern coastal concentration in summer and autumn [[Zheng2022]].

### what · Outbreaks
- GBS usually occurs sporadically, but outbreaks follow epidemics of triggering infections. Examples include northern China (2007) and Mexico (2011), both linked to *C. jejuni* [[Papri2021]].
- Northern China had annual summer epidemics of acute flaccid paralysis among rural children and young adults. A historical analysis covered more than 3,200 people [[McKhann1993]].
- French Polynesia, October 2013 to April 2014: 42 people were diagnosed with GBS. 41 (98%) had anti-Zika IgM or IgG, and all had neutralising antibodies, against 56% (54/98) of hospital controls (p<0.0001) [[CaoLormeau2016]].
- In that series, 88% had a transient illness a median of 6 days before neurological symptoms, 29% (12) needed respiratory assistance, and nobody died [[CaoLormeau2016]].
- That outbreak was a 20-fold increase in GBS. Incidence then rose about 3.2 to 5.1 times in areas of Latin America and the Caribbean affected by Zika (2014-2016) [[Papri2021]].
- Across 11 locations, an estimated 2.0 (95% credible interval 0.5-4.5) reported GBS cases occurred per 10,000 Zika infections [[MierYTeranRomero2018]].
- Colombia, November 2015 to March 2016: 68 people with GBS at six hospitals, 97% with prior Zika-compatible symptoms, median interval 7 days (IQR 3-10), bilateral facial paralysis in 50% and a demyelinating pattern in 78% of those tested (36/46) [[Parra2016]].
- Peru, 20 May to 27 July 2019: 683 suspected or confirmed cases across nine of 24 departments, an annualised 30.9 per 100,000 person-years, nearly 25 times the expected rate [[Munayco2020]].
- In that outbreak, 15.0% (64/426) needed mechanical ventilation and 68.0% (100/147) of those tested had AMAN. Campylobacter was detected in 5.2% (19) of faecal samples; isolates were sequence type 2993 [[Munayco2020]].
- A national analysis of 772 Brighton level 1-3 cases from the same outbreak found a case fatality of 4.3%, sequelae reported in 32.2% of survivors and axonal subtypes in 75.6% of those with nerve conduction studies [[ReyesVega2023]].
- A Lima hospital series from that outbreak (n=49) found recent *C. jejuni* evidence in 65% (28/43) and no recent arbovirus infection; 80% were pure motor and 43% axonal [[Ramos2021]].
- Pune, India, January to March 2025: 230 cases with 12 deaths (case fatality 5.2%), male to female ratio 2.2:1. *C. jejuni* was confirmed in 48.7% (72/148) and norovirus in 22.2% (40/180); water and poultry were implicated as sources [[Viswanathan2026]].

### what · COVID-era incidence and prevention signals
- UK cases treated with immunoglobulin fell from March to May 2020 against the same months of 2016-19: March 93 vs a mean of 132, April 70 vs 116, May 56 vs 113 [[Keddie2021]]. That is about 30%, 40% and 50% lower (calculated here from those counts).
- The authors suggest lockdown reduced the spread of triggers such as *C. jejuni* and respiratory viruses; regional GBS and COVID-19 incidence did not correlate (r=0.06, 95% CI -0.56 to 0.63) [[Keddie2021]].
- IGOS saw no rise in recruitment during the pandemic. Of 49 people enrolled from 30 January to 30 May 2020, 22% had a confirmed or probable preceding SARS-CoV-2 infection, a figure the authors say may reflect recruitment bias [[Luijten2021]].
- In New Zealand, three years after regulation cut Campylobacter contamination of fresh poultry meat, campylobacteriosis notifications had fallen 52% and GBS hospitalisations 13% [[Baker2012NZ]].

### what · Mortality
- Mortality is estimated at 3-10% even with the best medical care [[Leonhard2019]]; most studies worldwide report 2-10% [[Papri2021]].
- Reported mortality by region: 2-7% in Europe and North America, 13% in Hong Kong, 14-17% in Bangladesh, 16% in Egypt [[Papri2021]].
- IGOS: 5% (23/486) in Europe/Americas, 2% (1/45) in Asia, 17% (19/114) in Bangladesh (P<0.001) [[Doets2018]]. Overall, 7% (44/659) died during follow-up, at a median of 33 days after onset of weakness (IQR 16-88) [[Doets2018]].
- Netherlands, pooled cohort of 527: 2.8% died within 6 months and 3.9% within 12 months (356 with extended follow-up) [[vanDenBerg2013]].
- In that cohort, the median time to death was 76 days. 20% of deaths were in the progressive phase, 13% in the plateau and 67% in the recovery phase, most often from respiratory or cardiovascular complications [[vanDenBerg2013]].
- Risk factors for death in that cohort were age, severity of weakness at entry, mechanical ventilation, delay from onset to entry and time to peak disability [[vanDenBerg2013]].
- US hospitals, 2000-2004: in-hospital mortality was 2.58% (128/4,954) in adults. Endotracheal intubation predicted death (adjusted OR 5.09, 95% CI 3.21-8.05) [[Alshekhlee2008]].
- Dhaka, 2010-2013: 12% (50/407) died, at a median of 18 days. 20% of those deaths (10) were attributed to no ventilator being available [[Ishaque2017]].
- In the Dhaka cohort, lack of ventilator support when needed was the strongest risk factor (HR 11.9, 95% CI 4.6-30.7), followed by age 40 or over (HR 5.9, 2.1-16.7) and bulbar involvement (HR 5.4, 1.5-19.2) [[Ishaque2017]].
- Peru: case fatality 3.5% in routine data, highest in people over 60 [[Munayco2019]], and 4.3% in the 2019 outbreak [[ReyesVega2023]].
- A 2012 review of prospective studies in adequately treated adults reports mortality of about 4% within the first year [[Rajabally2012]].

### what · Ventilation and severity
- About 20% develop respiratory failure and need mechanical ventilation [[Leonhard2019]], up to 22% within the first week of admission [[Leonhard2019]]; Willison gives 20-30% for severe generalised disease with respiratory failure [[Willison2016]].
- IGOS: 19% (176/925) overall, 17% in Europe/Americas, 25% in Asia and 29% in Bangladesh (P=0.004) [[Doets2018]].
- Other cohorts: 21% in a Swedish prospective cohort of 42 [[Forsberg2004]]; 9.1% intubated in US hospital administrative data [[Alshekhlee2008]]; 31% in the 1991 northern China series (n=36) [[McKhann1991]].
- Unable to walk independently at nadir: 76% in Europe/Americas, 76% in Asia, 93% in Bangladesh (P<0.001) [[Doets2018]].
- Median MRC sum score at nadir: 46 in Europe/Americas, 48 in Asia, 16 in Bangladesh (P<0.001) [[Doets2018]].

### what · Walking and longer-term outcomes
- About 80% regain independent walking at 6 months [[Leonhard2019]], and elsewhere the same guideline gives 60-80% at 6 months with or without treatment [[Leonhard2019]]. About 20% worldwide cannot walk unaided at 6 months, rising to 30-40% where AMAN predominates and most people get no immunotherapy [[Papri2021]].
- IGOS, walking unaided at 6 months: Asia 88% (36/41), Europe/Americas 79% (331/418), Bangladesh 62% (60/97), P<0.001 [[Doets2018]].
- IGOS, walking unaided at 1 year: Asia 91% (31/34), Europe/Americas 83% (334/404), Bangladesh 69% (67/97), P=0.003 [[Doets2018]].
- IGOS, median time to independent walking: 63 days (IQR 28-186) in Europe/Americas, 39 days (17-94) in Asia and 95 days (36-190) in Bangladesh (P=0.002) [[Doets2018]].
- Of people who could not walk at nadir in IGOS, 77% regained independent walking by 6 months and 81% by 12 months [[Doets2018]].
- Sweden, prospective (n=42), at 2 weeks, 1 year and 2 years: unable to walk 10 m in 62%, 10% and 7%; submaximal overall strength in 100%, 62% and 55%; affected sensation in 93%, 55% and 52% [[Forsberg2004]].
- Severe persistent disability affects 14% at 1 year; loss of full strength, persistent pain and the need to change occupation occur in about 40% [[Rajabally2012]].
- Three to six years after onset (n=122), 84 people had no or minor signs, 24 moderate recovery and 14 severe residual signs. 25% had changed jobs and 44% had given up some leisure activities [[Bernsen2001]].
- Fatigue unrelated to residual motor deficits is found in 60-80% and is often among the most disabling complaints [[Leonhard2019]]. Severe fatigue was present in 80% of a mixed group of 113 people with immune-mediated neuropathies, including past GBS [[Merkies1999]].
- Severe pain is reported in at least one-third at 1 year and can persist beyond 10 years [[Leonhard2019]].
- Most people, even those tetraplegic at nadir or ventilated for a long time, recover extensively, especially in the first year, and recovery from residual complaints may still occur more than 5 years after onset [[Leonhard2019]].

### what · Recurrence and family occurrence
- Recurrence is rare, affecting 2-5%, though this is above the population's 0.1% lifetime risk [[Leonhard2019]].
- A Dutch series identified 32 people with recurrent GBS and 81 episodes. They were younger than 476 non-recurrent controls (mean 34.2 vs 46.9 years) and more often had Miller Fisher syndrome or milder symptoms [[Kuitwaard2009JNNP]].
- A survey of 245 Dutch people with GBS found 9 with confirmed recurrence, which is about 3.7% (calculated here). None of 106 who had flu vaccination (775 vaccinations in total) reported a recurrence afterwards [[Kuitwaard2009JPNS]].
- Pain or severe fatigue was reported in about 70% a median of 10 years after the diagnosis of GBS in that survey [[Kuitwaard2009JPNS]].
- Twelve Dutch families had at least two members with GBS, and the authors say this suggests a role for genetic factors [[Geleijns2004]].

### what · Costs and burden
- GBD 2019: 150,095 prevalent cases worldwide (95% UI 119,924-188,309), up from 90,249 in 1990, causing 44,407 years lived with disability (YLDs; 28,016-64,777) [[Bragazzi2021]].
- GBD 2019 age-standardised prevalence was 1.9 per 100,000 (1.5-2.4), a 6.4% rise since 1990 (3.6-9.5); the YLD rate was 0.6 per 100,000 (0.4-0.8) [[Bragazzi2021]].
- National GBD prevalence ranged from Japan at 6.4 (5.3-7.7) to China at 0.8 (0.6-1.0) per 100,000 [[Bragazzi2021]]. The GBD figures are modelled from hospital and claims data, and the authors flag this as a limitation [[Bragazzi2021]].
- United States, 2004: an estimated annual cost of $1.7 billion (95% CI $1.6-1.9 billion), of which $0.2 billion (14%) was direct medical and $1.5 billion (86%) indirect, mostly from premature deaths. Mean cost per person was $318,966 (95% CI $278,378-359,554) [[Frenzen2008]].
- China, 2016-2019: median hospital stay 13 days (IQR 8-18), median hospitalisation cost $2,371.60 ($1,281.80-5,463.60) [[Zheng2022]].
- Bangladesh: immunoglobulin costs about US$12,000-16,000 and plasma exchange about US$4,500-5,000, unaffordable for most; only 10-12% receive either. Private intensive care costs about US$300-1,200 a day, and small-volume plasma exchange about US$500 [[Papri2021]].

### what · The IGOS cohort
- IGOS is a prospective observational cohort of people with GBS of any age, severity, variant or treatment, enrolled within 2 weeks of onset of weakness. It started in May 2012, and by January 2017 had more than 1,400 participants from 143 centres in 19 countries on 5 continents [[Jacobs2017]].
- Doets 2018 analysed the first 1,000 inclusions with at least 1 year of follow-up, excluding 75 (8%), which left 925 from 18 countries [[Doets2018]].
- Three regions were compared: Europe/Americas (n=715), Asia without Bangladesh (n=69; Japan, Malaysia, Taiwan), and Bangladesh (n=125). Africa (n=11) and Australia (n=4) were too small for regional analysis [[Doets2018]].
- Main clinical variant: sensorimotor in Europe/Americas (69%, 387/562) and Asia (43%, 27/63), pure motor in Bangladesh (69%, 74/107) [[Doets2018]].
- Miller Fisher syndrome and its overlap: Asia 22% (14/63), Europe/Americas 11% (64/562), Bangladesh 1% (1/107) (P<0.001) [[Doets2018]].
- Demyelinating subtype: 55% in Europe/Americas, 45% in Asia, 40% in Bangladesh. Axonal subtype: 6%, 6% and 36% (P<0.001) [[Doets2018]].
- First treatment: no immunotherapy in 7% (Europe/Americas), 13% (Asia) and 86% (Bangladesh); IVIg in 86%, 73% and 6%; plasma exchange in 6%, 15% and 7% [[Doets2018]].
- In the first 1,300 IGOS inclusions, 83% of Bangladeshi participants (n=189) were untreated, while 92% (941/1,023) elsewhere received immunotherapy [[Verboon2019]].
- Median time from onset of weakness to treatment was 4 days in Europe/Americas, 5 in Asia and 7 in Bangladesh (P=0.003) [[Doets2018]].

### history · The story in order
- Octave Landry described "ascending paralysis" in 1859 in ten patients; his emphasis was motor paralysis with a poor prognosis. He noted illness convalescence, childbirth and exposure to cold as circumstances, and treated with massage, volatile liniments and electric stimulation [[Afifi1994]].
- Others including Duménil, Osler and Grainger Stewart reported similar cases before 1916, without lumbar puncture [[Freitas2017]].
- In 1916, Guillain, Barré and Strohl reported two French soldiers with acute flaccid paralysis, high CSF protein and normal cell counts [[Guillain1916,Goodfellow2016,Waclawik2018]]. The paper appeared in the bulletin of the Paris hospital medical society, and did not mention Landry [[Afifi1994]].
- Lumbar puncture was still new; Quincke had described it 25 years before, in 1891 [[Afifi1994]].
- André Strohl contributed the myographic studies of tendon reflexes, an early step towards bedside electrodiagnosis [[Waclawik2018]]. Later papers dropped his name, and the reasons given vary [[Afifi1994]].
- Haymaker and Kernohan's 1949 report of 50 fatal cases (32 died between days 2 and 10) placed early lesions in the spinal nerves, read the oedema as degenerative, and brought Landry's paralysis, acute febrile polyneuritis and GBS under one heading [[Haymaker1949,Berciano2017]].
- Waksman and Adams in 1955 produced experimental allergic neuritis in rabbits with peripheral nerve tissue and adjuvants, with raised CSF protein and usually a normal cell count. Their model pointed to an immune cause [[Waksman1955,Afifi1994]].
- Asbury, Arnason and Adams in 1969 showed perivenular mononuclear infiltrates with segmental demyelination across roots, ganglia, cranial nerves, plexuses and peripheral nerves, and disputed oedema as the main lesion [[Asbury1969,Afifi1994]].
- Fisher described the syndrome of ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and areflexia in 1956, as a variant of acute idiopathic polyneuritis [[Fisher1956]].
- Bickerstaff and Cloake wrote on mesencephalitis and rhombencephalitis in 1951, and Bickerstaff followed in 1957 with "a grave syndrome with benign prognosis" [[Bickerstaff1951,Bickerstaff1957]]. Odaka's 2003 series of 62 cases found anti-GQ1b IgG in 66% and flaccid tetraparesis in 60% [[Odaka2003]].
- In 1976, the US swine influenza vaccination programme was suspended on 16 December after reports of GBS. Surveillance found 1,098 cases, 532 recently vaccinated [[Schonberger1979]].
- The attributable risk was just under 1 case per 100,000 vaccinations in adults, concentrated within 5 weeks of vaccination [[Schonberger1979]]. Later influenza vaccine studies suggest about 1 additional case per million vaccinations [[Leonhard2019]].
- The reason for the 1976 signal is unresolved. Vaccines of that era and later ones induced anti-GM1 antibodies in mice, and *C. jejuni* was not detected in the 1976 vaccines (Early evidence) [[Nachamkin2008,Sejvar2011Flu]].
- NINCDS diagnostic criteria were published in 1978, prompted by the need for firm criteria after the vaccine episode [[NINCDS1978,Afifi1994]]. They were reaffirmed, with expanded electrodiagnostic criteria, in 1990 [[Asbury1990]].
- Also in 1978, a randomised trial of prednisolone (n=40) concluded that steroid treatment "is not beneficial and can be detrimental" [[Hughes1978]].
- Plasma exchange was first reported in acute polyneuropathy from Hammersmith Hospital in 1978 [[Brettle1978,Afifi1994]]. The US Study Group trial (n=245) confirmed benefit in 1985 [[GBSStudyGroup1985]], and the French Cooperative trial (n=220) confirmed it in 1987 and favoured albumin over fresh frozen plasma as replacement fluid [[FrenchPE1987]].
- Estelle Benson and her husband Robert founded what is now GBS|CIDP Foundation International in 1980, after his 1979 diagnosis of GBS (unverified: organisation and press sources only) [[GBSFoundationHistory,AANEM2020Benson]].
- Feasby and colleagues reported an acute axonal form in 1986: five quadriplegic people with inexcitable motor nerves, and one autopsy showing axonal degeneration without inflammation or demyelination [[Feasby1986]].
- Yuki and colleagues in 1990 reported two people with axonal GBS, high-titre IgG anti-GM1 antibodies and prior *C. jejuni* enteritis [[Yuki1990]].
- McKhann and colleagues studied the northern China summer epidemics (36 patients, median age 7) in 1991 [[McKhann1991]], then named AMAN in 1993 as a distinct syndrome [[McKhann1993]]. Autopsies published in 1995 gave it a structural basis [[Griffin1995]].
- In 129 Chinese patients in 1991-92, 65% had AMAN and 24% acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (AIDP). 66% of 38 tested had recent *C. jejuni* evidence, against 16% of village controls [[Ho1995]].
- The Dutch trial in 1992 (n=150) showed improvement by at least one grade at 4 weeks in 53% with IVIg vs 34% with plasma exchange, with a median time to improvement of 27 vs 41 days [[vanDerMeche1992]].
- Chiba and colleagues linked anti-GQ1b IgG to Miller Fisher syndrome in 1992, finding it in all six people tested and in no controls [[Chiba1992]].
- Yuki's group showed in 1993 that the lipopolysaccharide of a *C. jejuni* strain from a person with GBS carried a GM1-like structure, the first demonstration of this mimicry [[Yuki1993]]. In 2004 the group induced the disease in rabbits with that material [[Yuki2004]].
- A large trial in 1997 (n=383) found plasma exchange and IVIg equivalent, with no significant advantage from combining them [[PSGBS1997]]. An electrophysiological analysis of that trial, published in 1998, classified 69% as demyelinating and 3% as axonal [[Hadden1998]].
- A 1995 English and Welsh case-control study found recent *C. jejuni* in 26% of 103 people, against 2% of household and 1% of hospital controls, and linked it to slower recovery [[Rees1995]].
- IGOS began in May 2012 [[Jacobs2017]], and its first regional analysis appeared in 2018 [[Doets2018]].
- The Zika epidemics of 2013 and 2015 prompted a globally applicable guideline for clinicians in 2019, developed through the ZikaPLAN network [[Leonhard2019]]. EAN and PNS published an evidence-graded guideline in 2023 [[vanDoorn2023]].
- A century on, complement inhibition became the first targeted immunotherapy to enter trials [[Goodfellow2016]].

### what · Lay FAQ facts for the home page
- How common is it? About 1 to 2 people per 100,000 each year in most studied populations, and about 1 in 1,000 over a lifetime [[Sejvar2011,Leonhard2019]].
- Is it catching? GBS is an immune reaction, usually after an infection. The reaction itself is not described as spreading between people, but the infection before it can. This wording is an inference from the mechanism and outbreak reports, since these sources do not address the question directly [[Leonhard2019,Papri2021]].
- Is it inherited? GBS is not described as an inherited disease in these sources. A few families have more than one person affected, which suggests genes may play a part [[Geleijns2004]].
- Can it come back? Rarely: about 2-5% of people have another episode [[Leonhard2019]].
- How fast does it get worse? Most people reach their weakest point within 2 to 4 weeks [[Fokke2014,Doets2018]].
- How long does recovery take? It varies. In Europe and the Americas, the median time to walking independently was about 2 months (63 days), and most recovery happens in the first year [[Doets2018,Leonhard2019]].
- Will I walk again? About 8 in 10 people walk unaided by 6 months. That is a group figure and does not predict any one person's course [[Leonhard2019,Doets2018]].
- Do people die from it? Sometimes: 3-10% even with the best care, with higher figures where ventilators and treatment are scarce [[Leonhard2019,Papri2021,Ishaque2017]].
- Do people need a breathing machine? About 1 in 5 do [[Leonhard2019,Doets2018]].
- Does it leave lasting problems? It can. Tiredness, pain and some weakness are common for years, even after a good recovery [[Leonhard2019,Kuitwaard2009JPNS]].

### Exam hooks
- GBS is an acute, usually monophasic, immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy, and the commonest cause of acute flaccid paralysis worldwide [[vanDoorn2023,Shahrizaila2021]].
- Western incidence is about 1.11 per 100,000 person-years (range 0.81-1.89), rising 20% per decade of age [[Sejvar2011]].
- Male to female rate ratio is about 1.78 in the Western meta-analysis [[Sejvar2011]], and about 1.5:1 across most reports [[Papri2021]].
- About 20% need ventilation, about 20% cannot walk unaided at 6 months, and 3-10% die [[Leonhard2019,vanDoorn2008]].
- Up to two-thirds of deaths occur in the recovery phase, which is why monitoring continues after improvement starts [[Leonhard2019,vanDenBerg2013]].
- The strongest risk factor for death in Dhaka was no ventilator when needed (HR 11.9) [[Ishaque2017]].
- In IGOS, the Bangladesh axonal share was 36%, against 6% in Europe/Americas and other Asian countries [[Doets2018]].
- Winter excess is 14% overall, but the pattern reverses in the Indian subcontinent (0.86) and Latin America (0.75) [[Webb2015]].
- Summer epidemics in northern China are the classic AMAN setting [[McKhann1993,Ho1995]].
- About 2 reported GBS cases per 10,000 Zika infections [[MierYTeranRomero2018]].
- The 1976 swine influenza vaccine carried about 1 extra case per 100,000 vaccinations; later influenza vaccines about 1 per million [[Schonberger1979,Leonhard2019]].
- Albuminocytological dissociation dates from the 1916 report, in two soldiers [[Goodfellow2016,Waclawik2018]].
- CSF protein is raised in only 49% on the first day, rising to 88% after 2 weeks, so an early normal result does not exclude GBS [[Fokke2014]].
- Nadir after 4 weeks, or progression beyond 8 weeks, suggests another diagnosis or acute-onset CIDP [[Leonhard2019,vanDoorn2023]].
- Recurrence is 2-5% and lifetime risk 0.1% [[Leonhard2019]].
- US in-hospital mortality was 2.58% and Dutch 12-month mortality 3.9%. In-hospital figures can miss later deaths, since 67% of Dutch deaths came in the recovery phase [[Alshekhlee2008,vanDenBerg2013]].

### In plain words
- GBS happens when the immune system attacks the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord, usually after an infection.
- It often starts as weakness or tingling in the legs that moves upward over days.
- Most people reach their weakest point within 2 weeks, and nearly everyone within 4 weeks.
- After that comes a flat stretch, and then a slow recovery that can take months.
- It is rare: about 1 or 2 people in every 100,000 each year.
- Men get it somewhat more often than women, and the risk rises with age, but children can get it too.
- About 1 person in 5 needs a machine to help them breathe for a time.
- Most people walk on their own again. In large studies, about 8 in 10 were walking without help by 6 months.
- Numbers like these describe large groups. They cannot tell you what will happen to you or your family member.
- Where a person lives changes the outlook, mostly because treatment and intensive care cost money that many hospitals and families do not have.
- Getting GBS twice is uncommon. About 2 to 5 people in 100 have another episode.
- Tiredness and pain can last a long time, even when strength comes back. This is real, and it is worth telling your care team.
- GBS is named after French doctors who described it in 1916, though Landry wrote about a similar paralysis in 1859.
- Outbreaks happen when many people catch the same infection at once, as with Zika in the Pacific and food- or water-borne germs in Peru and India.

## Open questions and controversies
- Is incidence rising? McGrogan found a decrease between the 1980s and 1990s [[McGrogan2009]]; Papri calls Europe and North America stable across 1980-2000 [[Papri2021]]; Xu found a significant rise in Europe, Australia and America from 1960 to 2020 [[Xu2024]]; GBD models a 6.4% rise in prevalence since 1990 [[Bragazzi2021]]. Case definitions and data sources differ, so this is unsettled.
- How steep is the age slope? Sejvar's 20% per decade [[Sejvar2011]] against 50% per decade in the US Vaccine Safety Datalink [[Shui2012]]. The second relies on diagnosis codes with a 55% positive predictive value.
- GBD and incidence: GBD reports about 150,000 "total cases" as modelled prevalence for a largely acute disease [[Bragazzi2021]], and Willison gives about 100,000 new cases a year [[Willison2016]]. These are not the same measure and should not be shown side by side as if they were. GBD also counts disability only, not deaths.
- Why is Bangladesh's outcome worse? Young age, axonal disease, severe weakness at nadir, 86% without immunotherapy and scarce ventilators all travel together [[Doets2018,Ishaque2017]]. Observational data cannot separate their contributions.
- Bangladesh mortality figures differ: 12% in hospital series [[Ishaque2017]], 17% in IGOS at 1 year [[Doets2018]], and 14-17% in a review [[Papri2021]]. Setting and follow-up time explain at least part of this.
- Is time to nadir mostly 2 weeks? 80% in the Dutch cohort [[Fokke2014]], 96% in IGOS [[Doets2018]]. IGOS required enrolment within 2 weeks of onset, and the Dutch cohort drew partly on trials, so selection likely contributes.
- Zika phenotype: French Polynesia was reported as AMAN-compatible [[CaoLormeau2016]], Colombia as mostly demyelinating [[Parra2016]]. Timing and criteria for classification may matter (see pass C).
- Peru 2019 trigger: Ramos found *C. jejuni* evidence in 65% and calls the outbreak associated with it [[Ramos2021]]. Munayco's abstract says several findings "call into question" the link, while its discussion says results support it but note few reported diarrhoeal illnesses and wide geographic spread [[Munayco2020]]. Peru's baseline is also given differently: 1.2 per 100,000 per year in Munayco [[Munayco2020]] against 0.62-0.92 from discharge data [[Munayco2019]].
- COVID-19 and GBS: UK incidence fell and showed no link to COVID-19 [[Keddie2021]]; IGOS saw no recruitment rise but says GBS "may rarely" follow infection [[Luijten2021]]; a case-control study was called for. Detailed evidence is in pass B.
- Was the 1976 signal unique? The mechanism is unresolved, and mouse anti-GM1 induction was not unique to 1976 vaccines [[Nachamkin2008]].
- Who first described GBS? Landry is credited with the first report and Guillain, Barré and Strohl with the CSF finding. Strohl's name dropped out of use, for reasons that are disputed [[Afifi1994,Waclawik2018]].

- All 76 bibliographic sources were confirmed against PubMed `esummary` on 2026-11-09. Authors, title, journal, year and identifiers come from the record. Abstracts were read through `efetch`.
- Full text obtained from Europe PMC XML and read or searched for relevant passages: Leonhard 2019, Papri 2021, Afifi 1994, Munayco 2020, Bragazzi 2021 and Xu 2024.
- Table numbers from Doets 2018 (regional ventilation, age, treatment, time to walking, 6-month walking, nadir timing, overall deaths), Sejvar 2011 (rate ratio, crude range, age table) and Keddie 2021 (monthly counts) came from the publisher or PMC pages through a page-reading tool rather than my direct reading of the PDF, so builders should spot-check them against the tables if a figure is headline content. Abstract-level Doets numbers were read directly.
- The Doets 2018 figure of 7% (44/659) overall deaths does not sum exactly with the regional counts (43), which use different denominators; both are reported as given.
- Bragazzi 2021's abstract lists high-income Asia Pacific prevalence as 1.9 (1.5-2.4), identical to the global estimate, which looks like a possible reporting slip, so it is not used. Japan and China figures are used instead.
- Records without an abstract, cited for their existence and title with content taken from later descriptions: Haymaker 1949, Asbury 1969, Fisher 1956, Bickerstaff 1951 and 1957, Brettle 1978, NINCDS 1978, Guillain 1916 (1999 reprint, French text not read).
- Landry 1859 is not in PubMed and is cited only through Afifi 1994 and Freitas 2017. Afifi 1994 is a narrative historical review; its statement that Strohl's name was dropped for disputed reasons is secondary.
- Unverified: the 1980 foundation date and the name Robert Benson rest on one web page (PatientWorthy), and the 1979 diagnosis on one press release (Newswise).
