Part 2 · Diagnosis

Look-alikes

Conditions that can be mistaken for Guillain-Barré syndrome, sorted by where along the motor pathway they act, with the clues that tell them apart. Walk down the pathway, compare GBS with a mimic, and see how fluctuating GBS differs from acute-onset CIDP.

Mimics fall into two broad groups: those with symmetrical limb weakness and those with brainstem signs. Miller Fisher syndrome and pharyngeal-cervical-brachial GBS are often mistaken for brainstem stroke, botulism or myasthenia, and Bickerstaff encephalitis for Wernicke's encephalopathy 492. Telling direct viral causes of acute flaccid paralysis apart from GBS matters for public health and to avoid inappropriate treatment 425.

Stop 1 · roots and nerves

Roots and nerves: GBS and other neuropathies

GBS is an acute polyradiculoneuropathy, a disease of the nerve roots and peripheral nerves 473. Other neuropathies can look similar: about 5% first diagnosed with GBS turn out to have acute-onset CIDP 473; vasculitic neuropathy is asymmetric or multifocal and painful 81; acute porphyria brings abdominal pain, psychological features and proximal asymmetric weakness 7 and is confirmed by a markedly raised urinary porphobilinogen 18.

In plain wordsGBS damages the nerves that run from the spinal cord to the muscles. A few other nerve diseases can look like it at first.

Fluctuation or acute-onset CIDP?

Some people get worse again after first improving with treatment, a treatment-related fluctuation. In 170 people followed prospectively, 16 (9%) had one and 8 (5%) were later diagnosed with acute-onset chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (A-CIDP) 391. About 5% of people first diagnosed with GBS turn out to have A-CIDP 266,473. Choose a course and scrub through the weeks.

When fluctuations happen

The first fluctuation in GBS always came within 8 weeks (median 18 days, range 10–54); 5 people (31%) had a second and none had more 391. An earlier study proposed 9 weeks with the same 3-event rule 389.

What else points to A-CIDP

Marked sensory abnormality including sensory ataxia; no facial, bulbar or respiratory weakness; possibly more than 2 weeks from onset to nadir; widespread nerve enlargement on ultrasound; or an early large fall in motor conduction velocity 473. A-CIDP cannot be confirmed unless worsening continues at least 8 weeks after onset 473.

Do not over-diagnose

Severely weak people who improve slowly or not at all should not be labelled A-CIDP too readily; muscle wasting and denervation point to axonal GBS instead 473. In a chart review, A-CIDP more often had prominent sensory signs and less often autonomic involvement, facial weakness, preceding infection or ventilation 100.

What the guideline says

As published, in a fluctuation the guideline suggests considering re-treatment with IVIg or plasma exchange (observational evidence), and A-CIDP should be treated as CIDP 467,473. This is a report of guidance, not advice. More in Steroids, repeats & relapses.

How often the first diagnosis changes

No large study measuring GBS misdiagnosis directly was found. The figures below are indirect, come from different settings and measure different things, so they should not be merged.

Tertiary database

8.5% relabelled

12 of 141 people labelled GBS received another diagnosis during follow-up (percentage derived here) 447.

IGOS

6% excluded

62 of the first 1,000 were excluded for another diagnosis: A-CIDP 37, other peripheral neuropathy 8, central nervous system disorder 12, functional disorder 2, unspecified 3 102.

Hospital coding

Codes are unreliable

In Italian discharge coding for 1996, GBS was not confirmed in 103 of 228; the positive predictive value was 54.8% overall and 76.4% in neurology departments 49. In Ottawa, 152 of 392 coded cases were excluded, bundling coding errors with diagnostic error 53.

Missed GBS

Delays happen too

In 119 consecutive UK patients, 27 (22.7%) had a delay of at least 5 days from first presentation, linked to at least one earlier visit (OR 10.15) and pre-existing heart or lung disease (OR 3.98) 52.

The published pathway, in ten steps

Teaching toolA synthesis of published guidance for study, quoted as published. It is not instructions, and it is never for an individual's diagnosis or treatment.

  1. Suspect. Rapidly progressive bilateral leg or arm weakness without central nervous system involvement or another obvious cause, plus reduced reflexes, pain or sensory symptoms; check for atypical and paediatric forms 266.
  2. History. Time course, illness in the previous 6 weeks, drugs and toxins, tick or outbreak exposure; search the whole body for a tick 110,266,473.
  3. Red flags. Sensory level, sphincter problems at onset, fever, altered consciousness; if a cord cause is possible, whole-spine MRI 266,473.
  4. Blood tests. Complete blood count, glucose, electrolytes, kidney function and liver enzymes for everyone suspected; more tests guided by the differential 266.
  5. Lumbar puncture. At initial evaluation, mainly to exclude other causes; a normal protein in week 1 does not exclude GBS, and over 50 cells/µl prompts a search 266,473.
  6. Electrodiagnosis. Not required but recommended where possible, especially in atypical cases; normal first-week studies do not exclude GBS 266,473.
  7. Targeted tests. Anti-GQ1b in suspected Miller Fisher syndrome; MRI or ultrasound in atypical cases; treatment does not wait for antibody results 266,473.
  8. Safety alongside diagnosis. Breathing, swallowing and autonomic risk are assessed throughout; respiratory failure can occur without breathlessness 266.
  9. Treatment threshold. Published guidance is to start immunotherapy if a person cannot walk 10 m independently; trials support IVIg within 2 weeks of weakness onset and plasma exchange within 4 weeks 266.
  10. Reclassify. Review the diagnosis if recovery does not follow the expected course; consider A-CIDP at 3 or more fluctuations or worsening beyond 8 weeks, and nodal-paranodal antibodies when response is poor 391,473.

In plain wordsOther illnesses can look like GBS, such as spinal cord problems, botulism, a hidden tick or low potassium. Doctors look for clues that point elsewhere. Some people with GBS get a little worse again after treatment; that can happen in GBS. If someone keeps getting worse after about 8 weeks, or worse again three or more times, doctors may look at a long-term condition called CIDP.
