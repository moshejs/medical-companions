Part 3 · Acute care

Breathing & the 20/30/40 rule

GBS can weaken the muscles that move air in and out. Watch vital capacity fall before anyone feels short of breath, then try the bedside thresholds and the published risk scores used to judge who may need a ventilator.

EmergencyWeakness that worsens fast, or any trouble breathing or swallowing, needs emergency care. GBS can bring on breathing failure within hours to days, and missing it can cause death or brain injury from lack of oxygen 473.

About 1 in 5 people with GBS needs mechanical ventilation (MV) at some point, and up to 22% need it in their first week in hospital, according to the international "ten steps" guideline 266. Breathing failure can arrive without the person feeling breathless, so teams measure breathing strength routinely instead of waiting for symptoms 266. Pick a step or press Play.

Admission

Measure, do not wait

Weakness is climbing. Vital capacity (VC), the most air that can be blown out after a full breath, is still near this person's starting value. The EAN/PNS guideline's good practice point is to check forced vital capacity three to six times a day depending on severity, and about every 4 hours while it is falling 473.

In plain wordsYou may be asked to blow hard into a tube, or to count aloud on one breath, many times a day. It feels repetitive. It is how a team spots a change early 266,473.

Why breathing fails

The cause is progressive weakness of both the muscles that draw air in (the diaphragm and others) and the muscles that push it out. Weak throat muscles and a poor cough then let food or saliva go the wrong way, and parts of the lung collapse (atelectasis) 339.

Classical signs of respiratory distress appear too late to guide care. Vital capacity (VC) and the static mouth pressures are the useful measures 339. In 81 French people who were later intubated, mean VC was 57% of predicted at admission and 33% at intubation 340.

Swallowing. In 30 consecutive adults in intensive care, piecemeal swallowing and a poorly timed breathing-swallowing pattern each showed up in 19 (65.5%), yet only about half of them had an obvious swallowing problem at the bedside. The authors report swallowing impairment in over 80% early after ICU admission and suggest a systematic swallow assessment 337.

In IGOS, forced vital capacity (FVC) was lower in people with facial or bulbar weakness (median 2.4 L vs 3 L), and low FVC stayed linked to MV after adjustment 279. One case report found blood bypassing the lungs' air spaces in severe autonomic failure, a reminder that not every fall in oxygen is muscle weakness Early evidence 439.

The bedside numbers: 20/30/40

In 114 consecutive people with severe GBS in one tertiary intensive care unit (1976 to 1996), no single clinical feature predicted how breathing would decline, but serial lung function tests did. The authors' rule: risk of respiratory failure when vital capacity is under 20 mL/kg, maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) under 30 cmH2O, maximal expiratory pressure (MEP) under 40 cmH2O, or any of the three falls by more than 30% 263. Blood gases appear in the list of signs of imminent failure, not as an early screen; VC and the static pressures are the measures advised for timing 266,339. The guideline also lists reasons for admission to intensive care: evolving respiratory distress, severe autonomic cardiovascular problems, severe swallowing difficulty or a weak cough, and rapidly progressing weakness 266.

Who is at risk: early predictors

A 2018 meta-analysis of 34 studies pooled the odds of needing ventilation for single bedside features. The odds ratios are large, but the intervals are wide, and the authors found that combined models (area under the curve 0.79 to 0.96) looked more reliable than any single sign 165.

In this model, more than 85% of people with at least four of the six features needed MV. In a subgroup with VC measured (n=196), onset under 7 days before admission (OR 5.00), inability to lift the head (OR 5.00) and VC under 60% of predicted (OR 2.86) remained, and 85% with all three were ventilated 416.

The EAN/PNS guideline rates bulbar involvement, a short time from onset to admission and a low MRC sum score as high-certainty predictors; neck weakness, greater disability, low VC and low albumin as moderate-certainty 473. It also lists laboratory hints: raised liver tests, HSV or CMV infection, or demyelinating nerve conduction features 473.

In a prospective cohort of 154, the chance of ventilation was under 2.5% when the peroneal proximal-to-distal CMAP ratio exceeded 55.6% and VC exceeded 81% of predicted; demyelinating GBS was commoner in those later ventilated (85% vs 51%) 108.

Early evidence

Cortisol

In 93 people, baseline plasma cortisol was the only independent predictor of breathing failure beyond 24 hours after adjusting for validated clinical predictors (OR 2.45 per 10 ng/mL). A research marker, not a routine test 432.

Early evidence

Uncertainty and fear

In 110 adults, uncertainty about the course of the illness was independently linked to later MV (23 ventilated). Subjective distress may signal deterioration 417.

EGRIS: a published risk score

The Erasmus GBS Respiratory Insufficiency Score (EGRIS) adds three items recorded at admission: days from onset of weakness to admission, facial and/or bulbar weakness, and the MRC sum score, giving 0 to 7 104,266. It was derived in 397 prospective Dutch patients (22% ventilated in the first week, area under the curve 0.84) and validated in 191 (14%, 0.82) 496. The ten-steps guideline groups the scores into three bands 266.

mEGRIS: three muscle groups instead of twelve

The modified EGRIS (mEGRIS), built in IGOS, swaps the full MRC sum score for neck flexion and both hip flexors, and counts days to admission one by one. It scores 0 to 32, had an area under the curve of 0.84 (0.80 to 0.88), and predicts at several time points 279. The EAN/PNS guideline advises using a prognostic model at admission, names mEGRIS, and advises re-assessing risk factors regularly during the stay 473.

Open questions

When to intubate

Early pneumonia was commoner when intubation came more than 2 days after admission 340. A deliberately conservative blood-gas policy in one Indian cohort did not raise mortality or disability 215. Both studies are observational.

Non-invasive ventilation

A 2025 intensive care review considers mask ventilation inappropriate as a treatment in GBS, because of bulbar weakness and slow improvement, and keeps it for pre-oxygenation 504. A 2003 review of neurological patients in general saw an important and possibly growing role 365.

MIP and MEP numbers

Lawn's 30 and 40 cmH2O versus the EAN/PNS text's apparent swap of the two 263,473. Until corrected, the original series is the reference.

In plain wordsGBS can weaken the muscles you use to breathe, so about 1 in 5 people need a breathing machine for a while 266. Your team may ask you to blow into a device or count aloud on one breath, often many times a day. This finds a problem before you feel short of breath 266,473. A planned move to intensive care is a safety step, not a sign that all hope is gone 473.
