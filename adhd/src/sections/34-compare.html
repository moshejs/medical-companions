Effect-size translator

Formula-based, not trial data. Assumes two normal distributions with equal spread. Overlap = 2Φ(−d/2); probability of superiority = Φ(d/√2); approximate NNT = 1 / (2Φ(d/√2) − 1). Presets: [[Cortese2018,Wolraich2019,Daley2014]]

Context: non-drug treatments and blinding

Non-drug trials usually cannot hide which treatment a family received. Effects rated by the people closest to treatment (often parents who delivered it) are "unblinded"; those from raters unlikely to know the arm are "probably blinded". Toggle to see what survives blinding. Magnitudes are shown as positive numbers for improvement; scales and populations differ, so read them as a rough guide. [[SonugaBarke2013,Daley2014]]

Dashed lines: AAP effect sizes of about 1.0 for stimulants and 0.7 for non-stimulants. [[Wolraich2019,Daley2014,SonugaBarke2013,Cortese2016,Westwood2025,Cortese2015]]

Reading the evidence

Caveat

Most trials last weeks

Cortese 2018 found "insufficient data" at 26 and 52 weeks, so its ranking covers short-term treatment only. In the MTA follow-up to adulthood, extended medication use was "not associated with reduction of symptom severity". On the other side, youths switched to placebo after more than 2 years of methylphenidate worsened more than those who continued (clinician-rated worsening 40.4% vs 15.9%). Status: open; periodic review is advised. [[Cortese2018,Swanson2017MTA,Matthijssen2019]]

Controversy

How certain is the methylphenidate evidence?

Position A: guideline groups and the World Federation treat methylphenidate as effective, and critics of the Cochrane review reported "errors in the extraction of data". Position B: the Cochrane team rates all outcomes very low certainty: 191 of 212 trials were at high risk of bias, side effects can unblind trials, and treatment lasted a mean 28.8 days; the 2025 update keeps those conclusions. Both agree on a teacher-rated SMD near −0.74 to −0.82. [[Faraone2021,Banaschewski2016,Storebo2015BMJ,Storebo2023Cochrane,Storebo2025Cochrane]]

Response

If one stimulant fails, the other often works

The AAP cites MTA titration data suggesting "more than 70%" of children are methylphenidate responders, and reports that more than 90% respond to one of the stimulants if both classes are tried, with "approximately 40% responding to both" and "about 40% responding to only 1". [[Wolraich2019]]

Dose

More is not always better

In a 2026 dose-effect network meta-analysis, efficacy in children rose up to about 45 mg/day of methylphenidate, 25 mg/day of amphetamines and 4 mg/day of guanfacine, "with no evidence of additional benefit at higher doses". In adults, doses above the licensed range added little for methylphenidate (SMD −0.23) while doubling adverse-event dropouts, and nothing for amphetamines. [[Nourredine2026,Farhat2024JAMAPsych]]

Adults

Self-rated vs clinician-rated

In 113 adult RCTs, stimulants (SMD −0.39 self-rated, −0.61 clinician-rated) and atomoxetine (−0.38, −0.51) were the only interventions that reduced core symptoms on both kinds of scale; CBT, mindfulness and psychoeducation improved clinician ratings only. Medicines did not improve quality of life. An umbrella review rated adult CBT (SMD 0.53) similar to methylphenidate (0.50) on clinician ratings. [[Ostinelli2025,Gosling2025]]

Combined care

The MTA trial

In 579 children aged 7–9.9 over 14 months, carefully managed medication beat intensive behavioural treatment and community care for ADHD symptoms; combined treatment did not differ significantly from medication alone on core symptoms. See The MTA study. [[MTA1999]]

In plain words. ADHD medicines do not cure ADHD; they lower symptoms while they are in the body. Stimulants help most people, and if one type does not help, the other type often does. [[Faraone2021,Matthijssen2019,Wolraich2019]]
