Part 4 · Behavioural & psychological care

Parent training & classroom support

Behavioural parent training teaches practical tools for what happens before and after a child's behaviour. It is not a verdict on anyone's parenting. Step through one morning routine, then switch on the escalation loop to see what the tools are meant to replace.

What the guidelines say, by age

AAP 2019 · ages 4 to 5

Behavioural treatment first

For preschool children, the clinician "should prescribe evidence-based PTBM and/or behavioral classroom interventions as the first line of treatment, if available" (grade A for parent training). Methylphenidate "may be considered" if these do not bring enough improvement and moderate-to-severe disturbance continues. Before age 4, parent training "does not require a diagnosis to be applied". [[Wolraich2019]]

AAP 2019 · ages 6 to 11

Medication plus behavioural support, "preferably both"

The clinician should prescribe FDA-approved ADHD medication along with parent training and/or behavioural classroom intervention, "preferably both". Educational supports are "a necessary part of any treatment plan". For adolescents, medication is prescribed "with the adolescent's assent", and behavioural or training interventions are encouraged if available. [[Wolraich2019]]

NICE NG87 · under 5

Group parent training is first-line

NICE recommends offering an ADHD-focused group parent-training programme to parents or carers of children under 5 as first-line treatment. ADHD medication should not be offered to any child under 5 without a second specialist opinion from an ADHD service with expertise in young children. [[NICE2018F]]

NICE NG87 · 5 and over

Parent training when there is defiance, and individual formats

For children aged 5 and over with ADHD and oppositional or conduct symptoms, NICE recommends offering parents a parent-training programme as well as group ADHD-focused support. Individual parent training is considered when a family finds group sessions hard to attend. Medication is offered only if impairment persists after environmental changes have been made and reviewed. [[NICE2018F]]

Language

"Does not imply bad parenting"

NICE tells clinicians to explain that recommending parent training "does not imply bad parenting": its aim is to meet "the above-average parenting needs" of children with ADHD. When the parent also has ADHD, NICE asks clinicians to think about extra help with organisation, adherence and school routines. [[NICE2018F]]

Preschool evidence

The strongest data are in young children

In a US review of preschoolers at high risk for ADHD, 8 good-quality parent-training studies (424 children) pooled to SMD −0.68 for child behaviour, with high strength of evidence; only one good study tested methylphenidate. A meta-analysis of 16 preschool studies found parent-rated effects of g 0.51 on ADHD symptoms, but on independent assessment only negative parenting improved. In a community RCT of 78 three-year-olds, 53% of the parent-training group improved to a clinically significant degree. [[Charach2013,Rimestad2019,SonugaBarke2001]]

Blinded or not: why the same treatment gets different numbers

Parents who deliver the training also rate the results. The European ADHD Guidelines Group therefore split outcomes into ratings by the people closest to treatment and "probably blinded" ratings by people unlikely to know who had been treated. On blinded ratings the gains in parenting and conduct held up, but the effect on core ADHD symptoms did not. [[Daley2014,SonugaBarke2013]]

Controversy

Front-line for core symptoms?

On blinded evidence, behavioural intervention "cannot be supported as a front-line treatment for core ADHD symptoms", yet it is an important part of multimodal care because it improves parenting and reduces oppositional behaviour. A meta-analysis that counted all designs found effect sizes of 0.83 (between-group) to 3.78 (single-subject) and called behavioural treatment "highly effective". [[Daley2018,Fabiano2009]]

Established

Classed "well-established"

Under Society of Clinical Child and Adolescent Psychology criteria, behavioural parent training, behavioural classroom management, behavioural peer interventions and organisation training are "well-established". The 2018 update found children of more educated parents over-represented in trials. [[Evans2014,Evans2018]]

Early evidence

Which parts do the work?

Across 29 RCTs, techniques that change the setting before behaviour went with better parent competence and mental health, and more reinforcement of wanted behaviour went with bigger drops in negative parenting. More psychoeducation went with worse parent outcomes. In pooled individual data from 19 RCTs, gains in parenting and warmth tracked gains in the child, in both directions. [[Dekkers2022,Psyllou2025]]

Umbrella review 2025

Low certainty across the board

Across 221 re-estimated meta-analyses, no non-drug intervention for children reached moderate or high certainty for ADHD symptoms, and no intervention of any kind had high-certainty long-term evidence. Guidelines still recommend parent training for mild-to-moderate ADHD and oppositional behaviour. For ages 5 to 18, a 2011 Cochrane review found only 5 trials, with high or unclear risk of bias. [[Gosling2025,Zwi2011]]

School plans, explained neutrally

United States

504 plans and IEPs

The AAP notes that ADHD qualifies as a disability under a Section 504 plan, but qualifies for an Individualized Education Program (under "other health impairment") only when its severity impairs the child's ability to learn. In 97 US middle-school students with ADHD, 61.9% had an IEP and 38.1% a 504 plan; the services listed often had "little to no research supporting their effectiveness", and services with evidence were rarely included. Fewer than half of IEPs had measurable goals for the behaviour problems they described. [[Wolraich2019,Spiel2014]]

United Kingdom

Contact with school, and reasonable adjustments

NICE asks clinicians, with consent, to contact the school, college or university at diagnosis, when symptoms change and at each transition, covering the treatment plan, special educational needs and "reasonable adjustments and environmental modifications". NICE advises against universal screening for ADHD in schools. When a child with conduct problems and suspected ADHD is referred to the school's special educational needs coordinator (SENCO), parents should be told about local parent-training programmes. [[NICE2018F]]

Teenagers, social skills and the research-to-practice gap

Early evidence

STAND: works in trials, harder in clinics

Parent-teen skills therapy with motivational interviewing improved ADHD symptoms, organisation and parenting stress in a university RCT of 128 adolescents, with 85% of families completing it. Delivered by community therapists to 278 adolescents, it did not beat usual care overall; it did when therapists were licensed (22% of the sample), and it raised parent participation and medication engagement. [[Sibley2016STAND,Sibley2021STAND]]

Early evidence

CBT for medicated teenagers

In 46 medication-treated adolescents aged 14 to 18 with persistent symptoms, CBT lowered the blind-evaluator-rated parent-report score by a mean 10.93 points; responders were 50% versus 18% by parent report. See CBT, coaching & adult support. [[Sprich2016]]

No clinically relevant effect

Social skills training

A Cochrane review of 25 RCTs (2,690 children) found SMD 0.11 on teacher-rated social skills, about 1.22 points on a 0–102 scale where the smallest clinically relevant difference is 10. The AAP says social skills training has not been shown to be effective for children with ADHD. [[Storebo2019,Wolraich2019]]

Early evidence

Joining up home and school

For the inattentive presentation, a programme combining parent, teacher and child components beat both parent-focused treatment and usual care on teacher-reported inattention, organisation and social skills in 199 children aged 7 to 11. A 12-week school-home version delivered by school mental-health staff in 23 schools beat usual services on ADHD severity and organisation. [[Pfiffner2014,Pfiffner2016]]

Formats

Group, individual, generic or ADHD-specific

All formats have reported benefits, and experts advise respecting parents' preference. They found no reason to limit behavioural treatment to families with parenting difficulties or children with conduct problems; treating a parent's depression may increase the effect. Preschool programmes are typically group-based and may be relatively low cost, though not always covered by insurance. [[Daley2018,Wolraich2019]]

How parent training sits beside medication over years is the subject of The MTA study & long-term treatment, including a trial that compared starting with behavioural treatment against starting with medication.
