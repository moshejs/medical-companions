Genes & heritability

ADHD runs strongly in families, mostly through genes. But there is no single "ADHD gene": thousands of common variants with tiny effects add up, and a few rare variants with larger effects sit on top. Scrub through one family, twin studies, genome-wide studies and rare variants.

What the genetics does and does not mean

Established

Heritable does not mean fixed or untreatable

Heritability describes differences between people in a population. It does not say that 74% of one person's ADHD is "caused by genes", and it does not mean the environment cannot matter: gene–environment interplay is folded into the twin estimate. Calling a cause either "genetic" or "environmental" is, in Thapar's words, "unhelpful and incorrect". Treatments work whatever the cause: see How well treatments work. [[Faraone2021,Thapar2013]]

Established

No genetic test can diagnose ADHD

Known genetic risks are individually small or rare and raise risk for many conditions, so they "cannot be used for prediction, genetic testing or diagnostic purposes" beyond what a family history already tells you. The World Federation of ADHD consensus statement says genetic and brain findings help explain causes but are "not useful for diagnosing the disorder". Diagnosis comes from a careful history and assessment: see DSM-5-TR criteria. [[Thapar2013,Faraone2021]]

Shared genetics

Genetic overlap with other conditions

Of 219 traits tested, 43 had a significant genetic correlation (rg) with ADHD: years of schooling −0.53, intelligence −0.41, ever smoked 0.48, major depression 0.42, age at first birth −0.612, childhood obesity 0.22. Twin studies put the ADHD–autism genetic correlation above 0.50. Across 8 disorders, ADHD grouped with autism, Tourette syndrome and depression in an "early-onset neurodevelopmental" factor. An estimated 84–98% of ADHD-influencing variants are shared with other psychiatric disorders. [[Demontis2019,Faraone2019,PGCCDG2019,Demontis2023]]

History

The candidate-gene era: DRD4 and DAT1

Before genome-wide studies, researchers tested genes chosen from dopamine biology. A meta-analytic review found significant associations for DAT1 (SLC6A3), DRD4, DRD5, 5HTT, HTR1B and SNAP25, with effect sizes that varied across studies. Each has an odds ratio below 1.5. The DRD4 7-repeat association held in case-control and family studies and is "small" but real. Robust genome-wide loci came only from samples of more than 20,000 cases. [[Gizer2009,Faraone2019,Faraone2001DRD4,Demontis2019]]

Girls & women

A higher family load in diagnosed girls

ADHD is diagnosed 2 to 7 times more often in males. In Swedish registers, siblings of affected females had a higher familial risk than siblings of affected males (OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.11–1.18), but common-variant polygenic scores were not higher in female cases (OR 1.02), so autosomal common variants do not explain the sex ratio. This fits a higher threshold in girls, and also under-recognition: see Girls, women & late diagnosis. Hypothesis [[Martin2018SexBias]]

Rare syndromes

When one genetic change matters a lot

High rates of ADHD occur in 22q11.2 deletion (velo-cardio-facial) syndrome, fragile X, Turner, tuberous sclerosis, neurofibromatosis, Klinefelter and Williams syndromes. In rare cases ADHD-like symptoms follow a single genetic abnormality, extreme early deprivation or early brain injury: see Environment, risk & myths. [[Faraone2019,Faraone2021]]

Open question

The "missing heritability"

Twins give 74–88%; common variants explain 14–22% and rare-variant burden about 2.5%. Explanations under study: many more rare variants (one exome study estimated 1,057 contributing genes), gene–environment interplay counted inside twin estimates, and rater effects. [[Faraone2019,Larsson2014,Demontis2026,Olfson2024,Thapar2013]]

Early evidence

Genes and life outcomes: Mendelian randomisation

Each doubling of genetic liability to ADHD went with 1.7 fewer months of education (95% CI −2.8 to −0.7), and genetic liability to more education lowered ADHD risk (OR 0.33 per SD). Genetic liability to ADHD may raise the risk of major depression (OR 1.21), and higher BMI may raise ADHD risk. [[Dardani2022,Riglin2021,MartinsSilva2019]]

In plain words. If one child has ADHD, a brother or sister is several times more likely to have it too. That is useful for families and clinicians to know. It is not a verdict: genes shape likelihood, and support, school adjustments and treatment shape how things go. [[Chen2017JCPP,Faraone2021]]
