ADHD at a glance

What it is
ADHD, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, is a neurodevelopmental condition: a lasting pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that gets in the way of daily life. [[APA2022DSM]] ADHD runs in biological families, and parenting is not the main cause. [[Larsson2014,Sprich2000,Harold2013]]
Who gets it
About 5 in every 100 children and teenagers around the world meet the criteria. [[Polanczyk2007]] Worldwide, between about 3 and 7 adults in 100 have it, depending on how it is counted. [[Song2021]] Girls with ADHD are often quiet daydreamers rather than disruptive, so their ADHD can be missed. [[Hinshaw2022,Quinn2014]]
Main signs
People with ADHD show, on average, weaknesses in skills such as stopping an action, staying alert, working memory and planning, though not every person has them. [[Willcutt2005]] In the community the mainly inattentive form is the most common. [[Willcutt2012]] Hyperactivity often eases with age, while inattention stays fairly stable. [[APA2022DSM,Epstein2013,Wolraich2019]]
How it is diagnosed
Only a licensed clinician can diagnose ADHD. Rating scales, brain tests and brain imaging cannot diagnose it alone. [[Faraone2021]] The clinician looks at current and past symptoms and their effects, plus family and developmental history. [[Posner2020]]
Main treatments
For children aged 6 to 12, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends approved medication together with parent training in behaviour management and/or behavioural classroom support, preferably both. [[Wolraich2019]] For adults, NICE recommends at least a structured supportive psychological intervention focused on ADHD, with regular follow-up. [[NICE2018F]]
Outlook
Across 20 WHO surveys, 57% of people with childhood ADHD still had it as adults. [[Sibley2017Persistence,Fayyad2017]] On average people with ADHD face more risks, mostly from accidents, but many finish school, work, drive safely and live long lives. [[Dalsgaard2015,Jangmo2021,Erskine2016]]
