# A · Epidemiology, presentation across the lifespan & history

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: home, what, signs, women, history.

This pass covers how common ADHD is (children, adults, older adults; worldwide and in the United States), why prevalence estimates differ, trends in diagnosis and prescribing, global burden, the three presentations, how symptoms change from preschool to older age, persistence and the fluctuating course, sex differences and the under-recognition of ADHD in girls and women, and the history of the concept from 1775 to DSM-5-TR and ICD-11. Every number below is a population statistic from the cited study: it describes groups, not what will happen to one person.

## Findings

### home · ADHD at a glance
- About 5 in every 100 children and adolescents worldwide meet criteria for ADHD: the pooled worldwide prevalence in 102 studies (171,756 people aged ≤18) was 5.29% [[Polanczyk2007]].
- Different meta-analyses give 3.4% (95% CI 2.6–4.5; 41 probabilistic community studies, standardised interviews) [[Polanczyk2015]] to 7.2% (95% CI 6.7–7.8; 175 studies) [[Thomas2015]], and an umbrella review of 13 meta-analyses (588 primary studies, 3,277,590 participants) gives 8.0% (95% CI 6.0–10) [[Ayano2023Children]].
- Among adults worldwide, 2.58% (95% CI 1.51–4.45) have persistent ADHD (with childhood onset) and 6.76% (95% CI 4.31–10.61) have symptomatic adult ADHD (regardless of childhood onset), about 139.84 million and 366.33 million adults in 2020 [[Song2021]].
- In the United States in 2022, 11.4% of children aged 3–17 (about 7.1 million, roughly 1 in 9) had ever received an ADHD diagnosis, and 10.5% (6.5 million) had current ADHD [[Danielson2024]].
- In the United States in October–November 2023, 6.0% of adults (about 15.5 million, 1 in 16) reported a current ADHD diagnosis [[Staley2024]].
- ADHD is diagnosed more often in males: about 2:1 in community samples of youth by parent and teacher ratings [[Faraone2021]], with a much higher ratio in clinic-referred samples [[Biederman2002Gender,Young2020Females]].
- The World Federation of ADHD consensus states that ADHD "has not become more common over the past three decades" in the community, although it is more likely to be diagnosed today because of increased recognition (Established) [[Faraone2021]].

### what · Worldwide prevalence in children and adolescents
- Polanczyk 2007 searched MEDLINE and PsycINFO from January 1978 to December 2005, reviewed 303 full texts and included 102 studies with 171,756 participants from all world regions; the pooled prevalence was 5.29% with significant variability [[Polanczyk2007]].
- Posner and colleagues report this pooled estimate as 5.29% (95% CI 5.01–5.56), drawn from community samples in 35 countries on six continents [[Posner2020]].
- The 2015 meta-analysis of all childhood mental disorders (41 studies, 27 countries) found ADHD in 3.4% (95% CI 2.6–4.5), compared with 13.4% for any mental disorder, 6.5% for any anxiety disorder, 2.6% for any depressive disorder and 5.7% for any disruptive disorder [[Polanczyk2015]].
- Thomas 2015 pooled 179 estimates from 175 studies using DSM-III, DSM-III-R or DSM-IV criteria and found 7.2% (95% CI 6.7–7.8), with no statistically significant difference between DSM editions overall [[Thomas2015]].
- In Thomas 2015's multivariable model, estimates were lower with DSM-III-R than DSM-IV (P = .03) and lower in Europe than North America (P = .04); few studies used random population sampling [[Thomas2015]].
- Willcutt 2012 (86 child and adolescent studies, N = 163,688) found DSM-IV ADHD prevalence of 5.9–7.1% whether defined by parent ratings, teacher ratings or best-estimate diagnosis [[Willcutt2012]].
- A 2023 meta-analysis found ADHD in 7.6% (95% CI 6.1–9.4) of children aged 3–12 years (53 studies, 96,907 children) and in 5.6% (95% CI 4.8–7.0) of adolescents aged 12–18; studies using DSM-5 criteria gave higher prevalence than earlier criteria [[Salari2023]].
- The 2023 umbrella review found prevalence about twice as high in boys (10%) as in girls (5%), with the inattentive type most common, followed by the hyperactive-impulsive and combined types [[Ayano2023Children]].
- In 12 African studies the pooled prevalence was 7.47%, with 10.60% in boys versus 5.28% in girls (male:female 2.01:1) [[Ayano2020Africa]].
- The World Federation consensus cites a meta-analysis of 19 studies (over 55,000 participants) in which 5.9% of youths met diagnostic criteria [[Faraone2021]].

### what · Why the estimates differ: method, not place or decade
- In Polanczyk 2007's metaregression, diagnostic criteria, source of information, the requirement of impairment and geographic origin were all associated with prevalence; geography mattered only between North America and both Africa and the Middle East, and Europe did not differ from North America (Established) [[Polanczyk2007]].
- The authors concluded that the variability "seems to be explained primarily by the methodological characteristics of studies" [[Polanczyk2007]].
- The 2014 update (154 studies identified, 135 in the multivariate model) found that diagnostic criteria, impairment criterion and information source explained heterogeneity, while geographic location and year of study did not (Established) [[Polanczyk2014]].
- Over three decades there was "no evidence to suggest an increase" in the number of children in the community meeting criteria when standardised procedures were followed [[Polanczyk2014]].
- In the 2015 all-disorders meta-analysis, sample representativeness, sample frame and diagnostic interview were significant moderators; location and year of data collection were not; the model explained 88.89% of heterogeneity, and requiring functional impairment lowered estimates [[Polanczyk2015]].
- Willcutt 2012 found no significant differences in prevalence between countries or regions after controlling for the diagnostic algorithm, arguing against the idea that ADHD is a cultural construct restricted to the United States [[Willcutt2012]].
- The consensus statement cites a 135-study meta-analysis (about a quarter of a million youths) that found no significant differences between North America and Europe, Asia, Africa, South America and Oceania [[Faraone2021]].
- Practical meaning for students: when a study requires impairment and uses information from more than one informant, it finds fewer cases; when it counts symptoms reported by one informant, it finds more [[Polanczyk2007,Polanczyk2015]].

### what · Adults and older adults
- In the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication (probability subsample of 3,199 adults aged 18–44, with blinded clinical reappraisal of 154), current adult ADHD prevalence was 4.4%; most cases were untreated for ADHD [[Kessler2006]].
- The first WHO World Mental Health analysis (10 countries, n = 11,422 aged 18–44) found an average of 3.4% (range 1.2–7.3%), lower in lower-income (1.9%) than higher-income countries (4.2%) [[Fayyad2007]].
- The expanded WHO analysis (20 surveys, 26,744 respondents) found current adult ADHD averaging 2.8%: 3.6% in high-income, 3.0% in upper-middle-income and 1.4% in low/lower-middle-income countries [[Fayyad2017]].
- In those surveys ADHD was still present in adulthood in 57.0% of people with childhood ADHD and in 41.1% of those with subthreshold childhood ADHD [[Fayyad2017]].
- Only 21.8% of adults with 12-month ADHD in the WHO surveys received any mental health treatment in the past year, and only 3.3% were treated specifically for ADHD; most treatment targeted co-occurring conditions [[Fayyad2017]].
- An earlier meta-analysis (6 studies) put adult prevalence at 2.5% (95% CI 2.1–3.1), declining with age [[Simon2009]].
- Song 2021 found pooled crude prevalences of 4.61% (95% CI 3.41–5.99) for persistent adult ADHD and 8.83% (95% CI 7.23–10.57; 21 articles, 50,098 people) for symptomatic adult ADHD, before age-standardisation to the 2020 world population [[Song2021]].
- Persistent adult ADHD was more common in low- and middle-income countries (8.00%, 95% CI 3.79–13.57) than in high-income countries (3.25%, 95% CI 2.14–4.57) in Song 2021; this pattern runs opposite to the WHO survey results and is unresolved [[Song2021,Fayyad2017]].
- Prevalence falls with age: persistent adult ADHD went from 5.05% at 18–24 years to 0.77% at ≥60 years, and symptomatic adult ADHD from 8.99% to 4.51% [[Song2021]].
- An umbrella review of 5 meta-analyses (57 primary studies, 21,142,129 adults) found a pooled adult prevalence of 3.10% (95% CI 2.60–3.60), with the inattentive type most common [[Ayano2023Adults]].
- In older adults the estimate depends on how ADHD is counted: 2.18% using validated symptom scales, 0.23% using clinical diagnosis and 0.09% using treatment records (20 studies, 32 datasets) [[Dobrosavljevic2020]].
- European experts state that ADHD often persists into adulthood and old age and that it is underdiagnosed and undertreated in many European countries [[Kooij2019]].
- Adults with ADHD are more likely to come to psychiatric clinics for co-occurring depression, anxiety or alcohol problems than for ADHD itself, and their ADHD symptoms are often mistaken for those conditions [[Ginsberg2014]].

### what · United States figures
- 2022 National Survey of Children's Health (n = 45,169; ages 3–17): 11.4% ever diagnosed (7.1 million) and 10.5% with current ADHD (6.5 million) [[Danielson2024]].
- Among US children with current ADHD in 2022, 58.1% had moderate or severe ADHD and 77.9% had at least one co-occurring disorder [[Danielson2024]].
- Among US children with current ADHD in 2022, 53.6% received ADHD medication, 44.4% received behavioural treatment in the past year and 30.1% received no ADHD-specific treatment [[Danielson2024]].
- About 1 million more US children had ever received an ADHD diagnosis in 2022 than in 2016 [[Danielson2024]].
- 2016 NSCH (n = 45,736; ages 2–17): 9.4% ever diagnosed (6.1 million), 8.4% current (89.4% of those ever diagnosed); of current cases 62.0% took medication, 46.7% had behavioural treatment and 23.0% had neither [[Danielson2018]].
- National Health Interview Survey 2015–2016: diagnosed ADHD in 10.2% (95% CI 9.6–10.8) of children aged 4–17, 14.0% of boys versus 6.3% of girls [[Xu2018]].
- By race and ethnicity in 2015–2016, diagnosed ADHD was 6.1% in Hispanic, 12.0% in non-Hispanic White and 12.8% in non-Hispanic Black children [[Xu2018]].
- US adults, 2023 (Rapid Surveys System, 7,046 interviews): 6.0% (95% CI 5.3–6.8) had current ADHD; 55.9% of them were diagnosed at age 18 or older [[Staley2024]].
- Of US adults with current ADHD in 2023, 84.5% were under 50 years old (versus 51.2% of adults never diagnosed); 55.8% were men and 44.2% women [[Staley2024]].
- Treatment among US adults with ADHD in 2023: 36.5% none; 35.2% medication plus counselling; 15.1% medication only; 13.3% counselling only; 33.4% took a stimulant and 5.9% a non-stimulant in the past 12 months [[Staley2024]].
- Of adults taking a stimulant, 71.5% had difficulty filling their prescription in the past year because the medicine was unavailable (61.8% of all adults taking any ADHD medicine) [[Staley2024]].
- Telehealth: 46.0% of US adults with ADHD had ever used telehealth for ADHD; 8.9% of telehealth users were diagnosed through telehealth only; since March 2020, 30.5% used telehealth to get an ADHD prescription [[Staley2024]].

### what · Trends in diagnosis and prescribing, 1997–2023
- US diagnosed ADHD among children aged 4–17 rose from 6.1% in 1997–1998 to 10.2% in 2015–2016 (P for trend <.001), in every subgroup of age, sex, race and ethnicity, income and region [[Xu2018]].
- In Kaiser Permanente Northern California (5,282,877 adults), clinician-diagnosed adult ADHD prevalence rose from 0.43% in 2007 to 0.96% in 2016, and incidence from 9.43 to 13.49 per 10,000 person-years; prevalence in children aged 5–11 rose from 2.96% to 3.74% [[Chung2019]].
- Adult diagnosis rates in that system were consistently highest in White adults (0.67–1.42%) and lowest in Asian American and Pacific Islander adults (0.11–0.35% and 0.11–0.39%), suggesting lower detection in minority groups [[Chung2019]].
- In US commercial insurance claims, the share of people aged 5–64 with at least one stimulant fill rose from 3.6% in 2016 to 4.1% in 2021, with increases above 10% during 2020–2021 in women aged 15–44 and men aged 25–44 [[Danielson2023MMWR]].
- US ADHD medication fills (all payers) rose 23.8%, from 72,849,441 in 2019 to 90,183,437 in 2023; stimulant fills rose 20.4% and non-stimulant fills 60.1%, while stimulant fills in people aged 0–18 fell 6.5% [[Huskamp2025]].
- Nurse practitioners and physician assistants wrote about 1 in 3 ADHD prescription fills in 2023 [[Huskamp2025]].
- Across 13 countries and Hong Kong (154.5 million people, 2001–2015), ADHD medication use in children in 2010 was 0.95% in Asia and Australia, 4.48% in North America, 1.95% in northern Europe and 0.70% in western Europe, and rose over time in every country and region [[Raman2018]].
- Adult ADHD medication use in 2010 ranged from 0.003% to 1.48% across those countries (1.42% in North America); methylphenidate was the most used medicine in most countries [[Raman2018]].
- In Finland, lifetime prevalence of diagnosed ADHD rose 2.7-fold from 1.02% in 2015 to 1.80% in 2020 and 2.76% in 2022; new diagnoses doubled from 238 to 477 per 100,000 between 2020 and 2022 [[Auro2024]].
- In Finland, new diagnoses rose 2.6-fold in girls and young women aged 13–20 (577 to 1,488 per 100,000) and 3.0-fold in women aged 21–30 (361 to 1,100 per 100,000) during the pandemic years [[Auro2024]].
- The consensus statement attributes rising diagnosis to changes in administrative and clinical practice, not to a true rise in prevalence (Established) [[Faraone2021,Polanczyk2014]].

### what · Global burden
- GBD 2019 estimated the age-standardised prevalence of ADHD across all ages at 1.13% (95% UI 0.831–1.494) and incidence at 0.061%; ADHD accounted for 0.8% of global mental-disorder DALYs, with GBD mortality for ADHD set at zero [[Cortese2023GBD]].
- In GBD 2019, incidence, prevalence and DALYs were about 2.5 times higher in males than females; incidence peaked at 5–9 years and prevalence and DALYs at 10–14 years [[Cortese2023GBD]].
- A re-analysis of measured data (before 2013) found child and adolescent prevalence of 5.41% (95% CI 4.67–6.15), twice the GBD-modelled 2.68%, with no significant difference between low/middle- and high-income countries [[Cortese2023GBD]].
- GBD 2021 (people under 20, 204 countries): 46,890,733 prevalent cases, 4,111,621 new cases and 574,979 DALYs in 2021; prevalence 1.78% (95% UI 1.22–2.55), incidence 0.16%, DALY rate 21.8 per 100,000 [[Cortese2026GBD]].
- In GBD 2021, prevalence in people under 20 was 2.52% in males versus 0.99% in females, was highest in Australia (5.62%), and global prevalence and incidence fell modestly from 1990 to 2021 (−6.0% and −5.81%) [[Cortese2026GBD]].
- Both GBD critiques conclude that GBD probably underestimates ADHD prevalence and burden because of its methods [[Cortese2023GBD,Cortese2026GBD]].
- In a Danish cohort of 1.92 million people, mortality was 5.85 per 10,000 person-years in people with ADHD versus 2.21 without (adjusted mortality rate ratio 2.07, 95% CI 1.70–2.50), mostly from accidents [[Dalsgaard2015]].

### what · Relative age and the overdiagnosis debate
- In US states with a 1 September school-entry cutoff, claims-based ADHD diagnosis was 85.1 per 10,000 in children born in August versus 63.6 per 10,000 in those born in September (difference 21.5; 95% CI 8.8–34.0); treatment rates were 52.9 versus 40.4 per 10,000 [[Layton2018]].
- That August–September gap was not seen in states without a 1 September cutoff, and asthma, diabetes and obesity did not differ by birth month [[Layton2018]].
- A 2025 meta-analysis (32 studies) found that younger relative age raised the risk of ADHD diagnosis (RR 1.38) and of ADHD medication (RR 1.28); the effect appeared in teacher ratings but not in parent ratings [[Frisira2025]].
- In 17 of 19 studies (13 countries, over 15.4 million children), the youngest children in a school year were more likely to be diagnosed and/or medicated, including in countries with low prescribing rates [[Whitely2019]].
- Among 4,708 children with ADHD followed for 4 to 33 years, younger relative age was not linked to whether ADHD persisted (OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.99–1.06): diagnoses in the youngest children were no more likely to be disconfirmed later [[SIMBA2023]].
- A scoping review of 334 studies found evidence of a "reservoir" of undiagnosed ADHD (104 studies), rising diagnosis (45), additional cases at the milder end (25) and rising medication (83); only 5 studies weighed benefits and harms for the milder cases (Early evidence of overdiagnosis in milder cases) [[Kazda2021]].
- The AAP guideline states that "ADHD goes undiagnosed in a considerable number of children and adolescents" [[Wolraich2019]].
- Over- and under-diagnosis can coexist: adults (especially minority groups and women) appear under-detected [[Chung2019,Fayyad2017,Young2020Females]], while relative-age data suggest some young children in a class may be over-identified [[Layton2018,Frisira2025]].

### what · Screening tools as teaching examples
- The WHO Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS) has 18 questions on DSM-IV symptoms; a 6-question screener had sensitivity 68.7%, specificity 99.5% and accuracy 97.9% against blinded clinical diagnosis in a US community sample [[Kessler2005]].
- A screener is not a diagnosis: ADHD "can only be diagnosed by a licensed clinician" and "cannot be diagnosed by rating scales alone, neuropsychological tests, or methods for imaging the brain" [[Faraone2021]].

### signs · The three presentations and their shares
- DSM-5 describes three presentations (predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, combined); DSM-5 replaced "subtype" with "presentation" because the symptom pattern can change as people mature [[Posner2020,Epstein2013]].
- In the general population the predominantly inattentive presentation is the most common, but children with the combined presentation are more likely to be referred to clinics [[Willcutt2012]].
- In African community studies, the inattentive type (2.95%) was most common, followed by hyperactive-impulsive (2.77%) and combined (2.44%), and the inattentive type was most common in both boys (4.05%) and girls (2.21%) [[Ayano2020Africa]].
- In adults the inattentive type remains the most common presentation [[Ayano2023Adults]].
- Inattention is more strongly linked to academic impairment, low self-esteem, poorer work outcomes and lower overall adaptive functioning; hyperactivity-impulsivity is linked to peer rejection, aggression, risky driving and accidental injuries [[Faraone2021]].
- Many children diagnosed with the combined presentation later shift to the predominantly inattentive presentation, because inattention is relatively stable while hyperactivity-impulsivity often wanes [[Epstein2013]].
- ADHD occurs at every level of intelligence: in a birth cohort of over 5,700 children, high-, average- and low-IQ children with ADHD did not differ in age at meeting criteria or in rates of other disorders [[Faraone2021]].

### signs · Preschool children
- The AAP recommends that primary care clinicians evaluate for ADHD any child or adolescent aged 4 years to the 18th birthday who has academic or behavioural problems and symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity or impulsivity (Grade B, strong recommendation) [[Wolraich2019]].
- The AAP states that DSM-5 criteria can be applied to preschool-aged children (4 years to the sixth birthday), and that there is insufficient evidence to recommend diagnosis or treatment under age 4 [[Wolraich2019]].
- Diagnosing preschool children is harder because DSM-5 requires symptoms in more than one setting, which is difficult to observe in children who do not attend preschool or child care [[Wolraich2019]].
- Longitudinal studies suggest at least four trajectories: early (preschool, 3–5 years) onset; middle-childhood (6–14 years) onset with a persistent course; middle-childhood onset with adolescent offset; and adolescent or adult onset (16 years and older) [[Posner2020]].
- In Denmark (1.3 million children), ADHD diagnoses peaked at age 8 in boys and age 17 in girls [[Dalsgaard2020JAMAPsych]].

### signs · How symptoms change with age
- Hyperactive and impulsive symptoms decline faster with age than inattentive symptoms; in 128 boys followed for 4 years, inattention remitted in fewer participants than hyperactivity or impulsivity [[Biederman2000Decline]].
- Adolescents and young adults with childhood ADHD often show less hyperactivity and impulsivity but keep their inattention symptoms [[Faraone2021]].
- DSM-5 added examples of how symptoms look in adolescents and adults and lowered the threshold from 6 to 5 symptoms in a domain for people aged 17 and older, because the 6-of-9 threshold missed impaired adolescents and adults [[Epstein2013,Posner2020]].
- DSM-5 moved the age-of-onset criterion from impairment before age 7 to symptoms before age 12; some people, such as those with high intelligence, inattentive symptoms or highly structured environments, may not be impaired until demands rise in late elementary or middle school [[Epstein2013]].
- In a Missouri community sample of 9,380 people aged 7–29, current DSM-IV-like ADHD affected 9.2% (male:female 2.28:1), was most prevalent in children, and people with lifetime ADHD still had raised symptoms as adolescents and adults [[Ramtekkar2010]].
- In adults, ADHD is described as often presenting as a lifelong, impairing condition [[Kooij2019]].
- People with ADHD of all ages have medium-sized weaknesses in executive function (effect sizes 0.46–0.69 across 83 studies), strongest for response inhibition, vigilance, working memory and planning; these weaknesses are not present in every person with ADHD (Established) [[Willcutt2005]].

### signs · Emotional dysregulation
- Emotional dysregulation is common in ADHD throughout the lifespan and is a major contributor to impairment [[Shaw2014Emotion]].
- Emotional dysregulation is found in about 25–45% of children and 30–70% of adults with ADHD [[Shaw2014Emotion]].
- In a population study of 5,326 young people, mood lability was found in 38% of children with ADHD, a ten-fold increase over population rates [[Shaw2014Emotion]].
- Clinic studies of adults with ADHD report impairing emotional dysregulation in 34–70% [[Shaw2014Emotion]].
- Emotional dysregulation is not part of the DSM criteria; whether it is a core feature, a correlated dimension or a distinct subgroup remains open (Hypothesis) [[Shaw2014Emotion]].
- In both children and adults, ADHD is commonly accompanied by emotional lability (irritability, low frustration tolerance, mood changes) [[Young2020Females]].
- Historically, Still (1902) named "an abnormal degree of passionateness" (quick displays of frustration and anger) as the most common feature of the children he described [[Lange2010]].

### signs · Persistence into adulthood
- In a meta-analysis of controlled follow-up studies, about 15% of children with ADHD still met full criteria at age 25, but about 65% did when ADHD in partial remission was included [[Faraone2006]].
- The consensus statement summarises this as "only about one in six youths with ADHD still meet full diagnostic criteria for ADHD at age 25, and about half show signs of residual impairment" [[Faraone2021]].
- Posner and colleagues summarise meta-analytic data as 15% persisting with full criteria and 40–60% partial remitters [[Posner2020]].
- Reported remission rates depend mainly on the definition used: remission was highest for syndromatic remission (fewer than full criteria) and lowest for functional recovery [[Biederman2000Decline]].
- In the WHO surveys, 57.0% of people with childhood ADHD still had ADHD as adults [[Fayyad2017]].
- In a UK twin cohort, 54 of 247 children with ADHD (21.9%) still met criteria at age 18; persistence was associated with more childhood symptoms and lower IQ [[AgnewBlais2016]].
- In a Brazilian birth cohort, only 17.2% of children with ADHD at 11 still had ADHD at 18–19 [[Caye2016]].
- Knowledge of predictors of persistence versus remission is described as poor and largely inconsistent [[Franke2018]].

### signs · The fluctuating course (MTA)
- In the Multimodal Treatment Study of ADHD (558 children, eight assessments up to mean age 25.12), about 30% had full remission at some point, but 60% of those later had a recurrence [[Sibley2022]].
- Only 9.1% showed sustained recovery by the end of the study, 10.8% showed stable persistence, and 63.8% had fluctuating periods of remission and recurrence [[Sibley2022]].
- Ninety per cent of MTA children continued to have residual symptoms into young adulthood, which challenges the idea that about half of children "outgrow" ADHD [[Sibley2022]].
- People with fluctuating ADHD had a mean of 3.58 fluctuations over 16 years, with a 6- to 7-symptom difference between peaks and troughs; remissions usually first occurred in adolescence [[Sibley2024]].
- In that analysis, 15.6% had partial remission; the stable persistent group was linked to early mood-disorder risk, adolescent substance-use problems, low medication use and poorer response to childhood treatment [[Sibley2024]].
- The authors advise clinicians to expect fluctuation and to plan "as-needed return to care" when symptoms rise [[Sibley2024]].

### signs · Late-onset adult ADHD (controversy)
- In the Dunedin cohort (1,037 people followed to age 38), childhood ADHD prevalence was 6% (mostly male) and adult ADHD 3% (gender balanced); 90% of adult cases had no history of childhood ADHD (Early evidence) [[Moffitt2015]].
- In the E-Risk cohort, 112 of 166 young adults with ADHD (67.5%) had not met criteria at any childhood assessment [[AgnewBlais2016]].
- In the Pelotas cohort, only 12.6% of young adults with ADHD had the disorder in childhood; the childhood group was 63.9% male while the young-adult group had a female majority (39.0% male) [[Caye2016]].
- Prevalence in adults is higher than childhood persistence rates would predict, which suggests new cases emerge during development [[Posner2020]].
- DSM-5-TR still requires several symptoms before age 12; whether adult-emerging ADHD is the same disorder is unresolved (Hypothesis) [[Moffitt2015,Caye2016,Posner2020]].

### women · Sex ratio: clinic, community and adulthood
- Clinic-referred samples of children show a male-to-female ratio of about 10:1, compared with about 3:1 in community samples [[Biederman2002Gender]].
- Clinical referral ratios in boys versus girls range from 3:1 to 16:1, compared with about 3:1 in community samples, so many girls with ADHD are likely to remain unidentified and untreated [[Young2020Females]].
- Parent and teacher ratings in youth give a roughly 2:1 male-to-female ratio [[Faraone2021]].
- Girls meet diagnostic criteria at just under half the rate of boys, a ratio that becomes much closer to equal by adulthood [[Hinshaw2022]].
- In the WHO surveys, adult ADHD was more common in men than women (OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.3–1.9), and this gap reflected the higher childhood rate in males rather than a sex difference in persistence [[Fayyad2017]].
- In the Missouri community study, the 2.28:1 male:female ratio was lower than clinic ratios, which the authors read as under-diagnosis of females [[Ramtekkar2010]].
- In Sweden (9.6 million people, 2003–2019), males had higher incidence of diagnosed ADHD before age 14 [[Yang2024Sweden]].
- In Danish registers, ADHD was the most common mental disorder diagnosed in boys before age 18 (5.90%), and incidence peaked at age 8 in boys versus age 17 in girls; the authors suggest delayed detection in girls [[Dalsgaard2020JAMAPsych]].
- In Finland, the steepest pandemic-era rises in new ADHD diagnoses were in girls and women aged 13–30 [[Auro2024]].

### women · How ADHD can look different in girls and women
- Girls and women with ADHD show mainly inattention and related internalising problems, while boys and men show more hyperactive-impulsive symptoms and externalising problems [[Hinshaw2022]].
- In a clinic sample (140 girls, 140 boys with ADHD), girls were more likely than boys to have the predominantly inattentive type and less likely to have learning disability, conduct disorder or oppositional defiant disorder, which may bias referral against girls [[Biederman2002Gender]].
- Compared with boys with ADHD, girls with ADHD showed lower hyperactivity and fewer other externalising behaviours in the 1997 meta-analysis, and many differences depended on whether samples were clinic-referred [[Gaub1997]].
- Among 283 children aged 7–12 with high symptoms, emotional problems especially distinguished girls who met full criteria; parents under-rated diagnosed girls' hyperactive-impulsive symptoms compared with interviewer assessment [[Mowlem2019]].
- The AAP guideline notes that girls with ADHD are more likely than boys to have a co-occurring internalising condition such as anxiety or depression [[Wolraich2019]].
- Girls with ADHD may develop coping strategies that mask symptoms; their inattentive presentation is often seen as "subthreshold"; and co-occurring anxiety and depression can lead to missed diagnosis or misdiagnosis [[Quinn2014]].
- Compensatory behaviours (social adaptiveness, compliance, perfectionism) can mask impairment and delay referral; school or life transitions can "unmask" ADHD [[Young2020Females]].
- Satisfactory academic achievement should not rule out an ADHD diagnosis in a girl or woman [[Quinn2014]].
- Clinicians may overlook ADHD in females because symptoms are less overt (but still impairing) and because many adopt compensatory strategies [[Hinshaw2022]].
- NICE advises being aware that ADHD is thought to be under-recognised in girls and women, who are less likely to be referred, more likely to have undiagnosed ADHD, and more likely to receive an incorrect diagnosis of another mental health or neurodevelopmental condition [[NICE2018]].
- Women diagnosed in adulthood described four themes: effects on social-emotional wellbeing, difficult relationships, a feeling of lack of control, and self-acceptance after diagnosis (qualitative, 8 studies) [[Attoe2023]].

### women · Outcomes for girls and women
- In a 10-year follow-up of 140 girls with ADHD (ages 17–24 at follow-up, 95% retention), those with childhood ADHD had more impairment and higher rates of suicide attempts and self-injury than comparison girls, concentrated in the combined type [[Hinshaw2012Girls]].
- Females with ADHD have a particularly heightened risk for problems in close relationships and for self-harm, and developmental pathways to intimate partner violence and unplanned pregnancy [[Hinshaw2022]].
- After excluding people with oppositional defiant disorder, conduct disorder and substance use disorder, the mortality rate ratio linked to ADHD was 2.85 (95% CI 1.56–4.71) in girls and women versus 1.27 (0.89–1.76) in boys and men in Denmark [[Dalsgaard2015]].

### history · Early descriptions (1775–1932)
- 1775: German physician Melchior Adam Weikard described attention deficits ("Mangel der Aufmerksamkeit") in a medical textbook, which Barkley and Peters propose as the earliest known description [[Barkley2012Weikard,Faraone2021]].
- 1798: Scottish physician Sir Alexander Crichton described "mental restlessness", an incapacity to attend "with a necessary degree of constancy to any one object", that could be present from birth and "generally diminished with age" [[Lange2010,Palmer2001]].
- 1845: Frankfurt physician Heinrich Hoffmann published "Struwwelpeter", whose stories "Fidgety Philip" and "Johnny Look-in-the-air" (added 1847) depict restlessness and inattention; he drew the book in 1844 for his 3-year-old son [[Lange2010,Faraone2021,Thome2004]].
- 1887–1901: French clinicians including Bourneville described an equivalent of ADHD in medical and educational writing [[Faraone2021,MartinezBadia2015]].
- 1902: British paediatrician George Still gave three Goulstonian Lectures to the Royal College of Physicians, "On some abnormal psychical conditions in children", published in the Lancet [[Still1902,Lange2010]].
- Still described 20 children with a "defect of moral control" without intellectual impairment or physical disease: 15 boys and 5 girls, with onset before age 7 in 7 of the 9 cases where onset was known [[Lange2010]].
- Still's concept is not the same as ADHD; it included other kinds of behaviour, but his cases include clear inattention and impulsivity [[Lange2010]].
- 1917–1928: the encephalitis lethargica epidemic affected about 20 million people; many surviving children showed a "postencephalitic behaviour disorder" with hyperactivity and impulsivity, which pushed the idea that brain injury could cause these behaviours [[Lange2010]].
- 1932: German physicians Franz Kramer and Hans Pollnow described a "hyperkinetic disease of infancy", with onset at 3–4 years and a peak at 6; the term "hyperkinetic disorder" was later adopted by the WHO [[Lange2010,Faraone2021]].

### history · Stimulants and minimal brain dysfunction (1937–1966)
- 1937: Charles Bradley gave Benzedrine (an amphetamine) to children at the Emma Pendleton Bradley Home in Rhode Island to treat headaches after pneumoencephalography and noticed better behaviour and school performance [[Strohl2011,Bradley1937]].
- In Bradley's 30 children, "the remarkably improved school performance of approximately half the children" was the most striking change [[Lange2010]].
- Bradley's findings were largely ignored for about 25 years [[Strohl2011]].
- 1944: methylphenidate was first synthesised by Leandro Panizzon; it was named Ritalin after his wife and marketed by Ciba in 1954 [[Lange2010]].
- 5 December 1955: the original US approval date of RITALIN (NDA010187) in the Drugs@FDA record [[FDA-Ritalin]].
- 1960s: the FDA approved methylphenidate for behavioural disorders in children [[Faraone2021]].
- 1957: Laufer and colleagues described a "hyperkinetic impulse disorder" [[Lange2010]].
- 1940s–1950s: the term "minimal brain damage" assumed brain injury from behaviour alone; in 1963 the Oxford International Study Group of Child Neurology argued brain damage should not be inferred from behaviour and favoured "minimal brain dysfunction" [[Lange2010]].
- 1966: a US task force (Clements) defined minimal brain dysfunction in children of near-average or higher intelligence with problems including "control of attention, impulse or motor function" [[Lange2010]].
- Until the 1990s, ADHD was widely seen as a childhood disorder that children "grow out" of at puberty; in the 1990s it was recognised to persist into adulthood in many cases [[Lange2010]].

### history · Diagnostic manuals (1968–2022)
- 1968, DSM-II: "Hyperkinetic Reaction of Childhood", defined in two sentences focused on overactivity and noting that the behaviour "usually diminishes by adolescence" [[Lange2010,Epstein2013]].
- 1972: Virginia Douglas argued that deficits in sustained attention and impulse control mattered more than hyperactivity [[Lange2010]].
- 1980, DSM-III: renamed "Attention Deficit Disorder (with or without hyperactivity)", with separate symptom lists for inattention, impulsivity and hyperactivity [[Lange2010,Epstein2013]].
- 1987, DSM-III-R: renamed "Attention-deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)" and dropped the two subtypes [[Lange2010]].
- 1994, DSM-IV: three subtypes (predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, combined), based on a large field trial of children and adolescents up to age 17 [[Lange2010]].
- 2000, DSM-IV-TR: a text revision with changes mostly confined to the descriptive text [[Lange2010]].
- DSM-5 (described as newly released in 2013): onset before 12 instead of 7; 5 symptoms for older adolescents and adults; symptoms (not impairment) in two or more settings; "presentations" instead of "types"; autism no longer excludes ADHD; severity and partial-remission specifiers [[Epstein2013]].
- 2022: DSM-5-TR, the text revision of DSM-5, was published by the American Psychiatric Association [[APA2022DSM]].
- ICD-11 was approved by the World Health Assembly in May 2019, and reporting with it began on 1 January 2022 [[Reed2019ICD11]].
- ICD-11 moved ADHD from disruptive to neurodevelopmental disorders, replaced "hyperkinetic disorder" with ADHD, uses presentations, and gives no precise age of onset, duration or symptom count [[Posner2020]].
- ICD-11 codes ADHD as 6A05, with 6A05.0 predominantly inattentive, 6A05.1 predominantly hyperactive-impulsive and 6A05.2 combined presentation (ICD-10: F90 hyperkinetic disorders) [[TebartzVanElst2025]].
- 2021: the World Federation of ADHD International Consensus Statement listed 208 evidence-based conclusions, approved by 80 authors from 27 countries on 6 continents [[Faraone2021]].

### what · FAQ (8 answers)
- Q1. Is ADHD a modern invention? No. Physicians described attention problems in 1775 and 1798, and a scientific journal described it in 1902; the consensus calls ADHD "not a new disorder" [[Barkley2012Weikard,Lange2010,Faraone2021]].
- Q2. Is ADHD only an American condition? No. When studies use the same methods, prevalence does not differ significantly between North America and Europe, Asia, Africa, South America and Oceania [[Polanczyk2014,Willcutt2012,Faraone2021]].
- Q3. Is ADHD becoming more common? The share of children who meet criteria in community studies has not risen over three decades, but diagnosis and medication use have risen [[Polanczyk2014,Xu2018,Raman2018]].
- Q4. How many people have ADHD? About 5% of children and adolescents worldwide, and about 2.6% of adults (persistent) or 6.8% (any symptomatic adult ADHD) [[Polanczyk2007,Song2021]].
- Q5. Do children grow out of ADHD? Some do, but at age 25 about 15% still meet full criteria and about 65% when partial remission is counted; in the MTA most people's symptoms came and went, and only 9.1% fully recovered [[Faraone2006,Sibley2022]].
- Q6. Why are girls diagnosed less often? Girls more often have inattentive symptoms, internalising problems and coping strategies that hide symptoms, so they are referred less often and may be misdiagnosed with anxiety or depression [[Hinshaw2022,Quinn2014,Young2020Females]].
- Q7. Can adults be diagnosed for the first time? Yes. In the United States in 2023, 55.9% of adults with current ADHD were diagnosed at age 18 or older [[Staley2024]].
- Q8. Can a questionnaire diagnose ADHD? No. Rating scales help screening, but diagnosis needs a clinician's interview and evidence of symptoms across settings and impairment [[Faraone2021,Kessler2005]].

### Exam hooks
- Worldwide-pooled childhood ADHD prevalence (Polanczyk 2007): 5.29% [[Polanczyk2007]].
- Variation in prevalence estimates is explained mainly by methods (criteria, informant, impairment requirement), not geography or year [[Polanczyk2014]].
- Adult ADHD prevalence, WHO surveys: 2.8% [[Fayyad2017]]; global persistent adult ADHD 2.58%, symptomatic 6.76% [[Song2021]].
- US 2022: 11.4% of children ever diagnosed; 53.6% of those with current ADHD on medication [[Danielson2024]].
- US 2023: 6.0% of adults (15.5 million) have current ADHD; over half diagnosed in adulthood [[Staley2024]].
- Persistence at 25 years: about 15% full syndrome, about 65% including partial remission [[Faraone2006]].
- MTA: 63.8% fluctuating course; 9.1% sustained recovery [[Sibley2022]].
- Hyperactivity-impulsivity declines faster with age than inattention [[Biederman2000Decline]].
- Most common presentation in the community: predominantly inattentive; most common in clinics: combined [[Willcutt2012]].
- Male:female ratio about 10:1 in clinics versus about 3:1 in the community [[Biederman2002Gender]].
- Emotion dysregulation: 25–45% of children and 30–70% of adults with ADHD [[Shaw2014Emotion]].
- DSM-5 changes: onset before 12 (was 7); 5 symptoms at age ≥17; "presentations"; autism no longer exclusionary [[Epstein2013]].
- DSM-II (1968) name: "Hyperkinetic Reaction of Childhood"; DSM-III (1980): "Attention Deficit Disorder with or without hyperactivity"; DSM-III-R (1987): ADHD [[Lange2010]].
- ICD-11 code for ADHD: 6A05 (ICD-10 F90) [[TebartzVanElst2025]].
- Bradley 1937: Benzedrine improved behaviour and school performance in children [[Strohl2011]].
- George Still, 1902 Goulstonian Lectures: "defect of moral control", 15 boys and 5 girls [[Lange2010]].
- AAP: evaluate for ADHD from age 4 to the 18th birthday [[Wolraich2019]].
- Relative age: the youngest children in a class are more likely to be diagnosed (RR 1.38) [[Frisira2025]].

### In plain words
- ADHD is common. About 5 in 100 children around the world have it [[Polanczyk2007]].
- ADHD is found in every part of the world that has been studied, not just in one country [[Willcutt2012]].
- Many adults have ADHD too. In the United States, about 1 adult in 16 says they have it now [[Staley2024]].
- More than half of US adults with ADHD were first diagnosed as grown-ups [[Staley2024]].
- Being very active and restless often gets milder with age. Trouble paying attention usually lasts longer [[Biederman2000Decline,Faraone2021]].
- For many people, ADHD symptoms come and go over the years. Good stretches and harder stretches are both normal [[Sibley2022]].
- Girls with ADHD are often quiet daydreamers rather than disruptive, so their ADHD can be missed [[Hinshaw2022,Quinn2014]].
- Some girls and women work very hard to hide their struggles. Good grades do not rule out ADHD [[Quinn2014,Young2020Females]].
- Strong feelings that flare quickly, like frustration or anger, are common in ADHD [[Shaw2014Emotion]].
- Doctors described children with ADHD-like problems more than 200 years ago. ADHD is not new [[Faraone2021]].
- A questionnaire can point to ADHD, but only a trained clinician can diagnose it [[Faraone2021]].
- In a school class, the youngest children are more likely to be diagnosed, so a child's age within the class is worth keeping in mind [[Frisira2025]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Is ADHD becoming more common, or only more often diagnosed? Community prevalence was flat over three decades with standard methods [[Polanczyk2014,Faraone2021]]; diagnosis and medication rates have risen sharply [[Xu2018,Chung2019,Raman2018,Auro2024]]; and there is evidence of overdiagnosis in milder cases [[Kazda2021]]. Status: the gap is mostly explained by diagnosis and recognition, not a true rise; how much is benefit (finding missed cases) and how much is harm (labelling mild cases) is unresolved.
- How common is ADHD in children? Estimates range from 3.4% with strict community interviews [[Polanczyk2015]] to 7.2–8.0% in broader pooled reviews [[Thomas2015,Ayano2023Children]], and GBD models give lower figures that may underestimate [[Cortese2023GBD,Cortese2026GBD]]. Status: the figure depends on criteria, informants and the impairment requirement.
- Does adult ADHD prevalence depend on country income? WHO surveys found higher prevalence in high-income countries (3.6% vs 1.4%) [[Fayyad2017]]; Song 2021 found higher persistent ADHD in low- and middle-income countries (8.00% vs 3.25%) [[Song2021]]. Status: unresolved; likely reflects measurement and study mix.
- Is adult-onset ADHD real? Three population cohorts found that most adults with ADHD had not met criteria in childhood [[Moffitt2015,AgnewBlais2016,Caye2016]]; DSM-5 still requires childhood symptoms, and late-onset cases may be explained by missed childhood ADHD, other conditions, or a distinct syndrome (Hypothesis) [[Posner2020]]. Status: active debate.
- Do children "grow out" of ADHD? Single-endpoint studies suggested about half remit [[Faraone2006]]; repeated MTA assessments found that most people fluctuate and only 9.1% fully recover [[Sibley2022]]. Status: "remission" depends on the definition and timing [[Biederman2000Decline]].
- Are girls under-diagnosed or do they truly have ADHD less often? The clinic ratio (about 10:1) is far higher than the community ratio (about 3:1) [[Biederman2002Gender]], and the ratio approaches equality in adulthood [[Hinshaw2022]]; males still show more ADHD in community samples [[Ayano2023Children,Cortese2026GBD]]. Status: both referral bias and a true difference in childhood are likely.
- Is emotional dysregulation a core symptom? It is common (25–45% of children, 30–70% of adults) but not in DSM criteria; three models compete (Hypothesis) [[Shaw2014Emotion]].
- Does relative age mean misdiagnosis? The youngest in a class are diagnosed more often [[Layton2018,Frisira2025,Whitely2019]], yet their diagnoses persist as often as those of older classmates [[SIMBA2023]]. Status: unresolved; it may also mean older children are under-diagnosed.
- Who first described ADHD? Weikard 1775 [[Barkley2012Weikard]], Crichton 1798 [[Lange2010]] and Still 1902 [[Still1902]] are all proposed; historians caution that these descriptions only partly match modern ADHD [[Lange2010,MartinezBadia2015]].
