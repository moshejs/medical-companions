# D · Diagnosis: criteria, rating scales, assessment and differential

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: criteria, scales, differential, women.

This pass covers how ADHD is diagnosed: the DSM-5-TR criteria and what changed from DSM-IV, the ICD-11 entry (6A05), the main guidelines (AAP 2019, NICE NG87, the European adult consensus), rating scales and interviews with their published psychometrics, why screeners mislead at low prevalence, the medical and psychiatric conditions to rule out or look for alongside ADHD, and the under-recognition of ADHD in girls and women. Rating scales are described as teaching tools. None of them diagnoses ADHD on its own, and none is meant for self-diagnosis.

## Findings

### criteria · DSM-5-TR criteria: structure
- DSM-5-TR defines ADHD as a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interferes with functioning or development, set out in Criteria A to E [[APA2022DSM]].
- Criterion A1 (inattention) lists 9 symptoms: (a) careless mistakes or missing details; (b) trouble sustaining attention; (c) seems not to listen when spoken to directly; (d) does not follow through and fails to finish tasks; (e) difficulty organising tasks and activities; (f) avoids or dislikes tasks that need sustained mental effort; (g) loses things needed for tasks; (h) easily distracted by outside stimuli (in older adolescents and adults this can include unrelated thoughts); (i) forgetful in daily activities [[APA2022DSM]].
- Criterion A2 (hyperactivity-impulsivity) lists 9 symptoms: (a) fidgets, taps or squirms; (b) leaves seat when remaining seated is expected; (c) runs or climbs when it is inappropriate (in adolescents and adults this may be only a feeling of restlessness); (d) unable to play or do leisure activities quietly; (e) "on the go", as if "driven by a motor"; (f) talks excessively; (g) blurts out answers; (h) difficulty waiting their turn; (i) interrupts or intrudes on others [[APA2022DSM]].
- Each symptom must occur "often", must have lasted at least 6 months, must be inconsistent with developmental level, and must directly harm social and academic or occupational activities. Symptoms are not counted if they are only oppositional behaviour, defiance, hostility or failure to understand tasks [[APA2022DSM]].
- Symptom threshold: 6 or more symptoms in a domain for children up to age 16, and 5 or more for adolescents aged 17 and older and adults [[APA2022DSM,Posner2020]].
- Criterion B: several inattentive or hyperactive-impulsive symptoms were present before age 12 [[APA2022DSM]].
- Criterion C: several symptoms are present in two or more settings (for example home, school or work; with friends or relatives; in other activities) [[APA2022DSM]].
- Criterion D: there is clear evidence that the symptoms interfere with, or reduce the quality of, social, academic or occupational functioning [[APA2022DSM,Epstein2013]].
- Criterion E: symptoms do not occur only during schizophrenia or another psychotic disorder and are not better explained by another mental disorder (for example a mood, anxiety, dissociative or personality disorder, or substance intoxication or withdrawal) [[APA2022DSM]].
- Presentations (based on the past 6 months): combined (A1 and A2 both met; F90.2), predominantly inattentive (A1 met, A2 not; F90.0), and predominantly hyperactive-impulsive (A2 met, A1 not; F90.1) [[APA2022DSM]].
- "In partial remission" applies when full criteria were met before, fewer than full criteria have been met for the past 6 months, and symptoms still impair functioning [[APA2022DSM,Epstein2013]].
- Current severity is specified as mild, moderate or severe, based on how many symptoms exceed the threshold and how much impairment they cause [[APA2022DSM,Epstein2013]].
- "Other specified ADHD" (F90.8) and "unspecified ADHD" (F90.9) are for impairing ADHD-type symptoms that do not meet full criteria. "Other specified" records the reason [[APA2022DSM]].
- The same 18 symptoms apply at all ages under 18, from preschool to adolescence, and are only minimally different from DSM-IV [[Wolraich2019]].
- ADHD is a clinical diagnosis. It needs a detailed evaluation of current and past symptoms and impairment, plus a full family, gestational and developmental history [[Posner2020]].
- The World Federation of ADHD consensus states that ADHD can only be diagnosed by a licensed clinician who interviews the parent or caregiver and/or patient. It cannot be diagnosed by rating scales alone, neuropsychological tests or brain imaging [[Faraone2021]].
- The consensus also states that ADHD can be diagnosed in people with high intelligence. In a birth cohort of over 5,700 children, high-, average- and low-IQ children with ADHD did not differ in median age at meeting criteria, rates of learning, psychiatric or substance disorders, or stimulant treatment [[Faraone2021]].
- The consensus argues that ADHD meets the Robins and Guze (1970) validity criteria: trained clinicians in different settings and cultures agree on its presence, and the diagnosis predicts associated problems, outcomes and treatment response [[Faraone2021]].
- Predominantly inattentive symptoms are more strongly linked to academic impairment, low self-esteem, poor occupational outcomes and lower adaptive functioning. Hyperactive-impulsive symptoms are linked to peer rejection, aggression, risky driving and accidental injury [[Faraone2021]].
- Inattention stays fairly stable across development while hyperactivity-impulsivity often wanes with age. Many children first diagnosed with combined presentation later move to predominantly inattentive presentation [[Epstein2013,Wolraich2019]].
- ADHD is best understood as the extreme end of a continuum, differing from typical development by degree, not kind. DSM-5 and ICD-11 still use categorical thresholds, which the Lancet seminar calls "somewhat arbitrary" (Established) [[Posner2020]].

### criteria · What changed from DSM-IV to DSM-5
- DSM-5 left the 18 core symptoms and the two symptom domains of DSM-IV unchanged. It added examples of how symptoms look in adolescents and adults [[Epstein2013]].
- The symptom threshold for people aged 17 and older fell from 6 to 5 per domain [[Epstein2013,Posner2020]].
- Age of onset changed from "symptoms and impairment before age 7" to "several symptoms before age 12" [[Epstein2013,Posner2020]].
- Pervasiveness (Criterion C) changed from impairment in two or more settings to symptoms in two or more settings [[Epstein2013]].
- Impairment (Criterion D) now needs symptoms that "reduce the quality of" functioning, replacing "clinically significant" impairment. The authors note this is more inclusive and could raise prevalence [[Epstein2013]].
- Autism spectrum disorder was removed as an exclusion, so ADHD and autism can now be diagnosed together [[Epstein2013,Reiersen2008]].
- DSM-IV "subtypes" became "presentations", because symptom profiles often shift as people grow [[Epstein2013,Posner2020]].
- Two specifiers were added: severity (mild, moderate, severe) and "in partial remission" [[Epstein2013]].
- Raising the onset age to 12 in a British birth cohort of 2,232 children increased ADHD prevalence at age 12 by only 0.1%. Children with symptom onset at ages 7 to 12 did not differ in correlates or risk factors from those with onset before 7 [[Polanczyk2010AgeOnset]].
- Rationale given for age 12: adults diagnosed late often cannot recall exactly when symptoms began, and some groups (highly intelligent children, those with mainly inattentive symptoms, those in very structured settings) may not show impairment until demands rise in late primary or middle school [[Epstein2013]].
- Remaining criticisms: DSM-5 still offers no dimensional rating, and severity can be scored from either symptom count or impairment, which may make it unreliable between clinicians [[Epstein2013]].

### criteria · ICD-11 (6A05) and how it differs
- ICD-11 places ADHD under code 6A05 in the neurodevelopmental disorders grouping of chapter 06 (Mental, behavioural or neurodevelopmental disorders) [[WHO-ICD11]].
- ICD-11 moved ADHD out of the ICD-10 grouping of behavioural and emotional disorders with childhood onset, renamed "hyperkinetic disorder" as ADHD, and added inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive presentations, bringing it closer to DSM-5 [[Posner2020]].
- The ICD-11 diagnostic requirements describe a persistent pattern ("e.g., at least 6 months") of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, beyond the range expected for age and intellectual level, with evidence of significant symptoms before age 12 [[WHO-ICD11]].
- ICD-11 requires "several" symptoms from symptom clusters but sets no fixed symptom count, unlike DSM-5's 6/5 thresholds [[WHO-ICD11,Posner2020]].
- ICD-11 presentation codes are 6A05.0 predominantly inattentive, 6A05.1 predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, 6A05.2 combined, 6A05.Y other specified and 6A05.Z unspecified [[WHO-ICD11]].
- ICD-11 excludes symptoms caused by substances (for example cocaine) or medications (for example bronchodilators or thyroid replacement) acting on the central nervous system, including withdrawal, and symptoms caused by a disease of the nervous system [[WHO-ICD11]].
- In adults, ICD-11 calls a history of symptoms before age 12 an important corroborating feature, best established from school records or people who knew the person in childhood. Without such corroboration, ICD-11 advises caution in diagnosing older adolescents and adults [[WHO-ICD11]].
- ICD-11 describes a subgroup of mainly children with an exclusively inattentive presentation (daydreaming, mind-wandering, lack of focus), sometimes called a "restrictive inattentive pattern" or sluggish cognitive tempo [[WHO-ICD11]].
- ICD-11 statistical reporting began on 1 January 2022 [[Reed2019ICD11]].

### criteria · Guidelines: AAP 2019 (children and adolescents)
- AAP Key Action Statement (KAS) 1: the primary care clinician should start an ADHD evaluation for any child or adolescent aged 4 years to the 18th birthday who has academic or behavioural problems and symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity or impulsivity (grade B, strong recommendation) [[Wolraich2019]].
- The AAP found insufficient evidence to recommend diagnosis or treatment before age 4, other than parent training in behaviour management, which does not need a diagnosis [[Wolraich2019]].
- KAS 2: the clinician should confirm that DSM-5 criteria are met, document symptoms and impairment in more than one major setting using reports from parents, teachers, other school staff and involved mental-health clinicians, and rule out alternative causes (grade B, strong recommendation) [[Wolraich2019]].
- The AAP states that neuropsychological testing has not been found to improve diagnostic accuracy in most cases, though it can clarify learning strengths and weaknesses [[Wolraich2019]].
- For preschool children (age 4 to the sixth birthday), the AAP advises a parent interview, examining and observing the child, and DSM-based rating scales from parents and teachers. It encourages parents to complete a behaviour-management programme, if available, before a diagnosis is assigned [[Wolraich2019]].
- For adolescents, the AAP advises getting reports from at least 2 teachers or other adults (such as coaches or counsellors). It notes that adolescents tend to minimise their own symptoms, and that onset before age 12 must be established [[Wolraich2019]].
- The AAP warns that in adolescents, substance use (for example cannabis) can mimic ADHD, and that some may feign symptoms to obtain stimulants for performance enhancement [[Wolraich2019]].
- KAS 3: the evaluation should at least screen for co-occurring emotional or behavioural conditions (anxiety, depression, oppositional defiant disorder, conduct disorder, substance use), developmental conditions (learning and language disorders, autism) and physical conditions (tics, sleep apnoea) (grade B, strong recommendation) [[Wolraich2019]].
- At a minimum, adolescents newly diagnosed with ADHD should be assessed for substance use, anxiety, depression and learning disabilities [[Wolraich2019]].
- The AAP lists trauma, posttraumatic stress disorder and toxic stress as further co-occurring conditions and risk factors to consider [[Wolraich2019]].
- In the 2014 US national survey cited by the AAP, the median age at diagnosis was 7 years, about one-third of children were diagnosed before age 6, and over half were first diagnosed by a primary care clinician [[Wolraich2019]].
- The AAP states that boys are more than twice as likely as girls to be diagnosed, possibly because hyperactive behaviour is more visible. Girls with ADHD are more likely than boys to have a co-occurring internalising condition such as anxiety or depression [[Wolraich2019]].

### criteria · Guidelines: NICE NG87 and the European adult consensus
- NICE NG87 (England) was published 14 March 2018 and last updated 13 September 2019 [[NICE2018]].
- NICE recommends against universal screening for ADHD in nursery, primary and secondary schools (recommendation 1.2.3) [[NICE2018]].
- For a child whose problems suggest ADHD and affect development or family life, NICE suggests considering watchful waiting of up to 10 weeks and offering group-based ADHD-focused parent support. Referral to secondary care follows if problems persist with at least moderate impairment (1.2.7) [[NICE2018]].
- NICE states that primary care practitioners should not make the initial diagnosis or start medication in children or young people with suspected ADHD (1.2.9) [[NICE2018]].
- NICE requires that ADHD be diagnosed only by a specialist psychiatrist, paediatrician or other appropriately qualified professional, based on a full clinical and psychosocial assessment, developmental and psychiatric history, observer reports and mental-state assessment (1.3.1) [[NICE2018]].
- NICE states that a diagnosis should not rest on rating scales or observation alone, though scales such as the Conners and the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire are helpful added tools (1.3.2) [[NICE2018]].
- NICE requires at least moderate psychological, social, educational or occupational impairment in 2 or more important settings, and states that exclusion based on a pervasive developmental disorder or an uncertain time of onset is not recommended (1.3.3) [[NICE2018]].
- A 2024 addition to NICE 1.3.1 gives the option of using QbTest (a computerised attention and activity test) to help diagnose ADHD in people aged 6 to 17, as recommended in NICE's HealthTech guidance [[NICE2018]].
- NICE lists groups with higher ADHD prevalence: people born preterm, looked-after children, children with oppositional defiant or conduct disorder, children with mood disorders, close relatives of people with ADHD, people with epilepsy, other neurodevelopmental disorders, acquired brain injury or a history of substance misuse, adults with a mental-health condition, and people in the justice system (1.2.1) [[NICE2018]].
- NICE states that ADHD is thought to be under-recognised in girls and women: they are less likely to be referred, may be more likely to have undiagnosed ADHD, and may be more likely to receive an incorrect diagnosis of another condition (1.2.2) [[NICE2018]].
- For adults without a childhood diagnosis, NICE advises referral to a trained specialist when typical symptoms began in childhood, have persisted throughout life, are not explained by other diagnoses, and cause at least moderate impairment (1.2.10) [[NICE2018]].
- The Updated European Consensus Statement on adult ADHD (63 experts) states that adult ADHD is under-diagnosed and under-treated in many European countries, that screening and diagnostic instruments for adults exist, and that effective treatments are available (Established) [[Kooij2019]].
- A development-process article for the APSARD US guidelines for diagnosing and treating adult ADHD was published in 2023. No final guideline text was found in Europe PMC or Crossref as of this pass [[Goodman2023APSARD]].

### scales · Adult screeners: ASRS v1.1 and ASRS-5
- The WHO Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS) has 18 questions on DSM-IV Criterion A symptoms. Its 6-question screener (Part A) was selected by stepwise logistic regression [[Kessler2005]].
- In the validation sample (154 NCS-R respondents with blind clinical interviews), the 6-question screener outperformed the full 18 questions: sensitivity 68.7% vs 56.3%, specificity 99.5% vs 98.3%, total classification accuracy 97.9% vs 96.2%, kappa 0.76 vs 0.58 [[Kessler2005]].
- Agreement between single ASRS items and clinician ratings varied widely, with Cohen's kappa from 0.16 to 0.81 [[Kessler2005]].
- In 668 US health-plan members, the ASRS screener had an AUC of 0.90 against clinician DSM-IV diagnoses, internal consistency 0.63–0.72, and test-retest correlations 0.58–0.77 [[Kessler2007ASRS]].
- In 104 primary-care attenders who screened negative, the ASRS v1.1 screener had a test-retest Spearman rho of 0.78 and ICC of 0.75 [[Silverstein2018]].
- In 60 adults with ADHD, the 18-item ASRS symptom checklist had Cronbach's alpha 0.88 (self-report) and 0.89 (rater-administered), with an ICC of 0.84 against the clinician-rated ADHD Rating Scale [[Adler2006]].
- The DSM-5 version (ASRS-5) is a 6-question scale built with a machine-learning method (RiskSLIM) in 2 general-population samples, then tested in a clinical sample at NYU Langone (n=300) [[Ustun2017]].
- ASRS-5 in data weighted to an 8.2% population prevalence: sensitivity 91.4%, specificity 96.0%, AUC 0.94, positive predictive value (PPV) 67.3% [[Ustun2017]].
- ASRS-5 in the NYU clinical sample (prevalence 57.7%): sensitivity 91.9%, specificity 74.0%, AUC 0.83, PPV 82.8% [[Ustun2017]].
- Interpretation: a positive screen is a reason for full assessment, not a diagnosis. The ASRS was designed for case-finding in surveys and outreach [[Kessler2005,Ustun2017]].

### scales · Why screeners mislead at low prevalence
- PPV depends on prevalence. The same ASRS-5 gave a PPV of 67.3% at 8.2% weighted prevalence and 82.8% in a clinic where 57.7% had ADHD [[Ustun2017]].
- Worked example (calculated here from the published ASRS-5 sensitivity 91.4% and specificity 96.0%, not a published figure): at 2.5% prevalence, about 37% of positive screens would be true cases. At 8.2% it is about 67%, and at 30% about 91% [[Ustun2017]].
- Community example: in a school-based sample, the Vanderbilt parent scale had sensitivity 0.80, specificity 0.75, PPV 0.19 and NPV 0.98. About 4 in 5 positive parent screens were not ADHD by the study case definition [[Bard2013Parent]].
- Teacher example: in the same community study, the Vanderbilt teacher scale had sensitivity 0.69, specificity 0.84, PPV 0.32 and NPV 0.96. The authors say this low predictive validity shows the need for multiple observers [[Wolraich2013Teacher]].
- High NPV means a negative community screen is fairly reassuring, but a positive screen needs a full clinical assessment [[Bard2013Parent,Wolraich2013Teacher]].

### scales · Child and adolescent rating scales
- The Vanderbilt ADHD Diagnostic Parent Rating Scale (VADPRS) collects DSM-based symptom data. It also screens oppositional/conduct and anxiety/depression problems, and was designed to shorten lengthy interviews [[Wolraich2003]].
- VADPRS in a referred sample (N=243): internal consistency and factor structure were acceptable and matched DSM-IV [[Wolraich2003]].
- VADPRS in a community sample (587 parents): 4-factor structure (inattention, hyperactivity, conduct/oppositional, anxiety/depression), coefficient alpha 0.91–0.94, test-retest above 0.80 for all summed scales [[Bard2013Parent]].
- Vanderbilt teacher scale (VADTRS; 35 items, 601 teachers): KR20 0.85–0.94 and correlations above 0.72 with the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire [[Wolraich2013Teacher]].
- SNAP-IV in a school-district sample (1,613 parents, 1,205 teachers): two ADHD factors plus an ODD factor. A parent item-mean score above 1.2 raised the probability of concern (likelihood ratio above 10), and above 1.8 raised the probability of ADHD diagnosis (likelihood ratio above 3) [[Bussing2008]].
- SNAP-IV scores differed by child sex and poverty status (d = 0.49–0.56). Teacher scores also differed by race (d = 0.25–0.55), which the authors flag for further study [[Bussing2008]].
- SNAP-IV in 250 referred children aged 6–17 showed high sensitivity but low specificity against clinician diagnosis. The authors conclude it works best as a screening and outcome tool, not a diagnostic one [[Hall2020SNAP]].
- In clinic-referred children aged 6–11, the Conners 3 Parent Short Form and the CBCL attention scale supported identifying ADHD when tested against a structured interview (ROC analysis) [[Gomez2021Conners]].
- Parent and teacher ratings agree only weakly to moderately. In 6,659 children aged 4–17, agreement was weak for inattention and moderate-to-strong for hyperactivity-impulsivity, and it did not change with age. Each informant adds valid information [[Narad2015]].
- Parents rated ADHD symptoms as more severe than teachers did. Both reported more hyperactivity-impulsivity in younger children and similar inattention across ages [[Narad2015]].
- The female ADHD consensus notes that most rating-scale norms come from male or mixed samples, which may disadvantage girls. It recommends female norms where available and otherwise more weight on collateral information [[Young2020Females]].

### scales · Interviews, history and collateral information
- The DIVA 2.0 semi-structured adult interview agreed 100% with CAADID diagnoses in 40 outpatients. It correlated r = 0.544 with the Wender Utah Rating Scale, 0.720 with the ADHD Rating Scale and 0.674 with the Sheehan Disability Inventory [[RamosQuiroga2019]].
- DIVA 2.0 is described as the only adult ADHD interview offered free online for clinical and research use [[RamosQuiroga2019]].
- The Korean DIVA-5 (DSM-5 version; 279 participants, 9 clinics) had diagnostic accuracy 92%, sensitivity 91.30% and specificity 93.62% against psychiatrist diagnosis [[Hong2020DIVA5]].
- Reporting source changes results a lot. In hyperactive children followed to age 19–25, ADHD persisted in 5% by self-report vs 46% by parent report. With a developmentally referenced cut-off (+2 SD), it was 12% vs 66% [[Barkley2002]].
- In the MTA follow-up (476 adults from the ADHD group), persistence was higher with parent reports than self-reports, and with interviews than rating scales. Combining parent and self-report with an "or" rule and a norm-based threshold of 4 symptoms gave 60% symptom persistence and 41% symptom-plus-impairment persistence [[Sibley2017Persistence]].
- For adults, the female ADHD consensus advises collateral history from a parent or carer, or from someone who has known the person well for 5 years or more, plus school, college or employment reports where available [[Young2020Females]].
- ICD-11 advises getting teacher and parent reports for children and adolescents, and reports from a significant other, family member or co-worker for adults [[WHO-ICD11]].
- Late-onset presentations need careful assessment. In the MTA comparison group (239 people without childhood ADHD, assessed 8 times from about age 9.9 to 24.4), about 95% of those who first screened positive on checklists were excluded from a late-onset ADHD diagnosis [[Sibley2018LateOnset]].
- The most common reason for excluding late-onset cases was symptoms or impairment occurring only during heavy substance use. There was no evidence of adult-onset ADHD independent of a complex psychiatric history [[Sibley2018LateOnset]].
- Feigned ADHD is hard to detect. A review of 19 studies in college students found most self-report questionnaires insensitive to malingering, and simulators could produce plausible profiles. Failing 3 or more symptom validity tests was the most useful sign [[Musso2014]].

### scales · Objective tests and biomarkers (not diagnostic alone)
- Neuropsychological and imaging differences between groups with and without ADHD are typically small and overlap with other disorders, so they are not useful for diagnosis (Established) [[Faraone2021]].
- Continuous performance tests (CPTs), 19 studies: pooled sensitivity 0.75 (95% CI 0.66–0.82) and specificity 0.71 (0.62–0.78) for the total/ADHD score. Omission scores gave 0.63 and 0.74, and commission scores 0.59 and 0.66. AUCs were "barely acceptable" (0.7–0.8) [[Arrondo2024]].
- QbTest, 15 studies, 2,058 participants: total-score sensitivity 0.78 (0.69–0.85), specificity 0.70 (0.57–0.81), pooled AUC 0.72. The authors say it should not be used as a stand-alone screening, diagnostic or triage tool, but as an adjunct it can shorten the time to diagnosis [[Bellato2024QbTest]].
- EEG theta/beta ratio: a meta-analysis of 9 studies (1,253 with ADHD, 517 without) found effect sizes of 0.75 at ages 6–13 and 0.62 at ages 6–18 with significant heterogeneity, and concluded it is not a reliable diagnostic measure [[Arns2013]].

### differential · Co-occurrence in children
- In the 2022 US National Survey of Children's Health, 77.9% of children aged 3–17 with current ADHD had at least one co-occurring disorder (parent report) [[Danielson2024]].
- In the 2007 US survey of 61,779 children aged 6–17, children with ADHD vs without had: learning disability 46% vs 5%, conduct disorder 27% vs 2%, anxiety 18% vs 2%, depression 14% vs 1%, speech problems 12% vs 3% [[Larson2011]].
- In the same survey, 33% of children with ADHD had 1 co-occurring condition, 16% had 2, and 18% had 3 or more. Poor children were 3.8 times as likely as affluent children to have 3 or more (30% vs 8%) [[Larson2011]].
- Depression in children and adolescents with ADHD (24 studies, no intellectual disability): pooled rate 11.31% (range across studies 1.7%–60%), higher in girls than boys [[Wang2025Depression]].
- Autism traits: 21% of children and adolescents with ADHD met the threshold for autism (22 publications, N = 61,985). The figure was 19% in community samples and 24% in clinical samples, a non-significant difference [[Hollingdale2020]].
- Children with both ADHD and motor coordination problems are especially likely to have autism, and children with both conditions may respond less well to standard ADHD treatment or have more side effects [[Reiersen2008]].
- The WFADHD consensus lists depression, bipolar disorder, autism, anxiety, oppositional defiant disorder, conduct disorder, eating disorders and substance use disorders as common co-occurring conditions, and states that their presence does not rule out ADHD [[Faraone2021]].

### differential · Co-occurrence in adults
- In the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication (ages 18–44), estimated current adult ADHD prevalence was 4.4%. ADHD was highly comorbid with other DSM-IV disorders, and most cases were untreated for ADHD (population estimate) [[Kessler2006]].
- A systematic review of 32 adult studies found substance use disorder to be the most frequent co-occurring disorder, followed by mood, anxiety and personality disorders [[Choi2022AdultComorbidity]].
- Ranges in adults with ADHD from that review: any depressive disorder 8.6%–55% in general-population samples vs 1.2%–12.5% without ADHD. Any anxiety disorder 4.3%–47.1% vs 0.5%–9.5%. Any substance use disorder 2.3%–41.2% vs 0%–16.6% [[Choi2022AdultComorbidity]].
- Bipolar disorder: in 71 studies (646,766 participants), 7.95% (95% CI 5.31–11.06) of adults with ADHD also had bipolar disorder, and 17.11% (13.05–21.59) of adults with bipolar disorder had ADHD [[Schiweck2021]].
- Bipolar disorder began 3.96 years earlier (95% CI 2.65–5.26) in people who also had ADHD [[Schiweck2021]].
- Substance use disorder: 23.1% (95% CI 19.4–27.2) of people treated for SUD met DSM criteria for ADHD (29 studies) [[vanEmmerikvanOortmerssen2012]]. A 2023 meta-analysis of 31 studies estimated 21% [[Rohner2023]].
- When one standard method was used across 10 countries (3,558 SUD patients screened, 1,276 interviewed), adult ADHD prevalence ranged from 5.4% to 31.3% (DSM-IV) and 7.6% to 32.6% (DSM-5), compared with 2%–83% in earlier studies [[vandeGlind2014]].
- Anxiety: in 58 case-control studies (N = 18,821), adults with ADHD scored higher on anxiety measures (Hedges' g 0.77). The difference was larger against non-clinical controls (g 0.954) than against clinical controls (g 0.437) [[Hoskins2026Anxiety]].

### differential · Autism
- Since DSM-5, ADHD and autism can be diagnosed together. DSM-IV did not allow both [[Epstein2013,Reiersen2008]].
- In autism, the pooled prevalence of co-occurring ADHD is 28% (95% CI 25–32), the most common co-occurring mental-health diagnosis in a meta-analysis of 96 studies [[Lai2019Autism]].
- Other pooled co-occurring rates in autism: anxiety 20%, sleep-wake disorders 13%, disruptive/impulse-control/conduct 12%, depressive 11%, obsessive-compulsive 9%, bipolar 5%, schizophrenia spectrum 4% [[Lai2019Autism]].
- Children with ADHD have substantially more autism traits than those without ADHD (d = 1.23) [[Hollingdale2020]].
- NICE states that exclusion based on a pervasive developmental disorder is not recommended when diagnosing ADHD [[NICE2018]].

### differential · Mood, bipolar and trauma
- DSM-5-TR differential: bipolar disorder is episodic, with increased activity tied to elevated mood and grandiosity during discrete episodes. ADHD is a persistent, trait-like pattern from childhood [[APA2022DSM]].
- The female ADHD consensus advises judging whether a problem is trait-like or episodic when deciding between ADHD and co-occurring mood conditions [[Young2020Females]].
- When adults have both ADHD and bipolar disorder, a narrative review recommends stabilising mood before starting ADHD medication [[MacDonald2023]].
- PTSD and ADHD are linked in both directions. The relative risk of PTSD in ADHD was 2.9 overall, 3.7 against healthy controls and 1.6 against traumatised controls. The relative risk of ADHD in PTSD was 1.7, or 2.1 against traumatised controls [[Spencer2016PTSD]].
- The PTSD–ADHD association was not significant in samples using psychiatric controls [[Spencer2016PTSD]].

### differential · Learning, language and other neurodevelopmental conditions
- Learning disability was the most common co-occurring condition reported by US parents of children with ADHD (46% vs 5% without ADHD) [[Larson2011]].
- The AAP notes that learning and language problems commonly co-occur with ADHD and recommends screening for them [[Wolraich2019]].
- The female ADHD consensus warns that girls may be diagnosed with a specific learning difficulty such as dyslexia when ADHD is the better explanation. It recommends screening students with suspected learning difficulties for ADHD [[Young2020Females]].
- Children with ADHD were significantly more likely than controls to have amblyopia (almost 90% higher risk), astigmatism (over 80% higher) and heterotropia (twice the risk) in a Taiwanese national dataset [[Faraone2021]].

### differential · Sleep-disordered breathing and sleep
- Children with ADHD (722 vs 638 controls, 16 studies) had more bedtime resistance, sleep-onset difficulty, night waking, morning-waking difficulty, sleep-disordered breathing and daytime sleepiness. On objective measures they had a higher apnoea-hypopnoea index and lower sleep efficiency [[Cortese2009Sleep]].
- A 2026 meta-analysis of 11 studies (903 children) estimated the pooled prevalence of obstructive sleep apnoea in children with ADHD at 44%, with very wide uncertainty [[Leow2026]].
- CHAT trial: 464 children aged 5–9 with obstructive sleep apnoea were randomised to early adenotonsillectomy or watchful waiting [[Marcus2013CHAT,NCT00560859]].
- At 7 months in CHAT, the primary attention/executive-function score did not differ significantly (mean improvement 7.1 vs 5.1 points; P = 0.16). Behaviour, quality of life, symptoms and polysomnography improved more after surgery [[Marcus2013CHAT]].
- Polysomnography normalised in 79% after adenotonsillectomy vs 46% with watchful waiting [[Marcus2013CHAT]].
- In adults with ADHD (13 studies), 7 of 9 subjective sleep measures differed from controls (SMD 0.56–1.55). Actigraphy showed longer sleep-onset latency (SMD 0.80) and lower sleep efficiency (SMD −0.68), but polysomnography showed no significant differences [[DiazRoman2018]].

### differential · Medical rule-outs and look-alikes
- Childhood absence epilepsy: in a population-based Minnesota cohort, ADHD was diagnosed in 41.5% of 41 children with absence epilepsy vs 7.3% of 164 matched controls (5.7-fold). No child had seizures worsen with ADHD treatment [[Vanderwiel2024]].
- Staring spells can reflect absence seizures, ADHD or both. A published case shows hearing loss, absence epilepsy and ADHD confounding each other in one child [[LanceShapiro2013]].
- Thyroid: in families with generalised resistance to thyroid hormone (a rare thyroid-receptor-beta gene disorder), 70% of affected children vs 20% of unaffected relatives met ADHD criteria, and 50% vs 7% of adults had met criteria in childhood [[Hauser1993]].
- ICD-11 lists thyroid replacement medication and bronchodilators as examples of medicines whose central nervous system effects can explain attention or activity symptoms [[WHO-ICD11]].
- Lead: across 33 studies (10,232 children), lead exposure correlated with inattention (r = 0.16) and hyperactivity-impulsivity (r = 0.13), small-to-medium associations similar in size to lead's link with lower IQ [[Goodlad2013]].
- Substances: the AAP notes that cannabis can mimic ADHD in adolescents, and the most common reason for excluding late-onset adult ADHD was symptoms occurring only during heavy substance use [[Wolraich2019,Sibley2018LateOnset]].
- Rare causes: extreme early deprivation, a single-gene condition or early traumatic brain injury can cause ADHD-like symptoms [[Faraone2021]].

### differential · Relative age in class
- Across 25 studies with over 8 million participants, children who are young for their school year are more likely to be diagnosed with ADHD [[Faraone2021]].
- In countries with higher prescribing rates, the youngest in class were more likely to receive ADHD medication (risk ratio 1.27, 95% CI 1.19–1.35). 16 of 20 studies found a relative-age effect [[Holland2019]].
- In an individual-participant meta-analysis (41 studies, 4,708 children, 15 countries, follow-up 4–33 years), younger relative age did not predict whether ADHD persisted (OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.99–1.06) [[SIMBA2023]].

### women · Sex ratio and under-recognition
- In youth, parent and teacher ratings show a roughly 2:1 male-to-female ratio (29 parent-rating studies, over 42,000 participants; 24 teacher-rating studies, over 56,000) [[Faraone2021]].
- Clinic referrals of boys exceed those of girls, with ratios from 3:1 to 16:1. Community samples show a smaller gap of about 3:1. The female ADHD consensus says this gap suggests many girls stay unidentified [[Young2020Females]].
- In a US community sample of 9,380 people aged 7–29, current DSM-IV-like ADHD prevalence was 9.2% with a male-to-female ratio of 2.28:1. The authors suggest that lower ratios than in clinics mean females are under-diagnosed [[Ramtekkar2010]].
- Girls meet criteria at just under half the rate of boys, and the ratio becomes much closer to equal by adulthood [[Hinshaw2022Females]].
- In the Dunedin cohort, childhood ADHD was mostly male, while adult ADHD (prevalence 3%) was gender-balanced [[Moffitt2015]].
- Swedish register data (85,330 people with ADHD): females were first recorded with ADHD at a mean age of 23.5 years vs 19.6 for males, about 4 years later (95% CI of difference 3.74–4.11) [[Skoglund2024]].
- Before their ADHD was recorded, females had more psychiatric comorbidity, medication use and healthcare use than males with ADHD and than female controls [[Skoglund2024]].
- Early meta-analysis (18 studies): compared with boys with ADHD, girls showed lower hyperactivity, fewer other externalising behaviours and greater intellectual impairment. Some differences depended on whether samples were clinic-referred [[Gaub1997]].

### women · Presentation and masking
- Girls and women with ADHD show mainly inattention and internalising problems. Boys and men show more hyperactive-impulsive symptoms and externalising problems [[Hinshaw2022Females]].
- Clinicians may overlook symptoms in females because they are less overt and because females often adopt compensatory strategies [[Hinshaw2022Females,Young2020Females]].
- Inattention in girls and women may look like being easily distracted, disorganised, overwhelmed, or "lacking effort or motivation" [[Young2020Females]].
- Some girls and women camouflage their difficulties with compensatory strategies such as compliance, socially adaptive behaviour or over-preparation, which can lead to underestimation of their problems [[Young2020Females]].
- Symptoms in females may become more obvious at transitions (for example to secondary school or university), when demands rise and support falls [[Young2020Females]].
- Females with ADHD are more likely to first receive a diagnosis of an internalising or personality disorder, which can delay recognition of ADHD [[Young2020Females,NICE2018]].
- In 283 children aged 7–12 with high symptom scores, emotional problems separated diagnosed from undiagnosed girls more than boys. Parents under-rated diagnosed girls' hyperactive-impulsive symptoms compared with an objective interview, and over-rated boys' [[Mowlem2019]].
- The authors suggest girls' ADHD may need additional behavioural or emotional problems to be "made more prominent" before girls meet full criteria [[Mowlem2019]].
- In teacher vignette studies summarised by the consensus, changing only the child's name and pronouns from female to male made referral for support more likely [[Young2020Females]].
- In a clinical sample of medication-naive children, self-reported anxiety was the variable that best distinguished girls with ADHD from control girls. Rule-breaking (parent rating) best distinguished boys [[Skogli2013]].
- Females with ADHD have heightened risks of problems in close relationships, self-harm, intimate partner violence, unplanned pregnancy and co-occurring psychopathology [[Hinshaw2022Females]].

### women · Assessment adjustments
- Use female norms on rating scales where they exist. Interpret cut-offs cautiously, since rigid use can produce both false positives and false negatives [[Young2020Females]].
- Ask about compensatory strategies, emotional dysregulation, self-harm and eating patterns, and explore co-occurring autism, tics, mood, anxiety and eating disorders [[Young2020Females]].
- Consider whether problems are trait-like (more typical of ADHD) or episodic, and what is primary versus secondary to ADHD [[Young2020Females]].
- Hormonal transitions (puberty, the postpartum period, menopause) may change symptoms. The consensus suggests women track symptoms over these periods (Early evidence) [[Young2020Females]].
- Women with ADHD may present to services themselves in adulthood after becoming aware of their difficulties [[Young2020Females]].

### Exam hooks
- DSM-5-TR ADHD has 18 symptoms: 9 inattentive and 9 hyperactive-impulsive [[APA2022DSM]].
- Threshold: ≥6 symptoms in a domain under age 17, ≥5 at age 17 and over, for ≥6 months [[APA2022DSM,Posner2020]].
- Onset criterion: several symptoms before age 12 (DSM-IV required impairing symptoms before age 7) [[Epstein2013]].
- Pervasiveness: several symptoms in ≥2 settings [[APA2022DSM]].
- DSM-5 allowed ADHD and autism to be diagnosed together for the first time [[Epstein2013]].
- DSM-5 "presentations" replaced DSM-IV "subtypes" because profiles change over time [[Epstein2013]].
- Presentation codes: combined F90.2, inattentive F90.0, hyperactive-impulsive F90.1 (DSM-5-TR). ICD-11: 6A05.2, 6A05.0, 6A05.1 [[APA2022DSM,WHO-ICD11]].
- ICD-11 sets no fixed symptom count, unlike DSM-5 [[WHO-ICD11,Posner2020]].
- AAP: evaluate for ADHD from age 4 to the 18th birthday [[Wolraich2019]].
- NICE: no universal school screening, and no diagnosis from rating scales alone [[NICE2018]].
- ASRS v1.1 six-item screener: sensitivity 68.7%, specificity 99.5% in the validation sample [[Kessler2005]].
- A low-prevalence screen has low PPV: Vanderbilt parent scale PPV 0.19 in a community sample [[Bard2013Parent]].
- Neuropsychological tests, EEG and imaging are not diagnostic for ADHD [[Faraone2021,Arns2013]].
- About 1 in 4 people in SUD treatment have ADHD (23.1%) [[vanEmmerikvanOortmerssen2012]].
- About 28% of autistic people have co-occurring ADHD [[Lai2019Autism]].
- Bipolar disorder is episodic; ADHD is persistent from childhood [[APA2022DSM]].
- CHAT: adenotonsillectomy improved behaviour and polysomnography but not the primary attention score at 7 months [[Marcus2013CHAT]].
- Clinic sex ratio up to 16:1, community about 2–3:1 [[Young2020Females,Ramtekkar2010]].
- Females are diagnosed about 4 years later than males (Swedish register) [[Skoglund2024]].

### In plain words
- ADHD is diagnosed by a trained clinician who talks with you and your family. No single blood test, brain scan or computer test can diagnose it [[Faraone2021]].
- The clinician looks for long-lasting problems with attention and/or restlessness and impulsivity, starting in childhood, showing up in more than one place, and making daily life harder [[APA2022DSM]].
- Questionnaires from parents, teachers or yourself help collect information, but they are not a diagnosis on their own [[NICE2018]].
- A positive online screening quiz does not mean you have ADHD. It means a full assessment may be worth having [[Ustun2017,Bard2013Parent]].
- Many things can look like ADHD, such as poor sleep, seizures, hearing or vision problems, stress, trauma, anxiety, depression or substance use. A good assessment checks for these [[Wolraich2019,Young2020Females]].
- Having ADHD and another condition at the same time is common. Most children with ADHD have at least one other condition [[Danielson2024]].
- Autism and ADHD can be diagnosed together [[Epstein2013]].
- Teachers' and parents' reports often differ, and both are useful [[Narad2015]].
- Adults can be assessed for ADHD. Old school reports and someone who knew you as a child can help [[WHO-ICD11,Young2020Females]].
- Girls and women with ADHD are often missed, because they may be quieter, more inattentive than hyperactive, and good at hiding their struggles [[Young2020Females,Hinshaw2022Females]].
- Women are, on average, diagnosed about 4 years later than men [[Skoglund2024]].
- Being the youngest in a school class can make children more likely to be diagnosed, so age matters when comparing behaviour [[Holland2019]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Late-onset or adult-onset ADHD: in the Dunedin cohort, 90% of adults with ADHD had no childhood ADHD history and showed no childhood neuropsychological deficits or childhood-ADHD polygenic risk (Hypothesis that adult ADHD may differ from the childhood disorder) [[Moffitt2015]]. With repeated multi-informant assessment, about 95% of apparent late-onset cases were excluded and no adult-onset cases without a complex psychiatric history were found [[Sibley2018LateOnset]]. Status: unresolved. Guidelines still require symptoms before age 12.
- Fixed symptom counts vs clinical description: DSM-5 uses 6/5 thresholds [[APA2022DSM]]. ICD-11 describes "several" symptoms with no count. The Lancet seminar says ICD-11 gives no precise age of onset, duration or symptom number [[Posner2020]], while the current ICD-11 diagnostic requirements read for this pass do mention "e.g., at least 6 months" and symptoms before age 12 [[WHO-ICD11]]. Status: the seminar likely reflects an earlier draft. Builders should quote the current WHO text.
- Dimensional vs categorical diagnosis: evidence supports ADHD as the extreme of a continuum, with thresholds that are "somewhat arbitrary" [[Posner2020]]. DSM-5 kept a categorical model and added severity specifiers that may be unreliable [[Epstein2013]]. Status: open. Research frameworks are exploring dimensional and transdiagnostic models.
- Relative age: the youngest in class are more often diagnosed and medicated [[Holland2019,Faraone2021]], yet their diagnoses are not less likely to persist [[SIMBA2023]]. Interpretations: older children may be under-diagnosed, or a label may carry forward. Status: unresolved.
- Objective tests: NICE now allows QbTest as an option at ages 6–17 [[NICE2018]], while a meta-analysis says it should not be used alone and has only moderate accuracy [[Bellato2024QbTest]]. CPTs are similarly modest [[Arrondo2024]]. Status: adjunct role only.
- Sex ratio: is the higher male ratio partly an artefact of referral bias and male-normed tools? Clinic ratios of up to 16:1 vs community ratios of about 2–3:1 [[Young2020Females,Ramtekkar2010]], and near-equal ratios in adults [[Hinshaw2022Females,Moffitt2015]], point to under-recognition of females (Established for under-referral; the size of the true difference is debated).
- Who diagnoses: the AAP supports diagnosis in primary care from age 4 [[Wolraich2019]], while NICE says primary care should not make the initial diagnosis in children [[NICE2018]]. Status: a difference in health-system policy, not in evidence about criteria.
- US adult guideline: the APSARD guideline process was described in 2023 [[Goodman2023APSARD]], but no final text was found in indexed sources by this pass date. Status: pending. Recheck before the companion ships.
