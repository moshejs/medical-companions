# G · Course, comorbidity, outcomes & research toward better treatment

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: outcomes, differential, frontiers.

This pass covers how ADHD changes over the lifespan (persistence, remission, fluctuation), which conditions commonly occur alongside it in children and adults, the population-level outcomes linked to ADHD (injuries, crashes, mortality, criminal convictions, substance use, suicidal behaviour, school, work, teenage parenthood, life expectancy), what register studies say about medication and those outcomes (and the limits of those designs), and the research frontier (centanafadine, solriamfetol, mazindol, digital therapeutics, trigeminal nerve stimulation, EEG aids, neurofeedback, brain stimulation, polygenic scores, pharmacogenomic dosing, long-term trial gaps, and the rise in adult diagnoses and telehealth prescribing).

Access notes:

- Outcome figures (mortality ratios, hazard ratios, life-expectancy gaps) are population statistics from cohorts and registers. They describe groups, not any one person, and most are associations, not proof of cause.

- Solriamfetol's phase 3 FOCUS trial (NCT05972044) is marked completed on ClinicalTrials.gov with no posted results and no PubMed publication found; its outcome is therefore not stated.

## Findings

### outcomes · Persistence, remission and the course into adulthood
- A meta-analysis of controlled follow-up studies found that only about 15% of people diagnosed with ADHD in childhood still met full criteria at age 25, but about 65% did when cases in partial remission (residual, impairing symptoms) were counted [[Faraone2006]].
- The same meta-analysis concluded that estimates of persistence "rely heavily on how one defines persistence", and that whatever the definition, evidence of ADHD lessens with age [[Faraone2006]].
- Published adult persistence rates range from 5% to 75% depending on informant and threshold [[Sibley2017Persistence]].
- In the MTA follow-up, persistence rates were higher with parent reports than self-reports, and with a norm-based threshold of 4 symptoms than with DSM criteria [[Sibley2017Persistence]].
- Combining parent and self-report on rating scales with a norm-based threshold, 60% of the MTA ADHD group showed symptom persistence in adulthood and 41% met both symptom and impairment criteria [[Sibley2017Persistence]].
- In the MTA (N=558 children with ADHD, 8 assessments from 2 to 16 years after baseline, mean age about 25 at the end), about 30% had full remission at some point, but 60% of those then had a recurrence [[Sibley2022]].
- Only 9.1% of MTA participants showed sustained remission (recovery) by the study endpoint, 10.8% showed stable persistence, and 63.8% had a fluctuating course of remission and recurrence [[Sibley2022]].
- The MTA authors state that 90% of children with ADHD in the study continued to have residual symptoms into young adulthood, challenging the idea that about half "outgrow" ADHD [[Sibley2022]].
- In the fluctuating MTA subgroup, people had a mean of 3.58 (SD 1.36) fluctuations over 16 years, with a 6- to 7-symptom within-person swing between peaks and troughs [[Sibley2024]].
- Remission periods in the fluctuating MTA subgroup usually first appeared in adolescence and were linked to higher environmental demands [[Sibley2024]].
- In the MTA, the stable-persistent group (10.8%) was linked to early and lasting mood-disorder risk, adolescent and young-adult substance problems, low medication use and poorer response to childhood treatment [[Sibley2024]].
- The MTA recovery group (9.1%) had very low parental psychopathology, and the partial-remission group (15.6%) had higher rates of co-occurring anxiety [[Sibley2024]].
- At the 16-year MTA follow-up, the ADHD group still differed from local classmates on symptom severity (parent/self average, d=1.11), documenting persistence, with a source discrepancy between parent and self-ratings (d=0.60) [[Swanson2017MTA]].
- In the WHO World Mental Health Surveys (20 surveys, 26,744 respondents), current adult ADHD averaged 2.8%, and 57.0% of people with childhood ADHD still had it as adults [[Fayyad2017]].
- In the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication (adults aged 18-44), the estimated prevalence of current adult ADHD was 4.4%, and most cases were untreated [[Kessler2006]].
- Late-onset debate (Hypothesis): in the Dunedin birth cohort (n=1,037, followed to age 38), childhood ADHD (6%) and adult ADHD (3%) were "virtually nonoverlapping" groups; 90% of adult cases had no history of childhood ADHD [[Moffitt2015]].
- In Dunedin, the adult-ADHD group showed no childhood neuropsychological deficits and no polygenic risk for childhood ADHD [[Moffitt2015]].
- In the 1993 Pelotas birth cohort (Brazil), 8.9% had ADHD at age 11 and 12.2% met young-adult criteria (except age of onset) at 18-19, but only 17.2% of children with ADHD still had it as young adults and only 12.6% of young adults with ADHD had had it as children [[Caye2016]].
- The Pelotas childhood group was 63.9% male while the young-adult group had a female preponderance (39.0% male) [[Caye2016]].

### differential · Co-occurring conditions in children
- In the 2007 US National Survey of Children's Health (61,779 children aged 6-17, 5,028 with ADHD), parents reported a learning disability in 46% of children with ADHD versus 5% without ADHD [[Larson2011]].
- In the same survey, conduct disorder was reported in 27% vs 2%, anxiety in 18% vs 2%, depression in 14% vs 1%, and speech problems in 12% vs 3% (children with vs without ADHD) [[Larson2011]].
- Most children with ADHD had at least one other condition: 33% had one, 16% had two, and 18% had three or more [[Larson2011]].
- Children with ADHD in poor households were 3.8 times more likely than those in affluent households to have three or more co-occurring conditions (30% vs 8%) [[Larson2011]].
- Children with ADHD had higher odds of school problems (OR 5.18, 95% CI 4.47-6.01) and activity restriction (OR 4.14, 3.34-5.15), and functioning worsened stepwise with each added condition [[Larson2011]].
- A review of 17 studies (2001-2011) found a mean ADHD-learning disability co-occurrence rate of 45.1%, possibly higher than earlier estimates because writing disorders were included [[DuPaul2013]].
- In autism, a meta-analysis of 96 studies found a pooled ADHD prevalence of 28% (95% CI 25-32), the most common co-occurring mental health diagnosis in autistic people [[Lai2019Autism]].
- Among 1,374 people with Tourette syndrome, 72.1% met criteria for OCD or ADHD, and 85.7% had at least one psychiatric condition over their lifetime [[Hirschtritt2015]].
- In Tourette syndrome, genetic correlations with mood, anxiety and disruptive behaviour disorders may be accounted for by ADHD (and, for mood, by OCD) [[Hirschtritt2015]].
- Conduct disorder adds its own long-term risks: in a meta-analysis, conduct disorder was linked to failure to finish high school (OR 2.7) and violence (OR 3.5) [[Erskine2016]].

### differential · Co-occurring conditions in adults
- Adult ADHD in the NCS-R was "highly comorbid" with many other DSM-IV disorders and linked to substantial role impairment; many people had sought treatment for the other conditions rather than for ADHD [[Kessler2006]].
- Across the WHO World Mental Health Surveys, adult ADHD was highly comorbid with anxiety, mood, behaviour and substance disorders, and treatment seeking targeted the co-occurring conditions more than ADHD [[Fayyad2017]].
- A systematic review of 32 studies found substance use disorder to be the most frequent co-occurring psychiatric condition in adults with ADHD, followed by mood, anxiety and personality disorders [[Choi2022AdultComorbidity]].
- Among people treated for substance use disorders, 23.1% (95% CI 19.4-27.2%) met criteria for ADHD (29 studies) [[vanEmmerikvanOortmerssen2012]].
- ADHD was less common among people with cocaine dependence than among those with alcohol or opioid dependence, and estimates depended on the interview used [[vanEmmerikvanOortmerssen2012]].
- In prospective studies, children with ADHD were more likely than peers to develop nicotine, alcohol, cannabis, cocaine and other substance use disorders, and this was robust to sex, age and study design [[Lee2011]].
- A 2026 meta-analysis (11 studies, 2,120 adults with ADHD) estimated that 57% (95% CI 42-71) had at least one personality disorder, with extreme heterogeneity (I² >90%) driven by specialist clinic samples [[Adamis2026]].
- The most frequent personality disorders in that meta-analysis were passive-aggressive (25.3%), avoidant (23.1%) and borderline (21.9%) [[Adamis2026]].
- In Kaiser Permanente Northern California adults, an ADHD diagnosis was associated with eating disorder (OR 5.19), bipolar disorder (OR 4.72), depressive disorder (OR 4.12) and anxiety disorder (OR 2.44) [[Chung2019]].
- Childhood ADHD was linked to later depression in a meta-analysis of longitudinal studies (OR 2.3, 95% CI 1.5-3.7) [[Erskine2016]].
- In the MTA at mean age 24.7, emotional outcomes (emotional lability, anxiety and mood disorder) and substance use outcomes were worse only in the symptom-persistent group; the symptom-desistent group did not differ from classmates [[Hechtman2016]].

### outcomes · Mortality and life expectancy
- In a Danish national cohort of 1.92 million people (32,061 with ADHD), the mortality rate was 5.85 per 10,000 person-years with ADHD vs 2.21 without, a fully adjusted mortality rate ratio (MRR) of 2.07 (95% CI 1.70-2.50) [[Dalsgaard2015]].
- In that cohort, accidents were the most common cause of death [[Dalsgaard2015]].
- The Danish MRR was 1.86 (0.93-3.27) for ADHD diagnosed before age 6, 1.58 (1.21-2.03) for ages 6-17, and 4.25 (3.05-5.78) for diagnosis at 18 or older [[Dalsgaard2015]].
- After excluding people with oppositional defiant disorder, conduct disorder and substance use disorder, the Danish MRR was 1.50 (1.11-1.98), and it was higher in girls and women (2.85, 1.56-4.71) than in boys and men (1.27, 0.89-1.76) [[Dalsgaard2015]].
- In a Swedish register cohort of 2.68 million people (86,670 with ADHD), mortality was 11.57 vs 2.16 per 10,000 person-years; the association was stronger in adulthood (HR 4.64, 4.11-5.25) than in childhood (HR 1.41, 0.97-2.04) [[Sun2019]].
- In that Swedish cohort, the mortality hazard rose with the number of co-occurring psychiatric conditions: HR 1.41 (1.01-1.97) for ADHD alone vs 25.22 (19.60-32.46) with four or more conditions [[Sun2019]].
- After adjusting for later-onset psychiatric disorders, ADHD remained associated with death from unintentional injury (HR 2.14, 1.71-2.68) but not significantly with suicide (HR 1.13, 0.88-1.45) [[Sun2019]].
- A meta-analysis of 8 studies (396,488 people with ADHD) found all-cause mortality RR 2.13 (95% CI 1.13-4.02; I² 98%; low confidence) compared with the general population [[CatalaLopez2022]].
- In that meta-analysis, unnatural-cause deaths were raised (RR 2.81, 1.73-4.55) while natural-cause deaths were not significantly raised (RR 1.62, 0.89-2.96) [[CatalaLopez2022]].
- In the MTA follow-up to about age 25, there were 10 deaths in the ADHD group compared with 1 in the classmate comparison group [[Hechtman2016]].
- In UK primary-care data (30,039 adults with diagnosed ADHD, 300,390 matched controls), the estimated life-expectancy reduction was 6.78 years (95% CI 4.50-9.11) for men and 8.64 years (6.55-10.91) for women [[ONions2025]].
- Only about 1 in 9 UK adults with ADHD had a diagnosis in that study, so the results may not generalise to the mostly undiagnosed adult population [[ONions2025]].
- Using a life-expectancy calculator in 131 hyperactive children and 71 controls followed to young adulthood, childhood ADHD (combined type) was linked to an 8.4-year reduction in estimated total life expectancy and 12.7 years when ADHD persisted [[Barkley2019]].
- The Barkley estimate is a modelled projection from risk factors in a small clinical sample, not observed deaths, which is one reason the life-expectancy figures remain debated (see Controversies) [[Barkley2019,ONions2025]].

### outcomes · Injuries and motor-vehicle crashes
- A meta-analysis of 28 studies (350,938 children and adolescents with ADHD) found a pooled OR of 1.53 (95% CI 1.40-1.67) for unintentional injury; pooled HR from 4 studies was 1.39 (1.06-1.83) [[RuizGoikoetxea2018]].
- In that meta-analysis, studies that avoided confounding by indication (self-controlled or difference-in-differences) gave a pooled effect of 0.879 (0.838-0.922) for injuries during ADHD medication [[RuizGoikoetxea2018]].
- In Denmark, children with ADHD had more injuries than other children at age 10 (adjusted OR 1.29, 1.22-1.37) and age 12 (1.30, 1.23-1.37) [[Dalsgaard2015Injury]].
- In the same Danish study, injury prevalence among medicated children with ADHD fell from 19% at age 5 to 14% at age 10, vs about 17% in untreated children with ADHD; the difference-in-differences reduction was 31.5% at age 10 and 43.5% at age 12 [[Dalsgaard2015Injury]].
- Emergency ward visits fell by 28.2% at age 10 and 45.7% at age 12 with medication in the Danish difference-in-differences analysis [[Dalsgaard2015Injury]].
- In a Hong Kong self-controlled case series of 17,381 young people prescribed methylphenidate, trauma-related emergency admissions were lower during treated periods (IRR 0.91, 0.86-0.97), with no association for a negative-control outcome (IRR 0.99) [[Man2015]].
- A meta-analysis of 16 driving studies estimated a relative crash risk of 1.36 (1.18-1.57) for drivers with ADHD, 1.23 (1.04-1.46) after controlling for driving exposure [[Vaa2014]].
- The same meta-analysis rebutted the claim of an "almost fourfold" crash risk, linking higher risk (RR 1.86) to co-occurring oppositional defiant or conduct disorder rather than ADHD alone (RR 1.31, 0.96-1.81) [[Vaa2014]].
- In Sweden (17,408 adults with ADHD, 2006-2009), serious transport accidents were more common in men (adjusted HR 1.47, 1.32-1.63) and women (1.45, 1.24-1.71) with ADHD [[Chang2014Crash]].
- Within the same Swedish men, medication periods were associated with a 58% lower risk of serious transport accidents (HR 0.42, 0.23-0.75); there was no significant association in women [[Chang2014Crash]].
- In US insurance claims (2,319,450 people with ADHD, mean age 32.5), months on ADHD medication had 38% lower odds of emergency visits for crashes in men (OR 0.62, 0.56-0.67) and 42% lower in women (OR 0.58, 0.53-0.62) [[Chang2017Crash]].
- The US study estimated that up to 22.1% of crashes in people with ADHD might have been avoided with medication throughout follow-up (a model estimate) [[Chang2017Crash]].

### outcomes · Criminal convictions and the justice system
- In a meta-analysis of 9 samples (15,442 people with childhood ADHD), childhood ADHD was associated with later arrest (RR 2.2, 1.3-3.5), conviction (RR 3.3, 2.1-5.2) and incarceration (RR 2.9, 1.9-4.3) [[MohrJensen2016]].
- Childhood ADHD was linked to arrest in another meta-analysis (OR 2.4, 1.5-3.8) [[Erskine2016]].
- A 2018 meta-analysis of 102 studies estimated adult ADHD prevalence in detention at 26.2% (95% CI 22.7-29.6) [[Baggio2018]].
- An updated 2024 meta-analysis restricted to 11 unselected prison samples estimated 8.3% (3.8-12.8), about one in 12 [[Fazel2024]].
- A reanalysis of 28 adult studies by the original authors gave 22.2% (15.7-28.6), so prison prevalence estimates range from about 8% to 26% depending on inclusion rules (see Controversies) [[Baggio2024]].
- In Swedish registers (25,656 people with ADHD, 2006-2009), periods on ADHD medication were associated with a 32% lower conviction rate in men (HR 0.68, 0.63-0.73) and 41% lower in women (HR 0.59, 0.50-0.70), compared with the same people's unmedicated periods [[Lichtenstein2012]].
- In men, the Swedish reduction stayed between 17% and 46% across sensitivity analyses, including stimulant vs non-stimulant medicines and type of crime [[Lichtenstein2012]].

### outcomes · Substance use
- In US claims (2,993,887 adolescents and adults with ADHD), medicated months had 35% lower odds of substance-related emergency visits in men (OR 0.65, 0.64-0.67) and 31% lower in women (OR 0.69, 0.67-0.71) [[Quinn2017]].
- Two years after medication periods, odds were still 19% lower in men (OR 0.81, 0.78-0.85) and 14% lower in women (OR 0.86, 0.82-0.91), though long-term findings in women were less consistent [[Quinn2017]].
- In Swedish registers (38,753 people with ADHD), stimulant medication in 2006 was associated with a 31% lower rate of substance abuse in 2009 (HR 0.69, 0.57-0.84), with lower rates for longer treatment [[Chang2014SUD]].
- In the MTA, by mean age 17, neither the randomised childhood treatment, medication at follow-up, nor cumulative stimulant treatment was associated with adolescent substance use or substance use disorder, in either direction [[Molina2013]].
- MTA participants with ADHD had more substance use at every time point than non-ADHD classmates, regardless of sex [[Molina2013]].

### outcomes · Suicidal behaviour
- A meta-analysis of 57 studies found crude ORs of 2.37 (1.64-3.43) for suicide attempts, 3.53 (2.94-4.25) for suicidal ideation, 4.54 (2.46-8.37) for suicide plans and 6.69 (3.24-17.39) for death by suicide with ADHD; adjusted ORs gave similar results [[Septier2019]].
- In Sweden (37,936 people with ADHD, 2006-2009), a naive population-level comparison suggested more suicide-related events during medication (HR 1.31, 1.19-1.44), but the within-person comparison reversed the sign (HR 0.89, 0.79-1.00) [[Chen2014BMJ]].
- Among stimulant users, the within-person rate of suicide-related events was lower during treatment (HR 0.81, 0.70-0.94); for non-stimulant or mixed users there was no significant change (HR 0.96, 0.72-1.30) [[Chen2014BMJ]].

### outcomes · School, work and teenage parenthood
- In a meta-analysis of longitudinal studies, childhood ADHD was associated with failure to complete high school (OR 3.7, 2.0-7.0) and unemployment (OR 2.0, 1.0-3.9) [[Erskine2016]].
- In 657,720 Swedish students leaving compulsory school, ADHD was associated with substantially lower school performance independent of family socioeconomic factors [[Jangmo2019]].
- Three months of ADHD medication was associated with lower odds of not qualifying for upper secondary school (OR 0.80, 0.76-0.84) and a grade-point sum 9.35 points higher (out of 320) [[Jangmo2019]].
- Among 930 Swedish young adults who sat the national university entrance test more than once, scores were a mean of 4.80 points higher (out of 200; 95% CI 2.26-7.34) when they were taking ADHD medication [[Lu2017]].
- In 1.2 million Swedish school leavers followed 6-16 years, people with ADHD had on average 17% lower annual income (ratio 0.83), 12.19 more days of unemployment a year, and higher odds of disability pension (OR 19.0) [[Jangmo2021]].
- Co-occurring intellectual disability and developmental disorders explained most of the disability-pension association, while education only partly explained the income and unemployment gaps [[Jangmo2021]].
- In the MTA at about age 25, post-secondary education, job loss, income, public assistance and risky sexual behaviour followed a gradient: classmates best, symptom-persistent ADHD worst, symptom-desistent ADHD in between [[Hechtman2016]].
- In Danish registers (2.70 million people, 27,479 with ADHD), people with ADHD were more likely to become parents at 12-16 years (IRR females 3.62, males 2.30) and 17-19 years (females 1.94, males 2.27) [[Ostergaard2017]].
- In US adults, an ADHD diagnosis was associated with higher odds of sexually transmitted infections (OR 1.29, 1.25-1.33) and frequent health care use (OR 1.30) [[Chung2019]].

### outcomes · Medication and outcomes: what the register designs show
- In a Swedish target-trial emulation (148,578 people aged 6-64 with a new ADHD diagnosis), starting ADHD medication within 3 months was associated with lower 2-year all-cause mortality: 39.1 vs 48.1 per 10,000 (HR 0.79, 0.70-0.88) [[Li2024JAMA]].
- That benefit was seen for unnatural-cause deaths (25.9 vs 33.3 per 10,000; HR 0.75, 0.66-0.86) but not natural-cause deaths (13.1 vs 14.7 per 10,000; HR 0.86, 0.71-1.05) [[Li2024JAMA]].
- A second Swedish target-trial emulation (148,581 people; 88.4% of starters began methylphenidate) found lower first-event rates for suicidal behaviour (IRR 0.83), substance misuse (0.85), transport accidents (0.88) and criminality (0.87) over 2 years [[Zhang2025BMJ]].
- In that study, the first-event reduction for accidental injuries was not significant (IRR 0.98, 0.96-1.01), but recurrent injuries fell (IRR 0.96, 0.92-0.99) [[Zhang2025BMJ]].
- For recurrent events, the IRRs were 0.85 for suicidal behaviour, 0.75 for substance misuse, 0.84 for transport accidents and 0.75 for criminality [[Zhang2025BMJ]].
- A meta-analysis of large database and registry studies found a protective association of ADHD medication with academic outcomes, accidents and injuries, and mood disorders [[Boland2020]].
- Within-individual (self-controlled) designs compare the same person on and off medication, removing stable confounders such as genes and upbringing; the Swedish suicide study shows why this matters, because the naive estimate (HR 1.31) and within-person estimate (HR 0.89) pointed in opposite directions [[Chen2014BMJ]].
- These designs remain observational: a 2026 meta-analysis of psychotropic medicines and suicide outcomes stressed that such findings "are not causal" and may carry residual confounding [[Kozhevnikova2026]].
- The randomised MTA trial and the observational register studies can disagree: medication did not change adolescent substance outcomes in MTA, while US and Swedish registers found lower substance-related events during medicated periods [[Molina2013,Quinn2017,Chang2014SUD]].

### frontiers · Long-term treatment evidence gaps
- The 14-month MTA trial (579 children aged 7-9.9) found carefully managed medication superior to intensive behavioural treatment and to community care for core ADHD symptoms; combined treatment was not better than medication alone for core symptoms [[MTA1999]].
- At the 16-year MTA follow-up, extended medication use was not associated with lower adult symptom severity in naturalistic subgroups, but consistent or inconsistent use was associated with adult height 2.55 cm shorter than negligible use [[Swanson2017MTA]].
- The largest network meta-analysis of ADHD medicines (133 double-blind RCTs) "did not find sufficient data" for the 26-week and 52-week timepoints [[Cortese2018]].
- In adults, a component network meta-analysis of 113 RCTs (14,887 participants) found stimulants and atomoxetine the only treatments that reduced core symptoms on both self- and clinician-rated scales at about 12 weeks, and noted that longer-term evidence is under-investigated [[Ostinelli2025]].
- An umbrella review of 221 re-estimated meta-analyses found "no high certainty, long term evidence" (52 weeks) for any ADHD intervention [[Gosling2025]].
- In a randomised placebo-controlled discontinuation trial (94 young people on methylphenidate for more than 2 years), stopping led to worsening on the CGI-I in 40.4% vs 15.9% who continued; the ADHD-RS difference was -4.6 points in favour of continuing [[Matthijssen2019]].
- The discontinuation trial authors noted that some individuals can stop without deterioration, supporting periodic review of continued need [[Matthijssen2019]].
- The Lancet seminar notes that the effectiveness of medication has been questioned because it may not meet broader clinical needs over the longer term, and that non-pharmacological approaches have proven less effective than previously thought [[Posner2020]].
- The World Federation of ADHD consensus (208 evidence-based statements endorsed by 80 authors from 27 countries) was compiled to counter misconceptions that stigmatise people with ADHD and delay treatment [[Faraone2021]].

### frontiers · New medicines (pipeline)
- A 2024 review of 90 phase 2/3 RCTs in adults found centanafadine the only pipeline treatment with replicated efficacy on core symptoms (small effect size 0.28-0.40), and no candidate matching stimulants or atomoxetine for efficacy with better tolerability (Established for the pipeline landscape) [[Veronesi2024]].
- Centanafadine inhibits norepinephrine, dopamine and serotonin reuptake (an NDSRI) [[Adler2022]].
- In two adult phase 3 trials (ages 18-55, about 145-149 per arm), centanafadine SR 200 mg/day and 400 mg/day reduced the AISRS score more than placebo at day 42, by -3.16 and -2.74 points (study 1) and -4.01 and -4.47 points (study 2) [[Adler2022,NCT03605680,NCT03605836]].
- Effect sizes versus placebo in the adult trials were -0.24 to -0.40 (small), with low rates of serious adverse events and abuse-potential-related events [[Adler2022]].
- In a 52-week open-label adult safety study (662 enrolled), 61.4% reported at least one adverse event, most commonly insomnia (8.0%) and nausea (7.7%); 12.3% stopped because of adverse events [[Mattingly2025]].
- In adolescents aged 13-17 (n=459), centanafadine 328.8 mg improved ADHD-RS-5 more than placebo at week 6 (-18.50 vs -14.15), while 164.4 mg did not meet the primary endpoint [[Ward2026Adolesc,NCT05257265]].
- Adverse events in the adolescent trial occurred in 31.4% (164.4 mg), 50.3% (328.8 mg) and 23.8% (placebo); decreased appetite, nausea, headache and rash were most common [[Ward2026Adolesc]].
- A phase 3 trial in children aged 6-12 (NCT05428033, 574 enrolled) was completed in 2025; secondary analyses report improvement in executive functioning and learning-problem scales vs placebo [[NCT05428033,Wilens2026]].
- Centanafadine (Simtriyo, Otsuka; NDA 218145) was approved by the FDA as a new molecular entity with an original-approval status date of 24 July 2026 (Established) [[FDA-Simtriyo]].
- A 2026 secondary analysis describes centanafadine as "recently FDA approved" for adult and paediatric patients [[Ward2026Conners]].
- Solriamfetol (a wake-promoting agent) in a 6-week pilot RCT in 60 adults: 45% met the response definition vs 6.9% on placebo, with a week-6 AISRS effect size of 1.09 and no significant change in mean heart rate or blood pressure (Early evidence) [[Surman2023,NCT04839562]].
- The solriamfetol phase 3 adult trial FOCUS (NCT05972044, 516 enrolled, primary endpoint AISRS change at week 6) is completed without posted results; FOCUS-2 (NCT07717879, 468 planned) began recruiting in July 2026 [[NCT05972044,NCT07717879]].
- Mazindol controlled-release in a phase 2 adult trial (n=85, 6 weeks) reduced ADHD-RS-DSM5 by 13.2 points more than placebo at day 42 (effect size 1.09); 42.9% vs 11.9% had at least 50% reduction in target impairment (Early evidence) [[Wigal2018Mazindol,NCT02808104]].
- A 2025 review of phase 3 candidates highlights centanafadine, solriamfetol, CTx-1301 and NRCT-101SR [[Ferahkaya2025]].

### frontiers · Devices and digital therapeutics
- AKL-T01 (EndeavorRx) is a video-game-like attention training played 25 minutes a day, 5 days a week, for 4 weeks [[Kollins2020]].
- In STARS-ADHD (348 unmedicated children aged 8-12), AKL-T01 improved the TOVA Attention Performance Index more than a digital control (mean change 0.93 vs 0.03; median difference 0.88, 95% CI 0.24-1.49) (Early evidence) [[Kollins2020,NCT02674633]].
- STARS-ADHD had no serious adverse events; mild events were frustration (3%) and headache (2%), and children completed a mean 83 of 100 expected sessions [[Kollins2020]].
- The STARS-ADHD primary outcome was a computerised attention test, not parent- or clinician-rated ADHD symptoms [[Kollins2020]].
- FDA granted De Novo DEN200026 to EndeavorRx on 15 June 2020, creating product code QFT, "digital therapeutic software for ADHD", intended as an adjunct to clinician-supervised treatment [[FDA-EndeavorRx]].
- External trigeminal nerve stimulation (eTNS) in a double-blind sham-controlled pilot (62 children aged 8-12, nightly for 4 weeks) improved ADHD-RS (week 4 Cohen d 0.5) and CGI-I (number needed to treat 3) (Early evidence) [[McGough2019,NCT02155608]].
- In that pilot, TNS responders had lower baseline working memory and executive function and lower right-frontal EEG power (Hypothesis for predictors) [[Loo2021]].
- FDA granted De Novo DEN180041 to the Monarch eTNS System on 19 April 2019 (product code QGL, transcutaneous nerve stimulator for ADHD, electrodes on the forehead) [[FDA-MonarchETNS]].
- A larger UCLA efficacy trial of TNS for ADHD (NCT05374187, 218 enrolled) completed in April 2026 without posted results [[NCT05374187]].

### frontiers · EEG biomarkers and neurofeedback
- The NEBA System (theta/beta ratio EEG assessment aid) received FDA De Novo DEN110019 on 15 July 2013, product code NCG, for use only as an assessment aid alongside other valid diagnostic methods [[FDA-NEBA]].
- In the 13-site NEBA validation study (275 children and adolescents), integrating the EEG theta/beta ratio with clinician evaluation was reported to raise diagnostic accuracy from 61% to 88% (sponsor-linked; Early evidence) [[Snyder2015]].
- A meta-analysis of 9 studies found a theta/beta effect size of 0.75 in ages 6-13, but heterogeneity was large and the ADHD-control difference has shrunk over a decade, so the ratio "cannot be considered a reliable diagnostic measure" [[Arns2013]].
- In two multicentre datasets, five EEG processing methods produced different theta/beta ratios, and none distinguished children with and without ADHD [[vanDijk2020]].
- A 2016 meta-analysis of 13 neurofeedback RCTs found benefit only on the least-blinded ratings (SMD 0.35); effects were not significant with probably blinded raters or sham controls [[Cortese2016NF]].
- A 2025 meta-analysis of 38 RCTs (2,472 participants) found no improvement on probably blinded ratings (SMD 0.04, -0.10 to 0.18), with a small effect only for standard protocols (SMD 0.21) [[Westwood2025NF]].
- In a double-blind sham-controlled neurofeedback trial (144 children, theta/beta ratio at least 4.5), both groups improved strongly (d=1.5) but neurofeedback was not superior (d=0.01 at treatment end; responders 61% vs 54%) [[NFCG2021]].
- At 25 months, the same trial still found no difference on parent-rated inattention, with response rates 58.6% (neurofeedback) vs 66% (control) [[NFCG2023]].

### frontiers · Brain stimulation (TMS, tDCS)
- A 2021 meta-analysis of 18 rTMS and tDCS studies (311 participants) found trend-level gains in inhibition and processing speed but little evidence of clinical improvement, and did not support these as alternatives yet (Early evidence) [[Westwood2021NIBS]].
- The largest sham-controlled tDCS trial in adolescent boys (n=50, 15 sessions over the right inferior frontal cortex plus cognitive training) found no symptom or cognitive benefit; ADHD-RS was lower after sham [[Westwood2023tDCS]].
- In adults, tDCS beat placebo only on clinician-rated scales (SMD -0.78), not self-ratings, in a component network meta-analysis based on few small trials [[Ostinelli2025]].

### frontiers · Genetics, polygenic scores and precision dosing
- Twin, family and adoption studies give ADHD a heritability of about 74%, and about a third of that heritability is attributed to many common variants of small effect [[Faraone2019]].
- The 2023 GWAS meta-analysis (38,691 people with ADHD, 186,843 controls) found 27 genome-wide significant loci and estimated that 84-98% of ADHD-influencing variants are shared with other psychiatric disorders [[Demontis2023]].
- In 576 adults with ADHD and 530 controls, adding an ADHD polygenic score to the ASRS raised variance explained by only 0.58 percentage points (61.11% to 61.69%), so the score is "currently not a clinically useful diagnostic aid" (Hypothesis for future clinical use) [[Hoberg2024]].
- The CPIC guideline gives atomoxetine recommendations based on CYP2D6 genotype, because CYP2D6 variants change atomoxetine exposure, efficacy and safety (Established) [[Brown2019CPIC]].
- The US Strattera label states that atomoxetine AUC is about 10-fold higher and peak concentration about 5-fold higher in CYP2D6 poor metabolizers, and that poor metabolizers are about 7% of White and 2% of Asian and Black populations [[FDA-Strattera]].
- The label recommends a longer titration interval (4 weeks) in CYP2D6 poor metabolizers or with strong CYP2D6 inhibitors, quoted as published and not as instructions [[FDA-Strattera]].

### frontiers · Rising adult diagnoses, telehealth and the over/under-diagnosis debate
- In Kaiser Permanente Northern California (5.28 million adults), adult ADHD diagnosis prevalence rose from 0.43% in 2007 to 0.96% in 2016, and incidence rose from 9.43 to 13.49 per 10,000 person-years [[Chung2019]].
- Over the same years, prevalence in children aged 5-11 rose from 2.96% to 3.74% [[Chung2019]].
- Adult prevalence rose in every racial and ethnic group, but white adults had the highest rates (0.67%-1.42%) and Asian American adults the lowest (0.11%-0.35%), suggesting lower detection in minority groups [[Chung2019]].
- In a 2023 US national survey, an estimated 15.5 million adults (6.0%) reported a current ADHD diagnosis, about half diagnosed at age 18 or older [[Staley2024]].
- In that survey, about one third of adults with ADHD had taken a stimulant in the past year, 71.5% of whom had trouble filling a prescription because of unavailability, and about half had ever used telehealth for ADHD care [[Staley2024]].
- In a Northeastern US health system (7,944 people aged 12+ starting stimulants, 2020-2023), a purely telehealth relationship did not change later substance use disorder risk (aOR 0.85, 0.60-1.20), but an initial prescription at a telehealth visit was linked to higher stimulant use disorder risk (aOR 6.18, 1.34-28.46; wide CI, needs replication) [[Rao2025]].
- A scoping review of 334 studies found evidence of ADHD overdiagnosis and overtreatment in children and adolescents, with extra cases tending toward milder symptoms [[Kazda2021]].
- Only 5 of those 334 studies examined benefits and harms in the added milder cases, and these suggested diminishing returns [[Kazda2021]].
- In US states with a 1 September school-entry cutoff, August-born children had a higher ADHD diagnosis rate than September-born children (85.1 vs 63.6 per 10,000), a relative-age effect [[Layton2018]].
- A narrative review argues that rising prevalence reflects both broader criteria and greater awareness, including in women and under-represented groups, and that disparities in detection persist [[Abdelnour2022]].
- In the Danish cohort, girls and women with ADHD (after excluding major comorbidity) had a higher mortality rate ratio than boys and men (2.85 vs 1.27), and in UK data women had the larger life-expectancy gap (8.64 vs 6.78 years) [[Dalsgaard2015,ONions2025]].

### Exam hooks
- Persistence depends on definition: about 15% full syndrome vs about 65% including partial remission at age 25 [[Faraone2006]].
- MTA course: 9.1% recovery, 10.8% stable persistence, 63.8% fluctuating [[Sibley2022]].
- Danish ADHD mortality rate ratio 2.07; accidents are the leading cause of death [[Dalsgaard2015]].
- Mortality rate ratio is highest for ADHD diagnosed in adulthood (4.25) [[Dalsgaard2015]].
- Mortality risk climbs with the number of co-occurring psychiatric disorders (HR 1.41 for ADHD alone vs 25.22 with four or more) [[Sun2019]].
- About 1 in 4 people in substance use disorder treatment has ADHD (23.1%) [[vanEmmerikvanOortmerssen2012]].
- About 28% of autistic people have co-occurring ADHD [[Lai2019Autism]].
- Learning disability is the most common parent-reported co-occurring condition in US children with ADHD (46%) [[Larson2011]].
- Medication periods: 32% fewer convictions in men and 41% in women (Swedish within-person design) [[Lichtenstein2012]].
- Medicated months: 38% (men) and 42% (women) lower odds of crash-related emergency visits [[Chang2017Crash]].
- Medicated months: 35% (men) and 31% (women) lower odds of substance-related emergency visits [[Quinn2017]].
- Within-person designs can reverse a naive association (suicidal events HR 1.31 between-person vs 0.89 within-person) [[Chen2014BMJ]].
- Starting ADHD medication was associated with lower 2-year all-cause mortality (HR 0.79), driven by unnatural causes [[Li2024JAMA]].
- Theta/beta ratio is not a reliable diagnostic test for ADHD [[Arns2013,vanDijk2020]].
- Blinded neurofeedback trials show no specific benefit over sham [[NFCG2021,Westwood2025NF]].
- EndeavorRx (2020) is an FDA-authorised digital therapeutic for ADHD; Monarch eTNS (2019) is an FDA-authorised forehead nerve stimulator [[FDA-EndeavorRx,FDA-MonarchETNS]].
- Centanafadine is a norepinephrine-dopamine-serotonin reuptake inhibitor with small effect sizes (0.24-0.40) in adult phase 3 trials [[Adler2022]].
- CYP2D6 poor metabolizers have about 10-fold higher atomoxetine exposure [[FDA-Strattera]].
- No ADHD treatment has high-certainty evidence at 52 weeks [[Gosling2025]].
- Relative-age effect: the youngest children in a school year are more often diagnosed [[Layton2018]].

### In plain words
- ADHD often gets milder with age, but for most people some symptoms stay into adult life.
- Many people have ups and downs: symptoms can ease for a while and come back when life gets more demanding.
- Most children with ADHD also have at least one other condition, such as a learning difficulty, anxiety or behaviour problems. Checking for these matters.
- Adults with ADHD often also live with depression, anxiety or substance problems, and these are sometimes treated while the ADHD is missed.
- On average, people with ADHD have more accidents and injuries. Most of the extra risk of early death comes from accidents, not illness.
- These numbers describe large groups. They do not predict what will happen to any one person.
- In big studies, the same people had fewer crashes, injuries, convictions and substance problems during times when they were taking ADHD medicine.
- Those studies are strong but not perfect. Long, randomised trials of ADHD medicine are still rare.
- ADHD medicine does not seem to raise the risk of later drug problems.
- New treatments are being studied. A new medicine, centanafadine, was approved in the US in 2026, and a video-game therapy and a forehead nerve stimulator have FDA authorisation.
- Brain-wave training (neurofeedback) and brain-scan or gene tests are not proven ways to diagnose or treat ADHD yet.
- More adults are being diagnosed now. Some experts worry about overdiagnosis, others about people who are still missed, especially women and minority groups.
- Online questionnaires can help start a conversation, but only a full assessment by a trained clinician can diagnose ADHD.

## Open questions and controversies
- How often does ADHD persist? Strict full-syndrome definitions give about 15% at 25 [[Faraone2006]]; including partial remission gives about 65% [[Faraone2006]]; MTA long-term data show most people fluctuate and only 9.1% sustain remission [[Sibley2022]]; published rates span 5-75% by informant and threshold [[Sibley2017Persistence]]. Status: the "half outgrow it" message is being replaced by a fluctuating-course model.
- Is there a late-onset adult ADHD? Dunedin and Pelotas found adult and childhood cases largely did not overlap (Hypothesis) [[Moffitt2015,Caye2016]]; DSM still requires several symptoms before age 12, and other passes cover the diagnostic side. Status: unresolved; may partly reflect measurement and co-occurring conditions.
- How much shorter is life expectancy? UK observed mortality gives 6.78-8.64 years for diagnosed adults, but may not generalise to the undiagnosed majority [[ONions2025]]; a modelled estimate from a small clinical cohort gave 8.4-12.7 years [[Barkley2019]]; meta-analytic mortality RR 2.13 was rated low confidence with I² 98% [[CatalaLopez2022]]. Status: excess mortality is consistent, the size of the gap is not.
- Is the excess mortality due to ADHD itself or to co-occurring conditions? Risk remains raised after excluding ODD, CD and SUD (MRR 1.50) [[Dalsgaard2015]]; but the HR for ADHD alone is 1.41 and climbs to 25.22 with four or more conditions, and natural-cause risk in adults is largely explained by early-onset comorbidity [[Sun2019]]. Status: both matter; comorbidity is a major driver.
- How strong is the medication-outcome evidence? Within-person and target-trial register studies consistently show lower crash, injury, conviction, substance and suicidal-behaviour rates during treatment [[Lichtenstein2012,Chang2017Crash,Quinn2017,Zhang2025BMJ]]; these remain observational, open to time-varying confounding, and "not causal" in the strict sense [[Kozhevnikova2026]]; the randomised MTA follow-up found no long-term medication effect on adolescent substance use or adult symptom severity [[Molina2013,Swanson2017MTA]]. Status: association is robust; causal size uncertain.
- How common is ADHD in prison? 26.2% (2018 meta-analysis) [[Baggio2018]] vs 8.3% in unselected random samples [[Fazel2024]] vs 22.2% on reanalysis [[Baggio2024]]. Status: active methodological dispute about inclusion criteria.
- How large is the crash risk itself? Early claims of near-fourfold risk were driven by comorbid conduct problems; exposure-adjusted RR is 1.23 [[Vaa2014]]; Swedish registers give HR about 1.45-1.47 for serious transport accidents [[Chang2014Crash]]. Status: modest raised risk; comorbidity and driving exposure matter.
- Over- or under-diagnosis? A scoping review found evidence of overdiagnosis and overtreatment of milder cases in youth [[Kazda2021]]; relative-age effects suggest some misattribution of immaturity [[Layton2018]]; at the same time, adult diagnosis remains far below survey prevalence and minority groups are diagnosed less often [[Chung2019,ONions2025]], and broader awareness among women and minorities is cited as a reason for rises [[Abdelnour2022]]. Status: both can be true in different groups.
- Telehealth prescribing: telehealth-only relationships were not linked to more SUD, but a first stimulant prescription at a telehealth visit was linked to more stimulant use disorder (wide CI) [[Rao2025]]; half of US adults with ADHD have used telehealth for ADHD care [[Staley2024]]. Status: single-system data; replication needed.
- Neurofeedback and EEG markers: an FDA-authorised theta/beta assessment aid exists [[FDA-NEBA,Snyder2015]], but meta-analyses and multicentre data show the theta/beta ratio does not reliably separate ADHD from controls [[Arns2013,vanDijk2020]] and blinded trials show no specific neurofeedback effect [[NFCG2021,Westwood2025NF]]. Status: not recommended as diagnostic or core treatment on current evidence.
- Digital and device treatments: EndeavorRx improved a computerised attention test, not rated symptoms [[Kollins2020]]; eTNS evidence rests on a 62-child pilot (larger trial completed, unpublished) [[McGough2019,NCT05374187]]. Status: Early evidence; FDA authorisation via De Novo does not mean equivalence with medication.
- Long-term benefit of any treatment: no intervention has high-certainty 52-week evidence [[Gosling2025]]; NMA data beyond 12 weeks were insufficient [[Cortese2018]]; discontinuation trials show average benefit persists after 2 years but some people do well without medication [[Matthijssen2019]]. Status: major research gap.
- Will polygenic scores help? GWAS show high polygenicity and 84-98% sharing with other disorders [[Demontis2023]]; adding a PGS to rating scales adds under 1 percentage point of variance explained [[Hoberg2024]]. Status: research tool only (Hypothesis for clinical use).
