# E · Pharmacological treatment: stimulants and non-stimulants

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: stimulants, release, nonstim, compare, safety.

This pass covers the medicines used for ADHD: the stimulants (methylphenidate, dexmethylphenidate, amphetamine salts, dextroamphetamine, lisdexamfetamine) and the non-stimulants (atomoxetine, viloxazine ER, guanfacine ER, clonidine ER). It covers what the US labels say about each one (indications, ages, starting and maximum doses, time to peak, half-life, food effects, sprinkle options, boxed warnings, contraindications), how well they work compared with placebo and with each other, how fast they start working, their side effects, and the long-term safety questions (growth, blood pressure and heart, psychosis, tics, seizures, misuse, pregnancy, shortages). Doses are quoted as each label or guideline publishes them. They are teaching facts, not instructions. Outcome and risk figures are population statistics from trials and registers. They do not predict what will happen to one person.

## Findings

### stimulants · What the stimulants are and how they are regulated
- The current labels of every stimulant read here (methylphenidate, dexmethylphenidate, serdexmethylphenidate/dexmethylphenidate, mixed amphetamine salts, dextroamphetamine and lisdexamfetamine) carry the same boxed warning: "Abuse, misuse, and addiction". It states that the drug "has a high potential for abuse and misuse, which can lead to the development of a substance use disorder, including addiction" [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Vyvanse,FDA-AdderallXR,FDA-Focalin,FDA-Zenzedi,FDA-Azstarys]].
- The boxed warning says misuse and abuse "can result in overdose and death", and the risk "is increased with a higher dosage or unapproved methods of administration, such as snorting or injection" [[FDA-Concerta]].
- The boxed warning tells prescribers to assess each patient's risk of abuse, misuse and addiction before prescribing, to educate patients and families about safe storage and disposal, and to reassess the risk throughout treatment [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Methylphenidate (Concerta) and lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse, "a prodrug of amphetamine") are Schedule II controlled substances in the US [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Vyvanse]].
- The non-stimulants atomoxetine and guanfacine ER are not controlled substances. The Intuniv label says guanfacine ER "has no known potential for abuse or dependence" [[FDA-Strattera,FDA-Intuniv]].
- All stimulant labels read here contraindicate use with a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI), or within 14 days of stopping one, because of the risk of hypertensive crisis. Hypersensitivity to the drug is also a contraindication [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Before starting a stimulant, the labels ask prescribers to screen for heart disease (careful history, family history of sudden death or ventricular arrhythmia, and a physical exam) and for a personal or family history of tics or Tourette's syndrome [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Ritalin,FDA-AdderallXR]].
- Lisdexamfetamine is converted to dextroamphetamine and l-lysine, mainly in the blood by red blood cells. The label notes that substantial hydrolysis occurred even at low haematocrit levels (33% of normal) [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Mixed amphetamine salts (Adderall IR and XR) contain d-amphetamine and l-amphetamine in a 3:1 ratio [[FDA-Adderall,FDA-AdderallXR]].
- Methylphenidate is metabolised mainly by de-esterification to ritalinic acid, "which has little or no pharmacologic activity". 78% to 97% of a dose is excreted in the urine within 48 to 96 hours [[FDA-Ritalin]].
- Oral methylphenidate has low absolute bioavailability: 22 ± 8% for the d-enantiomer and 5 ± 3% for the l-enantiomer [[FDA-Ritalin]].

### stimulants · Label indications, ages and doses (quoted as published)
- Ritalin (methylphenidate IR) is indicated for ADHD "in pediatric patients 6 years and older and adults", and for narcolepsy. Children start at 5 mg twice daily (before breakfast and lunch), titrated weekly in 5–10 mg steps. Doses above 60 mg/day are "not recommended". The adult average is 20–30 mg/day in 2–3 doses, with a maximum of 60 mg/day [[FDA-Ritalin]].
- Concerta (OROS methylphenidate) is indicated for ADHD "in patients aged 6 to 65 years old". In patients new to methylphenidate it starts at 18 mg once daily (18 or 36 mg in adults), increased weekly in 18 mg steps. Maximum: 54 mg/day at 6–12 years and 72 mg/day at 13–17 years ("not to exceed 2 mg/kg/day") and in adults [[FDA-Concerta]].
- Concerta must be swallowed whole with liquids. It must not be split, crushed or chewed, because "doing so will compromise the extended-release characteristics" [[FDA-Concerta]].
- Ritalin LA (methylphenidate ER capsules) is indicated for ADHD in children aged 6 to 12 years. It starts at 20 mg once daily, increased weekly by 10 mg. Doses above 60 mg/day are not recommended. Capsules may be opened and sprinkled on applesauce [[FDA-RitalinLA]].
- Focalin (dexmethylphenidate IR) starts at 2.5 mg twice daily in patients new to methylphenidate. Patients already taking racemic methylphenidate start at half their current total daily dose. The maximum is 20 mg/day (10 mg twice daily) [[FDA-Focalin]].
- Focalin XR starts at 5 mg/day in children and 10 mg/day in adults. The maximum recommended daily dose is 30 mg in children and 40 mg in adults. Capsules may be sprinkled on applesauce [[FDA-FocalinXR]].
- Jornay PM (delayed-release, extended-release methylphenidate) is taken only in the evening, between 6:30 p.m. and 9:30 p.m. It starts at 20 mg and may be increased weekly by 20 mg to a maximum of 100 mg/day, for patients 6 years and older [[FDA-JornayPM]].
- Azstarys (serdexmethylphenidate/dexmethylphenidate) is indicated for patients 6 years and older. It starts at 39.2 mg/7.8 mg once daily, with a maximum of 52.3 mg/10.4 mg once daily [[FDA-Azstarys]].
- Daytrana (methylphenidate patch) is indicated for ages 6 to 17. The patch goes on the hip 2 hours before an effect is needed and comes off 9 hours after application. The nominal delivered doses are 10, 15, 20 and 30 mg per 9 hours [[FDA-Daytrana]].
- Adderall IR (mixed amphetamine salts) is "not recommended for children under 3 years of age". Children aged 3–5 start at 2.5 mg daily, and children 6 and older start at 5 mg once or twice daily. "Only in rare cases will it be necessary to exceed a total of 40 mg per day" [[FDA-Adderall]].
- Adderall XR is indicated for ADHD in adults and in children 6 years and older. Children aged 6–12 start at 10 mg once daily, up to a maximum of 30 mg/day. The recommended adult dose is 20 mg/day. It is not recommended in end-stage renal disease [[FDA-AdderallXR]].
- Adderall XR capsules may be opened and the contents sprinkled on applesauce, which should be eaten immediately without chewing the beads [[FDA-AdderallXR]].
- Zenzedi (dextroamphetamine IR) carries an older-format indication for "pediatric patients (ages 3 to 16 years)". Children aged 3–5 start at 2.5 mg daily, and "only in rare cases will it be necessary to exceed a total of 40 mg per day". Dexedrine Spansule is labelled for ages 6 to 16 years [[FDA-Zenzedi,FDA-Dexedrine]].
- Vyvanse (lisdexamfetamine) is indicated for ADHD in adults and children 6 years and older, and for moderate to severe binge eating disorder in adults. For ADHD it starts at 30 mg every morning, titrated by 10–20 mg weekly to 30–70 mg/day, with a maximum of 70 mg/day. The maximum is 50 mg/day in severe renal impairment and 30 mg/day in end-stage renal disease [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Vyvanse capsules can be swallowed whole or opened and mixed with yogurt, water or orange juice. Afternoon doses should be avoided "because of the potential for insomnia" [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Mydayis (mixed amphetamine salts, triple-bead) is indicated for patients 13 years and older. It starts at 12.5 mg, with a maximum of 50 mg in adults and 25 mg in adolescents. It is taken on waking "because the effects may last up to 16 hours" [[FDA-Mydayis]].
- Most extended-release stimulant labels say they are "not recommended in pediatric patients younger than 6 years of age because they had higher plasma exposure and a higher incidence of adverse reactions (e.g., weight loss)" [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-AdderallXR,FDA-FocalinXR,FDA-JornayPM]].

### release · Plasma curves, onset and duration (label pharmacokinetics)
- Methylphenidate IR: peak plasma concentration about 2 hours after a 10 mg tablet in adults, and 1.9 hours (range 0.3–4.4 h) in children. Food has no relevant effect on absorption [[FDA-Ritalin]].
- In 36 healthy adults, one 18 mg Concerta dose and three 5 mg IR methylphenidate doses 4 hours apart gave similar exposure. Concerta: Cmax 3.7 ng/mL, Tmax 6.8 h, half-life 3.5 h. IR ×3: Cmax 4.2 ng/mL, Tmax 6.5 h, half-life 3.0 h [[FDA-Concerta]].
- Concerta's plasma methylphenidate makes "an initial maximum concentration at about 1 hour", then rises gradually over the next 5 to 9 hours. Mean Tmax across doses was 6 to 10 hours [[FDA-Concerta]].
- 22% of the methylphenidate in Concerta is available for immediate release and 78% for extended release [[FDA-Concerta]].
- In Concerta's pivotal paediatric trials, results "were maintained through 12 hours after dosing". A high-fat breakfast did not change its pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamic performance [[FDA-Concerta]].
- Ritalin LA and Focalin XR use beads that give a bimodal profile with 2 peaks about 4 hours apart. Focalin XR's first peak comes at 1.5 h (range 1–4 h) and its second at about 6.5 h (range 4.5–7 h) [[FDA-RitalinLA,FDA-FocalinXR]].
- Dexmethylphenidate IR peaks at about 1–1.5 hours fasted, with a mean terminal half-life of about 2.2 hours. A high-fat breakfast delayed Tmax from 1.5 to 2.9 hours [[FDA-Focalin]].
- Jornay PM, taken in the evening, has a lag period and then a single peak with a median Tmax of 14.0 hours. Its apparent half-life in adults is about 5.9 hours [[FDA-JornayPM]].
- Daytrana patch: in patients with ADHD, peak methylphenidate levels come about 10 hours after a single application and 8 hours after repeated applications worn up to 9 hours. Applied to inflamed skin, Cmax and AUC were about 3-fold higher [[FDA-Daytrana]].
- Azstarys: dexmethylphenidate from serdexmethylphenidate alone peaks at about 8 hours. The terminal half-lives in adults were about 5.7 h for serdexmethylphenidate and 11.7 h for dexmethylphenidate [[FDA-Azstarys]].
- Adderall IR: d- and l-amphetamine peak about 3 hours after a dose. Half-life is 9.77–11 h for d-amphetamine and 11.5–13.8 h for l-amphetamine [[FDA-Adderall]].
- Adderall XR peaks at about 7 hours, about 4 hours later than IR. A 20 mg XR dose gave plasma profiles comparable to 10 mg IR twice daily 4 hours apart [[FDA-AdderallXR]].
- Adderall XR d-amphetamine half-life is 10 h in adults, 11 h in adolescents (≤75 kg) and 9 h in children aged 6–12. Children have higher clearance per kg body weight [[FDA-AdderallXR]].
- Mydayis peaks at about 7–10 hours in children and about 8 hours in adults. A high-fat meal prolongs d-amphetamine Tmax from 7.0 to 12.0 hours [[FDA-Mydayis]].
- Vyvanse in children aged 6–12 (fasted): lisdexamfetamine peaks at about 1 hour and dextroamphetamine at about 3.5 hours. The lisdexamfetamine prodrug's half-life is under 1 hour. Dextroamphetamine's half-life is 8.6–9.5 h in children aged 6–12 and 10–11.3 h in adults [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Food does not change Vyvanse's dextroamphetamine AUC or Cmax but delays Tmax by about 1 hour (3.8 h fasted, 4.7 h after a high-fat meal, 4.2 h with yogurt) [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- In the Vyvanse paediatric classroom study, the drug effect "reached statistical significance from hours 2 to 12 post-dose, but was not significant at 1 hour". In the adult workplace-simulation study, attention improved at every time point measured from 2 to 14 hours after the dose [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Stimulants act on the first dose. In an adult trial, lisdexamfetamine 30, 50 and 70 mg separated from placebo "beginning at week 1 and for each week throughout". Clinician-rated improvement rates were 57–62% versus 29% on placebo [[Adler2008LDX]].

### nonstim · Atomoxetine
- Strattera (atomoxetine) is a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor indicated for ADHD in adults and children 6 years and older, "as an integral part of a total treatment program" [[FDA-Strattera]].
- Strattera boxed warning (current label): "Suicidal thoughts and behaviors in pediatric patients 6 years of age and older". Atomoxetine "increased the risk of suicidal ideation in pediatric patients aged 6 years of age and older with ADHD in short-term studies" [[FDA-Strattera]].
- In 12 paediatric studies with over 2,200 patients, suicidal ideation occurred in 0.4% (5/1,357) of children on atomoxetine (including one suicide attempt) versus 0% (0/851) on placebo [[FDA-Strattera]].
- Atomoxetine dose by weight. Children under 70 kg: start 0.5 mg/kg/day, target 1.2 mg/kg/day, maximum 1.4 mg/kg/day or 100 mg/day, whichever is less. Children 70 kg or more and adults: start 40 mg/day, target 80 mg/day, maximum 100 mg/day. The label notes "No additional benefit has been demonstrated with STRATTERA dosages higher than 1.2 mg/kg/day" [[FDA-Strattera]].
- Atomoxetine is metabolised mainly by CYP2D6. In normal metabolisers the half-life is 5.2 h and median Tmax 1 h. In CYP2D6 poor metabolisers the half-life is 21.6 h, Tmax 2.5 h, accumulation 3.3-fold (vs 1.1-fold) and apparent oral clearance 0.03 vs 0.35 L/h/kg [[FDA-Strattera]].
- The label recommends considering genetic testing for CYP2D6 status. For poor metabolisers or patients on a strong CYP2D6 inhibitor, it recommends a longer titration interval of 4 weeks [[FDA-Strattera]].
- CYP2D6 poor metabolisers had larger heart rate rises: 9.4 vs 5 beats/min in children and 11 vs 7.5 beats/min in adults. They also more often stopped for adverse reactions (11% vs 6% in children) [[FDA-Strattera]].
- CPIC published a guideline for CYP2D6 genotype and atomoxetine dosing, because CYP2D6 polymorphisms affect atomoxetine metabolism, efficacy and safety [[Brown2019CPIC]].
- Atomoxetine contraindications: MAOIs (within 14 days), narrow-angle glaucoma, pheochromocytoma, and severe cardiac or vascular disorders that would worsen with clinically important rises in blood pressure or heart rate [[FDA-Strattera]].
- Strattera warns of severe liver injury. It "should be discontinued and not restarted in patients with jaundice or laboratory evidence of liver injury" [[FDA-Strattera]].
- Food decreased atomoxetine Cmax by 37% and delayed Tmax by 3 hours without changing AUC. Capsules should be taken whole and not opened [[FDA-Strattera]].
- Atomoxetine works more slowly than a stimulant. A review found it "can have an onset of action within 1-2 weeks", with response increasing gradually for up to 24 weeks or longer, and single daily doses giving 24-hour efficacy despite a 5-hour plasma half-life (Early evidence; nonsystematic review) [[Clemow2015]].
- In two 10-week adult trials (n = 280 and n = 256), atomoxetine beat placebo on inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive symptoms. Discontinuations for adverse events were under 10% [[Michelson2003ATX]].
- Children on atomoxetine (N = 1,597 vs 934 placebo) had more headache (19% vs 15%), abdominal pain (18% vs 10%), decreased appetite (16% vs 4%), somnolence (11% vs 4%) and vomiting (11% vs 6%) [[FDA-Strattera]].

### nonstim · Viloxazine ER
- Qelbree (viloxazine ER) is a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor indicated for ADHD in adults and children 6 years and older [[FDA-Qelbree]].
- Qelbree boxed warning: "higher rates of suicidal thoughts and behavior were reported in patients with ADHD treated with Qelbree than in patients treated with placebo" [[FDA-Qelbree]].
- In short-term paediatric trials, 0.9% of children (9/1,019) on Qelbree 100–400 mg reported suicidal ideation or behaviour, versus 0.4% (2/463) on placebo. Among adults, 1.6% (3/189) on Qelbree reported suicidal ideation versus 0/183 on placebo [[FDA-Qelbree]].
- Qelbree dosing. Ages 6–11: start 100 mg once daily, maximum 400 mg. Ages 12–17: start 200 mg, maximum 400 mg. Adults: start 200 mg, maximum 600 mg. Capsules can be sprinkled on applesauce or pudding [[FDA-Qelbree]].
- Viloxazine median Tmax is about 5 hours (range 3–9 h), mean half-life 7.02 h, with steady state after 2 days. A high-fat meal lowered Cmax and AUC by about 9% and 8% [[FDA-Qelbree]].
- Viloxazine is contraindicated with MAOIs and with sensitive or narrow-therapeutic-range CYP1A2 substrates [[FDA-Qelbree]].
- In children aged 6–11 taking 100 mg daily, 22% (34/154) had a heart rate rise of at least 20 bpm at some time point, versus 9% (15/159) on placebo [[FDA-Qelbree]].
- In a 6-week phase III trial in children aged 6–11 (n = 477), viloxazine ER 100 and 200 mg improved ADHD-RS-5 total scores versus placebo from week 1 onward. Discontinuation for adverse events was under 5% [[Nasser2020Children,NCT03247530]].
- In a phase III adult trial (n = 374; mean dose 504 mg), viloxazine ER reduced AISRS scores by 15.5 points versus 11.7 on placebo at week 6. Discontinuation for adverse events was 9.0% vs 4.9% [[Nasser2022Adults,NCT04016779]].
- Adults on Qelbree versus placebo reported more insomnia (23% vs 7%), headache (17% vs 7%), nausea (12% vs 3%), fatigue (12% vs 3%), dry mouth (10% vs 2%) and decreased appetite (10% vs 3%) [[FDA-Qelbree]].

### nonstim · Alpha-2 agonists: guanfacine ER and clonidine ER
- Intuniv (guanfacine ER) is a central alpha-2A-adrenergic agonist indicated for ADHD "as monotherapy and as adjunctive therapy to stimulant medications". Its safety and efficacy below age 6 have not been established. It was studied in children and adolescents aged 6 to 17 [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- Intuniv dosing: begin at 1 mg once daily and increase by no more than 1 mg/week. The target is 0.05–0.12 mg/kg/day (total 1–7 mg/day). Doses above 4 mg/day were not evaluated in children aged 6–12, and above 7 mg/day not in adolescents [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- Intuniv should not be taken with high-fat meals: a high-fat breakfast raised Cmax about 75% and AUC about 40%. Tablets must not be crushed or chewed. It is not interchangeable mg-for-mg with immediate-release guanfacine [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- Guanfacine ER peaks at about 5 hours in children and adolescents. In adults its Tmax is 6.0 h versus 3.0 h for IR, its half-life 18 ± 4 h versus 16 ± 3 h, and its relative bioavailability 58%. It is metabolised mainly by CYP3A4 [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- Abrupt discontinuation of guanfacine ER "can lead to clinically significant and persistent rebound hypertension". The label recommends tapering by no more than 1 mg every 3 to 7 days [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- Somnolence in Intuniv's fixed-dose trials rose with dose: 11% on placebo, 28% at 1 mg, 30% at 2 mg, 38% at 3 mg and 51% at 4 mg. Hypotension occurred in 7% across doses versus 3% on placebo [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- In an 8-week trial in ages 6–17 (n = 345), ADHD-RS-IV totals fell by 16.18, 16.43 and 18.87 points on guanfacine ER 2, 3 and 4 mg, versus 8.48 on placebo [[Biederman2008GXR]].
- Clonidine ER is indicated for ADHD as monotherapy or as an add-on to stimulants. Its safety and efficacy were established in ages 6 to 17 [[FDA-ClonidineER]].
- Clonidine ER starts with 0.1 mg at bedtime and rises by 0.1 mg/day weekly. It is given twice a day with an equal or larger bedtime dose. Doses above 0.4 mg/day "were not evaluated in clinical trials for ADHD and are not recommended". It should be tapered by no more than 0.1 mg every 3 to 7 days to avoid rebound hypertension [[FDA-ClonidineER]].
- Somnolence was common on clonidine ER: 38% at 0.2 mg/day and 31% at 0.4 mg/day versus 4% on placebo (monotherapy), and 19% versus 7% as an add-on to stimulants [[FDA-ClonidineER]].
- In an 8-week trial (n = 236, ages 6–17), clonidine ER 0.2 and 0.4 mg/day improved ADHD-RS-IV scores versus placebo, with significant separation "as soon as week 2" [[Jain2011]].
- Immediate-release clonidine peaks at about 3–5 hours and has a plasma half-life of 12–16 hours. The half-life rises to as much as 41 hours in severe renal impairment. Cmax of the ER form is 50% lower than IR [[FDA-ClonidineER]].

### compare · How well the medicines work and how they compare
- A network meta-analysis of 133 double-blind RCTs covered 10,068 children and adolescents and 8,131 adults for efficacy at about 12 weeks. On clinician ratings in children, every drug beat placebo: amphetamines SMD −1.02 (95% CI −1.19 to −0.85), methylphenidate −0.78 (−0.93 to −0.62), atomoxetine −0.56 (−0.66 to −0.45) [[Cortese2018]].
- In the same analysis, adults on clinician ratings: amphetamines SMD −0.79 (−0.99 to −0.58), methylphenidate −0.49 (−0.64 to −0.35), bupropion −0.46 (−0.85 to −0.07) and atomoxetine −0.45 (−0.58 to −0.32). Modafinil (0.16, −0.28 to 0.59) did not beat placebo [[Cortese2018]].
- On teacher ratings in children, only methylphenidate (SMD −0.82, −1.16 to −0.48) and modafinil (−0.76, −1.15 to −0.37) beat placebo among the comparisons available [[Cortese2018]].
- Tolerability (dropouts due to side effects) versus placebo. Amphetamines were worse in children (OR 2.30, 1.36–3.89) and adults (3.26, 1.54–6.92). Guanfacine was worse in children only (2.64, 1.20–5.81). Atomoxetine (2.33), methylphenidate (2.39) and modafinil (4.01) were worse in adults only [[Cortese2018]].
- Head to head, amphetamines beat modafinil, atomoxetine and methylphenidate on clinician ratings in children (SMDs −0.46 to −0.24) and adults (−0.94 to −0.29). The authors concluded the evidence "supports methylphenidate in children and adolescents, and amphetamines in adults, as preferred first-choice medications for the short-term treatment of ADHD" [[Cortese2018]].
- Cortese 2018 found "insufficient data" for the 26-week and 52-week timepoints, so the comparison covers short-term treatment only [[Cortese2018]].
- A 2025 component network meta-analysis of 113 adult RCTs (14,887 participants) found that stimulants and atomoxetine were the only interventions that reduced core symptoms on both self-rated and clinician-rated scales. Stimulants: SMD −0.39 self-rated, −0.61 clinician-rated. Atomoxetine: −0.38 and −0.51 [[Ostinelli2025]].
- In that analysis, atomoxetine (OR 1.43, 1.14–1.80) and guanfacine (3.70, 1.22–11.19) were less acceptable than placebo (all-cause discontinuation). Medicines did not improve quality of life [[Ostinelli2025]].
- The WFADHD consensus: compared with placebo on clinician ratings, amphetamines gave "large improvements in all age groups", methylphenidate large improvements in youths and moderate ones in adults, extended-release guanfacine moderate improvements in children, and atomoxetine moderate improvements in all ages (Established) [[Faraone2021]].
- The WFADHD consensus also states: "The stimulant medications for ADHD are more effective than non-stimulant medications but are also more likely to be diverted, misused, and abused" (Established) [[Faraone2021]].
- The AAP guideline gives an effect size of 1.0 for stimulants and 0.7 for non-stimulants. It notes that "approximately 40% responding to both" methylphenidate and amphetamine "and about 40% responding to only 1" [[Wolraich2019]].
- The AAP guideline cites MTA dose-titration data suggesting "more than 70% of children and adolescents with ADHD are methylphenidate responders". It cites other reports that more than 90% respond to one of the stimulants if both classes are tried [[Wolraich2019]].
- For school-age children, the AAP grades the evidence "particularly strong for stimulant medications" and "sufficient, but not as strong, for atomoxetine, extended-release guanfacine, and extended-release clonidine, in that order" [[Wolraich2019]].
- In the MTA trial (579 children aged 7–9.9, 14 months), carefully managed medication was better for ADHD symptoms than intensive behavioural treatment and community care, even though two thirds of community-care children received medication. Combined treatment did not differ significantly from medication alone on core symptoms [[MTA1999]].
- A 2026 dose-effect network meta-analysis (113 RCTs; 14,138 children and adolescents, 11,016 adults) found that in children, efficacy rose up to about 45 mg/day for methylphenidate, 25 mg/day for amphetamines and 4 mg/day for guanfacine, "with no evidence of additional benefit at higher doses" [[Nourredine2026]].
- In adults in the same analysis, amphetamines plateaued above about 50 mg/day, while methylphenidate efficacy kept rising without a clear plateau (sparse data). Dropouts due to adverse events rose for amphetamines above 25 mg/day in children and 50 mg/day in adults [[Nourredine2026]].
- In adults, doses above the licensed range gave little extra benefit. For methylphenidate, unlicensed doses gave SMD −0.23 (−0.44 to −0.02) more symptom reduction but doubled adverse-event dropouts (OR 2.02, 1.19–3.43). For amphetamines there was no extra benefit (SMD −0.08, −0.24 to 0.08) [[Farhat2024JAMAPsych]].
- In 65 paediatric RCTs (7,877 children), extra benefit in fixed-dose trials shrank beyond 30 mg methylphenidate or 20 mg amphetamine. In flexible-dose (titrate-to-response) trials, benefit kept rising across the licensed range [[Farhat2022MolPsych]].
- In a randomised discontinuation trial in 94 youths treated with methylphenidate for more than 2 years, those switched to placebo worsened more (ADHD-RS difference −4.6, 95% CI −8.7 to −0.56). Clinician-rated worsening was 40.4% after stopping versus 15.9% when continuing [[Matthijssen2019]].

### compare · Guideline ordering (NICE and AAP)
- NICE NG87: "Offer methylphenidate (either short or long acting) as the first line pharmacological treatment for children aged 5 years and over and young people with ADHD" [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87: consider switching to lisdexamfetamine in children and young people after "a 6-week trial of methylphenidate at an adequate dose" without enough benefit [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87 for adults: "Offer lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate as first-line pharmacological treatment for adults with ADHD". Atomoxetine is offered if neither is tolerated or effective after separate 6-week trials [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87: "Do not offer medication for ADHD for any child under 5 years without a second specialist opinion" from a specialist ADHD service. Group parent training is first-line for children under 5 [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87 (amended 2019): an ECG "is not needed before starting stimulants, atomoxetine or guanfacine" unless there are specified cardiac features or a co-prescribed medicine with cardiac risk. Refer to a paediatric hypertension specialist if blood pressure is consistently above the 95th centile [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87 monitoring: measure height every 6 months in children and young people, weight every 3 months in children 10 and under, and weight every 6 months in adults [[NICE2018]].
- AAP KAS 5a: for preschool children (age 4 to the sixth birthday), behavioural parent training and/or classroom interventions come first. Methylphenidate "may be considered" if these do not give significant improvement and moderate-to-severe disturbance continues (grade B) [[Wolraich2019]].
- AAP KAS 5b and 5c: for ages 6 to 12, prescribe FDA-approved medication plus parent training and/or classroom intervention. For adolescents, prescribe FDA-approved medication "with the adolescent's assent" (grade A). KAS 6: titrate doses "to achieve maximum benefit with tolerable side effects" [[Wolraich2019]].
- In the Preschool ADHD Treatment Study (303 enrolled, 165 randomised to titration), IR methylphenidate at 2.5, 5 and 7.5 mg three times daily reduced symptoms versus placebo. The mean optimal dose was 14.2 ± 8.1 mg/day (0.7 mg/kg/day), and effect sizes (0.4–0.8) were smaller than in school-age children [[Greenhill2006PATS]].
- In PATS, 11% of preschoolers (21 children) stopped because of drug-attributed adverse events, and 30% of parents reported moderate to severe adverse events during the study [[Wigal2006PATS]].

### safety · Common adverse effects (label tables and meta-analyses)
- Children on Vyvanse in a 4-week trial (n = 218 vs 72 placebo): decreased appetite 39% vs 4%, insomnia 22% vs 3%, upper abdominal pain 12% vs 6%, irritability 10% vs 0%, weight decrease 9% vs 1% [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Children aged 6–12 on Adderall XR (n = 374 vs 210): loss of appetite 22% vs 2%, insomnia 17% vs 2%, abdominal pain 14% vs 10%. In adolescents, loss of appetite was 36% vs 2% [[FDA-AdderallXR]].
- Adults on Concerta (n = 415 vs 212): decreased appetite 25% vs 7%, headache 22% vs 16%, dry mouth 14% vs 4%, nausea 13% vs 3%, insomnia 12% vs 6%, anxiety 8% vs 2%, tachycardia 5% vs 0% [[FDA-Concerta]].
- In Concerta's paediatric trials (n = 321 vs 318), the only adverse reaction at 5% or more was upper abdominal pain (6% vs 4%). Insomnia was 3% vs 1% [[FDA-Concerta]].
- Stimulants raise blood pressure (mean increase about 2 to 4 mm Hg) and heart rate (mean increase about 3 to 6 beats per minute) [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Stimulant labels warn about peripheral vasculopathy, including Raynaud's phenomenon, and about priapism. Priapism with methylphenidate "occurred in patients ... after some time, often subsequent to an increase in dosage" [[FDA-Concerta]].
- In a sleep meta-analysis (9 studies, N = 246, objective sleep measures), stimulants lengthened sleep latency (effect size 0.54), worsened sleep efficiency (−0.32) and shortened total sleep time (−0.59) [[Kidwell2015]].
- The Cochrane review (212 trials, 16,302 participants) found that methylphenidate may increase non-serious adverse events (RR 1.23, 95% CI 1.11–1.37), such as sleep problems and decreased appetite, but may not affect serious adverse events (RR 0.80, 0.39–1.67) (very low-certainty evidence) [[Storebo2023Cochrane]].
- WFADHD consensus: "The adverse effects of medications for ADHD are typically mild and can be addressed by changing the dose or the medication" (Established) [[Faraone2021]].

### safety · Growth
- Stimulant labels: children aged 7 to 13 on methylphenidate 7 days a week for 14 to 36 months had a temporary slowing in growth rate. On average this was about 2 cm less height and 2.7 kg less weight over 3 years, "without evidence of growth rebound" during that time [[FDA-Concerta]].
- In the MTA follow-up to age 25, the consistently or inconsistently treated group was 2.55 ± 0.73 cm shorter as adults than those with negligible medication use. Consistent users were 2.36 ± 1.13 cm shorter than inconsistent users. Extended use was "not associated with reduction of symptom severity" in adulthood [[Swanson2017MTA]].
- In PATS, 95 preschoolers who stayed on methylphenidate grew 20.3% slower in height than expected (5.41 vs 6.79 cm/yr) and gained 55.2% less weight (1.07 vs 2.39 kg/yr) over about 1 year [[Swanson2006PATS]].
- A meta-analysis of 18 studies (n = 4,868) found small reductions in height (SMD 0.27) and weight (SMD 0.33) z-scores with long-term methylphenidate. Weight effects were concentrated in the first 12 months and height effects in the first 24–30 months [[Carucci2021]].
- The ADDUCE cohort (1,410 children aged 6–17) found "little evidence of an effect on growth" after 24 months of methylphenidate (height velocity SD score difference −0.07, 95% CI −0.18 to 0.04). Pulse and blood pressure were higher in the methylphenidate group [[Man2023ADDUCE]].
- In children on atomoxetine for at least 2 years, CYP2D6 poor metabolisers gained 1.1 cm less height and 2.4 kg less weight than predicted. Other metabolisers gained 0.4 cm and 0.2 kg less than predicted [[FDA-Strattera]].

### safety · Blood pressure, heart rate and cardiovascular events
- A 2025 network meta-analysis (102 short-term RCTs; 13,315 children and adolescents, 9,387 adults) found that amphetamines, atomoxetine, lisdexamfetamine, methylphenidate and viloxazine all raised haemodynamic values. In children, pulse rose by 2.79 bpm (viloxazine) to 5.58 bpm (atomoxetine) versus placebo [[Farhat2025CV]].
- In that analysis, stimulants did not raise blood pressure or pulse more than atomoxetine or viloxazine. Guanfacine lowered them (children: SBP −2.83 mm Hg, pulse −4.06 bpm). The authors advise monitoring "any pharmacological intervention, and not stimulants only" [[Farhat2025CV]].
- A meta-analysis of 18 paediatric trials (5,837 participants, mean 28.7 weeks) found small pre-post rises in systolic BP with methylphenidate, amphetamine and atomoxetine. Amphetamine and atomoxetine also raised heart rate (SMD 0.37 and 0.43). 2% of patients stopped medication because of a cardiovascular effect [[Hennissen2017]].
- In 1,200,438 children and young adults aged 2–24, serious cardiovascular events were rare (3.1 per 100,000 person-years). Current use of ADHD drugs did not raise the risk (adjusted HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.31–1.85), although a doubling of risk could not be ruled out [[Cooper2011]].
- In 443,198 adults aged 25–64, current use of ADHD medication was not associated with more MI, sudden cardiac death or stroke. Adjusted RR was 0.83 (0.72–0.96) versus non-use and 1.03 (0.86–1.24) versus remote use. The apparent protection "likely represent[s] healthy-user bias" [[Habel2011]].
- A meta-analysis of 19 observational studies (3,931,532 participants) found no significant link between ADHD medication and any cardiovascular disease in children (RR 1.18, 0.91–1.53) or adults. A modest rise, especially for arrhythmia or cardiac arrest (RR 1.60, 0.94–2.72), "could not be ruled out" [[Zhang2022JNO]].
- A Swedish case-control study of 278,027 people with ADHD aged 6–64 found cardiovascular risk rising with cumulative duration of medication use: AOR 0.99 at ≤1 year, 1.09 at 1–2 years, 1.15 at 2–3 years, 1.27 at 3–5 years and 1.23 beyond 5 years. Each extra year carried a 4% higher risk (AOR 1.04, 1.03–1.05) [[Zhang2024JAMAPsych]].
- In the same study, hypertension risk was raised after 3–5 years (AOR 1.72, 1.51–1.97) and after more than 5 years (1.80, 1.55–2.08) of cumulative use [[Zhang2024JAMAPsych]].
- In a Swedish cohort of 26,710 methylphenidate users aged 12–60 and matched controls, cardiovascular events in the 6 months after starting were 1.51 per 10,000 person-weeks. Within-person IRR was 1.41 (95% HDI 1.09–1.88), with "little evidence for an increased risk of 20% or higher" [[GarciaArgibay2024]].

### safety · Psychosis, mania, suicidality, tics and seizures
- Stimulant users aged 13–25 with ADHD (221,846 matched patients) developed new psychosis in about 1 in 660. Rates were 0.10% on methylphenidate and 0.21% on amphetamine (HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.31–2.09) [[Moran2019NEJM]].
- In a case-control study of people aged 16–35, past-month prescription amphetamine use was associated with incident psychosis or mania (aOR 2.68, 1.90–3.77). The odds were 5.28-fold at doses above 30 mg dextroamphetamine equivalents. Methylphenidate was not associated (aOR 0.91, 0.54–1.55) [[Moran2024AJP]].
- In a Hong Kong self-controlled study of 20,586 young people, psychotic events were not more frequent during methylphenidate exposure (IRR 1.02, 0.53–1.97). Risk was higher in the period before the first prescription (IRR 4.64) [[Man2016Psychosis]].
- In a self-controlled study of 25,629 young people on methylphenidate, suicide attempts were most frequent in the 90 days before treatment (IRR 6.55). Risk was back to baseline during ongoing treatment (IRR 1.35, 0.77–2.38), which "do[es] not support a causal association" [[Man2017Suicide]].
- A meta-analysis of 22 RCTs (2,385 children) found that new or worsening tics were equally common on stimulants (5.7%) and placebo (6.5%) (RR 0.99, 0.78–1.27) [[Cohen2015Tics]].
- In 801,838 patients with ADHD, within-person analyses linked ADHD medication months with lower odds of seizures, both with a prior seizure history (OR 0.71, 0.60–0.85) and without one (OR 0.71, 0.62–0.82) [[Wiggs2018]].
- Among 21,557 Swedish individuals with a seizure history, ADHD medication periods had fewer acute seizures (HR 0.73, 0.57–0.94). The authors conclude "epilepsy should not automatically preclude" ADHD medication [[Brikell2019]].

### safety · Misuse, diversion and supply
- A meta-analysis estimated that 17% of college students (95% CI 13–23%) misuse stimulant medication, mostly for academic reasons. The most common source is peers with prescriptions [[Benson2015]].
- In US national surveys (2015–2016, N = 102,000 adults), 6.6% used prescription stimulants in a year, 1.9% misused them without a use disorder, and 0.2% had a use disorder. The most common motive was "to help be alert or concentrate" (56.3%) [[Compton2018]].
- A systematic review of 111 studies found that self-reported non-medical use of stimulants ranged from 2.1% to 58.7%. Most non-medical use was oral with no or minor medical effects, but serious outcomes including death occurred, "particularly when administered by non-oral routes" [[Faraone2020NMU]].
- The same review found "little evidence that academic performance is improved by NMU in individuals without attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder" [[Faraone2020NMU]].
- In 2023, about one third of US adults with ADHD took a stimulant in the previous year. Of those, 71.5% had difficulty getting the prescription filled because it was unavailable [[Staley2024]].
- A national shortage of immediate-release mixed amphetamine salts was announced in October 2022. IQVIA data showed a drop in IR mixed amphetamine salts dispensing to children that month, offset by more dexmethylphenidate dispensing [[He2025Pediatrics]].
- A drug-shortage database counted 26 shortages of ADHD stimulants between 2001 and 2023 (amphetamine salts, dexmethylphenidate, dextroamphetamine, lisdexamfetamine and methylphenidate), with 7 each in 2012, 2013, 2015 and 2023. The mean duration of resolved shortages was 20.7 months [[Sheykhsoltan2025]].
- A 2026 economic analysis linked the shortage to simultaneous production cutbacks by several manufacturers in late 2022 and early 2023, alongside a steep fall in US imports of raw amphetamine. This frames the shortage as more than DEA quotas (Early evidence) [[Currie2026]].
- Among commercially insured Americans aged 5–64, the share with at least one stimulant prescription fill rose from 3.6% in 2016 to 4.1% in 2021. Fills rose more than 10% in 2020–2021 among women aged 15–44 and men aged 25–44 [[Danielson2023MMWR]].

### safety · Pregnancy
- Stimulant labels describe a National Pregnancy Registry for ADHD Medications. They note that stimulants "can cause vasoconstriction and thereby decrease placental perfusion", and that premature delivery and low birth weight have been reported in infants of amphetamine-dependent mothers [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Vyvanse]].
- In 1.8 million US Medicaid pregnancies, first-trimester methylphenidate was linked to cardiac malformations (aRR 1.28, 0.94–1.74). The pooled US–Nordic estimate was 1.28 (1.00–1.64). Amphetamines showed no increase (aRR 0.96 for cardiac, 1.05 for any malformation) [[Huybrechts2018]].
- First-trimester atomoxetine (990 exposed pregnancies) was not linked to major congenital malformations (adjusted PR 0.99, 0.74–1.34). The cardiac estimate (PR 1.34, 0.86–2.09) was uncertain [[Broms2023]].
- In Medicaid pregnancies, early stimulant use was associated with preeclampsia (aRR 1.29, 1.11–1.49). Continuing after 20 weeks was associated with preterm birth (aRR 1.30, 1.10–1.55). The authors judged the absolute increases small. Atomoxetine was not associated with these outcomes [[Cohen2017Placenta]].
- In US cohorts of over 4 million pregnancies, stimulant exposure in the second half of pregnancy was "not likely to meaningfully increase the risk of childhood neurodevelopmental disorders". For amphetamine, the autism HR was 0.80 (0.56–1.14) [[Suarez2024]].

### Exam hooks
- Every US stimulant label now carries a boxed warning for "abuse, misuse, and addiction"; atomoxetine and viloxazine carry boxed warnings for suicidal thoughts and behaviours [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Strattera,FDA-Qelbree]].
- Stimulants are Schedule II; atomoxetine and guanfacine ER are not controlled substances [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Strattera,FDA-Intuniv]].
- MAOI use within 14 days is a contraindication for stimulants, atomoxetine and viloxazine [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Strattera,FDA-Qelbree]].
- Lisdexamfetamine is a prodrug: red blood cells convert it to dextroamphetamine and l-lysine [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Atomoxetine half-life: 5.2 h in normal CYP2D6 metabolisers and 21.6 h in poor metabolisers [[FDA-Strattera]].
- Guanfacine ER and clonidine ER must be tapered (guanfacine ≤1 mg every 3–7 days) to avoid rebound hypertension [[FDA-Intuniv,FDA-ClonidineER]].
- Do not give guanfacine ER with a high-fat meal: Cmax rises about 75% [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- Concerta delivers 22% of its methylphenidate immediately and 78% as extended release, and must be swallowed whole [[FDA-Concerta]].
- Jornay PM is the methylphenidate product taken in the evening; its median Tmax is 14 hours [[FDA-JornayPM]].
- Cortese 2018: preferred first choices are methylphenidate for children and adolescents and amphetamines for adults [[Cortese2018]].
- NICE adults: lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate first line; NICE children (5+): methylphenidate first line [[NICE2018]].
- AAP: for ages 4–5, behavioural parent training first; methylphenidate may be considered if it is not enough [[Wolraich2019]].
- AAP effect sizes: stimulants about 1.0, non-stimulants about 0.7 [[Wolraich2019]].
- Usual stimulant effect on vital signs: blood pressure +2 to 4 mm Hg, heart rate +3 to 6 bpm [[FDA-Concerta]].
- MTA follow-up: consistent childhood stimulant use was associated with about 2.5 cm shorter adult height [[Swanson2017MTA]].
- Amphetamine carries a higher psychosis risk than methylphenidate in adolescents and young adults (HR 1.65) [[Moran2019NEJM]].
- Stimulants do not increase tics compared with placebo in RCTs (RR 0.99) [[Cohen2015Tics]].
- Atomoxetine may start working within 1–2 weeks but keeps improving over months; stimulants separate from placebo within the first week [[Clemow2015,Adler2008LDX]].

### In plain words
- ADHD medicines do not cure ADHD. They lower the symptoms while they are in the body [[Faraone2021,Matthijssen2019]].
- There are two main groups. Stimulants (like methylphenidate and amphetamine) work within hours. Non-stimulants (like atomoxetine, viloxazine, guanfacine and clonidine) take longer to work [[FDA-Vyvanse,Clemow2015]].
- Stimulants help most people, and if one type does not help, the other type often does [[Wolraich2019]].
- Long-acting pills release the medicine slowly, so one morning dose can last a school or work day [[FDA-Concerta,FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Some capsules can be opened and sprinkled on applesauce for people who cannot swallow pills. Some tablets must never be crushed or chewed [[FDA-AdderallXR,FDA-Concerta]].
- The most common side effects are a smaller appetite, trouble falling asleep, stomach aches and headaches. These are usually mild and can often be fixed by changing the dose, the timing or the medicine [[FDA-Vyvanse,Faraone2021]].
- These medicines raise heart rate and blood pressure a little, so the care team checks them regularly [[FDA-Concerta,Farhat2025CV]].
- Children on stimulants may grow a little slower, so height and weight are measured and plotted on a growth chart [[FDA-Concerta,NICE2018]].
- Stimulants are controlled medicines. Keep them locked away, never share them, and get rid of leftovers safely [[FDA-Concerta]].
- Taking someone else's stimulant to study is not proven to raise grades, and snorting or injecting it can be deadly [[Faraone2020NMU,FDA-Concerta]].
- Guanfacine and clonidine should not be stopped suddenly, because blood pressure can jump [[FDA-Intuniv,FDA-ClonidineER]].
- On atomoxetine or viloxazine, families should watch for new sad, hopeless or suicidal thoughts and tell the doctor right away [[FDA-Strattera,FDA-Qelbree]].
- If you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, talk with your doctor about the risks and benefits of continuing ADHD medicine [[Cohen2017Placenta,Huybrechts2018]].

## Open questions and controversies
- How strong is the evidence for methylphenidate in children? Position A: guideline groups and the WFADHD consensus treat methylphenidate as effective and safe. Clinician-rated SMD is −0.78 [[Cortese2018,Faraone2021]], and critics of the Cochrane review found "errors in the extraction of data" and called its uncertainty conclusion "misplaced" [[Banaschewski2016]]. Position B: the Cochrane team rates all outcomes very low certainty, because 191/212 trials were at high risk of bias, side effects can unblind trials, and mean treatment lasted 28.8 days [[Storebo2015BMJ,Storebo2023Cochrane]]. Status: both sides agree on a teacher-rated SMD near −0.74 to −0.82. They disagree on how much certainty it deserves. The 2025 Cochrane update keeps the 2023 conclusions [[Storebo2025Cochrane]].
- Do benefits last beyond a year? Short-term efficacy is clear, but Cortese 2018 found too little 26- and 52-week data [[Cortese2018]]. MTA adult follow-up found no symptom difference by long-term medication pattern [[Swanson2017MTA]]. A discontinuation RCT after 2+ years showed continued benefit on average, though some youths did not worsen off medication [[Matthijssen2019]]. Status: open. Periodic review is advised.
- Long-term cardiovascular risk. Large cohorts found no rise in serious events in the short term [[Cooper2011,Habel2011,Zhang2022JNO]]. A Swedish study found risk rising with cumulative years, mostly hypertension [[Zhang2024JAMAPsych]], and a small short-term rise after starting methylphenidate [[GarciaArgibay2024]]. Status: absolute risks are low. Monitoring is recommended for all ADHD medicines [[Farhat2025CV]] (Early evidence for duration effect).
- Growth. The label and MTA show about 2–2.5 cm less height with consistent use [[FDA-Concerta,Swanson2017MTA]], and PATS showed a 20% slower height velocity in preschoolers [[Swanson2006PATS]]. The ADDUCE cohort found little effect over 2 years [[Man2023ADDUCE]], and a meta-analysis judged the effects small [[Carucci2021]]. Status: small average effect, larger in some individuals.
- Amphetamine dose and psychosis. Amphetamine carried a higher psychosis risk than methylphenidate [[Moran2019NEJM]], with a dose gradient above 30 mg dextroamphetamine equivalents [[Moran2024AJP]]. Status: observational, with selection bias acknowledged. Dose-effect meta-analysis also finds no extra benefit from amphetamine above about 25 mg/day in children and 50 mg/day in adults [[Nourredine2026]].
- Doses above label limits in adults. They may give a small extra benefit for methylphenidate at the cost of more dropouts, and none for amphetamines [[Farhat2024JAMAPsych,Nourredine2026]]. Status: use cautiously.
- Pregnancy. Methylphenidate has a small possible cardiac malformation signal (pooled RR 1.28, 1.00–1.64) [[Huybrechts2018]]. Stimulant use carries small preeclampsia and preterm risks [[Cohen2017Placenta]], with no meaningful neurodevelopmental signal [[Suarez2024]]. Status: the decision is individual, and untreated severe ADHD also carries risks.
- Supply. Shortages since 2022 left 71.5% of adult stimulant users with fill problems in 2023 [[Staley2024]]. The causes are debated: DEA quotas versus manufacturing and import collapse [[Currie2026]]. Status: unresolved as of 2026.
