# F · Non-pharmacological treatment: behavioural, psychological, school and combined

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: parenting, therapy, mta.

This pass covers behavioural parent training (BPT; the AAP calls it parent training in behaviour management, PTBM), classroom and school interventions, organisational skills training, social skills training, psychological therapies for adolescents and adults (CBT, meta-cognitive therapy, DBT-based skills groups, mindfulness, coaching), the MTA study and its follow-ups, the SMART sequencing trial, and the "probably blinded" meta-analyses of behavioural therapy, neurofeedback, cognitive training, diet and exercise. Medicines themselves belong to pass E; long-term course and comorbidity belong to pass G; daily-life and nursing advice belongs to pass H.

## Findings

### parenting · What behavioural parent training is
- Behavioural parent training teaches parents behavioural techniques. Some change what happens before a behaviour (antecedents, such as how instructions are given and how the setting is arranged); others reinforce wanted behaviour. Manuals combine dozens of such techniques; one meta-analysis coded 39 [[Dekkers2022]].
- Behavioural parent training is classed as a "well-established" treatment for children with ADHD under Society of Clinical Child and Adolescent Psychology criteria (Established) [[Evans2014]].
- In a meta-regression of 29 RCTs (138 effect sizes, N = 2,345) that coded 39 techniques from treatment manuals, the techniques that changed the setting before behaviour (antecedents) went with better parenting sense of competence and parent mental health, and more reinforcement of wanted behaviour went with larger drops in negative parenting. Higher doses of psychoeducation went with worse parent outcomes (Early evidence) [[Dekkers2022]].
- In the same meta-analysis, parent training had small-to-medium positive effects on all parent outcomes (positive and negative parenting, sense of competence, parent-child relationship, parent mental health) for both unblinded and probably blinded measures [[Dekkers2022]].
- An individual-participant-data meta-analysis of 19 RCTs (n = 1,720 children with ADHD) found that gains in parenting behaviour and parent-child warmth after the programme together explained concurrent gains in child ADHD severity, oppositional behaviour and functional impairment. The links ran in both directions, so cause and effect cannot be separated (Early evidence) [[Psyllou2025]].
- In that IPD meta-analysis, reductions in non-constructive parenting (such as physical punishment) explained more of the effect on child impairment in families that started with higher levels of it [[Psyllou2025]].
- Group and individual formats, and generic and ADHD-specific programmes, have all reported benefits. Experts advise that parents' own preference for a format be respected [[Daley2018]].
- Expert review found no evidence for limiting behavioural treatment to parents with parenting difficulties or children with conduct problems. Treating parental depression may increase the effect [[Daley2018]].

### parenting · How big the effects are, and why blinding matters
- The European ADHD Guidelines Group meta-analysis (32 RCTs, from 2,057 screened records) found these effects of behavioural interventions on ratings by the people closest to treatment (usually unblinded): positive parenting SMD 0.68, negative parenting SMD 0.57, parenting self-concept SMD 0.37, child ADHD symptoms SMD 0.35, conduct problems SMD 0.26, social skills SMD 0.47, academic performance SMD 0.28 [[Daley2014]].
- With probably blinded assessments in that meta-analysis, effects stayed significant for positive parenting (SMD 0.63), negative parenting (SMD 0.43) and child conduct problems (SMD 0.31), but not for ADHD symptoms [[Daley2014]].
- The authors concluded there is blinded evidence that behavioural interventions improve parenting and reduce conduct problems. They found no blinded evidence that they reduce core ADHD symptoms, and no improvement in parents' own mental well-being [[Daley2014]].
- The earlier EAGG meta-analysis of non-drug treatments (54 RCTs, from 2,904 screened records) found significant effects for all psychological treatments (SMD 0.40-0.64) when the raters were closest to treatment. With the best probably blinded rating, the effects of behavioural interventions, neurofeedback and cognitive training became non-significant [[SonugaBarke2013]].
- Practitioner guidance from the same group: on blinded evidence, behavioural intervention "cannot be supported as a front-line treatment for core ADHD symptoms", but it is an important part of multimodal care because it improves parenting and reduces oppositional and non-compliant behaviour [[Daley2018]].
- A different meta-analytic approach (174 behavioural-treatment studies from 114 papers, all designs) gave unweighted effect sizes of 0.83 for between-group studies, 0.70 for pre-post studies, 2.64 for within-group studies and 3.78 for single-subject studies. The authors concluded behavioural treatment is "highly effective" [[Fabiano2009]].
- The 2011 Cochrane review of parent training for children aged 5-18 found only 5 eligible trials (284 participants). Child externalising behaviour fell short of significance (SMD -0.32, 95% CI -0.83 to 0.18; 3 studies, n = 190). Internalising behaviour improved (SMD -0.48, 95% CI -0.84 to -0.13; 2 studies, n = 142). The parent-stress "child domain" improved (MD -10.52, 95% CI -20.55 to -0.48). Risk of bias was unclear or high [[Zwi2011]].
- A 2025 umbrella review of 221 re-estimated meta-analyses found that no non-drug intervention for children and adolescents reached moderate or high certainty evidence for ADHD symptoms, and no intervention, drug or non-drug, had high-certainty long-term evidence [[Gosling2025]].
- The same umbrella review notes that, although most non-drug interventions had low certainty or small-to-moderate effects, guidelines often recommend behavioural parent training and CBT for children with mild-to-moderate ADHD or for associated oppositional behaviour [[Gosling2025]].

### parenting · Preschool children
- AAP 2019, KAS 5a: for preschool children (age 4 to the sixth birthday) with ADHD, the primary care clinician "should prescribe evidence-based PTBM and/or behavioral classroom interventions as the first line of treatment, if available" (grade A, strong recommendation for PTBM) [[Wolraich2019]].
- AAP 2019, KAS 5a continued: methylphenidate "may be considered" if these behavioural interventions do not bring significant improvement and moderate-to-severe disturbance continues. Where behavioural treatment is not available, the clinician weighs the risks of medication before age 6 against the harm of delaying treatment (grade B) [[Wolraich2019]].
- AAP 2019 (full text): there is insufficient evidence to recommend diagnosis or treatment before age 4, other than PTBM, which "does not require a diagnosis to be applied". Clinicians are encouraged to recommend that parents complete PTBM, if available, before an ADHD diagnosis is given [[Wolraich2019]].
- AAP 2019 (full text): parent-child interaction therapy is named as one evidence-based PTBM, delivered as a dyadic therapy for parent and child. Preschool PTBM programmes are typically group programmes and may be relatively low cost, though not always covered by insurance [[Wolraich2019]].
- NICE NG87 rec 1.5.7: offer an ADHD-focused group parent-training programme to parents or carers of children under 5 with ADHD as first-line treatment [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87 recs 1.5.8-1.5.9: if significant impairment persists after group parent training and environmental changes, get advice from a specialist ADHD service with expertise in young children. Do not offer ADHD medication to any child under 5 without a second specialist opinion from such a service [[NICE2018]].
- A US AHRQ comparative-effectiveness review of preschoolers at high risk for ADHD pooled 8 good-quality parent behaviour training studies (n = 424): child behaviour SMD -0.68 (95% CI -0.88 to -0.47), with high strength of evidence and minimal heterogeneity. Only one good study (n = 114) evaluated methylphenidate, so its strength of evidence was low [[Charach2013]].
- A meta-analysis of 16 studies (1,003 children aged 2.5-6 with ADHD or ADHD symptoms) found parent-rated effects (Hedges g) of 0.51 for ADHD symptoms, 0.4 for conduct problems and 0.63 for negative parenting, sustained at 3-12 months. On independent assessment, only negative parenting was significant [[Rimestad2019]].
- In a community RCT of 3-year-olds with a preschool equivalent of ADHD (n = 78), parent training reduced ADHD symptoms and improved mothers' well-being compared with both non-directive parent counselling and a waiting list. 53% of the parent-training group showed clinically significant improvement [[SonugaBarke2001]].

### parenting · School-age children and adolescents: guidelines
- AAP 2019, KAS 5b: for children aged 6 to the 12th birthday, the clinician should prescribe FDA-approved ADHD medication along with PTBM and/or behavioural classroom intervention, "preferably both" (grade A for medication; grade A for PTBM and behavioural treatments with family and school) [[Wolraich2019]].
- AAP 2019, KAS 5b and 5c: educational interventions and individual supports (school environment, class placement, instructional placement, behavioural supports) are "a necessary part of any treatment plan" and often include an IEP or a 504 plan [[Wolraich2019]].
- AAP 2019, KAS 5c: for adolescents (age 12 to the 18th birthday), the clinician should prescribe FDA-approved medication "with the adolescent's assent" (grade A) and is encouraged to prescribe evidence-based training and/or behavioural interventions, if available [[Wolraich2019]].
- AAP 2019 (full text): social skills training has not been shown to be effective for children with ADHD. Mindfulness, cognitive training, diet modification, EEG biofeedback and supportive counselling have too little evidence to recommend them or have shown little or no benefit [[Wolraich2019]].
- NICE NG87 rec 1.5.11: for children aged 5 and over with ADHD and symptoms of oppositional defiant or conduct disorder, offer parents a parent-training programme (in line with the NICE conduct-disorder guideline) as well as group-based ADHD-focused support [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87 rec 1.5.12: consider individual parent training when the family finds group sessions hard to attend (disability, language, intellectual disability, parental ill-health, transport) or has needs too complex for a group [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87 rec 1.5.13: offer medication to children aged 5 and over only if ADHD symptoms still cause persistent significant impairment in at least one domain after environmental changes have been made and reviewed [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87 rec 1.5.14: consider a course of CBT for young people who have benefited from medication but are still significantly impaired, covering social skills with peers, problem-solving, self-control, active listening and dealing with and expressing feelings [[NICE2018]].
- Systematic reviews using Society of Clinical Child and Adolescent Psychology criteria class behavioural parent training, behavioural classroom management, behavioural peer interventions and organisation training as "well-established". Combined training programmes are "probably efficacious" (level 2), neurofeedback "possibly efficacious" (level 3) and cognitive training "experimental" (level 4) [[Evans2014]].
- The 2018 update found the evidence increasingly nuanced by age and programme type, and found children of parents with more education than average over-represented in trials [[Evans2018]].

### parenting · Adolescents
- STAND (Supporting Teens' Autonomy Daily) combines parent-teen skills therapy with motivational interviewing. In an RCT of 128 mostly Hispanic adolescents (78.5%), 85% of families completed it. It improved ADHD symptoms, organisation-time-planning skills, homework behaviour and parenting stress compared with usual care. At 6 months, effects on ADHD severity, OTP skills and parenting stress were maintained [[Sibley2016STAND]].
- In a community effectiveness trial of STAND across 4 clinics (278 adolescents, 82 community therapists), intent-to-treat analyses showed no significant group-by-time effects versus usual care. STAND beat usual care only when therapists were licensed (22% of the sample; parent-rated inattention d = 1.08). It did increase parent participation (d = 0.88) and medication engagement (OR 7.18) [[Sibley2021STAND]].
- In an RCT of CBT for 46 medication-treated adolescents aged 14-18 with persistent symptoms (wait-list crossover), CBT lowered the blind-evaluator-rated parent-report symptom score by a mean 10.93 points (95% CI -12.93 to -8.93). Responders were 50% vs 18% by parent report and 58% vs 18% by adolescent report [[Sprich2016]].

### parenting · Classroom and school interventions
- AAP 2019 (full text): school services that help a student meet age-appropriate expectations independently include daily report cards, training interventions, point systems and academic remediation. ADHD qualifies as a disability under a 504 plan, but qualifies for an IEP (under "other health impairment") only when its severity impairs the child's ability to learn [[Wolraich2019]].
- A meta-analysis of 60 school-intervention studies (1996-2010; 85 effect sizes) found mean behavioural effect sizes that were significant for within-subjects (0.72) and single-subject (2.20) designs but not for between-subjects designs (0.18). Contingency management, academic intervention and cognitive-behavioural strategies were all associated with positive effects [[DuPaul2012]].
- A UK Health Technology Assessment of non-drug interventions in school settings found 54 controlled trials. Across the 36 meta-analysed RCTs, effect sizes ranged from very small (d < 0.20) to large (d ≥ 0.80), with substantial heterogeneity. Daily report cards were the only intervention that educators consistently viewed positively [[Richardson2015]].
- A daily report card (DRC) is a behavioural school intervention in which the teacher rates a child's progress on a small set of individual behaviour goals each day, set up together with parents. A meta-analysis of single-case studies (14 articles, 40 cases) generally showed improvement with the DRC; study quality and class type moderated the results [[Pyle2017,Fabiano2010DRC]].
- In an RCT of 63 children with ADHD in special education, a DRC built from each child's IEP goals improved observed classroom functioning, IEP goal attainment and teacher-rated academic productivity and disruptive behaviour. It did not improve academic achievement or teacher-rated ADHD symptoms [[Fabiano2010DRC]].
- In a larger RCT of 213 students (kindergarten to seventh grade) receiving special education, an IEP-based DRC reduced classroom rule violations and teacher-rated ADHD symptoms and impairment versus school as usual. It did not improve teacher-rated academic performance or progress on special-education goals. Children with lower-quality IEPs gained most [[Fabiano2025]].
- The Child Life and Attention Skills (CLAS) programme, which integrates parent, teacher and child components for the inattentive presentation, beat both parent-focused treatment and usual care on teacher-reported inattention, organisational skills, social skills and global functioning in a 2-site RCT of 199 children aged 7-11 [[Pfiffner2014]].
- When school mental-health staff delivered a 12-week school-home version (Collaborative Life Skills) in a cluster RCT (23 schools; 135 students in grades 2-5), it beat usual services on parent- and teacher-rated ADHD severity, organisation, teacher-rated academic performance and parent-rated ODD symptoms [[Pfiffner2016]].

### parenting · Organisational skills training
- Organisational skills training (OST) teaches children to manage materials, track assignments and plan time. In an RCT of 158 children in grades 3-5 with ADHD, OST beat the wait list on parent-rated organisation (Cohen's d = 2.77) and teacher-rated organisation (d = 1.18). 60% of OST and PATHKO participants no longer met entry criteria for organisational impairment, compared with 3% of controls [[Abikoff2013]].
- In the same trial, PATHKO (training parents and teachers to reward goal attainment, without skills training) also beat the wait list on most outcomes, and OST beat PATHKO only on parent ratings (d = 0.63) [[Abikoff2013]].
- In a randomised trial of 280 middle-school students with ADHD, two brief school-delivered interventions run by school mental-health staff (HOPS and CHIEF; sessions averaging under 20 minutes) both produced large effect-size gains over the wait list on parent-rated homework problems and organisational skills [[Langberg2018]].
- The after-school Challenging Horizons Program (twice weekly for one school year; 326 students in grades 6-8) produced moderate effect-size gains in parent-rated organisation, homework problems and inattention, and small gains in GPA, compared with a mentoring version and community care. Gains lasted into the next school year [[Evans2016CHP]].

### parenting · Social skills training
- The 2019 Cochrane review (25 RCTs, 2,690 participants aged 5-17) found no clinically relevant effect of social skills training on teacher-rated social skills (SMD 0.11, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.22; 11 trials, 1,271 participants). This equals about 1.22 points on a 0-102 scale where the minimal clinically relevant difference is 10 points [[Storebo2019]].
- The same review found no effect on teacher-rated general behaviour (SMD -0.06, 95% CI -0.19 to 0.06). A small effect on teacher-rated core ADHD symptoms (SMD -0.26, 95% CI -0.47 to -0.05) was judged questionable because of high risk of bias, heterogeneity (I² = 69%) and low certainty [[Storebo2019]].
- In a 2-year RCT of 103 stimulant-responsive children aged 7-9, adding an intensive multimodal psychosocial package (parent training, social skills training, psychotherapy, academic help) to methylphenidate did not improve functioning and did not help children stop methylphenidate [[Abikoff2004]].

### mta · The MTA trial design (1999)
- The MTA randomised 579 children aged 7-9.9 with ADHD combined type to 14 months of medication management, intensive behavioural treatment, the two combined, or community care [[MTA1999]].
- Medication management meant titration followed by monthly visits. Behavioural treatment had parent, school and child components, with therapist involvement reduced gradually over time [[MTA1999]].
- High-quality medication management in the MTA meant careful but adequate dosing, methylphenidate usually three times a day, monthly follow-up and communication with schools [[Jensen2001MTA]].
- Outcomes were measured before, during and at the end of treatment, and the combined and medication-management arms kept taking medication at every assessment. Analyses were intent-to-treat [[MTA1999]].
- In the behavioural arm, 75% of children stayed unmedicated during the treatment year, and most stayed so for years afterwards, as later summarised by the SMART trial investigators (full text) [[Pelham2016]].

### mta · 14-month results
- All 4 groups improved substantially. For most ADHD symptoms, combined treatment and medication management beat behavioural treatment and community care [[MTA1999]].
- Combined treatment and medication management did not differ significantly on any direct comparison [[MTA1999]].
- Combined treatment was better than behavioural treatment and/or community care for oppositional/aggressive symptoms, internalising symptoms, teacher-rated social skills, parent-child relations and reading achievement, while medication management alone was not. The authors call these "modest advantages" on non-ADHD outcomes [[MTA1999]].
- Study medication strategies beat community care even though about two-thirds of community-care children received medication during the study [[MTA1999,MTA1999Moderators]].
- Secondary "success" rates (low symptom severity, averaging parent and teacher SNAP-IV ratings at end of treatment) were: combined 68%, medication management 56%, behavioural 34%, community care 25%. With this measure, the advantage of combined treatment over medication alone became significant (p < .05) [[Swanson2001MTA]].
- On a single composite outcome, combined treatment beat all other arms, with effect sizes from 0.28 (versus medication management) to 0.70 (versus community care) [[Conners2001]].
- In children with comorbid anxiety disorders, behavioural treatment beat community care and was no longer statistically different from the medication arms for ADHD-related and internalising symptoms [[MTA1999Moderators,Jensen2001MTA]].
- MTA results held in most subgroups (boys and girls, children with or without prior medication, with or without comorbid disruptive disorders) [[MTA1999Moderators]].
- Reductions in negative or ineffective discipline at home mediated better teacher-rated social skills. In families whose negative discipline fell the most, combined treatment brought teacher-rated disruptive behaviour into the normal range [[Hinshaw2000]].

### mta · 24- and 36-month follow-ups
- At 24 months (10 months after the end of the study treatment; 540 of 579 children, 93%), medication-based strategies still beat behavioural treatment and community care for ADHD and oppositional symptoms, but by less than at 14 months. Combined treatment no longer added benefit over medication management [[MTA2004]].
- At 24 months, the mean doses (methylphenidate equivalents) were 30.4 mg/day for combined treatment, 37.5 for medication management, 25.7 for behavioural treatment and 24.0 for community care [[MTA2004]].
- The effect size for the medication-strategy advantage fell by 50% between the end of treatment and the first follow-up. Children who stopped medication showed the largest deterioration, and children who started it improved [[MTA2004Growth]].
- At 36 months (485 of 579 children, 83.8%; mean age 11.9), the treatment groups no longer differed significantly on any measure [[Jensen2007MTA]].
- Between 14 and 36 months, the share of children taking medication more than 50% of the time rose from 14% to 45% in the behavioural arm, fell from 91% to 71% in the medication-algorithm arms, and stayed at 60-62% in community care [[Jensen2007MTA]].

### mta · 6-, 8- and 16-year follow-ups
- At 6 and 8 years (N = 436), the original groups did not differ significantly on nearly any measure, including grades, arrests and psychiatric hospitalisations. Medication use fell by 62% after the trial ended [[Molina2009]].
- ADHD symptom trajectory over the first 3 years predicted 55% of the 8-year outcomes. Three trajectory classes were identified: 34% improved gradually, 52% improved strongly and stayed improved, and 14% improved at first and then returned to pre-treatment levels (full text) [[Molina2009]].
- At 8 years, 32.5% (132/406) were taking medication on more than 50% of days, compared with 63.3% (257/406) at 14 months (full text) [[Molina2009]].
- At 8 years, MTA participants fared worse than a local comparison group of classmates without ADHD on 91% of the variables tested [[Molina2009]].
- Only 19.7% of MTA participants were girls (114/579) (full text) [[Molina2009]].
- In young adulthood (about 25 years), ADHD symptoms persisted compared with classmates (d = 1.11). In naturalistic subgroups, longer stimulant use was not associated with lower symptom severity, but consistent or inconsistent use was associated with being 2.55 ± 0.73 cm shorter than negligible use [[Swanson2017MTA]].
- At 16 years (mean age 24.7; 476 participants with ADHD and 241 classmates), 50% of the ADHD group still had persistent symptoms. Education, job, income and risky sexual behaviour outcomes were best in classmates, worst in the symptom-persistent group and in between for the symptom-desistent group [[Hechtman2016]].
- By 16 years there had been 10 deaths in the ADHD group and 1 in the comparison group. These are cohort counts, not individual risks [[Hechtman2016]].
- Across 8 assessments to age 25 (N = 558), about 30% had full remission at some point, but 60% of them had a recurrence. Only 9.1% showed sustained recovery, 10.8% showed stable persistence, and 63.8% fluctuated [[Sibley2022]].

### mta · Sequencing: which treatment first? (SMART trial)
- In a sequential multiple-assignment randomised trial (SMART; 146 children aged 5-12, 76% boys, one school year), children started with either low-intensity behavioural treatment (8 group parent-training sessions plus a teacher-run DRC) or low-dose extended-release methylphenidate. Insufficient responders were re-randomised to more of the same or to added treatment of the other type [[Pelham2016]].
- Children who began with behavioural treatment had fewer observed classroom rule violations at endpoint (8.4 vs 12.6 per hour; incidence rate ratio 0.66) (full text) [[Pelham2016]].
- Adding medication after behavioural treatment worked better than adding behavioural treatment after medication on the primary outcome and on ratings of oppositional behaviour [[Pelham2016]].
- More children who started with behavioural treatment needed added treatment at school by the end of the year (67% vs 47%; OR 2.23). At home, most children in both arms needed more treatment (82% vs 88%) (full text) [[Pelham2016]].
- Parents who started with behavioural treatment attended a mean 6 of 8 group sessions, and 69% received an adequate dose. In the medication-first arm, when parent training was added later, 60% of parents attended no sessions and only 11% got an adequate dose (full text) [[Pelham2016]].
- At endpoint, 78% of the behaviour-first group and 69% of the medication-first group had normalised teacher ratings (mean DBD rating of 1.0 or less) (full text) [[Pelham2016]].
- A school year of treatment that started with low-intensity behavioural treatment cost less ($961) than one that started with low-dose stimulant ($1,669) in 2013 US dollars, whichever second step followed [[Page2016]].

### mta · Does medication keep working? (discontinuation trial)
- In a double-blind discontinuation RCT, 94 children and adolescents (8-18) who had taken methylphenidate in regular care for over 2 years either continued (36 or 54 mg/day extended-release) or were tapered to placebo over 3 weeks [[Matthijssen2019]].
- After 7 weeks, mean ADHD-RS scores went from 21.4 to 21.9 with continuation and from 19.6 to 24.7 with discontinuation (between-group difference in change -4.6, 95% CI -8.7 to -0.56) [[Matthijssen2019]].
- CGI-I worsening occurred in 40.4% of the discontinuation group vs 15.9% of the continuation group. Some patients could stop without deterioration, which supports periodic review of the need to continue [[Matthijssen2019]].

### therapy · CBT and skills therapy for adults
- NICE NG87 rec 1.5.16: consider non-drug treatment for adults who have chosen not to take medication, have difficulty adhering, or found medication ineffective or not tolerated [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87 rec 1.5.17: consider non-drug treatment combined with medication for adults who have benefited from medication but are still significantly impaired [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87 rec 1.5.18: as a minimum, offer a structured supportive psychological intervention focused on ADHD, with regular follow-up in person or by phone. It may include elements of, or a full course of, CBT [[NICE2018]].
- In the JAMA RCT of 86 medication-treated adults with persistent symptoms, 12 sessions of CBT beat an attention-matched relaxation-plus-education control on blind-assessor ADHD rating scale scores (difference -4.63, 95% CI -8.30 to -0.96) [[Safren2010]].
- Responders were 67% with CBT vs 33% with control on the ADHD rating scale (OR 4.29, 95% CI 1.74 to 10.58) and 53% vs 23% on the CGI (OR 3.80, 95% CI 1.50 to 9.59). Gains were maintained at 6 and 12 months [[Safren2010]].
- Safren's CBT has 3 core modules: psychoeducation plus organising and planning (calendar and task list, problem-solving, breaking tasks into steps; 4 sessions), reducing distractibility (2 sessions), and cognitive restructuring (3 sessions). Optional sessions cover procrastination and a family-member session, ending with relapse prevention (full text) [[Safren2010]].
- In the earlier pilot RCT (31 adults on stable medication), 56% responded with CBT plus medication vs 13% with medication alone [[Safren2005]].
- Group meta-cognitive therapy (12 weeks of time management, organisation and planning) beat supportive group therapy in 88 adults. Odds ratio for response 5.41 (95% CI 1.77 to 16.55) [[Solanto2010]].
- R&R2ADHD (15 group and individual CBT sessions) beat treatment as usual on blind clinician-rated and self-rated ADHD symptoms in 95 medication-treated adults in Iceland, with gains maintained at 3 months. Comorbid symptoms (anxiety, depression) took longer to improve [[Young2015RR2]].
- A meta-analysis of adult CBT RCTs found CBT beat the wait list (SMD 0.76, 95% CI 0.21 to 1.31; 4 trials, N = 160) and active controls (SMD 0.43, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.71; 3 trials, N = 191) [[YoungZ2020]].
- A meta-analysis of psychosocial treatment for adults (9 RCTs and 3 uncontrolled studies) found post-treatment gains maintained for at least 12 months. Group DBT trials and trials with active matched controls gave lower effect sizes [[LopezPinar2018]].
- The 2025 umbrella review rated CBT in adults as moderate-certainty evidence on clinician ratings (SMD 0.53, 95% CI 0.29 to 0.77), similar to methylphenidate on clinician ratings (0.50, 0.39 to 0.61) (full text) [[Gosling2025]].
- A component network meta-analysis of 113 adult RCTs (14,887 participants) found that CBT (SMD -0.76), mindfulness (-0.79), psychoeducation (-0.77) and cognitive remediation (-1.35) beat placebo only on clinician-rated scales, not self-rated scales. Only stimulants and atomoxetine improved both self-rated and clinician-rated core symptoms at 12 weeks [[Ostinelli2025]].

### therapy · DBT-based group skills training (COMPAS)
- COMPAS randomised 433 adults (18-58) at 7 German centres, 2x2: highly structured DBT-based group psychotherapy or individual clinical management, each with methylphenidate or placebo, for 1 year (weekly for 12 weeks, then monthly) [[Philipsen2015]].
- At 3 months, the blinded-observer ADHD index fell from a baseline mean of 20.6 to 17.6 with group therapy and 16.5 with clinical management, with no significant difference between the two [[Philipsen2015]].
- Methylphenidate beat placebo (adjusted mean 16.2 vs 17.9; difference -1.7, 97.5% CI -3.0 to -0.4). Effects were stable at 1 year. Group therapy beat clinical management on the clinician's global assessment of effectiveness at all visits [[Philipsen2015]].
- In a Swedish RCT of the same DBT-based programme (51 adults), completers who stayed on stable medication had reduced self-rated ADHD symptoms with skills training but not with a loosely structured discussion group [[Hirvikoski2011]].

### therapy · Mindfulness and coaching
- In a multicentre single-blind RCT of 120 adults, 8 weekly group sessions of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy plus usual treatment reduced clinician-rated ADHD symptoms more than usual treatment alone (difference -3.44, d = 0.41), and the effect lasted to 6 months. 27% vs 4% achieved at least a 30% symptom reduction (Early evidence) [[Janssen2019]].
- A meta-analysis of mindfulness-based therapies found effect sizes of d = -0.66 for inattention (10 studies) and d = -0.53 for hyperactivity/impulsivity (9 studies), mostly from small and uncontrolled studies (Early evidence) [[Cairncross2020]].
- The 2025 umbrella review rated the large effects of mindfulness in adults as low or very low certainty evidence [[Gosling2025]].
- ADHD coaching (practical goal-setting and accountability support, often combining CBT and psychoeducation techniques) has only uncontrolled evidence. In 148 college students, an 8-week programme was followed by improvements in study and learning strategies, self-esteem and satisfaction. The authors call for randomised trials (Early evidence) [[Prevatt2015]].
- An early uncontrolled study of 45 adults reported self-rated benefits after coaching. It had no control group (Hypothesis) [[Kubik2010]].

### therapy · Neurofeedback
- A meta-analysis of 13 neurofeedback trials (520 children) found effects on unblinded ratings (total ADHD symptoms SMD 0.35, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.59). Effects were not significant on probably blinded ratings or in trials with active or sham controls [[Cortese2016]].
- The 2025 update (38 RCTs, 2,472 participants aged 5-40) found no effect on probably blinded total ADHD symptoms (SMD 0.04, 95% CI -0.10 to 0.18; k = 20, n = 1,214). There was a small effect in trials using standard protocols (SMD 0.21, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.40; k = 9) [[Westwood2025]].
- In a double-blind RCT of theta/beta-ratio neurofeedback with a realistic sham control (144 children aged 7-10), both groups improved strongly (d = 1.5), but neurofeedback was not superior at treatment end (d = 0.01) or at 13 months (d = 0.23). Responders were 61% vs 54% [[NFCG2021]].
- Neurofeedback is classed as "possibly efficacious" (level 3) in SCCAP-criteria reviews [[Evans2014]].

### therapy · Cognitive training and digital therapeutics
- In a meta-analysis of 16 cognitive training RCTs (759 children), total ADHD effects fell from SMD 0.37 (95% CI 0.09 to 0.66) on unblinded ratings to 0.20 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.40) on probably blinded ratings. Working-memory training alone had no effect on ADHD symptoms [[Cortese2015]].
- The same meta-analysis found that cognitive training improved laboratory working-memory tests (verbal SMD 0.52; visual SMD 0.47) but did not significantly improve academic performance, so trained skills did not transfer well to symptoms or school [[Cortese2015]].
- In STARS-ADHD, a double-blind RCT of a video-game-like digital therapeutic (25 minutes a day, 5 days a week, for 4 weeks) in 348 unmedicated children aged 8-12, the objective attention score (TOVA API) improved by a mean 0.93 vs 0.03 with a control game. Adverse events were mild (frustration 3%, headache 2%). The trial was industry-sponsored (Early evidence) [[Kollins2020]].

### therapy · Diet and supplements
- On probably blinded ratings, free fatty acid supplementation (SMD 0.16) and artificial food colour exclusion (SMD 0.42) stayed significant. Restricted elimination diets did not. Colour-exclusion trials often enrolled children selected for food sensitivity [[SonugaBarke2013]].
- A meta-analysis of 10 omega-3 trials (699 children) found a small but significant effect, larger with higher EPA doses and modest compared with medication [[Bloch2011]].
- In the INCA trial (100 children aged 4-8), a 5-week restricted elimination diet reduced masked-paediatrician ADHD rating scale scores by 23.7 points more than a healthy-diet control (95% CI 18.6 to 28.8). Among diet responders, 63% (19/30) relapsed on food challenge, regardless of IgG levels. The authors discourage IgG-based diets [[Pelsser2011]].
- A meta-analysis estimated that about 8% of children with ADHD may have symptoms related to synthetic food colours. Restriction diets had an effect of g = 0.29 (95% CI 0.07 to 0.53) [[Nigg2012FoodColor]].
- NICE NG87 recs 1.6.2, 1.6.4 and 1.6.5: do not advise eliminating artificial colourings and additives as a general treatment; do not advise or offer fatty acid supplements to treat ADHD in children and young people; tell families there is no evidence on the long-term effects or harms of a "few food" diet [[NICE2018]].
- NICE NG87 rec 1.6.1: stress the value of a balanced diet, good nutrition and regular exercise for children, young people and adults with ADHD [[NICE2018]].

### therapy · Physical exercise
- A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (n = 249) found that short-term aerobic exercise had moderate-to-large effects on attention (SMD 0.84), hyperactivity (0.56), impulsivity (0.56), anxiety (0.66) and executive function (0.58) in children with ADHD. Trials were small and mostly unblinded (Early evidence) [[Cerrillo2015]].
- A 2026 dose-response network meta-analysis (39 RCTs, 1,836 children and adolescents) found closed-skill exercise best for core symptoms (g = -0.54) and open-skill exercise best for executive function (g = -0.68). It estimated a minimum dose of about 600-800 MET-min/week, roughly 20-25 minutes a day of moderate activity (Early evidence) [[Zhang2026Exercise]].
- In a school-based RCT of 202 young children (mean age 6.8), 31 minutes a day of before-school physical activity for 12 weeks reduced home inattention and moodiness more than a sedentary classroom activity. The sedentary activity also seemed to help, and there was no untreated control (Early evidence) [[Hoza2015]].
- A meta-analysis of neuropsychological outcomes across non-drug interventions found the largest average effect for physical exercise (Morris d = 0.93), but it included only 18 studies (Early evidence) [[Lambez2020]].

### Exam hooks
- AAP 2019: for ages 4-5, behavioural parent training and/or behavioural classroom intervention comes first (grade A). Methylphenidate is considered only if these do not work well enough and moderate-to-severe impairment continues [[Wolraich2019]].
- AAP 2019: for ages 6-11, the recommendation is FDA-approved medication plus PTBM and/or behavioural classroom intervention, preferably both [[Wolraich2019]].
- AAP 2019: for ages 12-18, medication is prescribed with the adolescent's assent, and training/behavioural interventions are encouraged if available [[Wolraich2019]].
- NICE NG87: an ADHD-focused group parent-training programme is first-line for children under 5, and medication under 5 needs a second specialist opinion [[NICE2018]].
- Blinding is the key teaching point: behavioural treatment improves parenting and conduct problems on probably blinded measures, but its effect on core ADHD symptoms disappears when raters are blinded [[Daley2014,SonugaBarke2013]].
- MTA (N = 579, 14 months): medication management, alone or combined, beat intensive behavioural treatment and community care on core ADHD symptoms. Combined treatment did not beat medication alone on core symptoms [[MTA1999]].
- MTA "success" rates at 14 months: combined 68%, medication management 56%, behavioural 34%, community care 25% [[Swanson2001MTA]].
- MTA: by 36 months the randomised groups no longer differed, and at 6-8 years they still did not. The early 3-year symptom trajectory, not the assigned treatment, predicted later outcomes [[Jensen2007MTA,Molina2009]].
- MTA: children with comorbid anxiety did relatively well with behavioural treatment [[MTA1999Moderators]].
- SMART sequencing trial: starting with low-intensity behavioural treatment led to fewer classroom rule violations and cost less ($961 vs $1,669 per school year) than starting with medication [[Pelham2016,Page2016]].
- In the SMART trial, parents offered training after their child had started medication rarely attended (60% attended none) [[Pelham2016]].
- Social skills training: the Cochrane review found no clinically relevant effect on teacher-rated social skills (SMD 0.11) [[Storebo2019]].
- Neurofeedback: probably blinded meta-analysis SMD 0.04 (95% CI -0.10 to 0.18), and a double-blind sham-controlled RCT was negative [[Westwood2025,NFCG2021]].
- Cognitive (working-memory) training improves the trained task but not blinded ADHD symptoms or academic results [[Cortese2015]].
- Adult CBT (Safren 2010 JAMA): responders 67% vs 33% against an attention-matched control in medication-treated adults [[Safren2010]].
- COMPAS: a structured DBT-based group was no better than individual clinical management on the primary outcome, while methylphenidate beat placebo [[Philipsen2015]].
- Organisational skills training: 60% vs 3% of children no longer had organisational impairment [[Abikoff2013]].
- Methylphenidate discontinuation after 2 or more years: worsening in 40.4% vs 15.9%, so the need for medication should be reviewed periodically [[Matthijssen2019]].

### In plain words
- Parent training teaches parents practical tools: how to set up the day, give clear instructions and reward good behaviour. It is not a judgment on parenting [[Dekkers2022,Daley2018]].
- For children aged 4 and 5, doctors in the US and UK suggest parent training before medicine [[Wolraich2019,NICE2018]].
- Parent training clearly helps family life and reduces defiant behaviour. Its effect on attention itself is smaller than it first looks, because parents who did the training also rate the results [[Daley2014]].
- For school-age children, guidelines suggest combining medicine with parent training and help in the classroom [[Wolraich2019]].
- A daily report card is a short daily note between teacher and family about a few goals. Teachers tend to like it, and it can reduce classroom rule-breaking [[Richardson2015,Fabiano2025]].
- Learning to organise school materials and homework can make a big difference for some children [[Abikoff2013]].
- In the big MTA study, careful medicine worked best for core symptoms in the first year. Over the following years the groups ended up alike [[MTA1999,Jensen2007MTA]].
- Starting with a low level of behaviour therapy, then adding medicine if needed, worked at least as well as starting with medicine in one trial, and cost less [[Pelham2016,Page2016]].
- Adults with ADHD can benefit from CBT focused on planning, organising and handling distraction, especially alongside medicine [[Safren2010]].
- Mindfulness and coaching may help some adults, but the evidence is weaker [[Janssen2019,Prevatt2015]].
- Brain-training games and neurofeedback have not shown clear benefits when the people judging the results did not know who got the real treatment [[Cortese2015,Westwood2025]].
- Special diets help only a small group of children and should be supervised. Blood IgG food tests are not a good guide [[Pelsser2011,NICE2018]].
- Regular physical activity is healthy and may help attention a little. The studies are small [[Cerrillo2015,Zhang2026Exercise]].
- These study results describe groups of people. They cannot predict how one person will respond.

## Open questions and controversies
- Does behavioural treatment reduce core ADHD symptoms? The EAGG meta-analyses find effects on core symptoms only on unblinded ratings, so they do not support behavioural treatment as a front-line treatment for core symptoms [[Daley2014,SonugaBarke2013,Daley2018]]. A meta-analysis that included within-subject and single-case designs finds large effects (0.83-3.78) [[Fabiano2009]], and SCCAP reviews class BPT and classroom management as well-established [[Evans2014]]. Guidelines still recommend behavioural treatment, for its effects on parenting, behaviour and function [[Wolraich2019,NICE2018]]. Status: unresolved. The dispute turns on which outcomes count and on whether teacher ratings in behavioural programmes can really be blind.
- Combined versus medication alone: in the MTA, the advantage of combined treatment shows up on secondary, composite and categorical outcomes [[Swanson2001MTA,Conners2001]] but not on the main ADHD-symptom comparisons [[MTA1999]]. It was gone by 24 months [[MTA2004]]. Status: modest added value, mainly for non-ADHD outcomes and for some subgroups (comorbid anxiety; families who reduce harsh discipline) [[MTA1999Moderators,Hinshaw2000]].
- Long-term benefit of any childhood treatment: MTA group differences disappeared by 3 years and stayed absent at 6-8 years and in adulthood. Naturalistic medication use was not linked to adult symptom reduction but was linked to shorter adult height [[Jensen2007MTA,Molina2009,Swanson2017MTA]]. A randomised discontinuation trial nevertheless shows that medication is still working after 2 or more years in many children [[Matthijssen2019]]. Status: observational follow-ups cannot answer the question, because treatment was no longer randomised after 14 months.
- Which treatment first? One SMART trial favoured starting with low-intensity behavioural treatment on classroom behaviour and cost [[Pelham2016,Page2016]], but more behaviour-first children needed extra school treatment (67% vs 47%) [[Pelham2016]]. Status: a single trial of 146 children. It has not been replicated at scale.
- Efficacy versus effectiveness: STAND worked in a university trial [[Sibley2016STAND]] but not when delivered by mostly unlicensed community therapists [[Sibley2021STAND]]. School-delivered programmes (HOPS, CLS) are a counterexample [[Langberg2018,Pfiffner2016]]. Status: delivery models and therapist training are an active research area.
- Adult psychological therapy and rater type: CBT in adults has moderate-certainty evidence on clinician ratings [[Gosling2025]], but in a component network meta-analysis the psychological therapies beat placebo only on clinician-rated, not self-rated, scales [[Ostinelli2025]]. In COMPAS, a structured group was no better than individual clinical management [[Philipsen2015]]. Status: active-control choice and blinding of clinicians remain problems.
- Neurofeedback: no blinded effect overall, with a possible small effect for standard protocols [[Westwood2025]]. A double-blind trial found no specific effect [[NFCG2021]]. Status: it is unclear whether a subgroup benefits.
- Diet: a supervised elimination diet produced large masked-rater effects in one trial [[Pelsser2011]], but NICE advises against general elimination of additives and against fatty-acid supplements [[NICE2018]]. Status: possibly useful for a small food-sensitive subgroup (about 8% estimated for food colours) [[Nigg2012FoodColor]].
- Who is in the trials: SCCAP reviewers found children of more educated parents over-represented [[Evans2018]], and only about 1 in 5 MTA participants were girls [[Molina2009]]. Status: results may generalise poorly to girls, low-income families and adults.

- Validation: 67 journal sources were checked against PubMed esummary records on 2026-10-25 (authors, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, PMID, PMCID and DOI copied from the record). 3 school-psychology papers not indexed in PubMed (DuPaul2012, Fabiano2010DRC, Pyle2017) were checked against Crossref, and their abstracts were read via Crossref or OpenAlex. NICE NG87 was validated against its NCBI Bookshelf PubMed record (PMID 29634174), and its recommendation wording was read on nice.org.uk. No source is unverified.
- Abstracts were read for every paper a number was taken from. Full text was read for Wolraich2019 (PMC7067282), Pelham2016 (PMC4930381), Safren2010 (PMC3641654), Molina2009 (PMC3063150) and Gosling2025 (PMC12651917, via Europe PMC); bullets that use full-text-only numbers say "full text".
- Paywalled and not read in full: Sonuga-Barke 2013 and Daley 2014 (table-level probably blinded SMDs and CIs per treatment), MTA 1999 (component details of the behavioural package, such as session counts and the summer treatment programme), Evans 2018 (updated evidence levels). Commonly quoted table values from these papers were therefore left out rather than quoted from memory.
- Dropped: Pelham & Fabiano 2008 (PubMed record has no abstract; nothing cited from it); session-count details of the MTA behavioural arm (not in abstract); a per-treatment blinded SMD list for Sonuga-Barke 2013 (tables not accessible).
- Evidence labels: coaching rests on uncontrolled studies only (Kubik 2010, Prevatt 2015). Mindfulness, exercise and the digital therapeutic are labelled Early evidence. Kollins 2020 was industry-sponsored.
- Key notes: the shared canonical key NICE2018 is used here for NG87 (ADHD) as the brief directs. The Alzheimer's companion's sources.json uses the same key for NG97 (dementia). Sibley2022 and Nigg2012FoodColor match the keys already used for the same works in passes A and B.
- All outcome figures are group or population statistics from trials and cohorts. They are not predictions for an individual, and doses mentioned (for example, the MTA mean doses and the Matthijssen trial doses) are quoted as reported, not as dosing advice.
