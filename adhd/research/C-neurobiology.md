# C · Neurobiology: brain networks, catecholamines and how the medicines act

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: brain, dopamine, stimulants, nonstim.

This pass covers what brain imaging, neuropsychology and pharmacology show about ADHD: structural MRI (ENIGMA mega-analyses, cortical maturation), functional MRI networks (frontoparietal, ventral attention, default-mode), the cerebellum, the dopamine and noradrenaline evidence (PET), executive function and reward/delay effect sizes, and the mechanisms of the stimulant and non-stimulant medicines. Doses below are research doses or label statements quoted as published, never instructions. Prescribing doses, efficacy and adverse-effect rates belong to pass E.

## Findings

### brain · The big picture: what imaging can and cannot say
- The World Federation of ADHD consensus states that most group differences in brain and cognition between people with and without ADHD "are typically small", do not clearly separate ADHD from other disorders, and so are not useful for diagnosing ADHD (Established) [[Faraone2021]].
- The same consensus states that these brain differences are not caused by drug treatment and, for some people, lessen or change as they grow out of the disorder (Established) [[Faraone2021]].
- Posner and colleagues (Lancet seminar) note that most ADHD imaging studies are cross-sectional, so they cannot show whether a brain difference is a cause of ADHD or an adaptation to it [[Posner2020]].
- The seminar also notes that small samples, head-motion artefacts and weak phenotyping have produced poorly reproducible imaging findings, and that the small effect sizes in mega-analyses such as ENIGMA make imaging unlikely to be a diagnostic tool soon [[Posner2020]].
- A study of about 50,000 scans from three large datasets found brain-behaviour associations are much smaller than assumed. At the median neuroimaging sample size of about 25, studies are underpowered and report inflated effects; replication improves only with samples in the thousands (Established) [[Marek2022]].
- Early PET work (1990) found 8.1% lower global cerebral glucose metabolism in 25 never-medicated adults with childhood-onset hyperactivity than in 50 controls (9.05 vs 9.85 mg/min/100 g; P = 0.034). Metabolism was lower in 30 of 60 regions, most in premotor and superior prefrontal cortex [[Zametkin1990]].

### brain · Structural MRI: whole-brain and subcortical volumes
- The ENIGMA-ADHD mega-analysis pooled individual MRI data from 1,713 people with ADHD and 1,529 controls at 23 sites (median age 14 years, range 4–63) [[Hoogman2017]].
- In that sample, five subcortical volumes and intracranial volume were smaller in ADHD: amygdala d = −0.19, accumbens d = −0.15, putamen d = −0.14, caudate d = −0.11, hippocampus d = −0.11, intracranial volume d = −0.10 [[Hoogman2017]].
- Pallidum (p = 0.95) and thalamus (p = 0.39) volumes did not differ between people with and without ADHD [[Hoogman2017]].
- Effects were larger in children (<15 years) than adults (>21 years): accumbens −0.19 vs −0.10, amygdala −0.18 vs −0.14, caudate −0.13 vs −0.07, hippocampus −0.12 vs −0.06, putamen −0.18 vs −0.08, intracranial volume −0.14 vs 0.01. Differences in adults were not significant [[Hoogman2017]].
- Stimulant use (all p > 0.15), symptom scores and comorbid psychiatric disorders did not change the ENIGMA subcortical results. The authors wrote that this refutes the medication effects on brain volume suggested by earlier meta-analyses [[Hoogman2017]].
- The Lancet seminar puts all ENIGMA subcortical effects at Cohen's d < 0.2, that is, very small [[Posner2020]].
- A 2007 meta-analysis of structural MRI (searched to January 2005) found a global reduction across regions (standardised mean difference 0.408, p < .001). The largest differences were in the cerebellum, splenium of the corpus callosum, total and right cerebral volume, and right caudate [[Valera2007]].
- An NIMH longitudinal study (152 children and adolescents with ADHD, 139 controls, 544 scans) found total cerebral volume 3.2% smaller and cerebellar volume 3.5% smaller in ADHD at the first scan [[Castellanos2002]].
- In the same study, previously unmedicated children with ADHD had 5.8% smaller cerebral volume, 6.2% smaller cerebellar volume and 10.7% smaller total white matter than controls [[Castellanos2002]].
- Volume trajectories ran roughly parallel to controls through childhood and adolescence for all structures except the caudate, where the difference disappeared in adolescence. The authors read this as a fixed, non-progressive difference unrelated to stimulant treatment [[Castellanos2002]].
- A voxel-based meta-analysis (14 datasets, 378 people with ADHD, 344 controls) located the most robust grey-matter reduction in the right lentiform nucleus, extending to the caudate. Older age and a higher share of stimulant-treated participants were each linked to more typical volumes there [[Nakao2011]].
- A second meta-analysis found smaller right globus pallidus, putamen and caudate in children with ADHD, and a smaller anterior cingulate in adults. Samples with more treated participants showed smaller differences [[Frodl2012]].
- A comparative meta-analysis (27 ADHD voxel-based datasets: 931 with ADHD, 822 controls) found reduced grey matter and function in the bilateral basal ganglia and insula that was specific to ADHD when compared with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) [[Norman2016]].

### brain · Cortical thickness, surface area and maturation delay
- Shaw and colleagues measured cortical thickness at more than 40,000 points on 824 scans from 223 children with ADHD and 223 typically developing children [[Shaw2007]].
- The median age at which half of cortical points reached peak thickness was 10.5 years in ADHD vs 7.5 years in controls (log-rank P < 1.0 × 10^-20), about a 3-year delay [[Shaw2007]].
- The order of maturation was the same in both groups (primary sensory areas first, high-order association areas later). The delay was most marked in prefrontal regions that control attention and motor planning [[Shaw2007]].
- The Lancet seminar summarises this as a 2–3 year average delay in reaching peak thickness across much of the cerebrum, including prefrontal cortex [[Posner2020]].
- Cortical surface area is also delayed. In the right prefrontal cortex, half of vertices reached peak area at 14.6 years in ADHD vs 12.7 years in typically developing children (234 vs 231 children, 837 scans). Gyrification did not differ [[Shaw2012]].
- ENIGMA's cortical analysis (2,246 with ADHD, 1,934 controls, 36 centres) found lower surface area in children with ADHD, mainly in frontal, cingulate and temporal regions. The largest effect was total surface area, d = −0.21 [[Hoogman2019]].
- Fusiform gyrus and temporal pole thickness were also lower in children with ADHD. Neither surface area nor thickness differed in adolescents or adults [[Hoogman2019]].
- Surface-area differences showed familial effects in unaffected siblings, and in a population sample of 2,707 children, smaller surface area went with more attention problems. This supports ADHD as a continuous trait [[Hoogman2019]].
- In a prospective study of 43 adolescents with ADHD (scans at a mean of 12.5 and 16.4 years), cortex thinned faster in those not taking stimulants than in those taking them: 0.16 vs 0.03 mm/year. The authors found no evidence that stimulants slowed cortical growth [[Shaw2009]].

### brain · Functional MRI: networks beyond the frontostriatal loop
- A meta-analysis of 55 task-based fMRI studies (39 in children, 16 in adults) found under-activation in ADHD mainly in the frontoparietal (executive) network and ventral attention network in children [[Cortese2012]].
- The same meta-analysis found over-activation in children with ADHD in the default, ventral attention and somatomotor networks. In adults, under-activation was mainly frontoparietal and over-activation was in visual, dorsal attention and default networks [[Cortese2012]].
- These differences were present even in people with no comorbid disorders and no stimulant history. The authors concluded that ADHD involves sensorimotor, visual and default networks as well as prefrontal-striatal circuits [[Cortese2012]].
- In inhibition tasks (21 datasets: 287 with ADHD, 320 controls), people with ADHD showed reduced activation in the right inferior frontal cortex, supplementary motor area, anterior cingulate and striato-thalamic areas [[Hart2013]].
- In attention tasks (13 datasets: 171 with ADHD, 178 controls), they showed reduced activation in the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, posterior basal ganglia, thalamus and parietal regions [[Hart2013]].
- Supplementary motor area and basal ganglia under-activation during inhibition was seen only in children, and inferior frontal and thalamic under-activation only in adults. Long-term stimulant use was linked to more typical right caudate activation [[Hart2013]].
- In timing tasks (11 studies, 150 with ADHD, 145 controls), people with ADHD showed reduced activation in the left inferior prefrontal cortex/insula, cerebellum and left inferior parietal lobe. Right dorsolateral prefrontal activation was reduced in medication-naive people but typical in long-term stimulant-treated people [[Hart2012Timing]].
- Compared with OCD, people with ADHD showed a disorder-specific under-activation of the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex during inhibition (33 ADHD fMRI datasets: 489 with ADHD, 591 controls) [[Norman2016]].
- Hypofunction of the cingulo-frontal-parietal cognitive-attention network is among the most consistent imaging findings in ADHD (review) [[Bush2011]].
- Castellanos and Proal argued that frontoparietal, dorsal attention, motor, visual and default networks are all relevant to ADHD, and that network models may explain the moment-to-moment inconsistency in performance (Hypothesis) [[Castellanos2012]].
- A preregistered meta-analysis of 30 resting-state fMRI studies (1,094 with ADHD, 884 controls) found no significant spatial convergence of hyper- or hypo-connectivity across studies, including in sensitivity analyses limited to children [[Cortese2021RestingState]].

### brain · Default-mode network interference
- The default-mode interference hypothesis: slow (<0.1 Hz) spontaneous activity in the resting-state default-mode network can intrude on task-focused processing, producing periodic attention lapses that compete with goal-directed activity. It was proposed as a cause of performance variability in ADHD (Hypothesis) [[SonugaBarke2007]].
- In 20 adults with ADHD and 20 matched controls, the normal negative (antiphasic) relationship between dorsal anterior cingulate activity and default-mode activity was weakened, with lower anterior cingulate–precuneus/posterior cingulate connectivity (p < .0004, corrected) (Early evidence) [[Castellanos2008]].
- The Lancet seminar reports reduced connectivity within the default-mode network in children aged 6–17 years with ADHD, a pattern consistent with delayed maturation of that network (Early evidence) [[Posner2020]].
- The seminar adds that the default-mode network's link with mind-wandering may interfere with attention networks' ability to hold externally focused attention (Hypothesis) [[Posner2020]].

### brain · Cerebellum
- In the 2007 structural meta-analysis, cerebellar regions were among the most often measured regions with the largest reductions in ADHD [[Valera2007]].
- In the NIMH longitudinal cohort, cerebellar volume was 3.5% smaller in ADHD overall and 6.2% smaller in previously unmedicated children. The difference persisted with age (P = .003) [[Castellanos2002]].
- Cerebellar volume, along with frontal and temporal grey matter and caudate volume, correlated with parent- and clinician-rated severity in the ADHD group (r between −0.16 and −0.26) [[Castellanos2002]].
- A meta-analysis comparing ASD, ADHD and dyslexia found reduced grey matter in bilateral cerebellar lobule IX in ADHD (10 studies). This region belongs to the dorsal and ventral attention networks. Each disorder affected different cerebellar regions, with no overlap [[Stoodley2014]].
- The cerebellum is under-activated in ADHD during timing tasks [[Hart2012Timing]] and appears within the right-hemisphere attention network in fMRI meta-analysis [[Hart2013]].
- Del Campo and colleagues describe dopamine and noradrenaline as modulating fronto-striato-cerebellar circuits that support executive function [[DelCampo2011]].

### dopamine · The dopamine hypothesis and PET evidence
- The dopamine deficit theory proposes that low dopamine signalling in frontal-striatal circuits underlies ADHD symptoms and explains the response to stimulants (Hypothesis) [[Swanson2007]].
- In a PET study of 53 never-medicated adults with ADHD and 44 controls (Brookhaven, 2001–2009), both dopamine transporter (DAT; [11C]cocaine) and D2/D3 receptor ([11C]raclopride) binding were lower in ADHD in left-sided regions of the dopamine reward pathway [[Volkow2009]].
- DAT binding potential in the nucleus accumbens was 0.71 in controls vs 0.63 in ADHD (P = .004). In the midbrain it was 0.16 vs 0.09 (P ≤ .001) [[Volkow2009]].
- D2/D3 binding potential in the accumbens was 2.85 in controls vs 2.68 in ADHD (P = .004). In the midbrain it was 0.28 vs 0.18 (P = .01) [[Volkow2009]].
- In the left caudate, DAT was 0.66 vs 0.53 and D2/D3 was 2.80 vs 2.47 (controls vs ADHD) [[Volkow2009]].
- Attention ratings correlated with D2/D3 availability in the accumbens (r = 0.35), midbrain (r = 0.35), caudate (r = 0.32) and hypothalamic region (r = 0.31), and with midbrain DAT (r = 0.37) [[Volkow2009]].
- In secondary analyses (45 with ADHD, 41 controls), trait motivation (MPQ Achievement scale) was lower in ADHD (11 ± 5 vs 14 ± 3, P < 0.001) and correlated with accumbens D2/D3 (r = 0.39) and midbrain D2/D3 (r = 0.41) in ADHD but not in controls [[Volkow2011]].
- In 19 never-medicated adults with ADHD and 24 controls, intravenous methylphenidate released less dopamine in the caudate of people with ADHD (blunted [11C]raclopride displacement). The blunting was linked to inattention and to higher self-reported "drug liking" [[Volkow2007]].
- Contradicting results: an early SPECT study found higher striatal DAT binding in 10 untreated adults with ADHD than in matched controls (1.43 ± 0.18 vs 1.22 ± 0.05, P < 0.001). Binding fell to 1.02 ± 0.23 after 4 weeks of methylphenidate 5 mg three times daily [[Krause2000]].
- A PET study with the selective ligand [11C]altropane (47 treatment-naive, non-smoking, non-comorbid adults with and without ADHD) found higher DAT binding in the right caudate in ADHD [[Spencer2007DAT]].
- A meta-analysis of 9 SPECT/PET studies (169 with ADHD, 173 controls) found striatal DAT density 14% higher on average in ADHD, with large heterogeneity. Density was higher in previously medicated people and lower in medication-naive people [[FusarPoli2012]].
- In 18 never-medicated adults, 12 months of methylphenidate raised striatal DAT availability by 24% (caudate, putamen, ventral striatum; p < 0.01). Controls rescanned after 12 months showed no change. DAT did not differ from controls before treatment [[Wang2013DAT]].
- The authors suggest that DAT up-regulation with long-term treatment could partly explain why DAT studies disagree, and could reduce benefit or worsen symptoms off medication (Hypothesis) [[Wang2013DAT]].
- Swanson and colleagues identified the long-assumed higher-than-normal DAT density in ADHD as the most challenged dopamine finding [[Swanson2007]].

### dopamine · Reward, motivation and the dual-pathway model
- The dual-pathway model describes two partly independent routes to ADHD: poor inhibitory control linked to the mesocortical dopamine branch (prefrontal cortex), and delay aversion, an altered delay-of-reward gradient linked to the mesolimbic branch (nucleus accumbens) (Hypothesis) [[SonugaBarke2002]].
- Sonuga-Barke placed these pathways in a developmental context and argued for multiple neurodevelopmental pathways to ADHD rather than one core deficit (Hypothesis) [[SonugaBarke2005]].
- A triple-pathway study (71 children with ADHD aged 6–17, 71 siblings, 50 controls) found that temporal processing, inhibitory control and delay-related deficits were independent components. They co-occurred no more than by chance, and many children had only one (Early evidence) [[SonugaBarke2010]].
- A meta-analysis of 21 studies (25 case-control comparisons, total N = 3,913) found steeper monetary delay discounting in ADHD (Cohen's d = 0.43; p < 10^-15), with no difference by age, by real vs hypothetical rewards, or by conduct/oppositional comorbidity (Established) [[Jackson2016]].
- A second meta-analysis (37 comparisons, 3,763 participants, 53% with ADHD) confirmed small-to-medium effects favouring small immediate rewards. In the simple-choice paradigm, real rewards almost doubled the odds ratio for people with ADHD [[Marx2021]].
- An fMRI meta-analysis found a medium effect (Cohen's d = 0.48–0.58) for ventral-striatal under-response during reward anticipation in ADHD. In the general population, by contrast, trait impulsivity correlates positively with ventral-striatal activation [[Plichta2014]].
- The dopamine transfer deficit theory proposes that in ADHD, dopamine cell firing transfers poorly from the reward itself to the cues that predict it. This would weaken anticipation and make delayed or partial reinforcement less effective (Hypothesis) [[Tripp2008]].
- The Lancet seminar notes that people with ADHD often find it hard to delay gratification, that symptoms are worse in long, low-stimulation tasks than in engaging ones, and that performance errors exceed controls' mainly at very fast and very slow stimulus rates [[Posner2020]].

### brain · Executive function and cognition: effect sizes
- A meta-analysis of 83 studies (3,734 with ADHD, 2,969 without) found impairment on all executive function tasks, with medium effect sizes (0.46–0.69). The strongest effects were for response inhibition, vigilance, working memory and planning [[Willcutt2005]].
- The same authors concluded that executive function weaknesses are "neither necessary nor sufficient" to cause all cases of ADHD [[Willcutt2005]].
- A review of 34 meta-analyses (253 standardised mean differences) found that 96% favoured controls, with a mean effect of 0.45. Unweighted domain means ranged from 0.35 (set shifting) to 0.54 (working memory) [[Pievsky2018]].
- Weighted by number of studies, the largest domains were reaction-time variability (0.66), intelligence/achievement (0.60), vigilance (0.56), working memory (0.54) and response inhibition (0.52). Differences were larger in children and adults than in adolescents [[Pievsky2018]].
- Meta-analyses that received drug-company funding reported larger effect sizes than those without such funding [[Pievsky2018]].
- In children, working memory effect sizes were larger for spatial than verbal tasks: spatial storage 0.85 (CI 0.62–1.08), spatial central executive 1.06 (0.72–1.39), verbal storage 0.47 (0.36–0.59), verbal central executive 0.43 (0.24–0.62) [[Martinussen2005]].
- A meta-analysis of 319 studies found greater reaction-time variability in children/adolescents (Hedges' g = 0.76) and adults (g = 0.46) with ADHD than in typically developing groups [[Kofler2013]].
- Stimulant treatment reduced this variability (g = −0.74), while non-stimulant medical and psychosocial treatments did not. People with ADHD were not slower on average once variability was accounted for [[Kofler2013]].
- Reaction-time variability is not specific to ADHD: adolescents and adults with ADHD did not differ from clinical control groups, and children only slightly (g = 0.25) [[Kofler2013]].
- A stop-signal meta-analysis found longer stop-signal reaction time in both children and adults with ADHD. Basic reaction time was longer in children but not adults [[Lijffijt2005]].
- A timing meta-analysis (55 studies) found difficulty discriminating very brief (sub-second) intervals, more variable estimates of multi-second intervals, and time estimation and production errors suggesting an "accelerated internal clock" [[Marx2022Timing]].
- Nigg and colleagues showed large overlap in executive function scores between groups with and without ADHD, so only a subgroup has an executive deficit. They suggested defining a provisional "executive deficit type" (Hypothesis) [[Nigg2005Heterogeneity]].
- The Lancet seminar adds that executive function deficits vary greatly from person to person, some people with ADHD show none, and similar deficits occur in many other psychiatric conditions [[Posner2020]].

### dopamine · Noradrenaline, the prefrontal cortex and the inverted-U
- The prefrontal cortex regulates attention by relevance (screening distractions, sustaining and shifting attention) and inhibits inappropriate impulses and emotions. The right prefrontal cortex is especially important for this regulation [[Arnsten2009CNS]].
- Prefrontal networks of pyramidal cells hold goals "in mind" by exciting each other. Small changes in noradrenaline or dopamine have large effects on these networks [[Arnsten2009CNS]].
- Too little catecholamine (fatigue, boredom) or too much (stress) impairs prefrontal function, and optimal levels are released when a person is alert and interested. This is the inverted-U relationship (Established in animal studies) [[Arnsten2009CNS]].
- Even mild, acute uncontrollable stress can cause a rapid and dramatic loss of prefrontal cognitive abilities, and prolonged stress changes prefrontal dendrite architecture (the high end of the inverted-U) [[Arnsten2009NRN]].
- Noradrenaline's beneficial effects act at postsynaptic alpha-2A receptors on prefrontal dendritic spines, strengthening network inputs. Moderate dopamine acts at D1 receptors, weakening irrelevant inputs on other spines [[Arnsten2009CNS]].
- In monkeys, alpha-2A stimulation inhibits cAMP and closes HCN channels, which are co-located with alpha-2A receptors on prefrontal dendritic spines. This strengthens delay-period firing and working memory. Excess cAMP made network firing collapse [[Wang2007Cell]].
- Blocking prefrontal alpha-2A receptors with yohimbine in animals produces an ADHD-like profile: locomotor hyperactivity, impulsivity and poor working memory [[Arnsten2009CNS]].
- In monkeys performing a working memory task, methylphenidate and atomoxetine produced inverted-U dose-response curves: moderate doses improved performance, higher doses did not [[Gamo2010]].
- Their benefits were blocked by an alpha-2 antagonist (idazoxan) or a D1 antagonist (SCH23390). At the cellular level, atomoxetine raised firing for preferred directions ("signal") and lowered firing for non-preferred directions ("noise") [[Gamo2010]].
- In rats, low oral methylphenidate doses (2.0 mg/kg) that produced clinically relevant plasma levels and improved attention and working memory substantially raised noradrenaline and dopamine in the prefrontal cortex, with minimal effect outside it and no locomotor activation [[Berridge2006]].
- Arnsten proposed that methylphenidate and atomoxetine act more potently in the prefrontal cortex than in subcortical structures, which may help explain why properly given stimulant medicines do not lead to misuse (Hypothesis) [[Arnsten2009CNS]].

### stimulants · Rapoport: stimulant effects are not "paradoxical"
- In a 1978 double-blind study, a single dose of dextroamphetamine (0.5 mg/kg) in 14 typically developing boys (mean age 10 years 11 months) markedly reduced motor activity and reaction time and improved cognitive test performance [[Rapoport1978]].
- The authors concluded that this similarity to the response of children with hyperactivity "casts doubt" on models assuming a specific or "paradoxical" stimulant response in ADHD [[Rapoport1978]].
- A 1980 crossover study found that typically developing boys, boys with hyperactivity and typically developing men all showed decreased motor activity, increased vigilance and better learning after dextroamphetamine [[Rapoport1980]].
- In that study, the men reported euphoria while the boys reported feeling "tired" or "different" [[Rapoport1980]].
- A response to a stimulant therefore cannot confirm or rule out ADHD (Established) [[Rapoport1978,Rapoport1980]].
- A meta-analysis of 48 studies (1,409 healthy participants) found only small effects of methylphenidate and amphetamine on inhibitory control and short-term episodic memory, small and inconsistent effects on working memory, and publication bias. The authors judged the overall cognitive benefit in healthy people "probably modest" [[Ilieva2015]].

### stimulants · Methylphenidate: DAT and NET blockade
- US labels state that methylphenidate's mode of therapeutic action in ADHD is not known. It "blocks the reuptake of norepinephrine and dopamine into the presynaptic neuron and increases the release of these monoamines into the extraneuronal space" [[FDA-Ritalin,FDA-Concerta]].
- Methylphenidate is a racemic mixture. The d-threo enantiomer is more pharmacologically active than the l-threo enantiomer [[FDA-Ritalin]].
- Oral methylphenidate blocked DAT in a dose-dependent way 120 minutes after dosing (7 healthy adults): 12% at 5 mg, 40% at 10 mg, 54% at 20 mg, 72% at 40 mg, 74% at 60 mg [[Volkow1998]].
- The estimated dose blocking 50% of DAT (ED50) was 0.25 mg/kg. At the weight-adjusted doses used therapeutically (0.3–0.6 mg/kg), occupancy is likely above 50% [[Volkow1998]].
- Oral methylphenidate did not reach peak brain concentration until 60 minutes after dosing (baboon PET), matching the reported time to peak behavioural effect [[Volkow1998]].
- Methylphenidate also occupies the noradrenaline transporter (NET) in humans: ED50 0.14 mg/kg (11 healthy adults, [11C]MRB PET). Average clinical maintenance doses (0.35–0.55 mg/kg) give 70–80% NET occupancy [[Hannestad2010]].
- Because the NET ED50 (0.14 mg/kg) is lower than the DAT ED50 (0.25 mg/kg), NET blockade may contribute to methylphenidate's therapeutic effect [[Hannestad2010]].
- Oral methylphenidate (mean 0.8 ± 0.11 mg/kg) increased extracellular dopamine in the striatum of 11 healthy adults, shown by a 20 ± 12% drop in D2 receptor availability ([11C]raclopride; p < 0.0005) [[Volkow2001]].
- Volkow and colleagues proposed that methylphenidate amplifies spontaneously released dopamine, so it boosts task-related signals relative to background noise and may make tasks more salient or interesting (Hypothesis) [[Volkow2001]].
- Faraone's review lists methylphenidate actions as DAT and NET inhibition, agonist activity at the serotonin 1A receptor and redistribution of VMAT-2 [[Faraone2018Pharm]].
- In rats, methylphenidate raised noradrenaline and dopamine equally in the prefrontal cortex and also raised dopamine in the striatum and nucleus accumbens to the same level [[Bymaster2002]].
- An fMRI meta-analysis (14 datasets, 212 children with ADHD) found that stimulants most consistently increased right inferior frontal cortex/insula activation, the same region most often under-active in ADHD [[Rubia2014Stim]].

### stimulants · Route and speed: why oral use differs from misuse
- In PET studies, [11C]methylphenidate and [11C]cocaine bound the same striatal sites, but methylphenidate cleared from the striatum much more slowly (90 vs 20 minutes). Brain uptake of methylphenidate was 7.5 ± 1.5% of the dose [[Volkow1995]].
- For both drugs, the subjective "high" tracked fast uptake into the striatum. With methylphenidate, the high faded rapidly even while the drug stayed bound [[Volkow1995]].
- Intravenous methylphenidate, like cocaine, is reinforcing at doses above a DAT-blockade threshold of about 60%. Oral clinical doses also exceed this threshold but rarely produce reinforcing effects [[Swanson2003MPH]].
- Volkow and Swanson identified four variables separating therapeutic from reinforcing effects: dose, pharmacokinetics (fast rise with injection or snorting vs slow rise with oral use), individual sensitivity, and context [[Volkow2003MPH]].
- Their model: reinforcing effects follow large, fast dopamine rises that mimic phasic firing, while therapeutic effects follow slow, steady rises that mimic tonic firing (Hypothesis) [[Volkow2003MPH,Swanson2003MPH]].
- Every US stimulant label carries a boxed warning on abuse, misuse and addiction. It states that misuse can cause overdose and death and that risk rises with higher doses or unapproved routes "such as snorting or injection" [[FDA-Ritalin,FDA-AdderallXR,FDA-Vyvanse,FDA-Concerta]].
- Amphetamine is a Schedule II controlled substance [[FDA-AdderallXR]].

### stimulants · Amphetamine: release, VMAT2 and TAAR1
- US labels describe amphetamines as "non-catecholamine sympathomimetic amines with CNS stimulant activity" whose mode of therapeutic action in ADHD is not known. They block reuptake of noradrenaline and dopamine and increase their release [[FDA-AdderallXR,FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Amphetamines are unusual in mainly releasing catecholamines by a non-exocytic route. The two main mechanisms are redistribution of transmitter from synaptic vesicles to the cytosol and reverse transport out through plasma-membrane transporters [[Sulzer2005]].
- Amphetamines also inhibit uptake and affect exocytosis, transmitter synthesis and metabolism [[Sulzer2005]].
- Most amphetamines are transporter substrates that "pervert" the relay between the vesicular and plasma-membrane transporters, causing monoamine efflux into the synapse. Some amphetamine-related compounds act as transporter inhibitors instead, and both classes are strongly psychostimulant [[Sitte2015]].
- Faraone's review lists amphetamine actions as DAT and NET inhibition, VMAT-2 inhibition and monoamine oxidase inhibition [[Faraone2018Pharm]].
- Trace amine-associated receptor 1 (TAAR1) helps regulate monoamine transporters and mediates part of the action of amphetamine-like stimulants (Early evidence) [[Miller2011TAAR1]].
- Adderall XR contains d- and l-amphetamine salts in a 3:1 ratio. Peak plasma levels occur about 3 hours after the immediate-release form and about 7 hours after the extended-release capsule [[FDA-AdderallXR]].
- The mean elimination half-life of d-amphetamine from Adderall XR is 10 hours in adults, 11 hours in adolescents ≤75 kg and 9 hours in children aged 6–12. For l-amphetamine it is 13 hours in adults [[FDA-AdderallXR]].

### stimulants · Lisdexamfetamine: a prodrug converted in red blood cells
- Lisdexamfetamine is a prodrug of dextroamphetamine. The parent molecule does not bind the noradrenaline or dopamine reuptake sites in vitro [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Lisdexamfetamine is converted to dextroamphetamine and l-lysine mainly in blood, by red blood cell hydrolysis. Substantial hydrolysis occurred even at 33% of normal haematocrit, and it is not metabolised by cytochrome P450 enzymes [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- In children aged 6–12 with ADHD, peak levels came about 1 hour after the dose for lisdexamfetamine and 3.5 hours for dextroamphetamine [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- After a 70 mg radiolabelled dose, about 96% of radioactivity appeared in urine. Of the dose, 42% was amphetamine-related, 25% hippuric acid and 2% intact lisdexamfetamine [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- In laboratory work, intact lisdexamfetamine was absorbed by carrier-mediated transport (likely the PEPT1 peptide transporter) in the small intestine but not the colon, and was hydrolysed only by red blood cells in whole blood [[Pennick2010]].
- Heal and colleagues describe lisdexamfetamine's unusual metabolic route as central to its sustained effect and to its lower potential for recreational abuse [[Heal2013]].

### nonstim · Atomoxetine: selective NET inhibition, prefrontal dopamine
- The Strattera label states that atomoxetine's precise mechanism in ADHD is unknown but "is thought to be related to selective inhibition of the pre-synaptic norepinephrine transporter" [[FDA-Strattera]].
- Atomoxetine binds human NET, serotonin and dopamine transporters with Ki values of 5, 77 and 1,451 nM, showing selectivity for NET [[Bymaster2002]].
- In rats, atomoxetine raised extracellular noradrenaline about 3-fold and dopamine about 3-fold in the prefrontal cortex, without changing dopamine in the striatum or nucleus accumbens [[Bymaster2002]].
- The authors hypothesised that this prefrontal-selective rise in noradrenaline and dopamine mediates atomoxetine's therapeutic effect in ADHD (Hypothesis) [[Bymaster2002]].
- Atomoxetine raised the neuronal activity marker Fos 3.7-fold in the rat prefrontal cortex but not in the striatum or accumbens. The authors predicted no motor or misuse liability [[Bymaster2002]].
- In monkey PET, atomoxetine occupied both NET (IC50 31 ng/mL plasma) and the serotonin transporter (IC50 99 ng/mL). At clinically relevant doses it was estimated to occupy more than 90% of NET and more than 85% of SERT (Early evidence) [[Ding2014Atomoxetine]].
- Atomoxetine is metabolised mainly by CYP2D6. In CYP2D6 poor metabolisers, absolute bioavailability is 94% vs 63% in others, and steady-state accumulation is 3.3-fold vs 1.1-fold. The metabolite 4-hydroxyatomoxetine is equipotent at NET [[FDA-Strattera]].
- Strattera carries a boxed warning on suicidal thoughts and behaviours in pediatric patients aged 6 and older [[FDA-Strattera]].

### nonstim · Viloxazine: NET inhibition plus serotonin receptor actions
- The Qelbree label states that viloxazine's mechanism in ADHD "is unclear" but is thought to be through noradrenaline reuptake inhibition. It binds NET (Ki = 0.13 μM) and is a partial agonist at 5-HT2C receptors (Ki = 0.66 μM) [[FDA-Qelbree]].
- In vitro, viloxazine antagonised 5-HT2B receptors and was an agonist at 5-HT2C receptors. In rat microdialysis, it raised extracellular serotonin in the prefrontal cortex and had moderate NET-inhibiting effects [[Yu2020Viloxazine]].
- Those authors proposed calling viloxazine a "serotonin norepinephrine modulating agent" (Hypothesis) [[Yu2020Viloxazine]].
- A 2026 study confirmed binding to NET (Ki 0.13 μM), 5-HT2C (0.66 μM), 5-HT2B (0.83 μM) and 5-HT7 (1.90 μM). Viloxazine acted as a 5-HT2C partial agonist and a 5-HT2B and 5-HT7 antagonist [[GarciaOlivares2026]].
- At clinically relevant unbound plasma levels (0.4–3.6 μM), viloxazine was estimated to occupy nearly 95% of NET, more than 80% of 5-HT2C and 5-HT2B, and 65% of 5-HT7 receptors. NET occupancy in rats ex vivo was 67–94% [[GarciaOlivares2026]].
- Qelbree was first approved in the US on 2 April 2021 and carries a boxed warning on suicidal thoughts and behaviours [[FDA-Qelbree]].

### nonstim · Guanfacine and clonidine: alpha-2 agonists
- Guanfacine is a central alpha-2A agonist and is not a CNS stimulant. Its label states that its mechanism in ADHD is not known [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- Guanfacine has 15 to 20 times higher affinity for alpha-2A receptors than for alpha-2B or alpha-2C [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- Guanfacine directly stimulates postsynaptic alpha-2A receptors on prefrontal pyramidal-cell spines. This inhibits cAMP signalling and closes ion channels on the spines (HCN channels in monkey studies), strengthening network connections (Established in animal studies) [[Arnsten2010,Wang2007Cell]].
- Arnsten notes that imaging shows reduced prefrontal grey matter, weaker prefrontal connections and altered prefrontal function in ADHD, so medicines that strengthen prefrontal networks may help (Hypothesis) [[Arnsten2010]].
- Guanfacine's blood-pressure and heart-rate effects come from stimulating central alpha-2A receptors, which lowers sympathetic outflow to the heart and blood vessels [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- Extended-release guanfacine (Intuniv) has 60% lower Cmax and 43% lower AUC than immediate-release guanfacine (relative bioavailability 58%), so the two cannot be substituted mg for mg [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- In adults, Intuniv 1 mg had a median tmax of 6.0 hours (vs 3.0 for immediate release) and a half-life of 18 ± 4 hours (vs 16 ± 3). In children and adolescents with ADHD, tmax was about 5 hours [[FDA-Intuniv]].
- Clonidine stimulates alpha-2 adrenergic receptors in the brain and is not a CNS stimulant. Its label states that its mechanism in ADHD is not known [[FDA-OnydaXR]].
- Clonidine lowers sympathetic outflow from the brainstem, reducing peripheral and renal vascular resistance, heart rate and blood pressure [[FDA-OnydaXR]].
- Both extended-release alpha-2 agonists are labelled for ADHD as monotherapy and as adjunctive therapy to stimulants [[FDA-Intuniv,FDA-OnydaXR]].

### brain · EEG theta/beta ratio
- A 2006 meta-analysis of 9 studies (N = 1,498) reported a theta/beta ratio effect size of 3.08 (95% CI 2.90–3.26) for ADHD vs controls, extrapolated to 94% sensitivity and specificity. The authors cautioned that real-world accuracy would likely be lower [[Snyder2006]].
- A 2013 meta-analysis (9 studies, 1,253 with ADHD, 517 without, eyes open at Cz) found much smaller effects: 0.75 for ages 6–13 and 0.62 for ages 6–18, with significant heterogeneity making even these overestimates [[Arns2013]].
- The group difference shrank across the decade of studies because the theta/beta ratio rose in the non-ADHD groups. The authors concluded that excess theta/beta ratio "cannot be considered a reliable diagnostic measure" of ADHD but may have prognostic value in a subgroup [[Arns2013]].
- Saad and colleagues note that the FDA approved the theta/beta ratio as a diagnostic marker of ADHD despite inconsistent findings and reduced specificity, and argue that fixed EEG frequency bands may be a key flaw in youth [[Saad2018]].

### Exam hooks
- ENIGMA-ADHD subcortical effects are small (d ≈ −0.1 to −0.19), largest in the amygdala and accumbens, absent in pallidum and thalamus, and not significant in adults [[Hoogman2017]].
- ADHD cortical maturation is delayed by about 3 years: half of cortical points peak at 10.5 vs 7.5 years, most delayed in prefrontal cortex [[Shaw2007]].
- In ENIGMA, total cortical surface area is the largest cortical effect in children (d = −0.21). No cortical differences were found in adolescents or adults [[Hoogman2019]].
- Brain imaging cannot diagnose ADHD: group differences are small and overlap with other disorders [[Faraone2021,Posner2020]].
- In fMRI, children with ADHD show under-activation of frontoparietal and ventral attention networks and over-activation of the default-mode network [[Cortese2012]].
- Inhibition tasks show reduced right inferior frontal cortex, supplementary motor area and anterior cingulate activation in ADHD [[Hart2013]].
- The default-mode interference hypothesis links attention lapses and variable performance to intrusion of resting-state network activity [[SonugaBarke2007]].
- PET shows lower DAT and D2/D3 availability in the dopamine reward pathway (accumbens, midbrain) of never-medicated adults with ADHD [[Volkow2009]].
- DAT findings conflict: striatal DAT averages 14% higher in ADHD, but higher in previously medicated and lower in medication-naive people [[FusarPoli2012]].
- Oral methylphenidate's DAT ED50 is 0.25 mg/kg [[Volkow1998]]; its NET ED50 is lower, at 0.14 mg/kg [[Hannestad2010]].
- Amphetamines are transporter substrates that release monoamines by reverse transport and vesicular redistribution (VMAT2) [[Sulzer2005,Sitte2015,Faraone2018Pharm]]; methylphenidate works by blocking DAT and NET [[Volkow1998,Hannestad2010]].
- Lisdexamfetamine is converted to d-amphetamine and l-lysine by red blood cells, not by CYP450 [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Atomoxetine, a selective NET inhibitor, raises dopamine as well as noradrenaline in the prefrontal cortex but not dopamine in the striatum or accumbens (rat) [[Bymaster2002]].
- Guanfacine has 15–20 times higher affinity for alpha-2A than for alpha-2B/2C receptors and acts on postsynaptic receptors on prefrontal dendritic spines [[FDA-Intuniv,Arnsten2010]].
- Stimulant effects follow an inverted-U: moderate catecholamine levels improve prefrontal function and too little or too much impairs it [[Arnsten2009CNS,Gamo2010]].
- Stimulants have similar effects in children with and without ADHD, so the response is not "paradoxical" and does not confirm the diagnosis [[Rapoport1978,Rapoport1980]].
- Delay discounting is moderately steeper in ADHD (d = 0.43) [[Jackson2016]].
- Executive function effect sizes are medium (0.46–0.69), and executive deficits are neither necessary nor sufficient for ADHD [[Willcutt2005]].
- Reaction-time variability is raised in ADHD (g = 0.76 in children, 0.46 in adults) and is reduced by stimulants (g = −0.74) [[Kofler2013]].
- The EEG theta/beta ratio effect fell from 3.08 (2006) to 0.62–0.75 (2013) and is not a reliable diagnostic test [[Snyder2006,Arns2013]].

### In plain words
- On average, some brain areas in children with ADHD are slightly smaller or develop a little later. The differences are tiny, and a brain scan cannot tell whether a person has ADHD [[Hoogman2017,Faraone2021]].
- The thinking part at the front of the brain reaches its full thickness about three years later in many children with ADHD. It still follows the normal order of growth [[Shaw2007]].
- In adults with ADHD, the brain size differences seen in children mostly disappear [[Hoogman2017,Hoogman2019]].
- The brain has a "daydreaming" network that should quiet down when we focus. In ADHD it may break in more often, causing short lapses of attention [[SonugaBarke2007,Posner2020]].
- Dopamine and noradrenaline are chemical messengers that help the brain focus and stay motivated. ADHD medicines raise their levels where nerve cells talk to each other [[Faraone2018Pharm,DelCampo2011]].
- The focus centre works best with a "just right" amount of these messengers. Too little, as when bored or tired, or too much, as under stress, both make focusing harder [[Arnsten2009CNS]].
- Many people with ADHD find waiting for a reward harder and prefer a smaller reward now to a bigger one later [[Jackson2016,Marx2021]].
- Stimulant medicines affect people with and without ADHD in similar ways, so feeling more focused on a stimulant does not prove someone has ADHD [[Rapoport1978,Rapoport1980]].
- Taken by mouth at prescribed doses, stimulants raise dopamine slowly. Snorting or injecting them makes dopamine rise fast, and that is what makes misuse risky [[Volkow2003MPH,FDA-Ritalin]].
- Lisdexamfetamine is an inactive form that red blood cells slowly switch into the active medicine [[FDA-Vyvanse]].
- Non-stimulants work differently: atomoxetine and viloxazine mainly block noradrenaline recycling, and guanfacine and clonidine act on noradrenaline receptors in the brain [[FDA-Strattera,FDA-Qelbree,FDA-Intuniv,FDA-OnydaXR]].
- A brain-wave (EEG) measure has FDA approval as a marker for ADHD, but research shows it is not reliable enough to make the diagnosis on its own [[Arns2013,Saad2018]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Delay or deviation? Cortical peaks are delayed by about 3 years in children [[Shaw2007]], and ENIGMA finds child-only differences consistent with delayed maturation [[Hoogman2017,Hoogman2019]]. But adults whose ADHD persists also lack detectable structural differences in these analyses, which the "delay" model does not fully explain; ENIGMA calls for longitudinal studies [[Hoogman2019]]. Status: open.
- Do stimulants change brain structure? Earlier meta-analyses linked treatment to more typical basal ganglia and cingulate volumes [[Nakao2011,Frodl2012]], and one prospective study found less cortical thinning on stimulants [[Shaw2009]]. ENIGMA found no influence of stimulant use on subcortical volumes [[Hoogman2017]], and the consensus statement says brain differences are not caused by drug treatment [[Faraone2021]]. Status: no evidence of harm. Evidence of "normalisation" is observational and inconsistent.
- Is striatal DAT high or low in ADHD? Higher DAT was reported by SPECT and altropane PET [[Krause2000,Spencer2007DAT]]. Lower DAT was reported in the reward pathway of never-medicated adults [[Volkow2009]]. Meta-analysis suggests prior stimulant exposure drives the difference [[FusarPoli2012]], and 12 months of methylphenidate raised DAT by 24% [[Wang2013DAT]]. Status: unresolved, with treatment history a major confounder.
- Is ADHD a dopamine disorder, a noradrenaline disorder, or neither? Dopamine-deficit models [[Swanson2007,Volkow2009]] sit alongside prefrontal noradrenaline/alpha-2A models [[Arnsten2009CNS]]. Del Campo and colleagues judge that the precise cortical and subcortical mechanisms of treatment "remain to be fully specified" [[DelCampo2011]]. Effective drugs also act on serotonin receptors (viloxazine) [[GarciaOlivares2026]]. Status: open. Catecholamine involvement is established, but a single-transmitter cause is not.
- Single core deficit or several pathways? Executive deficits are neither necessary nor sufficient [[Willcutt2005]] and affect only a subgroup [[Nigg2005Heterogeneity]]. Dual- and triple-pathway models add delay aversion and timing [[SonugaBarke2002,SonugaBarke2010]]. Status: heterogeneity is the consensus. Validated neuropsychological subtypes do not yet exist.
- How reliable are network findings? Task fMRI meta-analyses converge on frontoparietal, ventral attention and default networks [[Cortese2012,Hart2013]], but resting-state studies show no spatial convergence [[Cortese2021RestingState]], and typical imaging sample sizes are far too small for reproducible brain-behaviour associations [[Marek2022]]. Status: default-mode interference remains a hypothesis [[SonugaBarke2007]].
- EEG theta/beta ratio: a very large early effect [[Snyder2006]] shrank to a moderate, heterogeneous one [[Arns2013]], yet an FDA-authorised marker exists [[Saad2018]]. Status: not a reliable stand-alone diagnostic test. A possible prognostic role in a subgroup is under study.
- Why do oral stimulants rarely cause euphoria in clinical use when they block DAT as much as intravenous doses? The rate-of-rise (phasic vs tonic) model [[Volkow2003MPH,Swanson2003MPH]] and preferential prefrontal action at low doses [[Berridge2006,Arnsten2009CNS]] are leading explanations. The labels still carry abuse, misuse and addiction boxed warnings [[FDA-Ritalin,FDA-AdderallXR]]. Status: mechanistic hypotheses with human PET support.
- Atomoxetine selectivity: in vitro binding shows NET selectivity [[Bymaster2002]], but monkey PET estimates more than 85% SERT occupancy at clinical doses [[Ding2014Atomoxetine]]. Status: early evidence. Human SERT occupancy has not been confirmed.
