The MTA study & long-term treatment

In the 1990s, 579 children with ADHD were randomly given one of four treatment strategies for 14 months, then followed into their mid-twenties. Scrub through the follow-ups to see the arms separate, then come back together, and why.

14 months · end of treatment

Medication strategies lead

All four groups improved a lot. For most ADHD symptoms, combined treatment and medication management beat behavioural treatment and community care, and did not differ from each other on any direct comparison. On a secondary "success" measure (low symptoms on averaged parent and teacher ratings), the rates were 68% combined, 56% medication, 34% behavioural and 25% community care, and by that measure combined treatment did beat medication alone. Study medication beat community care although about two-thirds of community-care children took medication. 295,438

In plain wordsIn the first year, carefully managed medicine worked best for the core symptoms.

The design: four arms, one randomisation

Children aged 7 to 9.9 with ADHD combined type were randomised to one of four strategies for 14 months, and all outcomes were analysed by intention to treat. Play the randomisation, then pick an arm to read its protocol. The last frame colours the children who met the "success" definition (low symptoms on averaged parent and teacher ratings) at the end of treatment. 295,438

Arms drawn with equal shares; the published arm sizes are not shown. Only 19.7% of participants were girls (114 of 579), which limits how far the results generalise. 289

The design

Randomisation makes the arms comparable

Because a random process, not families or doctors, chose each child's arm, differences at 14 months can be put down to the treatment strategies. Outcomes were measured before, during and at the end of treatment, and the medication arms were still taking medication at every assessment. Pick an arm to read its protocol. 295

In plain words"Success" here means low symptoms at the end of treatment on averaged parent and teacher ratings. It is a group result, not a prediction for any child.

What the MTA did and did not show

Established

What it showed

  • Over 14 months, carefully managed medication, alone or combined, beat intensive behavioural treatment and community care on core ADHD symptoms. 295
  • Combined treatment did not beat medication alone on core symptoms, but gave "modest advantages" on oppositional behaviour, internalising symptoms, social skills, parent-child relations and reading, and on a composite outcome (effect size 0.28 against medication management). 79,295
  • The study medication strategy beat community care even though about two-thirds of community-care children were taking medication. 295
  • Children with anxiety disorders did relatively well with behavioural treatment. 296
Limits

What it could not show

  • Treatment was randomised only for 14 months. After that, families chose freely, so the follow-ups compare self-selected treatment, not the original arms. 221,289
  • It cannot say whether years of medication improve adult outcomes: in naturalistic subgroups, longer stimulant use was not linked to lower adult symptoms, but it was linked to shorter adult height. 442
  • About 1 in 5 participants were girls, and all had the combined presentation. 289,295
  • Register studies find fewer substance-related emergencies during medicated months, while the MTA found no effect on adolescent substance use: different designs, different questions. 290,363

Which comes first? The sequencing trial

A sequential multiple-assignment randomised trial (146 children aged 5 to 12, one school year) started children on either low-intensity behavioural treatment (8 group parent-training sessions plus a teacher-run daily report card) or low-dose extended-release methylphenidate. Children who needed more were re-randomised to more of the same or to add the other treatment. 340

Start with

One trial of 146 children; it has not been replicated at scale. Costs are in 2013 US dollars. 336,340

Sequencing trial · start with behavioural treatment

Behaviour first

Children who began with low-intensity behavioural treatment had fewer observed classroom rule violations at the end of the year (8.4 vs 12.6 per hour; incidence rate ratio 0.66), and 78% had normalised teacher ratings. Adding medication after behavioural treatment worked better than the reverse order. More of these children needed added treatment at school (67% vs 47%). Parents attended a mean 6 of 8 sessions, and 69% received an adequate dose. A school year cost $961. 336,340

In plain wordsStarting with a low level of behaviour therapy, then adding medicine if needed, worked at least as well as starting with medicine in one trial, and cost less.
Exam hookPelham 2016 SMART: behaviour first → fewer rule violations (8.4 vs 12.6/h), cheaper ($961 vs $1,669).

Does medication keep working? Breaks and discontinuation

Only the start and week-7 means are published; the lines between them are drawn straight. Doses are quoted as reported in the trial, not as advice. 279

Matthijssen 2019 · week 7.0

Stopping after 2 years

Mean ADHD-RS went from 21.4 to 21.9 with continued methylphenidate and from 19.6 to 24.7 after tapering to placebo (between-group difference in change −4.6, 95% CI −8.7 to −0.56). Clinical worsening on the CGI-I: 40.4% after stopping, 15.9% with continuation. 279

In plain wordsFor many young people the medicine was still helping after 2 years, but some did fine without it. That is why the need for it is reviewed from time to time.
Exam hookDiscontinuation RCT: worsening 40.4% (stop) vs 15.9% (continue). Supports periodic review. 279
Established

A randomised answer, after 2 years

94 children and adolescents who had taken methylphenidate in regular care for over 2 years either continued (36 or 54 mg/day extended-release) or were tapered to placebo over 3 weeks. Scores worsened more after withdrawal (difference −4.6, 95% CI −8.7 to −0.56), yet some could stop without deterioration, which supports periodic review of the need to continue. 279

NICE NG87

Planned breaks and trial periods

NICE suggests considering a planned break over school holidays to allow "catch-up" growth if medication has clearly affected a child's height, and trial periods of stopping or reducing the dose when the balance of benefit and harm suggests it. 324

Early evidence

Drug holidays in practice

A review of 22 studies found drug holidays in 25% to 70% of families, mostly in school holidays. Longer breaks were linked to better growth, and shorter breaks could reduce insomnia and improve appetite. These were mainly observational studies. 213

Evidence gap

Long-term evidence is thin for every treatment

An umbrella review of 221 re-estimated meta-analyses found "no high certainty, long term evidence" at 52 weeks for any ADHD intervention, and the largest network meta-analysis of ADHD medicines did not find sufficient data at 26 and 52 weeks. 85,178
