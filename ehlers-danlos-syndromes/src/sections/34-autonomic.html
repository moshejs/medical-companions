POTS & the autonomic link

Many people with hypermobile EDS feel dizzy, faint or have a racing heart when they stand. Watch a tilt-table test move a person from lying to upright, see where the published thresholds sit, and see how strong the link with EDS really is.

Rules used: POTS needs a heart-rate rise of at least 30 beats/min (40 at ages 12–19) within 10 minutes, with symptoms, and no sustained systolic fall of 20 mmHg or more. The definition says "sustained" without a fixed count; this tool calls a change sustained when it is present at two or more standing readings. [[Vernino2021,Sheldon2015]]

How often? POTS and orthostatic intolerance in hEDS cohorts

The association is seen again and again in clinics: a review of 10 studies found orthostatic intolerance consistently more common in joint hypermobility or EDS than in controls. But nearly every cohort was recruited in an autonomic or hypermobility clinic, so the percentages are likely upper estimates, not how common POTS is in everyone with hEDS. An international expert review states that incidence, prevalence and natural history "remain unquantified". [[Roma2018,Hakim2017]]

Sources, top to bottom: [[Gazit2003]] [[DeWandele2014Mech]] [[Celletti2020]] [[DeWandele2016]] [[Miller2020]] [[Boris2018]]. Older studies used the labels "joint hypermobility syndrome" and "EDS hypermobility type", which predate the 2017 criteria.

Established

More common in clinic cohorts

Orthostatic intolerance was found in 78% of people with joint hypermobility syndrome vs 10% of controls, and in 74% of women with EDS hypermobility type vs 34% of matched controls, most often as postural tachycardia. In clinic populations the association is consistent; population prevalence is unknown. [[Gazit2003,DeWandele2014Mech,Roma2018]]

Selection bias

Why the numbers run high

People referred to autonomic or hypermobility clinics are those with the most symptoms. The NIH consensus gives "about 20–30%" of POTS patients meeting hEDS criteria and up to 40% of people with hEDS or HSD having orthostatic intolerance and tachycardia, but notes both come from small samples or uncontrolled surveys. [[Vernino2021,Hakim2017]]

From the other side

Hypermobility in POTS clinics

Of 91 adults with POTS assessed with the 2017 checklist, 31% met hEDS criteria and a further 24% had generalised joint hypermobility without hEDS. In 708 children in a POTS programme, symptoms started earlier with hypermobility (median 12.6 vs 13.7 years) and lasted longer before diagnosis (3.3 vs 1.5 years). [[Miller2020,Boris2018]]

Hypothesis

Stretchy veins, pooled blood

The HRS 2015 consensus explains the link through the veins: abnormal connective tissue in dependent blood vessels may let veins distend excessively under ordinary hydrostatic pressure, so more blood pools on standing. This is a mechanism proposed in a consensus statement, not one measured in a controlled hEDS study. [[Sheldon2015]]

Early evidence

Fatigue and the whole-body burden

Autonomic symptom scores were higher in EDS hypermobility type (57.9) than in controls (11.3), classical EDS (32.3) and vascular EDS (29.1). On tilt, fatigue rose by 3.1 points vs 0.5 in controls, and orthostatic intolerance, pain, distress, lower activity and sedative use together explained 47.7% of fatigue severity. [[DeWandele2014ASP,DeWandele2016]]

Early evidence

Chiari and the craniocervical junction

Chiari I malformation and craniocervical instability are reported in hEDS mostly from neurosurgical referral cohorts; a systematic review found only level III–V evidence. The HRS 2015 consensus lists surgical correction of Chiari I among procedures not recommended for routine POTS care and potentially harmful. [[Lohkamp2022,Henderson2017,Sheldon2015]]

What is tried, and where to read more

The guidelines' starting point

The NIH 2019 consensus states there are no FDA-approved medicines for POTS and that evidence for many medicines used is not robust. In hEDS, an expert review reserves drug treatment for moderate-to-severe impairment of daily function after conservative measures have not helped enough. The non-drug measures, the medicines and the strength of evidence for each are laid out step by step in the medicines module. [[Vernino2021,Hakim2017]]

In plain words

What this means day to day

Feeling faint, foggy or having a pounding heart when standing is common in hypermobile EDS, and it is a real, measurable change in the body, not imagined. A simple lying-and-standing test can show it. Not everyone with EDS has POTS, and not everyone with POTS has EDS. [[DeWandele2016,Aziz2025,Miller2020]]
