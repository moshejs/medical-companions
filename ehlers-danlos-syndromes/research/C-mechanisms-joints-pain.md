# C · Mechanisms: tissue, joints, skin, pain & fatigue

Pass date: 2026-10-25. Modules served: joints, pain, skin.

This pass covers why lax, fragile connective tissue produces the clinical picture of the Ehlers-Danlos syndromes (EDS) and hypermobility spectrum disorder (HSD): joint instability mechanics, proprioception and motor control, dislocations, osteoarthritis and bone, the mechanisms and burden of pain and fatigue, headache and craniocervical claims, and the skin in the main types. Classification history, genes, diagnostic criteria, treatment and outcomes belong to passes A, B, D, E, F, G and H and are only touched where a mechanism needs them.

## Findings

### joints · Why the joints are loose: tissue mechanics
- The EDS are heritable connective tissue disorders defined by joint hypermobility, skin hyperextensibility and tissue fragility. The 2017 classification recognises 13 types. Every type except hypermobile EDS (hEDS) needs a causative variant for a definite diagnosis (Established) [[Malfait2017]].
- Most known EDS genes encode the fibrillar collagens I, III and V, the enzymes that modify or process them, or enzymes that modify the glycosaminoglycan chains of proteoglycans. The molecular cause of hEDS is unknown (Established) [[Malfait2020]].
- Connective tissue is everywhere, so EDS features appear to varying degrees in virtually every organ system (Established) [[Malfait2020]].
- Tendons are softer in hEDS. In 25 women with hEDS (then "EDS hypermobility type") compared with 25 matched controls, Achilles tendon stiffness was significantly lower. Passive muscle tension in the plantar flexors was also lower, with a larger maximal joint angle at similar resistive torque (Early evidence) [[Rombaut2012Tendon]].
- Tendon stiffness differs by type. In classical EDS (cEDS, n=7), patellar tendon stiffness and Young's modulus were about 50% of the values in benign joint hypermobility syndrome (n=8) and controls (n=8). Large, irregular collagen fibrils appeared in 4 of 5 cEDS tendons; no tendon pathology explained the hypermobility in benign joint hypermobility syndrome (Early evidence) [[Nielsen2014]].
- Not all flexibility comes from looser tissue. In 9 women with benign joint hypermobility syndrome compared with 9 controls, passive energy absorption of the hamstrings was the same at a given stretch angle. Their greater range came with a higher tolerance to stretch: maximal stretch angle Δ1.35 vs Δ0.98 rad (Early evidence) [[Magnusson2001]].
- In the same study, forearm skin extensibility was 3.6 ± 0.5 cm in benign joint hypermobility syndrome vs 1.3 ± 0.2 cm in controls, and the Beighton score was 6.6 vs 0.4 [[Magnusson2001]].
- Hip capsule thickness tracks generalised hypermobility. In 100 hip-arthroscopy patients, a Beighton score ≥4 went with a capsule thinner than 10 mm, and a score below 4 with a capsule of 10 mm or more. Median thickness was 7.5 mm in women and 12.5 mm in men (Early evidence) [[Devitt2017]].
- Symptomatic hypermobile children have laxity beyond the joints. Fifteen prepubertal children with generalised hypermobility and musculoskeletal complaints were compared with asymptomatic hypermobile peers. They had more skin extensibility (+3.5 mm/15 kPa), lower bone speed of sound (−27.9 m/s) and higher urinary collagen degradation products (Early evidence) [[Engelbert2003]].
- In tenascin-X-deficient EDS, single muscle fibres stiffen through titin, with markedly raised passive tension. This intracellular change partly offsets weakness by raising submaximal force, a proposed response to a more compliant extracellular matrix (Early evidence) [[Ottenheijm2012]].
- A recent review proposes fascia as a central tissue in hEDS/HSD, citing abnormal fascial thickness, interfascial gliding, myofibroblast activation and tissue stiffness. This model is a narrative synthesis, not a tested mechanism (Hypothesis) [[Wang2025Fascia]].

### joints · Proprioception and motor control
- Knee position sense is worse in hypermobility syndrome. In 10 women with hypermobility syndrome, movement-detection thresholds were higher than in matched controls at 30° and at 5° of flexion (both P < 0.001). The normal gain in acuity towards full extension was absent (Early evidence) [[Hall1995]].
- Finger position sense is also impaired. Twelve patients with Beighton ≥4 made larger proximal interphalangeal joint matching errors than controls at all angles (P < 0.0001) and underestimated how far the joint was displaced towards end of range (Early evidence) [[Mallik1994]].
- A meta-analysis of 5 studies (254 people) found poorer lower-limb joint position sense and movement-detection thresholds in benign joint hypermobility syndrome (both p < 0.001). Upper-limb findings were mixed: shoulder p = 0.10, finger p < 0.001 (Established for the lower limb) [[Smith2013BJHS]].
- Children are affected too. Twenty-nine children with hypermobility syndrome (mean age 11.9) had poorer knee kinaesthesia and joint position sense than 37 controls (both P < 0.001), as well as lower knee extensor and flexor torque (both P < 0.001) (Early evidence) [[Fatoye2009]].
- People with EDS are as accurate as controls in locating a hidden hand but less precise, with about twice the scatter at all locations. Scatter did not correlate with chronic pain scores (Early evidence) [[Clayton2015]].
- In 20 children (8–16 years) with joint hypermobility syndrome and hypermobile knees, knee acuity was similar in hyperextension and early flexion. The mean just-noticeable difference was 0.11° (95% CI −0.26° to 0.47°). Three children could not discriminate movements in hyperextension better than chance (Early evidence) [[Pacey2014]].
- Strength drives function, with proprioception as a confounder. In 24 hEDS patients vs 24 controls, muscle strength correlated with the 30-second chair rise (r = 0.67), the 6-minute walk (r = 0.58) and the Health Assessment Questionnaire (r = 0.63). Proprioceptive inaccuracy confounded these associations (Early evidence) [[Scheper2017Strength]].
- Muscle activation strategy changes with generalised joint hypermobility (GJH). In 39 children and 36 adults, agonist activation was lower and the coactivation ratio higher during knee flexion. Torque steadiness was impaired in adults, while maximal strength was not affected (Early evidence) [[Jensen2013GJH]].
- In 27 women with hEDS/HSD and multidirectional shoulder instability, scapular upward rotation was lower than in controls and people with lax but stable shoulders. Infraspinatus, middle trapezius and posterior deltoid EMG activity was higher (Early evidence) [[Spanhove2022]].
- Shoulder exercises move the humeral head more in hEDS/HSD with multidirectional instability. In 27 patients vs 20 controls, the change in acromiohumeral distance was larger during isometric extension, elbow extension and holding a 2 kg dumbbell. External rotation and dumbbell loading translated the head inferiorly (Early evidence) [[Spanhove2020]].
- An 8-week home programme of closed-kinetic-chain exercises improved knee proprioceptive acuity in 16 of 18 people with joint hypermobility syndrome (P < 0.001). Balance, quadriceps and hamstring strength, pain (P = 0.003) and SF-36 scores also improved. This was an uncontrolled study; pass E covers the treatment evidence (Early evidence) [[Ferrell2004]].

### joints · Muscle strength and mass
- Women with hEDS are much weaker without having less muscle. In 43 women with hEDS vs 43 matched controls, knee extensor and flexor strength and endurance were 30% to 49% lower. DXA lower-limb muscle mass was similar, and the authors suggest muscle dysfunction rather than reduced mass (Early evidence) [[Rombaut2012Strength]].
- Weakness persists over time. In 30 women with hEDS/HSD followed for 8 years, maximal strength and strength endurance were lower than controls at both baseline and follow-up (P ≤ 0.007). Muscle density fell only in the hEDS/HSD group (Early evidence) [[Coussens2021]].
- Neuromuscular involvement was common across EDS types (40 patients: vascular, classical, tenascin-X-deficient, and hypermobile with TNXB haploinsufficiency) [[Voermans2009]]:
  - mild-to-moderate weakness: 85%
  - reduced vibration sense: 60%
  - axonal polyneuropathy on nerve conduction: 13%
  - myopathic features on needle EMG: 26%
  - mixed neurogenic-myopathic EMG pattern: 60%
  - atrophy on muscle ultrasound: 50%
  - mild myopathic features on biopsy: 28%

### joints · Balance, gait and falls
- In 22 women with hEDS vs 22 matched controls, sway velocity, sway excursion and sway area were all larger. Gait velocity, step length and stride length were shorter, with bigger dual-task decrements. 95% of patients had fallen in the past year (Early evidence) [[Rombaut2011Balance]].
- Postural control is less automatic. Thirteen adults with hEDS had larger centre-of-pressure displacements and lower sample and approximate entropy than 20 controls, read as needing more attention to stay upright (Early evidence) [[Rigoldi2013]].
- On 3D gait analysis, 12 adults with hEDS had shorter steps, a longer stance phase and lower peak ankle moment and power in terminal stance than 20 controls. Hip and ankle stiffness parameters were reduced (Early evidence) [[Galli2011]].
- Children with hypermobility syndrome (n=29) had more passive knee range than 37 controls. They walked with less knee flexion and more mid-stance knee extension, at the same speed (Early evidence) [[Fatoye2011]].

### joints · Dislocations, subluxations and instability by joint and age
- hEDS is described as likely the most common hereditary connective tissue disorder. Its picture is mainly joint hypermobility, subluxations and dislocations, and skin and soft-tissue features. Many patients report activity-related pain, and some go on to have daily pain (Established as clinical description) [[Tinkle2017]].
- In 189 Italian patients with hEDS/joint hypermobility syndrome (aged 2–73), the Beighton score and hypermobility at other joints fell with age at diagnosis. The rate and sites of joint instability complications did not vary with age, except soft-tissue injuries (Early evidence) [[Morlino2017]].
- In the hEDS natural-history model, the first decade is a "hypermobility" phase, with sprains and strains in about 40%. The second decade is a "pain" phase, with widespread chronic pain, more instability and less hypermobility. A later "stiffness" phase brings a marked loss of hypermobility and lower quality of life (Early evidence; descriptive clinical model) [[Malek2021C,Castori2013]].
- One cited cut-off: after about age 33, most people diagnosed with hypermobility no longer reach a Beighton score of 4 [[Malek2021C]].
- In the general population, the probability of meeting GJH (Beighton ≥4) falls about 5.5% for each additional year of age. In one study of 200 people over 70, nobody scored above 2 [[Malek2021C]].
- In 75 molecularly confirmed people with classical EDS, gJHM complications were [[Ritelli2020C]]:
  - dislocations: 21.3% overall; 34.1% in adults vs 3.2% in children (p = .001)
  - subluxations: 29.3%
  - sprains: 59.1% in adults vs 25.8% in children
  - two or more complications: 73.8% in adults vs 35.7% in children
  - fixed dislocations: 6.7%
  - bilateral pes planus: 82.7%
- In the same cEDS cohort, dislocations were mainly of the shoulder, digits, hip, radius and clavicle, and usually reduced spontaneously [[Ritelli2020C]].
- In 205 children with EDS (6–19 years; 55.6% hypermobile type) seen in orthopaedic or sports clinics [[Stern2017]]:
  - commonest presenting sites: knee 43.4%, back 32.2%, shoulder 31.2%
  - generic "pain" diagnosis, with no specific cause found: 46.8%
  - chronic pain syndrome: 8.3%
  - surgery recommended: 28.8%
- In 58 people with EDS types I–IV, history of dislocation did not differ by type. Back or neck pain was reported by 67.2%, and type III (now hEDS) had the most impaired walking and hand function (Early evidence) [[Stanitski2000]].
- In a US claims database of 91,747 people, a joint hypermobility syndrome diagnosis carried higher odds of patellofemoral instability: aOR 11.40 (95% CI 9.23–14.25) at 1 year and 8.73 at 2 years. The odds were highest in EDS at 1 year: aOR 16.32 (12.54–21.67) (Early evidence) [[Kutschke2024]].
- Shoulder function is poor in hEDS. In a Norwegian survey, the Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index total was 49.9 in joint hypermobility syndrome/hEDS vs 83.3 in controls, with pain (NRS) 6.4 vs 2.7. Neck pain was reported by 90% vs 27%, shoulder pain by 80% vs 37%, and generalised pain by 96% (Early evidence) [[Johannessen2016]].
- The jaw is involved. Among 42 people with GJH from inherited connective tissue disease (Marfan or EDS), 71.4% had temporomandibular disorder symptoms and 85.7% had disc displacement with reduction. Recurrent jaw dislocations were more frequent than in controls (P < .01) (Early evidence) [[DeCoster2005]].
- After total hip arthroplasty, 2 of 13 people with EDS (15.4%) dislocated vs 2 controls (5.1%), with no difference in reoperation (Early evidence; very small sample) [[Guier2020]].
- Prospective evidence links childhood hypermobility to later pain at loaded joints. In the ALSPAC cohort (n = 2,901), hypermobility at 13.8 years (Beighton ≥6; 4.6%) predicted at least moderately troublesome pain at 17.8 years in the shoulder (OR 1.68), knee (OR 1.83) and ankle/foot (OR 1.82). For knee pain the effect was larger with obesity (OR 11.01) than without (OR 1.57) (Established for population hypermobility) [[Tobias2013]].
- In the general population, the link is modest. In a UK survey of 12,853 adults, hypermobile people were 40% more likely to report the most severe chronic widespread pain (RRR 1.4, 95% CI 1.1–1.7). The authors note possible confounding, for example by psychological distress (Established) [[Mulvey2013]].

### joints · Early osteoarthritis: what the evidence shows
- The idea that hypermobility speeds joint wear comes from mechanism studies. Poor proprioception may let joints take biomechanically unsound positions and "may allow acceleration of degenerative joint conditions" (Hypothesis) [[Hall1995]].
- Thumb-base osteoarthritis is the clearest link. Of 50 women with hand OA and thumb-base symptoms, 17 had Beighton ≥4 vs 9 of 94 controls (p < 0.001). Hypermobility clustered with first carpometacarpal OA rather than interphalangeal OA (Early evidence) [[Jonsson1996]].
- Large cohorts point the other way for other joints:
  - In 716 postmenopausal women, a higher Contompasis hypermobility score went with less knee OA (OR 0.48, 95% CI 0.27–0.83) and 3% higher hip BMD (Established for population hypermobility) [[Dolan2003]].
  - In 1,043 people from osteoarthritis families, hypermobility went with lower odds of PIP-joint OA (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.16–0.71), with no rise in OA at any hand joint group [[Kraus2004]].
  - In two cohorts, hypermobility went with less hand and knee OA and lower serum cartilage oligomeric matrix protein [[Chen2008GJH]].
  - In 1,987 adults in the Johnston County cohort, GJH was not associated with radiographic knee, hip or lumbar OA. It was associated with low-back symptoms in white participants only (aOR 1.54) [[Flowers2018]].
- These population studies concern benign hypermobility in older adults, not hEDS. No hEDS cohort with radiographic OA outcomes was found in this pass (see Open questions).
- Early disc degeneration and spondylotic disease with segmental instability are described in EDS in a consensus review, without epidemiological data (Hypothesis) [[Henderson2017]].

### joints · Bone density and fractures
- Bone findings in hEDS and cEDS are mixed:
  - In 23 people with EDS vs matched controls, BMD was lower at the femoral neck (−0.9 SD) and lumbar spine (−0.74 SD). Previous low-impact fracture was reported by 86.9% vs 8.7% [[Dolan1998]].
  - In 50 eugonadal adults with classical or hypermobile EDS vs 50 matched controls, lumbar spine Z-score was −0.45 vs 0.09, trabecular bone score 1.299 vs 1.382, and morphometric vertebral fractures 32% vs 8% (p < 0.05) [[EllerVainicher2016]].
  - In 23 people with type III EDS, a lower femoral-neck BMD disappeared after adjusting for height, weight and physical activity [[Carbone2000]].
  - In 25 premenopausal women with benign hypermobility syndrome, hypermobility raised the risk of low bone mass 1.8-fold (95% CI 1.01–3.38) [[Gulbahar2006]].
  - In 30 Polish women, hEDS did not raise the risk of low bone mass compared with cEDS (OR 0.067, 95% CI 0.0–20.927) [[Kaluza2025]].
- Smaller bones may matter more than density. In 39 women with hEDS/HSD vs 43 controls, cortical bone area and thickness and muscle cross-sectional area were smaller, and fractures more common. Areal and volumetric BMD did not differ, and there was no faster bone loss over 8 years (Early evidence) [[Banica2020]].
- hEDS and HSD did not differ from each other on bone parameters (20 vs 20 women). Both groups had lower cortical bone mineral content and cortical area than 37 controls (Early evidence) [[Coussens2023]].
- In a 2026 hEDS cohort of premenopausal women (mean age 36), BMD was adequate on average, but 50% had had a fracture as adults (Early evidence) [[Vasireddi2026]].
- A review concludes that low BMD and fractures are frequent in some rare types (kyphoscoliotic, arthrochalasia, spondylodysplastic, classical-like). In hEDS and cEDS, BMD is at most mildly lower and higher fracture rates are not clearly shown, though vertebral shape abnormalities seem common (Established) [[Basalom2020]].

### pain · How common and how severe
- Chronic pain in EDS is common and may be severe. According to one study, "nearly 90%" of patients report some form of chronic pain, and pain is often one of the first symptoms (Established) [[Chopra2017]].
- In 273 members of the Dutch EDS patient organisation, chronic pain was highly prevalent and associated with regular analgesic use [[Voermans2010Pain]]:
  - It was more prevalent and more severe in the hypermobility type than in the classical type.
  - Severity correlated with hypermobility, dislocations, previous surgery and poor sleep.
  - It added to functional impairment independently of fatigue.
- In 51 people with various EDS types, chronic pain began early, most often in the shoulders, hands and knees, and was usually refractory to treatment (Early evidence) [[Sacheti1997]].
- In a 2023 online survey (396 analysed, 80% hEDS), 98% reported pain. Sites: neck 76%, lower back 76%, upper back 66%, knees 64%, shoulders 60%, hips 60%. Pain days averaged 6.4 per week in hEDS and 5.9 in generalised HSD (self-selected sample) [[TeranWodzinski2023]].
- In 84 women with HSD or hEDS, 98.8% reported chronic pain. The mean COMPASS-31 autonomic score was 54.45 (self-selected sample) [[CollinsHutchinson2025]].
- Pain burden matches fibromyalgia and exceeds rheumatoid arthritis. In 206 women (72 hEDS, 69 fibromyalgia, 65 rheumatoid arthritis), physical and overall function in hEDS was similar to fibromyalgia and worse than rheumatoid arthritis. Pain severity was higher than in rheumatoid arthritis but lower than in fibromyalgia (Established) [[Rombaut2011Pain]].
- At diagnosis in an Italian registry (n = 96 EDS/HSD), 49% reported moderate-to-severe pain. Independent predictors were older age (OR 1.06 per year), an hEDS rather than HSD diagnosis (OR 6.25, 95% CI 1.31–29.74) and headache (OR 3.97) (Early evidence) [[Morri2026]].
- Classical EDS also hurts, though usually less. In 19 people with cEDS, 32% rated average pain ≥5/10 over the past month [[Colman2023]]. In 75 people with cEDS, chronic disabling pain was infrequent and opioid use very uncommon [[Ritelli2020C]].
- Pain is multifactorial. Listed contributors include subluxations and dislocations, previous surgery, muscle weakness, proprioceptive disorders and vertebral instability. Pain also arises from the gut, the temporomandibular joint, dysmenorrhoea and vulvodynia (Established as a description; the weight of each cause is unknown) [[Chopra2017]].
- A practical topographic classification divides pain in joint hypermobility syndrome/hEDS into [[Castori2012]]:
  - articular limb pain (acute or subacute, and chronic)
  - muscular limb pain (myofascial and fibromyalgia)
  - neuropathic limb pain
  - back/neck pain
  - abdominal and pelvic pain
  - headache

### pain · Nociceptive, neuropathic, nociplastic: the mechanism debate
- Nociceptive input from unstable joints is the starting point in most models. One study proposes that persistent nociceptive input from joint abnormalities "probably triggers central sensitization" in dorsal horn neurons (Hypothesis) [[DiStefano2016]].
- Central sensitisation signs in 27 consecutive people with joint hypermobility syndrome/hEDS (Early evidence) [[DiStefano2016]]:
  - lowered cold and heat pain thresholds
  - increased wind-up ratio
  - positive fibromyalgia screening
  - no damage to the somatosensory nervous system on nerve conduction or laser-evoked potentials
- Generalised hyperalgesia in adults: in 23 women with hEDS vs 23 controls, mean pressure-pain threshold was 2.9 (SD 1.62) vs 5.2 (SD 1.88) kg/cm² (P < 0.001). It was lower even at body sites where patients reported no pain (Early evidence) [[Rombaut2015]].
- In the same study, a predominantly neuropathic pain component was likely in half of the patients [[Rombaut2015]].
- Children show generalised hyperalgesia too. Pressure-pain thresholds were 22% to 59% below normative values in children with hypermobility syndrome/hEDS (Early evidence) [[Scheper2017Hyperalgesia]].
- Generalised hyperalgesia (threshold 30.8 N/cm² for males and 29.0 N/cm² for females) separated hypermobility syndrome/hEDS from asymptomatic hypermobility and controls with an odds ratio of 6.0 [[Scheper2017Hyperalgesia]].
- Adolescents (13–17 years) with HSD/hEDS (n = 37) had lower pressure-pain thresholds than 47 controls at all four muscle groups tested. Exercise-induced hypoalgesia was partly reduced, with unchanged sensitivity in the remote muscle (Early evidence) [[SchubertHjalmarsson2025]].
- Classical EDS shows altered sensation and weaker pain inhibition. Nineteen people with cEDS vs 19 controls had higher vibration detection thresholds, more paradoxical heat sensations and lower mechanical pain thresholds. Conditioned pain modulation was smaller (P = .005 to .046) (Early evidence) [[Colman2023]].
- Functional neurological signs are frequent. Of 24 people with hEDS/HSD and suspected neuropathic pain, 92% showed at least one motor or sensory functional neurological sign; no healthy control showed a motor sign (Early evidence; retrospective, small) [[Fernandez2024]].
- Pain and psychological distress travel together. In 106 people with classical or hypermobile EDS, 42.5% had a psychiatric disorder. Any pain symptom raised the odds of a psychiatric disorder (OR 9.68), while hypermobility and skin features did not (Early evidence) [[Hershenfeld2016]].
- In 59 people with HSD/hEDS, 53% screened positive for major depression (PHQ-9 ≥10). Depressive symptoms correlated with pain catastrophising (ρ = 0.611) and fatigue (ρ = 0.593) (Early evidence) [[Fletcher2025]].
- Fear of movement is common. In 42 people with joint hypermobility syndrome/hEDS, kinesiophobia was present in most of the sample. It correlated with general fatigue severity but not with pain intensity (Early evidence) [[Celletti2013]].

### pain · Small-fibre neuropathy (labelled findings)
- On skin biopsy, all 24 adults with EDS had reduced intraepidermal nerve fibre density consistent with small-fibre neuropathy (20 joint hypermobility syndrome/hEDS, 3 vEDS, 1 cEDS) [[Cazzato2016]]:
  - Pain intensity was moderate (NRS 4–6) in 8 and severe (NRS ≥7) in 11.
  - Sural nerve conduction was normal in all.
  - The cohort was small and selected (Early evidence).
- In a prospective controlled study, 19 of 31 people with hEDS (61%) met criteria for small-fibre neuropathy, and 10 (32%) for postural tachycardia syndrome. Intraepidermal nerve fibre density was reduced at both thigh and lower leg compared with 31 controls (Early evidence) [[Igharo2023]].
- The same study found generalised thermal and tactile hypoaesthesia on quantitative sensory testing. Its authors call small-fibre neuropathy "an important, but not the only" mechanism [[Igharo2023]].
- Studies disagree on nerve damage. Two report histological small-fibre neuropathy [[Cazzato2016,Igharo2023]]. Another found no somatosensory nervous system damage on nerve conduction and laser-evoked potentials, and favours central sensitisation [[DiStefano2016]]. Status: unresolved; none of these studies had large or population-based samples.
- Neuropathic pain screens (DN4) and suspected mast-cell symptoms were equally prevalent in hEDS (n = 61) and HSD (n = 36) (Early evidence) [[AubryRozier2021]].

### pain · Natural history of pain and symptom clustering
- One review proposes a staged natural history of pain, fatigue and headache in joint hypermobility syndrome/hEDS. It compares the condition with functional somatic syndromes such as fibromyalgia and chronic fatigue syndrome (Hypothesis; based on literature review and author experience) [[Castori2013]].
- Adults with hEDS/HSD fell into two severity clusters, separated on 47 of 59 questionnaire items, and the clusters did not mirror either the old or the 2017 criteria (Early evidence; n = 105) [[Copetti2019]].
- hEDS was more severe than HSD on only 3 of 16 items (pain, motricity problems, bleeding). Bone fragility, neuropathic pain and mast-cell symptoms were equally common; the authors propose treating them as one entity (Early evidence; n = 97) [[AubryRozier2021]].
- In 2,695 people seen at a US EDS clinic, 60.6% were diagnosed with HSD and 18.3% with hEDS. HSD patients reported more symptoms and comorbidities above controls (58 of 100 items) than hEDS patients did (20 of 100) (Early evidence; self-report) [[Fairweather2025]].

### pain · Headache, Chiari and craniocervical instability claims
- Migraine is common in joint hypermobility syndrome. Among women, 75% of 28 patients had migraine vs 43% of 232 controls (adjusted OR 3.19, 95% CI 1.24–8.21). Migraine-related disability was nearly three times higher (rate ratio 2.99) (Early evidence) [[Bendik2011]].
- Compared with 66 matched migraine controls, 33 people with joint hypermobility syndrome/hEDS had earlier migraine onset (12.6 vs 17 years) and more migraine days (15 vs 9.3 per month), with higher HIT-6 and MIDAS disability scores (Early evidence) [[Puledda2015]].
- In 18 people with EDS and chronic headache, the types were migraine with aura (4), migraine without aura (4), tension-type (4), mixed (4) and post-traumatic (2). The CNS lesions found did not match the headaches clinically (Early evidence; case series) [[Jacome1999]].
- In 12 people with new daily persistent headache, 11 had cervical spine hypermobility and 10 had widespread hypermobility. This was a small uncontrolled series (Hypothesis) [[Rozen2006]].
- Chiari association: in 2,813 people with Chiari malformation type I, 357 (12.7%) met criteria for EDS or a related connective tissue disorder [[Milhorat2007]]. In that subgroup, upright imaging showed:
  - shorter basion–dens interval (3.6 mm)
  - smaller clivus–axis angle (10.8°)
  - These changes reversed with traction or lying down (Early evidence; single surgical centre).
- Craniocervical instability lacks agreed diagnostic criteria. A systematic review found 16 articles with 78 surgical patients, evidence levels III–V, and 10 different radiographic parameters. It advises fusion only with clear radiographic instability and matching symptoms (Established that the evidence is weak) [[Lohkamp2022]].
- A spine-surgery review finds "a paucity of data" supporting the proposed radiographic parameters, and a lack of high-quality evidence that occipitocervical fusion helps chronic pain in EDS [[Mao2022]].
- A retrospective series of 53 people with EDS who had occipitocervical fusion reported improved headache, neck pain and Karnofsky score at 5–28 months. It had no control group and was run by the operating team, and the authors call for a prospective multicentre trial (Early evidence) [[Henderson2024]].
- In people with hEDS but without radiological instability, the ligaments suspending the spinal cord (myodural bridges) showed collagen fibril damage on electron microscopy (8 vs 8). Ultrasound showed more cardiac-pulsatile and irregular cord motion (20 vs 18 controls). This is proposed as a cause of neck pain and headache (Hypothesis) [[Klinge2021]].
- A consensus review lists migraine, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, Tarlov cysts, tethered cord and dystonia as associations "anecdotally reported, but where epidemiological evidence is not yet available" (Hypothesis) [[Henderson2017]].

### pain · Fatigue: prevalence, scores and drivers
- In 273 Dutch people with EDS, more than three-quarters had severe fatigue. Five possible determinants were found: sleep disturbance, concentration problems, social functioning, self-efficacy about fatigue, and pain severity (Established) [[Voermans2010Fatigue]].
- In an online sample of 117 people with joint hypermobility syndrome/hEDS, 79.5% reported significant fatigue. Five predictors, four of them modifiable, explained 52.3% of the variance [[Krahe2018]]:
  - self-perceived extent of hypermobility
  - orthostatic dizziness with heat and exercise
  - participation in relationships and the community
  - current physical activity
  - dissatisfaction with diagnosis and care
- Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) scores: median 5.9 (IQR 5.00–6.44) in 155 people with hEDS vs 4.0 (2.88–5.00) in 127 with Marfan syndrome. Clinically relevant fatigue was present in 80% vs 31.5%, and everyday fatigue in 72.2% vs 25.2% (Early evidence) [[Udugampolage2025]].
- Checklist Individual Strength total score was 98.2 (SD 18.63) in hEDS vs 45.8 (SD 16.62) in controls (Early evidence) [[DeWandele2016]].
- Orthostatic intolerance is a major driver (80 hEDS vs 52 controls; tilt subsample 39 vs 35) [[DeWandele2016]]:
  - orthostatic intolerance: 74.4% vs 34.3%
  - postural tachycardia on tilt: 41.0% of the hEDS group
  - fatigue rise during tilt: +3.1 vs +0.5 points on a numeric scale
  - orthostatic intolerance, pain, affective distress, low physical activity and sedative use together explained 47.7% of fatigue variance
- Weakness tracks fatigue. In 30 people with EDS, fatigue severity correlated with hand-held dynamometry (r = 0.461), manual muscle testing (r = −0.408) and self-reported weakness (r = 0.603). Weakness and pain each predicted fatigue (Early evidence) [[Voermans2011Fatigue]].
- In women with hEDS, pain and fatigue were "omnipresent" and rose markedly during strength testing (Early evidence) [[Rombaut2012Strength]].
- Deconditioning: habitual physical activity, especially sport participation, was lower in 32 women with hEDS than in 32 matched controls. All eight RAND-36 domains were impaired (Early evidence) [[Rombaut2010]].
- Expert reviews list these contributors to fatigue in hEDS [[Hakim2017Fatigue]]:
  - sleep disorders
  - chronic pain
  - deconditioning
  - cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction
  - bowel and bladder dysfunction
  - psychological issues
  - nutritional deficiencies
- The same review advises ruling out anaemia, hypothyroidism and chronic infection, and notes that no specific drug treats fatigue in EDS (Established as expert consensus) [[Hakim2017Fatigue]].
- The incidence and natural history of autonomic dysfunction in hEDS remain unquantified, though specialist clinics see it often (Early evidence) [[Hakim2017]].
- Twelve adolescents had chronic fatigue syndrome, EDS (6 classical, 6 hypermobile) and orthostatic intolerance. The authors proposed that lax connective tissue lets dependent veins over-distend and pool blood (Hypothesis) [[Rowe1999]].

### pain · Sleep and breathing
- Obstructive sleep apnoea was more common in EDS. Of 100 adults with EDS (46% hypermobile, 35% classical) vs 100 matched controls, 32% vs 6% had OSA (OR 5.3, 95% CI 2.5–11.2). Epworth sleepiness was median 11 vs 7, and OSA severity tracked daytime sleepiness and lower quality of life (Early evidence) [[Gaisl2017]].
- A meta-analysis pooled OSA prevalence at 39.4% (95% CI 28.8–51.1) in EDS and 59.7% in Marfan syndrome. People with EDS or Marfan were about six times as likely to have OSA as the general population (OR 6.28, 95% CI 3.31–11.93). Heterogeneity was high (I² 79% in EDS) (Early evidence) [[Sedky2019]].
- Of 34 people with EDS referred for fatigue and poor sleep, all had sleep-disordered breathing on polysomnography, including flow limitation. Nasal resistance was raised in those tested, and nasal CPAP improved symptoms (Early evidence; referred series) [[Guilleminault2013]].

### skin · Measuring skin hyperextensibility
- Skin hyperextensibility is "formally defined" as stretch beyond a cut-off at 3 of these areas: 1.5 cm at the distal forearms and back of the hands, and 3 cm at the neck, elbows and knees (Established; restating the 2017 criteria) [[Ritelli2020C,Malfait2017]].
- A dermatology review describes the cEDS test as lifting the skin more than 1.5 cm from the dorsal surface of the non-dominant distal forearm or the back of the hand. It notes that 3 cm or more at the elbows, lateral neck or knees suggests cEDS, and that other EDS types show lesser extensibility [[Stembridge2023]].
- Skin hyperextensibility rises with joint hypermobility, and the relation may vary with ethnicity (Early evidence) [[Stembridge2023]].
- Skin in EDS is also thinner on ultrasound. 20-MHz ultrasound showed reduced skin thickness in 21 people with cEDS and 9 with hEDS, most at the chest and distal lower leg; the authors proposed it as a minor criterion (Early evidence) [[Eisenbeiss2003]].
- In hEDS, dermal thickness was significantly lower than in controls at the feet and thorax (p = 0.04). Nailfold microhaemorrhages were more common, and both correlated with the Beighton score (r = 0.4 and r ≥ 0.5) (Early evidence; pilot) [[Sulli2024]].

### skin · How the skin differs by type
- cEDS skin: in 75 molecularly confirmed people, 92% (69/75) had extensive, widened atrophic scars [[Ritelli2020C]]:
  - papyraceous scars: 65.3%
  - haemosiderotic scars: 54.7%
  - "cigarette paper" scars: 40%
  - 16 of 75 (21.3%) did not meet the combined major skin criterion; 6 had no skin hyperextensibility at all
- Other cEDS skin features in the same cohort [[Ritelli2020C]]:
  - piezogenic papules of the heels: 86.7%
  - acquired cutis laxa or premature skin ageing: 41.3%
  - striae: 40%
  - keratosis pilaris: 30.7%
  - molluscoid pseudotumours and subcutaneous spheroids: rare, but "highly diagnostic"
- In 145 molecularly confirmed people with cEDS, 85.6% met both major criteria (GJH, and hyperextensible skin with atrophic scarring). Vascular complications occurred in 1.4% (Established) [[Colman2021]].
- In cEDS, skin hyperextensibility and fragility lead to atrophic scarring and significant bruising (Established) [[Bowen2017]].
- Molluscoid pseudotumours are fleshy, fibrotic nodules up to 3 cm across, found with scars at pressure points such as elbows and knees. Subcutaneous spheroids are hard, pea-sized calcified fat lobules over the ulna and tibia, visible on X-ray (Established) [[Stembridge2023]].
- hEDS skin is milder in every respect than cEDS skin. The 2017 hEDS items include [[Stembridge2023,Malfait2017]]:
  - soft or velvety skin
  - mild hyperextensibility
  - unexplained striae (back, groin, thighs, breasts, abdomen)
  - atrophic scars at two or more sites, without papyraceous or haemosiderin scars
  - bilateral piezogenic papules of the heel
- True skin fragility, such as an open wound from trivial trauma, is not a typical feature of hEDS [[Stembridge2023]].
- vEDS skin: vEDS usually lacks marked skin hyperextensibility. Thin, translucent skin with visible veins is most evident on the back of the hands, upper chest and shoulders; with fine wrinkling of the face, hands and feet it is called acrogeria [[Stembridge2023]].
- vEDS bruising occurs without trauma and at unusual sites such as the cheeks and back. Its scars are narrower than in cEDS (Established) [[Stembridge2023]].
- Thin translucent skin and a bleeding tendency are diagnostic clues in vEDS alongside arterial, bowel and uterine rupture (Established) [[Germain2002,Byers2017]]. In a Dutch cohort of 142, 54.3% of index patients had an appearance "highly suggestive" of vEDS, and in them major events came at a younger age (P = 0.01) [[Demirdas2024]].
- dEDS skin: dermatosparaxis EDS is recessive and caused by ADAMTS-2 deficiency, the enzyme that cleaves the N-propeptide of procollagens I, II and III (Established) [[VanDamme2016]]. Its features [[Stembridge2023]]:
  - extreme, premature skin fragility, with tears at or after birth
  - redundant, sagging skin at wrists and ankles
  - palmar wrinkling and soft, doughy skin
  - severe bruising with subcutaneous haematomas
- Five adults with dEDS (22–42 years) had iatrogenic skin injury, redundant skin folds often needing resection, and multiple fractures. The authors advise careful skin closure and manual handling (Early evidence; case series) [[Angwin2025]].
- Skin electron microscopy in 85 people with suspected EDS types I–IV [[Hausser1994]]:
  - "composite collagen fibrils" with flower-like cross-sections in the classical-to-hypermobile spectrum
  - in clinically type IV (vascular), a dermis thinned to a third of normal, with small-diameter fibrils
- Electron microscopy is no longer specific. Of 177 biopsies at the London EDS service, no finding was specific to a subtype, although collagen "flowers" were present in most genetically confirmed cEDS. 71% of people with a confirmed monogenic EDS had an abnormal biopsy (Established) [[Angwin2020C]].

### skin · Piezogenic papules and striae
- Piezogenic papules are herniations of fat into the dermis that appear on the heels when standing. In 322 healthy Dutch children (4–13 years), 72% had at least one, none were painful, and hypermobile children had the same number as others (Established that the sign is common and non-specific) [[vanStraaten1991]].
- Painful piezogenic pedal papules were seen in 10 of 29 people with EDS (34.5%). Biopsies showed thin subcutaneous fibrous septa with poor fat compartmentalisation (Early evidence) [[Kahana1987]].
- In cEDS, piezogenic papules were found in 86.7% and striae in 40%, with striae more frequent in adults and in females [[Ritelli2020C]].

### skin · Bruising and bleeding: the mechanism
- Easy bruising occurs to a variable degree in all EDS types. It is attributed to fragility of capillaries and perivascular connective tissue. Clotting factors, platelet aggregation and bleeding time are usually normal; only the Hess (Rumpel-Leede) capillary fragility test may be abnormal (Established) [[DePaepe2004]].
- In 141 people with EDS, 41.7% had an abnormal ISTH bleeding score. Platelet function abnormalities were found in 90% of those with an abnormal score and 78% of those without. Bleeding risk rose with the number of platelet abnormalities (OR 5.19 for more than three) (Early evidence) [[Artoni2018]].
- In 52 people with classical, classical-like, hypermobile or vascular EDS vs 52 controls, the mean ISTH-BAT score was 9.1 vs 0.1 and abnormal in 62% vs 0% (Early evidence) [[Kumskova2023]]:
  - commonest symptoms: bruising, muscle haematomas, heavy menstrual bleeding and nosebleeds
  - life-threatening or surgical heavy menstrual bleeding: 14%
- In children, cEDS bruising often appears when they start crawling and walking, on the shins and forearms. The differential includes clotting disorders and non-accidental injury (Established) [[Stembridge2023]].

### skin · Wound healing and dehiscence
- cEDS skin heals poorly. Its hallmarks are delayed wound healing with atrophic scars and easy bruising (Established) [[Malfait2020,Bowen2017]].
- In cEDS, the commonest scar types are papyraceous (65.3%), haemosiderotic (54.7%) and cigarette paper (40%). Haemosiderotic scars are more common in the young [[Ritelli2020C]].
- In dEDS, skin closure technique and manual handling need special care because of iatrogenic tears (Early evidence) [[Angwin2025]].
- No cohort-level wound-dehiscence rate for EDS was found in a validated abstract in this pass (see Open questions).

### Exam hooks
- 13 EDS types (2017). All except hEDS need a genetic variant for a definite diagnosis [[Malfait2017]].
- The gene for hEDS is unknown [[Malfait2020]].
- Skin hyperextensibility cut-off: >1.5 cm at the distal forearm and back of the hand; 3 cm at the neck, elbows and knees [[Ritelli2020C,Stembridge2023]].
- cEDS signature: hyperextensible skin plus widened atrophic (papyraceous, haemosiderotic) scars, present in 92% of confirmed cases [[Ritelli2020C]].
- vEDS skin is thin and translucent with visible veins, not markedly hyperextensible [[Stembridge2023]].
- dEDS: ADAMTS-2 deficiency, extreme skin fragility and redundant skin [[VanDamme2016,Stembridge2023]].
- Easy bruising in EDS comes from capillary and perivascular fragility. Standard clotting tests are usually normal; the Hess test may be abnormal [[DePaepe2004]].
- Piezogenic papules occur in 72% of healthy children, so the sign is not specific [[vanStraaten1991]].
- Knee proprioception is reduced in joint hypermobility syndrome (meta-analysis, 5 studies, 254 people) [[Smith2013BJHS]].
- Women with hEDS have 30% to 49% lower knee strength with normal muscle mass [[Rombaut2012Strength]].
- 95% of 22 women with hEDS fell in the past year [[Rombaut2011Balance]].
- Pressure-pain thresholds in hEDS were 2.9 vs 5.2 kg/cm² in controls, even at pain-free sites: generalised hyperalgesia [[Rombaut2015]].
- hEDS phases: hypermobility, then pain, then stiffness [[Malek2021C,Castori2013]].
- Fatigue: more than three-quarters of people with EDS have severe fatigue [[Voermans2010Fatigue]].
- Orthostatic intolerance: 74.4% in hEDS vs 34.3% in controls [[DeWandele2016]].
- OSA: 32% in EDS vs 6% in matched controls (OR 5.3) [[Gaisl2017]].
- Small-fibre neuropathy on biopsy: 61% of 31 people with hEDS [[Igharo2023]].
- Craniocervical instability has no validated imaging criteria, and the evidence is level III–V [[Lohkamp2022]].
- Hypermobility in the general population does not predict knee or hand OA; thumb-base OA is the exception [[Dolan2003,Kraus2004,Jonsson1996]].
- hEDS and HSD differed on only 3 of 16 severity items [[AubryRozier2021]].

### In plain words
- In EDS, the body's "glue" (connective tissue) is looser or weaker than usual, so joints can move too far and skin can stretch or tear [[Malfait2020]].
- Loose joints do not tell the brain clearly where they are. That makes it easier to land a joint in a bad position and hurt it [[Hall1995,Smith2013BJHS]].
- Many people with hEDS have weaker muscles even when the muscles look normal in size. Strong, well-controlled muscles help hold joints steady [[Rombaut2012Strength,Scheper2017Strength]].
- Kids tend to be very bendy. Teens and young adults often have more pain. Later, joints may stiffen up [[Malek2021C]].
- Long-lasting pain is very common in hEDS. Over time, the nervous system can become "turned up" and feel pain more easily, even in places that are not injured [[Chopra2017,Rombaut2015]].
- Tiredness in EDS has many causes: pain, poor sleep, dizziness on standing, low activity and weak muscles. Many of these can be worked on [[Krahe2018,DeWandele2016]].
- Bruising happens because tiny blood vessels and the tissue around them are fragile. Blood clotting tests are usually normal [[DePaepe2004]].
- In classical EDS, cuts can heal into wide, thin, shiny scars, so wounds need extra care [[Ritelli2020C,Bowen2017]].
- In vascular EDS, the skin is often thin enough to show the veins underneath. This type needs a doctor who knows it well [[Stembridge2023,Byers2017]].
- Small soft bumps on the heels when you stand (piezogenic papules) are common in healthy people too. On their own they do not mean EDS [[vanStraaten1991]].
- The Beighton score and the hEDS checklist help doctors, but they are not a do-it-yourself test. Only a trained clinician can diagnose EDS or HSD [[Malfait2017,Castori2017]].
- Headaches and neck pain are common in EDS. Claims about neck "instability" surgery are still being studied, and experts disagree [[Lohkamp2022,Mao2022]].
- These numbers describe groups of people, not what will happen to any one person.

## Open questions and controversies
- **What causes the pain?** Nociceptive input from unstable joints is the usual starting point. Central sensitisation is supported by generalised hyperalgesia and wind-up, with no nerve damage on electrophysiology [[DiStefano2016,Rombaut2015]]. Peripheral small-fibre neuropathy is supported by biopsy in 61–100% of small selected cohorts [[Igharo2023,Cazzato2016]]. Status: probably mixed and individual; no large population-based study.
- **Is hEDS different from HSD?** Severity is similar except for pain, motricity and bleeding [[AubryRozier2021]]. HSD patients report more comorbidities than hEDS in a large clinic sample [[Fairweather2025]]. Bone parameters do not differ [[Coussens2023]]. Status: the 2017 split is a research and diagnostic boundary; mechanisms look shared.
- **Does hypermobility cause early osteoarthritis?** It may, through proprioceptive failure (hypothesis) [[Hall1995]], and it does associate with thumb-base OA [[Jonsson1996]]. Against it: knee and hand OA are less common in hypermobile older adults [[Dolan2003,Kraus2004,Chen2008GJH]]. Status: no radiographic OA cohort in hEDS or HSD; population data concern mild hypermobility.
- **Bone:** lower BMD and more vertebral fractures in some studies [[EllerVainicher2016,Dolan1998]]; no BMD difference in others [[Carbone2000,Kaluza2025]]; smaller cortical bone with normal BMD, possibly from low loading [[Banica2020]]. Status: fracture risk may come from bone size, falls and poor proprioception rather than density.
- **Craniocervical instability and fusion:** proponents report improvement after occipitocervical fusion [[Henderson2024]]. Systematic and narrative reviews find level III–V evidence, no agreed radiographic definition, and no high-quality outcome data [[Lohkamp2022,Mao2022]]. Status: unresolved; a prospective multicentre trial has been called for.
- **Chiari and EDS:** 12.7% of a single surgical centre's Chiari cohort met connective tissue disorder criteria [[Milhorat2007]]. The consensus review calls many neurological associations anecdotal [[Henderson2017]]. Status: association reported; prevalence outside referral centres unknown.
- **Fascia-centred model:** proposed in a 2025 narrative review [[Wang2025Fascia]]. Status: hypothesis, not tested.
- **Skin-measurement site:** Ritelli2020C restates the 2017 criteria as the "distal part of the forearms and the dorsum of the hands". Stembridge2023 says "dorsal surface of the non-dominant distal forearm". The brief's "volar forearm" wording could not be checked against the Malfait2017 full text in this pass [[Ritelli2020C,Stembridge2023,Malfait2017]].
- **Wound dehiscence rates:** no cohort-level surgical wound dehiscence rate for any EDS type was found in a validated abstract. Statements stay qualitative [[Malfait2020,Bowen2017]].
- **Fatigue and orthostatic intolerance:** orthostatic intolerance explains part of the fatigue in hEDS [[DeWandele2016,Krahe2018]]. Whether lax venous connective tissue causes it remains a hypothesis [[Rowe1999,Hakim2017]].

- Validation: all 116 sources were checked against Europe PMC core records (EXT_ID:<PMID> AND SRC:MED). Authors (first six, then et al.), title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages, PMID, PMCID and DOI were taken from those records. The record is the validating service for each line. PubMed E-utilities were rate-limited by parallel passes, so Europe PMC was used throughout.
- Facts were read from the abstracts in those records. Four open-access full texts were read for numbers not in the abstracts:
  - Ritelli2020C: cEDS feature rates and the skin-stretch cut-offs
  - Stembridge2023: skin features by type, and the hEDS skin items restated from the 2017 criteria
  - Malek2021C: the hEDS three-phase description, the age 33 cut-off and the 5.5% per year decline
  - AubryRozier2021: hEDS vs HSD

The Wiley site and the PDF route were blocked for automated access, and NCBI Bookshelf (GeneReviews) returned a CAPTCHA. Statements credited to these papers are limited to their abstracts, or to criteria as restated in the open-access papers above.
