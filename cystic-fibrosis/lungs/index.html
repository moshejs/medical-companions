Part 2 · How CF affects the body

Lungs: mucus, infection & bronchiectasis

Lung disease causes most of the illness and deaths in CF. It starts with mucus that does not clear, which invites infection and a fierce neutrophil response, and over years that response widens and scars the airways (bronchiectasis). Scrub through a lifetime as the pre-modulator studies recorded it, then step round the cycle that drives it.

Lung disease in CF works through bronchiectasis, small-airway obstruction and progressive loss of breathing capacity 115,364. Failure of CFTR causes mucus retention and chronic infection, which drive airway inflammation that harms the lungs 115. Most of the figures below come from cohorts followed before highly effective CFTR modulators; the last part shows what has changed since.

Infancy: mucus and inflammation, before infection

Neutrophils, free neutrophil elastase and IL-8 were already raised in screened infants as young as 4 weeks, even with negative cultures 236. In AREST CF, bronchiectasis was present in 29.3% of infants at 3 months and seen as young as 10 weeks 426. Mucus "flakes" and neutrophils were raised in preschoolers with little or no infection, so mucus burden appears to come first Early evidence 119.

The vicious cycle: obstruction, infection, inflammation

Each turn of the cycle feeds the next: mucus that does not clear shelters bacteria, bacteria draw in neutrophils, and neutrophil products damage the airway wall, which then clears mucus even less well. Step round it.

Obstruction: mucus that does not clear

In AREST CF preschoolers, total mucins and MUC5B/MUC5AC mucus flakes were raised with or without infection, and children with normal CTs already had raised flakes and neutrophils Early evidence 119. The flakes did not dissolve with N-acetylcysteine or DNase, only with an experimental reducing agent 119. Thick mucus on CF epithelium develops steep oxygen gradients even before infection 503.

Pulmonary exacerbations

An exacerbation is a spell when breathing symptoms get worse, often treated with antibiotics. Exacerbations are among the most important events in the course of CF 172. The "Fuchs criteria", from the 1994 dornase alfa trial, define one as antibiotic treatment plus at least 4 of 12 respiratory signs and symptoms; a later analysis found that more than a third of respiratory events met 4 or more criteria without investigators calling them exacerbations 480. A prospective score from symptoms and chest findings predicted exacerbations with 86% sensitivity and 86% specificity 380.

How often

27%

of people on placebo had at least one exacerbation needing parenteral antibiotics over 24 weeks in the dornase alfa trial (968 people), against 22% and 19% on once- and twice-daily dornase 158.

Recovery

1 in 4

of 8,479 IV-treated exacerbations (US registry, 2003–2006) did not get back to 90% of the pre-exacerbation best FEV1 within 3 months. Female sex, pancreatic insufficiency, undernutrition, persistent P. aeruginosa, B. cepacia complex or MRSA, ABPA and a bigger initial FEV1 drop made this more likely 398.

Long-term impact

HR 4.05

for death or lung transplant over 3 years in 446 Ontario adults with more than 2 exacerbations a year versus fewer than 1 (95% CI 1.15–14.28); HR 1.55 for a 5% FEV1 decline 92.

Bleeding, air leaks, fungus and sinuses

Massive haemoptysis

In US registry data for 1990–1999 (28,858 people), massive haemoptysis (coughing up a large amount of blood) had an average annual incidence of 0.87% and occurred in 4.1% overall. Mean age was 24.2 years and nearly 60% had FEV1 below 40% predicted. It was linked to S. aureus (OR 1.3) and diabetes (OR 1.1), and followed by higher 2-year mortality 150.

Pneumothorax

Spontaneous pneumothorax (air leaking around a lung) had an annual incidence of 0.64% and occurred in 3.4% overall; mean age 21.9 years, and nearly 75% had FEV1 below 40%. Risk was higher with P. aeruginosa (OR 2.3), B. cepacia (OR 1.8), FEV1 below 30% (OR 1.5), ABPA (OR 1.5) and prior massive haemoptysis (OR 1.4) 151.

How these are managed

CF Foundation recommendations on haemoptysis and pneumothorax were reached by Delphi expert consensus, because the data were too thin for evidence-based ones 153. Both were followed by higher 2-year mortality in the registry analyses 150,151.

ABPA

Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis is an allergic reaction to the Aspergillus mould. In the European registry (12,447 people) prevalence was 7.8%, low under 6 years and about 10% after; it rose with worse lung function and was linked to pneumothorax and massive haemoptysis 273. The US reported only 2%, probably under-diagnosis 164. Its features overlap with CF lung disease itself, so a consensus conference set CF-specific criteria 448.

Sinuses and nasal polyps

Chronic rhinosinusitis is highly prevalent in CF; CF polyps are more neutrophilic, with fewer eosinophils, than other polyps 194. On endoscopy, 37% of 211 adults had nasal polyps 191. One year of ETI cut the median Lund-Mackay sinus CT score from 5.8 to 3.3 416.

Neutrophil elastase

Across 269 people aged 9–54, sputum elastase correlated with lower FEV1 (r = −0.35), and each 1-log rise went with a further 2.9% FEV1 decline over time 276. In 35 children followed for 3 years, it was the most informative single biomarker of faster decline 390.

Population statistics, not predictions. In the registry cohort on ETI, annual death (0.47%) and lung transplant (0.16%) rates were well below the 2019 registry rates (1.65% and 1.08%) 39. Pre-modulator decline rates of 1–2.4 FEV1 points a year should not be applied to people on these medicines, and the long-term effect on established bronchiectasis is unknown 39,243,323. Even on modulators, germs already in the lungs often stay, so regular check-ups still matter 323.
