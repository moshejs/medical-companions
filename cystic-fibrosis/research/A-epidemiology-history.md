# A · Epidemiology, survival, presentation and history

Pass date: 2026-11-06. Modules served: home, what, history.

This pass covers what cystic fibrosis (CF) is, which organs it affects, how it classically presents and how newborn screening changed that, how common it is by region and ancestry, how many people live with it, the shift from a childhood disease to a mostly adult population, survival trends and how survival statistics are calculated, causes of death, sex, socioeconomic and ethnic differences in outcome, patient FAQ answers, and a dated history of the disease from folklore to the 2024 vanzacaftor approval. Gene and variant detail is in pass B, diagnosis and screening in pass E, modulators in pass F, and infection and transplant in pass G. They appear here only where they mark a milestone or answer an FAQ.

## Findings

### what · Definition and organs involved
- CF is a monogenic, autosomal recessive disease "considered to affect at least 100 000 people worldwide" [[Shteinberg2021]].
- Mutations in CFTR, the gene for an epithelial channel that normally carries chloride and bicarbonate, impair mucus hydration and clearance [[Shteinberg2021]].
- Classical CF is described as chronic lung infection and inflammation, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency and male infertility, often with comorbidities such as CF-related diabetes or CF liver disease [[Shteinberg2021]].
- A Lancet seminar lists the organs affected by CFTR dysfunction in epithelial cells: lungs (bronchiectasis, small-airway obstruction), pancreas (malabsorption), liver (biliary cirrhosis), sweat glands (heat shock) and vas deferens (infertility) [[Elborn2016]].
- Lung disease causes "the vast majority" of morbidity and mortality in CF [[Ratjen2015]].
- The Lancet Respiratory Medicine Commission's table of classic features lists bronchiectasis with chronic infection, pneumothorax, haemoptysis, respiratory failure, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, meconium ileus, distal intestinal obstruction syndrome, rectal prolapse and pancreatic insufficiency, among others [[Bell2020Commission]].
- Some CFTR variants leave residual protein function. These can cause disease that is harder to diagnose and sometimes affects only one organ [[Shteinberg2021]].
- CF was once described as the most common life-threatening inherited disorder of children of European ("Caucasian") descent. It is now also a disease of adults [[Scotet2020]].

### what · Classic presenting features
- Salty skin: the sweat-gland duct cannot reabsorb chloride normally, so sodium chloride stays in the sweat [[Quinton1983]].
- The sweat electrolyte defect was described in 1953 [[DiSantAgnese1953]]. In the first comparison, sweat chloride in children with CF was 3 to 5 times normal [[Friedman2025]].
- Meconium ileus is often the first sign of CF and occurs in about 20% of people diagnosed with CF [[Sathe2017MI]].
- In the 1990 US registry, 16% of newly diagnosed patients had meconium ileus at birth [[FitzSimmons1993]].
- Pancreatic insufficiency affects about 85% of people with CF. Most are pancreatic insufficient soon after birth; some keep pancreatic sufficiency for part or all of life [[Singh2017PI]].
- Pancreatic status follows genotype: two severe alleles (for example F508del, G542X, G551D) go with insufficiency, and one or two mild alleles (for example R117H, R334W, A455E) with sufficiency [[Kristidis1992]].
- Untreated pancreatic insufficiency leads to malabsorption and poor growth. Andersen's 1938 patients were malnourished children who did not have coeliac disease [[Friedman2025,Andersen1938]].
- Before newborn screening, diagnosis was often late. In the Wisconsin trial the control (unscreened) group was diagnosed at a mean age of 72 weeks versus 12 weeks after screening, and was more malnourished at diagnosis [[Farrell1997]].
- Recurrent chest infections: lung infection was described as a frequent cause of death from the earliest reports [[Davis2006]].
- Nasal polyps were found in 37% of 211 adults with CF examined by nasal endoscopy in one study [[Hadfield2000]].
- Up to 97–98% of men with CF are infertile, because of obstructive azoospermia from congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD). Sperm production is usually intact [[Kaltsas2025]].
- In a meta-analysis of men with CBAVD (not all of whom have CF), 78% carried at least one CFTR variant and 46% carried two [[Yu2012CBAVD]].
- Heat prostration: during a New York heat wave (1948–49), dehydrated children with CF led di Sant'Agnese to study their sweat [[Friedman2025,McBennett2022]].
- People with CF of African ancestry in the US had less meconium ileus but more distal intestinal obstruction syndrome than white patients, and poorer nutritional status independent of age and genotype [[Hamosh1998]].

### what · How presentation changed with newborn screening
- A 1979 study showed that immunoreactive trypsin(ogen) (IRT) is raised in infants with CF and can be measured on a dried blood spot, the basis of newborn screening [[Crossley1979]].
- The Wisconsin randomised trial (650,341 newborns, 1985–1994) found better growth in the screened group throughout childhood. Severe malnutrition after delayed diagnosis may not be reversible [[Farrell2001,Farrell1997]].
- By 2011, CF newborn screening had been enacted in all 50 US states and the District of Columbia [[Rock2011]].
- UK-wide newborn bloodspot screening for CF began in 2007 [[Schluter2020]].
- In UK registry children born 2000–2015, screening was associated with higher lung function (+1.56 FEV1 percentage points, 95% CI 0.1 to 3.02) and later chronic Pseudomonas infection. It did not narrow early health inequalities [[Schluter2020]].
- About 10 million newborns are screened for CF each year worldwide [[Bell2020Commission]].
- In 1990 the median age at diagnosis in the US registry was 7 months, and 90% of patients were diagnosed by age 12 [[FitzSimmons1993]]. A 2022 review reports the median age at diagnosis is now under 1 month [[McBennett2022]].
- A positive screen still needs confirmation: CF Foundation guidelines recommend that a CFTR-related diagnosis, from newborn to adult, be established by a sweat chloride test [[Farrell2017Dx]].
- Screening has made infants with a positive screen but inconclusive tests a recognised group (CRMS/CFSPID) [[Farrell2017Dx]].

### what · Incidence by region and ancestry
- Incidence was traditionally quoted as 1 in 2,500 live births in populations of European descent. Newborn-screening data now suggest 1 in 3,000 to 1 in 6,000, which corresponds to carrier rates of 1 in 28 to 1 in 40 [[Scotet2020]].
- A 1985 paper described CF as affecting 1 in 2,000 children of European descent, showing how much higher the older estimates were [[Tsui1985]].
- Europe ranges from 1 in 1,353 births in Ireland to 1 in 25,000 in Finland. Western Europe averages about 1 in 4,500, and Northern and Central Europe about 1 in 6,000 [[Scotet2020]].
- Ireland's newborn screening programme (650,809 infants, 2011–2021) found a birth incidence of 1 in 2,203 [[McGuire2026]].
- EU prevalence was 0.737 per 10,000 people across 27 countries, similar to 0.797 in the US. The outlier was Ireland at 2.98 per 10,000 [[Farrell2008EU]].
- Australasia averages about 1 in 3,000 births, Canada about 1 in 3,300 and the US about 1 in 4,000, with large ethnic variation in the US [[Scotet2020]].
- The US registry paper gives CF as occurring in about 1 in 3,500 US births [[Knapp2016]].
- Using registry data, US incidence was about 1 in 3,200 white births and 1 in 15,000 Black births [[Hamosh1998]].
- In the Wisconsin trial, classical CF (sweat chloride ≥60 mEq/L) had an incidence of 1 in 4,189; including probable and autopsy cases, the maximum was 1 in 3,938 [[Farrell2001]].
- Founder populations have very high rates: 1 in 569 among the Ohio Amish and 1 in 902 in Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean, Quebec [[Scotet2020]].
- Latin America averages about 1 in 8,000 to 1 in 10,000, from 1 in 6,100 in Argentina to 1 in 15,000 in Costa Rica [[Scotet2020]].
- In Brazil the rate ranges from 1 in 1,600 Euro-Brazilian to 1 in 14,000 Afro-Brazilian infants [[Bell2020Commission]].
- Asia ranges from 1 in 2,560 in Jordan to 1 in 350,000 in Japan, and is estimated at 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 100,000 in India. CF is more frequent in the Middle East, where consanguinity is common, than in East Asia [[Scotet2020]].
- A gnomAD analysis estimated CF births per 100,000: 44–52 European, 11–14 African/African American, 7 Admixed American, 6 South Asian, 4 East Asian and 0.2–1 Middle Eastern (Early evidence) [[Bar2026]].
- Because of higher birth rates, the same analysis estimated 1,426–1,582 CF births a year in India and 330–390 in Brazil, versus about 1,000 in the US and 300 in the UK (Early evidence) [[Bar2026]].
- Common screening panels miss 25% to over 70% of pathogenic alleles in non-European groups; the expanded Wisconsin panel missed 1–30% (Early evidence) [[Bar2026]].
- Incidence is falling in most countries studied, but not all; Colorado (1983–2006) and Wisconsin (1994–2011) saw no decline [[Scotet2020]].
- In Brittany, France, incidence fell from 1 in 1,983 in the late 1970s to 1 in 3,268 in 2005–2009, with a break after prenatal diagnosis became available [[Scotet2020]].
- Israel, which began population carrier screening in 1999, saw incidence fall by about 60% over 22 years. Part of Italy with carrier screening saw a fall of about 35% [[Scotet2020]].

### what · How many people live with CF
- An estimated 162,428 people (range 144,606–186,620) live with CF across 94 countries. About 105,352 (65%) are diagnosed and an estimated 57,076 are undiagnosed [[Guo2022Access]].
- In that 2022 analysis, only 19,516 people (12% of the estimated total) were receiving triple combination therapy [[Guo2022Access]].
- Estimates of undiagnosed CF in low- and middle-income countries are highly uncertain [[Guo2022Access]].
- The European CF Society Patient Registry collects yearly data on more than 55,000 people with CF [[Orenti2026]].
- The ECFS registry held 48,204 people in its 2017 report, with a median age of 18.5 years [[Scotet2020]].
- The UK has about 10,000 people with CF. The UK registry included 10,428 people in 2011–2015 [[Keogh2018]].
- The US registry grew from about 21,000 to 26,000 patients between 2000 and 2010, and the median patient age rose from 14.3 to 16.7 years [[MacKenzie2014]].
- The US registry had 30,775 patients in its 2018 report [[Scotet2020]].
- Since 1986 the US CF Foundation Patient Registry has held records on 48,463 individuals. An audit found it captures 95% of clinic visits and 90% of hospitalisations [[Knapp2016]].

### what · From a children's disease to an adult one
- In the US, the share of patients who were adults rose fourfold, from 8% in 1969 to 33% in 1990 [[FitzSimmons1993]].
- In Canada, adults rose from 29.5% of patients in 1984 to 61.5% in 2018 [[Scotet2020]].
- By 2016–2018, adults were more than half of registry patients in Australia (53.7%), the US (54.6%), the UK (54.7%), France (55.9%), Ireland (58.5%), Belgium (61.2%) and Canada (61.5%) [[Scotet2020]].
- In most countries with established CF care, adults now outnumber children [[Burgel2023Chest,Bell2020Commission]].
- In 20 European countries with high registry coverage, the number of adults rose 45.0% between 2014 and 2024, from 50.9% to 60.5% of the CF population. The number of adults older than 30 nearly doubled [[Orenti2026]].
- In those countries, uptake of elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor (ETI) rose from 2% in 2019 to 71% in 2024. Mean percent predicted FEV1 in non-transplanted adults rose from 66.1% to 78.8% [[Orenti2026]].
- A 2015 forecast for Western Europe expected the CF population to grow by about 50% by 2025: about 20% more children and 75% more adults [[Burgel2015ERJ]].

### what · How survival is measured
- Median age at death describes those who died. It is not a survival measure, because it ignores everyone still alive [[Scotet2020]].
- The predicted (estimated) median age of survival applies current age-specific death rates to a hypothetical birth cohort. It is the age beyond which half of babies born today would be expected to live if death rates did not change [[Scotet2020,Keogh2018Guide]].
- Conditional survival gives the expected median survival for people who have already reached a given age, such as 30 [[Keogh2018Guide]].
- Registries use different methods and terms, which has confused patients and clinicians; standard reporting has been recommended [[Keogh2018Guide]].
- Including people with mild disease or borderline diagnoses raises apparent survival, and excluding them lowers it [[McBennett2022]].
- Direct comparisons between national registries are hard because of differences in method, data processing and ascertainment [[Stephenson2017]].

### what · Survival over time (population statistics)
- When CF was described in 1938, life expectancy was about 6 months [[Davis2006]]. Patients usually died in their first year of life [[Scotet2020]].
- In 1954 the median survival age was estimated at 4–5 years [[McBennett2022]].
- In the US, median survival age doubled from 14 years in 1969 to 28 years in 1990 [[FitzSimmons1993]].
- In Canada, median survival rose from 31.9 years (95% CI 28.3–35.2) in 1990 to 49.7 years (95% CI 46.1–52.2) in the 5 years ending 2012 [[Stephenson2015ERJ]].
- In 2011 the reported median survival was 36.8 years in the US and 48.5 years in Canada [[Stephenson2017]].
- Using one standard method for 2009–2013, median survival was 50.9 years in Canada and 40.6 years in the US. The adjusted risk of death was 34% lower in Canada (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.54–0.81) [[Stephenson2017]].
- The Canada–US gap was not seen for US patients with private insurance. Canada also had more transplants (10.3% vs 6.5%) [[Stephenson2017]].
- For US children born and diagnosed in 2010, median survival was projected at 37 years for females and 40 for males at 2010 death rates. It would exceed 50 years if mortality kept falling at the 2000–2010 rate [[MacKenzie2014]].
- Adjusted US mortality fell by 1.8% per year (95% CI 0.5% to 2.7%) between 2000 and 2010 [[MacKenzie2014]].
- For the 5 years 2010–2014, the predicted median survival for a US child born with CF was 40 years. Based on 2019 data it was 48.4 years [[McBennett2022]].
- In 2016–2018 registry reports, the predicted median survival was 44.4 years in Ireland, 47.3 in the UK, 47.4 in the US and 52.1 in Canada. Median age at death ranged from 29.0 years (ECFS) to 35.6 years (Australia) [[Scotet2020]].
- In 2015–2019, median survival was 65.9 years in France (95% CI 59.8–76.0), 55.4 in Canada, 54.8 in New Zealand and 53.3 in Australia. After adjustment, the risk of death was higher in Canada (HR 1.85) and Australia (HR 2.08) than in France [[Coriati2023]].
- In UK data (2011–2015), median survival at birth for F508del homozygotes was 46 years for males and 41 for females. For those who reached 30, it rose to 52 and 49 [[Keogh2018]].
- UK mortality fell about 2% a year in 2006–2015. If that continued, median survival for F508del-homozygous babies would be 65 (males) and 56 (females) [[Keogh2018]].
- In UK cohorts, observed survival to 2003 was 55% (males) and 49% (females) for those born in 1978, versus 97% and 96% for those born in 1992 [[Dodge2007]].
- In the 1970s–80s, median survival in Boston was 21 years and in Toronto 30 years, with similar lung function. The authors suggested Toronto's high-fat diet, as opposed to the traditional fat restriction, might explain the gap [[Corey1988]].
- In the US, CF deaths averaged 478 a year in 1999–2011, 452 a year in 2012–2018 and 263 a year in 2019–2024. That is a 47% fall from the pre-modulator period [[Grover2026]].
- The US age-adjusted CF mortality rate fell from 0.17 per 100,000 in 1999 to 0.07 in 2024, with a sharp decline from 2016 (APC −9.76% a year). This ecological study cannot prove cause [[Grover2026]].

### what · Survival in the modulator era (population statistics)
- Randomised trials of modulators are too short to use death as an outcome. Registry evidence on mortality was first available only for ivacaftor [[BalfourLynn2022]].
- In US registry data, ivacaftor-treated patients had a lower risk of death than matched comparators (0.6% vs 1.6%) and of transplantation (0.2% vs 1.1%) [[Bessonova2018Registry]].
- Over 5 years (2012–2016), US ivacaftor-treated patients lost 0.7 FEV1 percentage points versus 8.3 in comparators [[Volkova2020]].
- In 16,116 US people who started ETI in 2019–2020, the annual death rate was 0.47% (95% CI 0.39–0.55), versus 1.65% in the 2019 registry: 72% lower. Lung transplants were 85% lower [[Bower2023]].
- In US people aged 12 and over (2017–2021), all-cause mortality was 0.76% in ETI-treated people versus 3.45% in an ETI-ineligible comparator group (79% reduction, 95% CI 56–88%) [[Merlo2026]].
- A microsimulation projected a median survival of 71.6 years for F508del homozygotes treated with ETI, 33.5 years more than best supportive care. If ETI is started at age 12–17, the projection is 82.5 years (Early evidence; model-based) [[Lopez2023Survival]].
- A Canadian model projected that ETI available in 2021 rather than 2025 could reduce deaths by 15% and raise median survival by 9.2 years over 10 years (Early evidence; model-based) [[Stanojevic2021]].
- A 2023 Lancet review says the long-term effects of modulators are still being studied, and that a health-outcome gap has opened for people who cannot get, take or tolerate them [[TaylorCousar2023Lancet]].

### what · Causes of death
- Mortality and morbidity are still dominated by recurrent lung infections, often multidrug-resistant, which lead to lung destruction and respiratory failure [[Bell2020Commission]].
- In France (2007–2010), half of 256 CF deaths occurred after lung transplant, from transplant complications. Most of the other half, in people never transplanted, were from respiratory failure or massive haemoptysis [[Martin2016Deaths]].
- Of the French patients who died without a transplant, only 19% died while on the waiting list. Most had been referred late or not at all [[Martin2016Deaths]].
- In US registry data, transplant complications are the second leading cause of death in CF [[McBennett2022]].
- Low lung function (FEV1 below 30% predicted) is the main predictor of worse survival. Others are female sex, older age at diagnosis, severe genotype, ethnic background, lower socioeconomic status, poor nutrition, pancreatic insufficiency, early Pseudomonas and diabetes [[Scotet2020]].
- In Canada (2000–2012), malnutrition (HR 2.1), multiple exacerbations (HR 4.5) and CF-related diabetes in women (HR 1.8) were linked to a higher risk of death [[Stephenson2015ERJ]].

### what · Sex differences ("gender gap")
- In 21,047 US patients (1988–1992), females aged 1–20 were 60% more likely to die than males (RR 1.6, 95% CI 1.4–1.8). Median survival was 25.3 years for females and 28.4 for males [[Rosenfeld1997]].
- The gap remained after adjusting for nutrition, lung function, microbiology, pancreatic status, age at diagnosis and race (RR 1.6, 95% CI 1.2–2.1) [[Rosenfeld1997]].
- In the 1990 US registry, median survival was 25 years for females and 30 for males [[FitzSimmons1993]].
- In 32,766 US registry patients over 13 years, median life expectancy was 36.0 years for females and 38.7 for males; female sex had an adjusted HR of 2.22 [[HarnessBrumley2014]].
- In US data for 2000–2010, males had a 19% (95% CI 13–24%) lower adjusted risk of death than females [[MacKenzie2014]].
- At one US centre, median survival for females with CF-related diabetes was 30.7 years, versus 47.0 for females without diabetes and 47.4–49.5 for males [[Milla2005]].
- One Canadian analysis suggests that the sex gap may be explained by a higher risk of death in women with CF-related diabetes [[Stephenson2015ERJ]].
- In US death certificates for 1999–2024, age-adjusted CF mortality rates were the same for both sexes within each period. The authors call the shift in female-to-male death ratio (1.05 to 0.97) hypothesis-generating [[Grover2026]].

### what · Socioeconomic and ethnic disparities
- Among 23,817 US white patients, those in the lowest income areas (under $20,000) had a 44% higher risk of death than those in the highest (at least $50,000): 90.3 versus 62.6 deaths per 10,000 person-years [[OConnor2003]].
- US children with CF on Medicaid (1986–1994) had an adjusted risk of death 3.65 times higher (95% CI 3.03–4.40) and FEV1 9.1% lower than those not on Medicaid [[Schechter2001]].
- In the UK, children from the most deprived areas had FEV1 4.12 percentage points lower (95% CI −5.01 to −3.19) and higher odds of chronic Pseudomonas (OR 1.89) than the least deprived. The gaps appeared early and did not widen [[TaylorRobinson2013]].
- Hispanic patients are 8.5% of US people with CF, the largest growing minority. In 2010–2014 registry data, their mean age at death was 22.4 years versus 28.1, and their adjusted death rate was 1.27 times higher (95% CI 1.05–1.53) [[Rho2018]].
- In California (1991–2010), mortality was 9.1% in Hispanic versus 3.3% in non-Hispanic patients. After adjustment for socioeconomic and clinical factors, the HR was 2.81 (95% CI 1.70–4.63) [[Buu2016]].
- Life expectancy in Canada, the US and the EU is in the mid-40s or above. It is about half that in Brazil and lower still in El Salvador, India, the Middle East and non-EU countries [[Bell2020Commission]].
- Differences in survival between and within high-income countries may reflect access to specialised care, not just disease severity [[Burgel2023Chest]].

### what · Patient FAQ answers (cited)
- What is CF? An inherited condition in which a faulty CFTR channel makes mucus thick and sticky in the lungs, pancreas and other organs [[Shteinberg2021,Elborn2016]].
- Is CF contagious? No. CF is a genetic condition passed from parents to child, not an infection [[Shteinberg2021,Ratjen2015]]. Germs can spread between people with CF, which is why centres keep people with CF apart from each other [[McBennett2022]].
- How is it inherited? It is autosomal recessive [[Shteinberg2021]]. When both parents are carriers, each pregnancy has a 1-in-4 chance of CF [[Scotet2020]]. Carrier rates in populations of European descent are about 1 in 28 to 1 in 40 [[Scotet2020]]. (Detail: pass B.)
- What are the symptoms? Salty skin, cough and chest infections, poor weight gain, bowel blockage at birth (meconium ileus), nasal polyps and, in most men, infertility [[Elborn2016,Sathe2017MI,Hadfield2000,Kaltsas2025]].
- How is it diagnosed? Usually by newborn blood-spot screening, then confirmed with a sweat chloride test [[Crossley1979,Farrell2017Dx]]. (Detail: pass E.)
- What is the life expectancy? For babies born in 2019, the US predicted median survival was 48.4 years [[McBennett2022]]. Modulators are expected to raise it further, but long-term data are still being collected [[Lopez2023Survival,TaylorCousar2023Lancet]]. These are population averages.
- Is there a cure? Not yet. Modulators target the basic defect and have transformed health for many, but their long-term effects are still being studied, and other approaches, including gene-based therapies, are still in development [[Shteinberg2021,TaylorCousar2023Lancet,Elborn2016]].
- What are modulators? Pills that help the faulty CFTR protein reach the cell surface (correctors) or open better (potentiators). The current triple combination is approved for people aged 2 and over with at least one responsive or protein-producing CFTR variant [[Friedman2025,FDA-Trikafta]]. (Detail: pass F.)
- Can adults with CF work and have families? Most people with CF in many countries are now adults, and care now includes work, relationships and parenthood [[Scotet2020]].
- Can men with CF have children? Usually yes, with help: sperm can be retrieved and used with IVF/ICSI, with high success rates [[Kaltsas2025]].

### history · Folklore and early descriptions
- Busch traced European folk sayings about licking a child's forehead: a salty taste meant the child was "bewitched" and would soon die. He found such sayings in 12 European countries [[Busch1990]].
- A medieval European saying, as quoted in a 2025 Lasker perspective: "Woe to the child who tastes salty from a kiss on the brow" [[Friedman2025]].
- A 1606 Spanish text by Alonso y de los Ruyzes de Fonteca noted that the fingers taste salty after rubbing a "bewitched" child's forehead [[Trouve2024]].
- References to conditions that probably reflect CF go back to the Middle Ages [[Quinton1999]].
- Fanconi (1936) is probably the first to describe "CF with bronchiectasis" [[Trouve2024]].
- Dorothy Andersen, a Columbia University pathologist, described "cystic fibrosis of the pancreas" in 1938 and set it apart from coeliac disease [[Andersen1938,Davis2006]].
- Andersen also found that infants who survived the newborn period often developed progressive lung disease [[Friedman2025]].
- Farber introduced the term "mucoviscidosis" in 1945, because he thought the cause was generally thickened mucus [[Trouve2024]].
- The autosomal recessive inheritance of CF was described in 1946 [[Andersen1946,Trouve2024]].
- From the 1940s, Andersen and di Sant'Agnese used sulfonamides and penicillin against lung infections, which helped only temporarily [[Friedman2025,Trouve2024]].

### history · Sweat test and organised care
- In 1953 di Sant'Agnese and colleagues reported abnormal sweat electrolytes in CF [[DiSantAgnese1953]].
- In 1959 Gibson and Cooke standardised sweat collection by pilocarpine iontophoresis, which is still the basis of the sweat test [[Gibson1959,Trouve2024]].
- The sweat test let doctors identify milder cases, so CF was no longer seen only as a disease of mucus [[Davis2006]].
- From 1955, care centres running aggressive, comprehensive care programmes began a striking improvement in survival. Their pillars of nutrition, airway clearance and infection treatment are still in use [[Davis2006]].
- A comprehensive care programme began in Cleveland in 1957. Mist-tent therapy rose and fell in popularity between the 1950s and 1970s [[Labonte2018]].
- Injectable tobramycin was approved by the FDA in 1975 [[McBennett2022]].
- In the early 1980s Burkholderia cepacia appeared in CF clinics and for a time halted the rise in survival. Separating infected patients helped control it [[McBennett2022]].

### history · Finding the basic defect and the gene
- 1981: Knowles and colleagues found that nasal potential difference in 24 people with CF was more than 3 standard deviations above controls, consistent with excess sodium absorption [[Knowles1981]].
- 1983: Quinton showed low chloride permeability in CF sweat ducts, explaining salty sweat [[Quinton1983]].
- 1984–85: studies showed the chloride defect was cAMP-regulated and located at the apical membrane of airway cells [[Friedman2025]].
- 1985: a DNA marker linked to the CF gene narrowed its location to about 1% of the human genome [[Tsui1985]].
- 1989: three Science papers cloned the CF gene on chromosome 7q (spanning about 250,000 base pairs) and named the protein CFTR [[Rommens1989,Riordan1989,Kerem1989,Friedman2025]].
- About 70% of CF mutations were found to be one three-base-pair deletion, which removes phenylalanine 508 (F508del) [[Kerem1989]].
- 1990: adding normal CFTR to CF airway cells corrected the chloride channel defect; the F508del form did not [[Rich1990]]. Retrovirus-mediated gene transfer corrected the defect in CF pancreatic cells [[Drumm1990]].
- 1990: the F508del protein was shown to be held in the endoplasmic reticulum [[Friedman2025]].
- 1992: the first CF mouse was made by gene targeting. Mice showed failure to thrive, meconium ileus and gland obstruction, and usually died of intestinal obstruction before 40 days [[Snouwaert1992]].
- 1992: altering CFTR's ion selectivity showed that CFTR is itself a chloride channel [[Friedman2025]].
- 1993: adenovirus-delivered CFTR transiently corrected the chloride defect in the nasal lining of 3 people with CF, an early gene-therapy test [[Zabner1993]].
- 1994: an adenovirus CFTR vector was given to the airways of 4 people with CF; it expressed CFTR, and the highest dose caused a transient systemic and pulmonary syndrome [[Crystal1994]].

### history · Symptomatic drugs and screening
- 1979: the dried blood-spot IRT test made newborn screening possible [[Crossley1979]].
- 30 December 1993: FDA approved dornase alfa (Pulmozyme) [[FDA2026CFApprovals]].
- In its pivotal trial (968 patients, 24 weeks), dornase alfa cut the risk of respiratory exacerbations by 28% once daily and 37% twice daily [[Fuchs1994Dornase]].
- 22 December 1997: FDA approved tobramycin inhalation solution (TOBI) [[FDA2026CFApprovals]].
- In its trials (520 patients), inhaled tobramycin raised FEV1 by 10% at week 20 versus a 2% decline on placebo, and cut hospitalisation by 26% [[Ramsey1999TOBI]].
- 1985–1994: the Wisconsin randomised trial of newborn screening [[Farrell1997]]. In the US, CF newborn screening was in every state by 2011, and in the UK nationwide by 2007 [[Rock2011,Schluter2020]].

### history · Venture philanthropy and modulators
- In 1999–2000 the CF Foundation funded Aurora Biosciences to run high-throughput screens for CFTR correctors and potentiators. A Lasker perspective calls it "the most successful example of Venture Philanthropy to date" [[Friedman2025]].
- Vertex acquired Aurora in 2001. With continued CF Foundation funding, screening hits were found by 2003, and ivacaftor was identified by 2005 [[Friedman2025]].
- 2009: in lab studies, VX-770 (ivacaftor) raised chloride secretion in G551D/F508del bronchial cells about 10-fold, to about 50% of non-CF levels [[VanGoor2009VX770]].
- 2011: in STRIVE (G551D, age 12 and over), ivacaftor raised percent predicted FEV1 by 10.6 points versus placebo through week 24 and lowered sweat chloride by 48.1 mmol/L [[Ramsey2011STRIVE]].
- 31 January 2012: FDA approved ivacaftor (Kalydeco), the first CFTR modulator [[FDA2026CFApprovals,McBennett2022]].
- 2 July 2015: FDA approved lumacaftor–ivacaftor (Orkambi). In its trials of F508del homozygotes, FEV1 improved by 2.6 to 4.0 points versus placebo [[FDA2026CFApprovals,Wainwright2015TRAFFIC]].
- 12 February 2018: FDA approved tezacaftor–ivacaftor (Symdeko). In EVOLVE, FEV1 improved 4.0 points versus placebo [[FDA2026CFApprovals,TaylorCousar2017EVOLVE]].
- 2018: the phase 2 results of the VX-445 (elexacaftor) triple were published [[Keating2018VX445]].
- 2019: in people with one F508del and one minimal-function allele, ETI raised FEV1 by 14.3 points through 24 weeks versus placebo and cut exacerbations by 63% [[Middleton2019ETI]]. In F508del homozygotes it added 10.0 points over tezacaftor–ivacaftor at 4 weeks [[Heijerman2019ETI]].
- 21 October 2019: FDA approved elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor (Trikafta) [[FDA2026CFApprovals]].
- Trikafta efficacy supplements were approved on 21 December 2020, 8 June 2021, 20 December 2024 and 27 March 2026; the granule formulation was approved on 26 April 2023 [[FDA2026CFApprovals]].
- The current Trikafta label covers people aged 2 and over with at least one CFTR variant that is responsive on clinical or in vitro data, or that produces CFTR protein [[FDA-Trikafta]].
- 20 December 2024: FDA approved vanzacaftor–tezacaftor–deutivacaftor (Alyftrek), a once-daily triple [[FDA2026CFApprovals]]. In SKYLINE it was non-inferior to ETI on FEV1 [[Keating2025SKYLINE]].
- Vertex won approval of 4 CF drugs in 7 years. It was the first time the FDA accepted efficacy tests in primary human cells [[Friedman2025]].
- 2025: the Lasker–DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award went to Michael Welsh, Jesús González and Paul Negulescu for work that led to the triple combination [[Friedman2025]].

### Exam hooks
- CF is autosomal recessive and caused by CFTR mutations. Lung disease causes most of the morbidity and mortality [[Ratjen2015]].
- The classic triad of chronic lung infection, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency and male infertility, plus a raised sweat chloride [[Shteinberg2021]].
- Meconium ileus occurs in about 20% of people with CF and is often the first sign [[Sathe2017MI]].
- About 85% of people with CF are pancreatic insufficient [[Singh2017PI]].
- Up to 97–98% of men with CF are infertile from CBAVD, with intact sperm production [[Kaltsas2025]].
- Incidence in European-descent populations: about 1 in 3,000 to 1 in 6,000 births today (traditionally 1 in 2,500). Ireland has the highest rate in Europe (1 in 1,353) [[Scotet2020]].
- US incidence by ancestry: about 1 in 3,200 white and 1 in 15,000 Black births [[Hamosh1998]].
- Median age at death is not a survival statistic; predicted median survival applies to a baby born today [[Scotet2020,Keogh2018Guide]].
- 1938: Andersen describes "cystic fibrosis of the pancreas" [[Andersen1938]].
- 1953: di Sant'Agnese finds the sweat electrolyte defect; 1959: Gibson and Cooke's pilocarpine iontophoresis sweat test [[DiSantAgnese1953,Gibson1959]].
- 1983: Quinton shows chloride impermeability of the sweat duct [[Quinton1983]].
- 1989: CFTR is cloned on chromosome 7; about 70% of CF alleles are F508del [[Kerem1989,Rommens1989]].
- 2012: ivacaftor for G551D, the first modulator; 2019: elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor [[FDA2026CFApprovals,Ramsey2011STRIVE]].
- The CF "gender gap": US females aged 1–20 had a 60% higher risk of death than males (1988–1992) [[Rosenfeld1997]].
- Low socioeconomic status predicts worse outcomes: US Medicaid patients had a 3.65-fold adjusted risk of death [[Schechter2001]].
- Adults now outnumber children in most countries with established CF care [[Burgel2023Chest]].
- The 2009–2013 median survival was 50.9 years in Canada vs 40.6 in the US, partly linked to transplant access and insurance [[Stephenson2017]].

### In plain words
- CF is something you are born with. It is not caught from other people [[Shteinberg2021]].
- A child gets CF only when both parents pass on a changed CFTR gene [[Shteinberg2021]].
- The changed gene makes mucus thick and sticky. It clogs the lungs, the pancreas and other organs [[Elborn2016]].
- Long ago, people noticed that some babies tasted salty when kissed. Doctors later found this salty sweat is a sign of CF [[Busch1990,DiSantAgnese1953]].
- Most babies with CF are now found by a heel-prick blood test soon after birth. A sweat test confirms it [[Rock2011,Farrell2017Dx]].
- In the 1950s, most children with CF died very young. Today, in many countries, more than half of people with CF are adults [[McBennett2022,Scotet2020]].
- Survival numbers are averages for large groups. They cannot tell you how long one person will live [[Keogh2018Guide]].
- New medicines called modulators fix part of the problem at its source. Many people on them are much healthier [[TaylorCousar2023Lancet]].
- These medicines are not a cure, and not everyone can get or use them [[TaylorCousar2023Lancet,Guo2022Access]].
- People with CF should keep apart from each other, because they can pass lung germs to one another [[McBennett2022]].
- Where you live and how much money your family has can affect how well people with CF do [[OConnor2003,TaylorRobinson2013]].
- Most men with CF can't have children naturally, but many can become fathers with fertility treatment [[Kaltsas2025]].

## Open questions and controversies
- How much have modulators extended survival? Registry studies show lower death rates on ivacaftor and ETI [[Bessonova2018Registry,Bower2023,Merlo2026]], and models project median survival above 70–80 years [[Lopez2023Survival]]. Trials are too short to measure death, and observational and model data carry confounding and assumption risk [[BalfourLynn2022,Grover2026]]. Status: open; long-term registry follow-up continues.
- Is the gender gap closing? Older US cohorts show clearly worse female survival [[Rosenfeld1997,HarnessBrumley2014,MacKenzie2014]]. UK data still show it at birth [[Keogh2018]], but US death-certificate data for 1999–2024 show equal age-adjusted rates by sex [[Grover2026]]. One Canadian study links the gap to CF-related diabetes in women [[Stephenson2015ERJ]]. Status: unresolved; modulator-era sex-specific analyses are needed.
- Why do countries differ in survival? Canada outlived the US by about 10 years in 2009–2013 [[Stephenson2017]], and France outlived Canada and Australia in 2015–2019 [[Coriati2023]]. Proposed reasons include transplant access, insurance and health-system structure, but registry methods also differ [[Stephenson2017,Keogh2018Guide]]. Status: partly explained.
- Is incidence falling? Most regions report declines tied to prenatal diagnosis and carrier screening [[Scotet2020]], but Colorado and Wisconsin did not [[Scotet2020]], and older estimates were inflated or biased by under-diagnosis [[Scotet2020]]. Status: varies by region and policy.
- How big is the hidden global burden? About 35% of people with CF may be undiagnosed [[Guo2022Access]]. Genetic estimates suggest CF births in India and Brazil rival those in the US and UK (Early evidence) [[Bar2026]], and European-designed screening panels miss many non-European alleles [[Bar2026,Bell2020Commission]]. Status: data poor outside high-income registries.
- Do disparities persist after newborn screening and modulators? UK screening did not narrow early inequalities [[Schluter2020]]. Hispanic and low-income US patients had higher mortality even after adjustment [[Rho2018,Buu2016,Schechter2001]]. Modulators may widen the gap for people who cannot access them [[TaylorCousar2023Lancet,Guo2022Access]]. Status: open.

- Fanconi 1936 and Farber 1945 could not be found in PubMed or Crossref. They are not listed as sources; their dates and terms come from Trouvé 2024.
- FDA approval dates come from openFDA Drugs@FDA (`api.fda.gov/drug/drugsfda.json`), using the ORIG submission with status AP, plus approved efficacy supplements for Trikafta. - McBennett 2022 gives 1998 for the approval of inhaled tobramycin. Drugs@FDA gives 22 December 1997 for TOBI (NDA050753), and that date is used here.
- Not cited for lack of an abstract or open text: Grasemann & Ratjen 2023 (NEJM), Collins 2019 (NEJM editorial), Clague 2014 (Andersen profile). Quinton 1999 and Davis 2006 were used from abstracts only, because full text returned HTTP 403.
