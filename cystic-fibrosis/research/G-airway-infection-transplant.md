Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: clearance, infection, transplant.

Corrections to the brief:
- The CFF 2019 referral guideline does not say "<30% list". For adults it recommends referral no later than when FEV1 is <30% predicted. It recommends discussing transplant at FEV1 <50%. At FEV1 <40%, referral is recommended when markers of shortened survival are present. Listing is a transplant-centre decision the guideline does not set a threshold for [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- The ISHLT CF median survival used here (9.5 years among adults with CF) is the figure the CFF guideline quotes from the ISHLT registry report [[Ramos2019Referral]]. I did not validate an ISHLT registry report abstract that gives a CF-specific median.
- The Saiman 2003 abstract reports the FEV1 benefit of azithromycin in litres (+0.094 L), not as "+6.2%". The 6.2% figure is not used, because I could not trace it to a validated abstract.

## Findings

### clearance · Guideline grades for chronic lung medications (CFF 2013)
- The CFF chronic-medications guideline graded its recommendations with the US Preventive Services Task Force scheme. A = strongly recommend, B = recommend, C = consider for selected patients, D = recommend against, I = insufficient evidence [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- Lung-disease severity bands used by the guideline (FEV1 % predicted): normal >90%, mild 70–89%, moderate 40–69%, severe <40% [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- Dornase alfa, age ≥6 years: grade A for moderate-to-severe lung disease and grade B for asymptomatic or mild disease [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- Inhaled hypertonic saline, age ≥6 years: grade B [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- Inhaled tobramycin for persistent Pseudomonas aeruginosa, age ≥6 years: grade A for moderate-to-severe disease and grade B for mild disease [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- Inhaled aztreonam for persistent P. aeruginosa, age ≥6 years: grade A for moderate-to-severe disease and grade B for mild disease [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- Chronic azithromycin, age ≥6 years: grade B with persistent P. aeruginosa and grade C without it [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- High-dose oral ibuprofen: grade B for ages 6–17 with FEV1 ≥60% predicted (target peak plasma level 50–100 μg/mL), and grade I for age ≥18 [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- Grade D (recommended against): routine inhaled corticosteroids and chronic oral corticosteroids in people aged ≥6 without asthma or ABPA, and prophylactic oral anti-staphylococcal antibiotics [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- Grade I (insufficient evidence): other inhaled antibiotics (carbenicillin, ceftazidime, colistin, gentamicin), chronic oral antipseudomonal antibiotics, leukotriene modifiers, N-acetylcysteine, inhaled glutathione, inhaled anticholinergics and inhaled β2-agonists [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].

### clearance · Dornase alfa (rhDNase)
- Fuchs 1994 randomised 968 adults and children with CF to once-daily or twice-daily rhDNase or placebo for 24 weeks. It was a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial [[Fuchs1994Dornase]].
- One or more exacerbations needing parenteral antibiotics occurred in 27% on placebo, 22% on once-daily rhDNase and 19% on twice-daily rhDNase [[Fuchs1994Dornase]].
- Age-adjusted exacerbation risk fell by 28% with once-daily dosing (P = 0.04) and by 37% with twice-daily dosing (P < 0.01), compared with placebo [[Fuchs1994Dornase]].
- FEV1 improved by a mean of 5.8 ± 0.7% (once daily) and 5.6 ± 0.7% (twice daily) over the study. No anaphylaxis occurred. Voice alteration and laryngitis were more common with rhDNase but rarely severe, and resolved within 21 days [[Fuchs1994Dornase]].
- The Cochrane review included 19 trials (2,565 participants). Dornase alfa probably improves FEV1 at 1, 3 and 6 months and at 2 years compared with placebo. It probably reduces pulmonary exacerbations in trials of up to 2 years (moderate-quality evidence) [[Yang2021Dornase]].
- In that review, voice alteration and rash were the only adverse events seen more often than with comparators. In one costing trial, dornase-related savings offset 18% to 38% of the drug cost [[Yang2021Dornase]].
- The US label indicates dornase alfa, with standard therapies, to improve pulmonary function in children and adults with CF. It states that in people with FVC ≥40% predicted, daily use also reduced the risk of respiratory infections needing parenteral antibiotics [[FDA-Pulmozyme]].
- Label dose, quoted as published: 2.5 mg (one single-dose ampule) inhaled once daily via a recommended nebulizer; "some patients may benefit from twice daily administration" [[FDA-Pulmozyme]].

### clearance · Hypertonic saline and mannitol
- Mechanism: in 24 people with CF, 7% hypertonic saline four times daily produced a sustained (≥8 hours) rise in mucus clearance compared with amiloride pretreatment. One-hour clearance was 14.0 ± 2.0% versus 7.0 ± 1.5% (P = 0.02) [[Donaldson2006]].
- In the same study, FEV1 rose by a mean of 6.62% (95% CI 1.6 to 11.7) with hypertonic saline plus placebo. With amiloride pretreatment there was no significant rise (2.9%). The authors attributed the benefit to sustained hydration of the airway surface (Established) [[Donaldson2006]].
- Elkins 2006 randomised 164 people aged ≥6 to 4 mL of 7% saline or 0.9% saline twice daily for 48 weeks, each dose preceded by a bronchodilator [[Elkins2006HS]].
- In Elkins 2006 the primary outcome (rate of change in lung function) did not differ (P = 0.79). Averaged across visits, FVC was 82 mL higher (95% CI 12 to 153) and FEV1 68 mL higher (95% CI 3 to 132) with hypertonic saline [[Elkins2006HS]].
- Hypertonic saline cut pulmonary exacerbations by 56% relative to control (P = 0.02). 76% stayed exacerbation-free versus 62% (P = 0.03), with no worsening of infection or inflammation [[Elkins2006HS]].
- ISIS randomised 321 children aged 4–60 months to 7% or 0.9% saline twice daily for 48 weeks. The exacerbation rate was 2.3 events per person-year in both groups (adjusted rate ratio 0.98, 95% CI 0.84 to 1.15) [[Rosenfeld2012ISIS]].
- In an ISIS infant lung-function subgroup, FEV0.5 change was 38 mL greater with hypertonic saline (95% CI 1 to 76). Cough was the most common moderate or severe adverse event (39% versus 38%) [[Rosenfeld2012ISIS]].
- The Cochrane review (24 trials, 1,318 participants aged 1 month to 56 years) rated the evidence for FEV1 benefit at 4 weeks as very low certainty (MD 3.30% predicted, 95% CI 0.71 to 5.89) [[Wark2023HS]].
- In preschool children, 48 weeks of hypertonic saline gave a small improvement in lung clearance index versus isotonic saline (MD −0.60, 95% CI −1.00 to −0.19; 2 trials, 192 participants) [[Wark2023HS]].
- In one comparison (moderate-to-severe disease, 12 weeks), rhDNase may raise FEV1 more than hypertonic saline (MD 8.00% predicted, 95% CI 2.00 to 14.00; low certainty) [[Wark2023HS]].
- Inhaled dry-powder mannitol 400 mg twice daily (n = 192) versus a 50 mg control (n = 126) for 26 weeks improved FEV1 by a mean 105 mL (8.2% above baseline). The relative improvement versus control was 3.75% (P = 0.029) [[Aitken2012Mannitol]].
- In that trial, exacerbations were fewer with mannitol but not significantly so (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.42 to 1.32) [[Aitken2012Mannitol]].
- In a second phase III trial (324 randomised), mannitol improved FEV1 by 92.9 mL over control at 26 weeks (118.9 mL [6.5%] versus 26.0 mL [2.4%]; P < 0.001), whether or not people also used rhDNase [[Bilton2011Mannitol]].
- In that trial the incidence of having an exacerbation fell by 35.4% (P = 0.045). Common mannitol-related adverse events were cough, haemoptysis and throat (pharyngolaryngeal) pain [[Bilton2011Mannitol]].

### clearance · Anti-inflammatory therapy: azithromycin and ibuprofen
- Saiman 2003 randomised 185 people aged ≥6 with P. aeruginosa for ≥1 year and FEV1 ≥30%. The dose was 250 mg (<40 kg) or 500 mg (≥40 kg) of azithromycin 3 days a week for 168 days, versus placebo [[Saiman2003Azithro]].
- At day 168, FEV1 rose by 0.097 L with azithromycin and by 0.003 L with placebo (difference 0.094 L, 95% CI 0.023 to 0.165; P = 0.009) [[Saiman2003Azithro]].
- Exacerbation risk was lower with azithromycin (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.44 to 0.95), and weight was 0.7 kg higher. Nausea (17% more), diarrhoea (15% more) and wheezing (13% more) were more frequent [[Saiman2003Azithro]].
- The Cochrane review (14 studies, 1,467 participants, all azithromycin) found a slight FEV1 improvement at up to 6 months (MD 3.97% predicted, 95% CI 1.74 to 6.19; high certainty). It found no clear difference at 12 months [[Southern2024Macrolide]].
- Azithromycin probably lengthens time to exacerbation (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.75; moderate certainty). There was no difference in new P. aeruginosa acquisition at 12 months (HR 1.00) [[Southern2024Macrolide]].
- In a 4-year double-blind trial, 85 people aged 5–39 with FEV1 ≥60% took high-dose ibuprofen titrated to peak plasma 50–100 μg/mL, or placebo [[Konstan1995Ibuprofen]].
- The annual FEV1 decline was slower with ibuprofen: −2.17 ± 0.57% versus −3.60 ± 0.55% per year (P = 0.02). In adherent participants it was −1.48% versus −3.57% (P = 0.03) [[Konstan1995Ibuprofen]].

### clearance · Stopping mucoactive therapy on elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor (SIMPLIFY)
- SIMPLIFY ran two parallel open-label, randomised non-inferiority trials at 80 US clinics. People on ETI who also used hypertonic saline (≥3%) or dornase alfa were randomised to stop or continue it for 6 weeks [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]].
- Entry criteria: ages 12–17 with ppFEV1 ≥70%, or ≥18 with ppFEV1 ≥60%. Mean baseline ppFEV1 was 96.9%, and the non-inferiority margin was −3% [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]].
- Hypertonic saline trial (370 randomised): 6-week ppFEV1 change was −0.19% when stopping versus +0.14% when continuing. The between-group difference was −0.32% (95% CI −1.25 to 0.60), so stopping was non-inferior [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]].
- Dornase alfa trial (477 randomised): 6-week change was +0.18% when stopping versus −0.16% when continuing. The difference was 0.35% (95% CI −0.45 to 1.14), so stopping was non-inferior [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]].
- Lung clearance index also did not change meaningfully (hypertonic saline trial difference 0.06, 95% CI −0.31 to 0.44). There were no treatment-related serious adverse events [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]].
- Respiratory adverse events were slightly more common among those who stopped, mainly in the small adult subgroup with ppFEV1 60 to <70%. The trial was not powered for exacerbations (Early evidence for people with lower lung function) [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]].

### clearance · Airway clearance techniques and exercise
- The CFF 2009 airway-clearance guideline recommends airway clearance "on a regular basis in all patients". It states that no technique has been shown superior, so the prescription should be individualised [[Flume2009ACT]].
- The same guideline recommends aerobic exercise as an adjunct to airway clearance and for overall health. It rated the evidence for airway clearance as fair, with moderate benefit [[Flume2009ACT]].
- Airway clearance versus none (Cochrane, 12 studies, 194 participants): ACTs may increase mucus transport in the short term. No conclusions could be drawn about long-term effects, and the evidence predates widespread CFTR modulator use [[Warnock2023ACT]].
- Conventional chest physiotherapy (postural drainage and percussion) versus other techniques (21 studies, 778 participants; all very low certainty): no difference in FEV1 change or decline. Most studies suggested equivalence with PEP, ACBT, oscillating PEP, autogenic drainage and exercise [[Main2023CCPT]].
- The Cochrane overview of 6 reviews found little evidence that any one technique is better. It advises people with CF to choose by comfort, convenience, flexibility, practicality and cost [[Wilson2019ACT]].
- The overview's only moderate-quality comparison: PEP versus oscillating devices after 6 months showed no FEV1 difference (MD −1.43% predicted, 95% CI −5.72 to 2.87) [[Wilson2019ACT]].
- PEP (Cochrane, 28 studies, 788 participants): exacerbation rates were lower with mask PEP used for at least 1 year than with other techniques (5 studies, 232 participants; moderate-to-high quality). Preference favoured PEP in all studies lasting ≥1 month [[McIlwaine2019PEP]].
- A 1-year Canadian RCT (107 enrolled) compared PEP mask with high-frequency chest wall oscillation (vest). Mean exacerbations were 1.14 with PEP versus 2.0 with the vest, and time to first exacerbation was 220 versus 115 days (p = 0.02) [[McIlwaine2013HFCWO]].
- In that trial lung function, quality of life and satisfaction did not differ, and PEP took less treatment time. The authors concluded the results do not support the vest as the primary technique [[McIlwaine2013HFCWO]].
- Oscillating devices (Cochrane, 39 studies, 1,114 participants; low or very low quality): there was no clear evidence that oscillation is better or worse than other physiotherapy, and no evidence that one device beats another [[Morrison2020Osc]].
- Exercise versus other techniques (Cochrane, 4 RCTs, 86 participants aged 7–41; very low certainty): too few data to say whether exercise can replace them. No adverse events occurred during exercise sessions [[Heinz2022Exercise]].
- Exercise training (Cochrane, 15 studies, 487 participants): very low- to low-quality evidence of benefit to aerobic capacity, lung function and quality of life. The authors found "no reason to actively discourage" exercise [[Radtke2017Exercise]].
- Non-invasive ventilation as an airway-clearance adjunct (Cochrane, 14 studies, 261 participants, all ≤3 months): evidence is very uncertain for sputum clearance, lung function and length of stay. Participants reported feeling less tired after BiPAP than after a PEP mask [[Sousa2025NIV]].

### infection · Why infection matters: Pseudomonas, MRSA, Burkholderia
- In 3,323 US children aged 1–5 years, P. aeruginosa in respiratory cultures in 1990 carried a 2.6-fold higher 8-year risk of death (95% CI 1.6 to 4.1) than its absence [[Emerson2002]].
- In US registry data (137,819 patient-years), mortality was 27.7 per 1,000 patient-years with MRSA versus 18.3 without. The adjusted hazard ratio was 1.27 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.45) [[Dasenbrook2010]].
- In a UK centre, 31 of 33 people with initial B. cenocepacia infection became chronically infected, versus 8 of 16 with B. multivorans (p < 0.001). B. cenocepacia was linked to shorter survival than P. aeruginosa (p = 0.01) [[Jones2004]].
- Deaths from "cepacia syndrome" occurred with both Burkholderia species [[Jones2004]].
- On ETI, sputum densities of S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, Stenotrophomonas, Achromobacter and Burkholderia fell by 2–3 log10 CFU/mL after 1 month. Most people stayed culture-positive for the organisms they had before (Established) [[Nichols2023]].

### infection · Early Pseudomonas eradication
- The CFF 2014 guideline strongly recommends (grade A) inhaled antibiotics for initial or new P. aeruginosa growth. The favoured regimen is inhaled tobramycin 300 mg twice daily for 28 days [[Mogayzel2014Pa]].
- The same guideline recommends against prophylactic antipseudomonal antibiotics to prevent acquisition (grade D). It recommends oropharyngeal cultures, rather than routine bronchoscopy, for people who cannot expectorate (grade B) [[Mogayzel2014Pa]].
- ELITE treated 88 people (≥6 months old) with 28 days of tobramycin inhalation solution 300 mg twice daily, then randomised them to stop or take another 28 days. Median time to recurrence was similar in both arms [[Ratjen2010ELITE]].
- In ELITE, 93% (28-day) and 92% (56-day) were free of P. aeruginosa 1 month after treatment. At the final visit, 66% and 69% were still free [[Ratjen2010ELITE]].
- EPIC randomised 304 children aged 1–12 within 6 months of first P. aeruginosa to cycled or culture-based inhaled tobramycin (28 days), each with oral ciprofloxacin or placebo, for 18 months [[Treggiari2011EPIC]].
- In EPIC, exacerbation rates did not differ between cycled and culture-based therapy (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.54 to 1.66). Adding ciprofloxacin gave no benefit (HR 1.45, 95% CI 0.82 to 2.54) [[Treggiari2011EPIC]].
- In the Cochrane eradication review (11 trials, 1,449 participants), fewer people were still P. aeruginosa-positive at 1 month after inhaled tobramycin than after placebo (OR 0.06, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.18; low certainty) [[LangtonHewer2023Erad]].

### infection · Chronic Pseudomonas suppression (inhaled antibiotics)
- Ramsey 1999 ran two placebo-controlled trials in 520 people (mean age 21) with P. aeruginosa. Inhaled tobramycin 300 mg twice daily was given in three 28-days-on/28-days-off cycles (24 weeks) [[Ramsey1999TOBI]].
- At week 20, FEV1 rose 10% on tobramycin versus a 2% fall on placebo (P < 0.001). Sputum P. aeruginosa density fell 0.8 log10 CFU/g versus a 0.3 rise [[Ramsey1999TOBI]].
- Hospitalisation was 26% less likely on tobramycin (95% CI 2% to 43%), with no detectable ototoxicity or nephrotoxicity [[Ramsey1999TOBI]].
- Isolates with tobramycin MIC ≥8 μg/mL rose from 25% to 32% on tobramycin, versus 20% to 17% on placebo [[Ramsey1999TOBI]].
- TOBI label, quoted as published: 300 mg twice daily by inhalation "in alternating periods of 28 days on drug, followed by 28 days off drug", for people aged ≥6 years with P. aeruginosa [[FDA-TOBI]].
- The TOBI label notes that safety and efficacy were not shown under age 6, at FEV1 <25% or >75% predicted, or with Burkholderia cepacia. Its warnings cover bronchospasm, ototoxicity (tinnitus, hearing loss), nephrotoxicity, neuromuscular effects and embryo-fetal toxicity [[FDA-TOBI]].
- AIR-CF1 (164 people aged ≥6, FEV1 25–75%, no recent antipseudomonal antibiotics or azithromycin) gave 28 days of aztreonam lysine 75 mg three times daily [[RetschBogart2009AZLI]].
- In AIR-CF1, CFQ-R respiratory score improved by 9.7 points and FEV1 by 10.3% predicted versus placebo [[RetschBogart2009AZLI]].
- AIR-CF2 (211 people on frequent inhaled tobramycin) found aztreonam lysine lengthened median time to needing more antipseudomonal antibiotics by 21 days (92 versus 71 days; P = 0.007). FEV1 improved by 6.3% [[McCoy2008AZLI]].
- Cayston label, quoted as published: one 75 mg vial 3 times a day for a 28-day course (followed by 28 days off), for people aged 7 and older, with a bronchodilator before each dose and only with the Altera nebulizer [[FDA-Cayston]].
- Inhaled colistimethate dry powder (Colobreathe) was non-inferior to tobramycin inhalation solution for FEV1 at 24 weeks in 380 randomised people (ITT difference −0.98%, 95% CI −2.74 to 0.86) [[Schuster2013Colistin]].
- In that trial colistin resistance stayed at ≤1.1%. 90.7% versus 53.9% rated their device easy to use [[Schuster2013Colistin]].
- Levofloxacin inhalation solution was non-inferior to tobramycin inhalation solution for FEV1 in 282 people (difference 1.86% predicted, 95% CI −0.66 to 4.39). Dysgeusia was the most common adverse event [[Elborn2015LIS]].
- In a placebo-controlled trial (330 people), levofloxacin inhalation did not lengthen time to exacerbation. It improved relative FEV1 by 1.31% at 28 days [[Flume2016LIS]].

### infection · Pulmonary exacerbations and IV antibiotics
- In 8,479 US registry exacerbations treated with IV antibiotics (2003–2006), 25% failed to recover to 90% of baseline FEV1 within 3 months [[Sanders2010]].
- Risk factors for not recovering included female sex, pancreatic insufficiency, undernutrition, Medicaid insurance, chronic P. aeruginosa, B. cepacia complex or MRSA, and ABPA [[Sanders2010]].
- STOP2 (982 adults) randomised early responders to 10 or 14 days of IV antimicrobials. Mean ppFEV1 gains were 12.8 versus 13.4 points (difference −0.65, 95% CI −3.3 to 2.0), so 10 days was non-inferior [[Goss2021STOP2]].
- In STOP2, slower responders were randomised to 21 or 14 days. Gains were 3.3 versus 3.4 points (difference −0.10, 95% CI −1.3 to 1.1), so 21 days was not superior [[Goss2021STOP2]].
- Adjunctive oral prednisone in people not responding by day 7 of IV antibiotics did not improve FEV1 recovery. 50% versus 39% recovered baseline FEV1 by day 14 (difference 11%, 95% CI −11 to 34%) [[Waters2024Prednisone]].
- US registry exacerbation rates fell from 70.6 to 14.7 (per the registry's rate unit) after the pandemic and ETI. In Canada they fell from 53.1 to 30.2. The authors estimated ETI's effect at 2.48 times the pandemic effect [[Stephenson2025PEx]].

### infection · Aminoglycoside toxicity monitoring
- TOPIC compared once-daily with three-times-daily IV tobramycin (with ceftazidime) for exacerbations (219 completed per protocol). FEV1 gains were similar: 10.4% versus 10.0% predicted (difference 0.4%, 95% CI −3.3 to 4.1) [[Smyth2005TOPIC]].
- In children, once-daily dosing was less nephrotoxic: creatinine change −4.5% versus +3.7% (difference −8.0%, 95% CI −15.7 to −0.4) [[Smyth2005TOPIC]].
- In 18 adults given once-daily IV tobramycin 10 mg/kg/day for ≥10 days, cochleotoxic audiometric change was found in 89% at 2 weeks and 93% at 4 weeks after treatment [[Harruff2021Ototox]].
- That study's authors note that reported ototoxicity rates with tobramycin vary from 0 to 56%, depending on methods [[Harruff2021Ototox]].
- A prospective cohort found significant pure-tone threshold shifts after a single IV tobramycin course (n = 18) compared with no therapy (n = 13). The authors argue for routine ototoxicity monitoring [[Garinis2021Ototox]].
- Risk factors for aminoglycoside-associated acute kidney injury in CF included an aminoglycoside in the prior 90 days, longer duration, low serum albumin and co-administered trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole [[Downes2015AKI]].

### infection · Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM)
- In a 21-centre US study of 986 people with CF aged ≥10, NTM prevalence in sputum was 13.0% (range by centre 7–24%). M. avium complex made up 72% and M. abscessus 16% [[Olivier2003]].
- Recommendation 2 of the CFF/ECFS consensus: NTM cultures annually in people who spontaneously expectorate and have a stable clinical course [[Floto2016NTM]].
- Recommendation 3: people who cannot produce sputum and have no features of NTM disease do not need screening cultures [[Floto2016NTM]].
- Recommendation 23: people on chronic azithromycin who have a positive NTM culture should not continue azithromycin while NTM disease is evaluated, "as azithromycin monotherapy may lead to resistance" [[Floto2016NTM]].
- M. abscessus treatment, intensive phase: a daily oral macrolide (preferably azithromycin), 3–12 weeks of IV amikacin, and one or more of IV tigecycline, imipenem or cefoxitin [[Floto2016NTM]].
- M. abscessus treatment, continuation phase: oral macrolide plus inhaled amikacin plus 2–3 of minocycline, clofazimine, moxifloxacin and linezolid. Monotherapy "should never be used" [[Floto2016NTM]].
- Clarithromycin-sensitive MAC disease: a daily oral macrolide (preferably azithromycin), rifampin and ethambutol [[Floto2016NTM]].
- Treatment duration: 12 months beyond culture conversion (three consecutive negative cultures) [[Floto2016NTM]].
- Monitoring: sputum NTM cultures every 4–8 weeks during treatment, and a drug-toxicity schedule covering hearing loss, visual loss, renal impairment and liver tests [[Floto2016NTM]].
- Whole-genome sequencing of 168 M. abscessus isolates from 31 people at one UK adult centre found two clusters of near-identical M. a. massiliense in 11 people. This strongly suggests person-to-person transmission despite conventional cross-infection measures (Established) [[Bryant2013Lancet]].
- A global genomic study concluded that most M. abscessus infections come from transmission of recently emerged dominant clones, possibly via fomites and aerosols. These clones were linked to worse clinical outcomes [[Bryant2016]].
- In contrast, a 2003 US multicentre study found unique NTM strains in almost all people within each centre, so it did not support person-to-person spread at that time [[Olivier2003]].

### infection · Infection prevention and control (CFF 2013 update), quoted as published
- Core principle: "all individuals with CF may harbor potentially transmissible pathogens", so the precautions apply to everyone with CF, whatever their culture results [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- Quoted as published: "Separate all people with CF from others with CF, regardless of their respiratory tract culture results, at least 6 feet (2 meters) in all settings" [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- The guideline cites data that droplets can travel as far as 6 feet (2 metres) [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- People with CF should wear a surgical mask in healthcare settings. Staff should use Contact Precautions (gown and gloves) for all people with CF, in both clinic and hospital [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- People with CF, their families and friends should all perform hand hygiene [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- Inpatients with CF should have a single-patient room. Only people with CF from the same household may share [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- Quoted as published: "Only 1 person with CF may attend a CF Foundation–sponsored indoor event" [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- The guideline recommends against CF-specific camps and retreats, and encourages camps and sports with people who do not have CF [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- Pulmonary function testing options include testing in the exam room at the start of the visit, a negative-pressure room, a HEPA-filtered lab, or allowing 30 minutes between people with CF [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- The guideline cites P. aeruginosa recovered from hospital-room air 45 minutes to 2 hours after a person with CF left [[Saiman2014IPC]].

### transplant · Advanced lung disease complications
- Massive haemoptysis had an average annual incidence of 0.87% and occurred in 4.1% of people overall in US registry data. Nearly 60% of affected people had FEV1 <40% predicted. It carried increased 2-year mortality [[Flume2005Hemoptysis]].
- Pneumothorax had an average annual incidence of 0.64% (about 1 in 167 people per year) and occurred in 3.4% overall. Nearly 75% of affected people had FEV1 <40% predicted [[Flume2005Pneumothorax]].
- Pneumothorax risk was higher with P. aeruginosa (OR 2.3), B. cepacia (OR 1.8) and FEV1 <30% (OR 1.5) [[Flume2005Pneumothorax]].
- The CFF hemoptysis and pneumothorax recommendations were built by Delphi expert consensus, because evidence was insufficient for evidence-based grading [[Flume2010]].
- Among 3,340 US adults who reached FEV1 <30% predicted, median transplant-free survival was 6.6 years (95% CI 5.9 to 7.0). 37.4% died without transplant and 28.5% had a transplant (population statistics) [[Ramos2017FEV30]].
- In that cohort, predictors of death without transplant were supplemental oxygen (HR 2.1), B. cepacia (HR 1.8), BMI ≤18 (HR 1.6), female sex (HR 1.6), insulin-treated CF-related diabetes (HR 1.4) and ≥1 exacerbation per year (HR 1.7) [[Ramos2017FEV30]].

### transplant · CFF 2019 lung transplant referral guideline, quoted as published
- The guideline makes 21 recommendation statements. Recommendation 2: CF care teams should start a discussion about lung transplantation with everyone with CF and an FEV1 less than 50% predicted [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- Adults (≥18): refer no later than when FEV1 is <50% and rapidly declining (>20% relative decline in 12 months), or <40% with markers of shortened survival, or <30% predicted [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- Children (<18): refer no later than when FEV1 is <50% and rapidly declining, or <50% with markers of shortened survival, or <40% predicted [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- Markers that prompt referral regardless of FEV1: 6-minute walk distance <400 m, hypoxaemia, hypercarbia (PaCO2 >50 mmHg), or pulmonary hypertension (PA systolic >50 mmHg on echo or RV dysfunction) [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- Further referral triggers when FEV1 is <40%: BMI <18 (adults), >2 exacerbations per year needing IV antibiotics, massive haemoptysis (>240 mL) needing ICU or embolisation, or pneumothorax [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- Special consideration for referral is advised for women, especially younger women, and for people shorter than 162 cm, even when other thresholds are not met [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- About 10% of US individuals with a stable FEV1 <30% die without transplant each year after reaching that threshold [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- In France, 50% of CF deaths occurred without transplant [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- Before deciding someone is not a candidate, the guideline recommends consulting at least two transplant centres. CF and transplant teams should communicate at least every 6 months for candidates [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- Organisms that may affect candidacy, depending on the centre: B. cenocepacia, B. gladioli, B. dolosa, M. abscessus and Scedosporium. The guideline advises consulting both local and distant centres [[Ramos2019Referral]].

### transplant · Transplant outcomes and special infections
- The CFF guideline cites the ISHLT registry report: median post-transplant survival among adults with CF of 9.5 years (population statistic) [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- 2005–2016 CF lung transplant survival at 1, 3 and 5 years: US (2,653 recipients) 88.3%, 71.8% and 60.3%; Canada (470) 90.5%, 79.9% and 69.7%. People in the US were also more likely to die on the waitlist [[Stephenson2021Transplant]].
- Among people with FEV1 <40%, US residents had a higher risk of death without transplant than Canadians (HR 1.79, 95% CI 1.52 to 2.1) and a lower transplant rate (HR 0.66) [[Ramos2021ALD]].
- These gaps were larger for people with Medicaid/Medicare and for non-white people [[Ramos2021ALD]].
- In 121 CF transplants at one US centre, people infected with B. cepacia complex before transplant had higher 6-month mortality (33% versus 12%, p = 0.01). Genomovar III (B. cenocepacia) carried the highest risk (5 of 12 versus 0 of 8 deaths) [[Aris2001Bcc]].
- CFF/ECFS NTM consensus: everyone being considered for transplant should be evaluated for NTM disease. Current or previous NTM-positive samples should not by themselves preclude consideration [[Floto2016NTM]].
- Persistent M. abscessus or MAC despite optimal therapy is "not an absolute contraindication to lung transplant referral". Treatment should start before listing [[Floto2016NTM]].

### transplant · Transplant demand in the ETI era
- In US transplant registry data, new CF waitlistings fell 78% and CF lung transplants fell 72% in the ETI era, compared with the pre-ETI modulator era. Waitlist removals for improving condition rose 18-fold [[Merlo2025Transplant]].
- In France, first-time CF lung transplants fell from 13.4 to 1.8 per 1,000 non-transplanted people per year (2015–2019 versus 2020–2024; P < 0.0001). A small group still needed transplant [[Dhote2026Transplant]].
- In the US, 488 of 1,666 eligible lung transplant recipients with CF (29.3%) were newly prescribed ETI after transplant. CF-centre prescribing pattern was a strong predictor [[Burdis2025ETILTx]].

### transplant · Palliative care (CFF 2021 consensus), quoted as published
- The CFF recommends that CF care teams deliver primary palliative care as part of usual CF care "at the time of diagnosis and throughout the disease course" [[Kavalieratos2021Palliative]].
- It recommends specialty palliative care consultation "when an individual with CF is considering or declines transplantation" [[Kavalieratos2021Palliative]].
- It recommends screening people aged ≥12 with the Integrated Palliative Care Outcome Scale (IPOS) annually and at disease milestones. It recommends offering at least one primary caregiver annual screening with the Brief Assessment Scale for Caregivers [[Kavalieratos2021Palliative]].
- It recommends goals-of-care discussions and advance care planning across the lifespan, and caregiver support from diagnosis through bereavement [[Kavalieratos2021Palliative]].

### Exam hooks
- Dornase alfa (rhDNase) cleaves extracellular DNA in purulent sputum. In Fuchs 1994 it cut exacerbations by 28% (once daily) and raised FEV1 by about 5.8% [[Fuchs1994Dornase]].
- 7% hypertonic saline works osmotically to rehydrate the airway surface. Amiloride blocks the benefit [[Donaldson2006]].
- Hypertonic saline reduced exacerbations by 56% in people aged ≥6 [[Elkins2006HS]], but not in children under 6 (ISIS, rate ratio 0.98) [[Rosenfeld2012ISIS]].
- CFF grade A chronic therapies for moderate-to-severe disease: dornase alfa, inhaled tobramycin and inhaled aztreonam (the latter two with persistent P. aeruginosa) [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- CFF recommends against routine inhaled or oral corticosteroids (grade D) in people without asthma or ABPA [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
- Azithromycin 3 days a week: hazard ratio for exacerbation 0.65 in Saiman 2003 [[Saiman2003Azithro]].
- Before starting chronic azithromycin, NTM must be considered. Stop azithromycin if NTM grows, to avoid macrolide resistance [[Floto2016NTM]].
- TOBI is cycled 28 days on, 28 days off [[FDA-TOBI,Ramsey1999TOBI]]. Cayston is given three times daily for 28 days [[FDA-Cayston]].
- First P. aeruginosa: inhaled tobramycin for 28 days (grade A). 56 days was no better [[Mogayzel2014Pa,Ratjen2010ELITE]].
- STOP2: in early responders, 10 days of IV antibiotics was non-inferior to 14. For slow responders, 21 days was not better than 14 [[Goss2021STOP2]].
- About 1 in 4 exacerbations treated with IV antibiotics does not recover to baseline FEV1 [[Sanders2010]].
- Once-daily IV tobramycin is as effective as three-times daily and less nephrotoxic in children [[Smyth2005TOPIC]].
- No airway clearance technique has been shown superior. Choice is individualised [[Flume2009ACT,Wilson2019ACT]].
- PEP mask beat the vest (HFCWO) on exacerbations in a 1-year RCT [[McIlwaine2013HFCWO]].
- Infection control quoted as published: 6 feet (2 m) between people with CF, masks in healthcare settings, and one person with CF at indoor CFF events [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- M. abscessus spreads between people with CF. Whole-genome sequencing showed this [[Bryant2013Lancet,Bryant2016]].
- B. cenocepacia (genomovar III) is the Burkholderia species linked to worse survival and higher post-transplant mortality [[Jones2004,Aris2001Bcc]].
- Adult transplant referral thresholds: discuss at FEV1 <50%, refer by <40% with risk markers, and refer by <30% in all cases [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- SIMPLIFY: on ETI with good lung function, stopping hypertonic saline or dornase for 6 weeks was non-inferior for ppFEV1 [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]].

### In plain words
- Thick mucus traps germs in the lungs. Airway clearance means daily breathing techniques, devices or exercise that help move that mucus up and out [[Flume2009ACT]].
- There is no single "best" airway clearance method. The best one is the one you can do well and keep doing, chosen with your care team [[Wilson2019ACT]].
- Dornase alfa is a mist that breaks up DNA in thick mucus, so it is easier to cough out [[Fuchs1994Dornase]].
- Salty-water mist (hypertonic saline) pulls water into the airways and thins the mucus [[Donaldson2006]].
- Some inhaled antibiotics are taken in cycles of 4 weeks on and 4 weeks off. This keeps germs like Pseudomonas down while limiting side effects [[FDA-TOBI]].
- When Pseudomonas is found for the first time, a short course of inhaled antibiotic can often clear it [[Ratjen2010ELITE]].
- People with CF can pass some germs to each other. That is why guidance asks people with CF to keep at least 6 feet (2 metres) apart and wear masks at the clinic. This is quoted as published, not as personal advice [[Saiman2014IPC]].
- Strong IV antibiotics like tobramycin can affect hearing and the kidneys. Care teams check hearing and blood tests to catch problems early [[Garinis2021Ototox,Downes2015AKI]].
- On newer CFTR modulators, some people with good lung function can stop some inhaled treatments for a while without losing lung function. Any change should be planned with the CF team [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]].
- Talking about lung transplant early does not mean things are hopeless. It is a normal part of planning ahead [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- Palliative care is extra support for comfort and quality of life. It can start at any age and go alongside all other CF care [[Kavalieratos2021Palliative]].
- Survival numbers after transplant come from large registries. They describe groups of people, not what will happen to any one person [[Stephenson2021Transplant]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Do people on ETI still need daily mucoactive inhalers? SIMPLIFY found stopping hypertonic saline or dornase non-inferior over 6 weeks in people with good lung function [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]]. It was short, not powered for exacerbations, and showed more respiratory adverse events in the small ppFEV1 60–70% subgroup. Status: longer-term data pending (HERO-2, named in the SIMPLIFY report).
- Can exercise replace airway clearance? Guidelines endorse exercise as an adjunct [[Flume2009ACT]]. Cochrane evidence on substitution is very low certainty [[Heinz2022Exercise]]. Status: open, and a top research priority for people with CF per that review.
- Is the vest (HFCWO) as good as PEP? One long-term RCT favoured PEP on exacerbations [[McIlwaine2013HFCWO]]. Systematic reviews call overall device evidence low quality and find no clear superiority [[Morrison2020Osc,Wilson2019ACT]]. Status: unresolved.
- Does hypertonic saline help under 6 years? ISIS showed no exacerbation benefit [[Rosenfeld2012ISIS]]. Cochrane found a small lung clearance index gain at 48 weeks in preschool children [[Wark2023HS]]. Status: depends on the outcome measured.
- How is M. abscessus acquired? A 2003 multicentre study found mostly unique strains [[Olivier2003]]. Later whole-genome studies showed transmission and globally spreading clones [[Bryant2013Lancet,Bryant2016]]. Status: transmission is now accepted as a major route (Established), and the exact route is still debated (Hypothesis: fomites and aerosols).
- How long should IV antibiotics run? STOP2 supports 10 days for early responders and no gain from 21 days [[Goss2021STOP2]]. Paediatric data are lacking, since STOP2 enrolled adults only.
- How does ETI change infection control and microbiology? Pathogen density falls 2–3 log10 but most infections persist [[Nichols2023]]. The 2013 IP&C rules predate modulators [[Saiman2014IPC]]. Status: no updated CFF IP&C guideline was validated in this pass.
- Transplant referral after ETI: transplant numbers fell sharply [[Merlo2025Transplant,Dhote2026Transplant]]. The 2019 FEV1-based referral thresholds were built before ETI [[Ramos2019Referral]]. Status: how to time referral for people who respond partly to ETI is unsettled.
- Burkholderia and transplant: the higher post-transplant mortality data come from single centres and the early 2000s [[Aris2001Bcc]]. Centre policies vary [[Ramos2019Referral]].
- Aminoglycoside hearing damage: rates range from 0 to 56% depending on how hearing is measured [[Harruff2021Ototox]]. The best monitoring schedule is not standardised [[Garinis2021Ototox]].
