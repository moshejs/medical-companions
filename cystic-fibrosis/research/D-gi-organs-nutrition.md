# D · Digestive, liver, endocrine and other organs; nutrition and enzymes

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: digestive, organs, nutrition.

This pass covers the parts of cystic fibrosis (CF) outside the lungs: the pancreas (exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, pancreatitis), the gut (meconium ileus, DIOS, constipation, reflux, gastrointestinal cancer), the liver and bile ducts, CF-related diabetes (CFRD), bone, male and female reproduction (the male side only; fertility counselling and pregnancy belong to pass H), joints, kidneys, hearing, salt loss and pseudo-Bartter syndrome, and nutrition: growth and BMI goals, energy needs, pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT), fat-soluble vitamins, salt, tube feeding, and weight gain on CFTR modulators.

## Findings

### digestive · Exocrine pancreas: how common insufficiency is, and how it is measured
- Pancreatic insufficiency (PI) affects about 85% of people with CF; most are PI soon after birth, and some keep pancreatic sufficiency (PS) for part or all of life [[Singh2017PI]].
- A Cochrane review states that 80% to 90% of people with CF need pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) to prevent malnutrition [[Somaraju2020]].
- In 538 people with CF with documented pancreatic status, each complete CFTR genotype was linked only with PI or only with PS, never both; PS occurred with one or two "mild" alleles (e.g. R117H, R334W, R347P, A455E, P574H), and PI with two "severe" alleles (e.g. F508del, G542X, W1282X, G551D) [[Kristidis1992]].
- About 10% of CF alleles may confer pancreatic sufficiency (Kristidis 1992, citing earlier work by the same group) [[Kristidis1992]].
- Fecal elastase-1 (FE-1): in 725 people with CF and 243 healthy controls from four countries, the best cut-off separating CF-PI from normal function was 184 µg/g; the authors suggest using a range (160–200 µg/g) rather than a single cut-off [[Walkowiak2002]].
- The CFF infant guideline (2009) lists starting PERT when there are symptoms, fecal elastase <200 µg/g, coefficient of fat absorption <85%, or two CFTR variants associated with PI [[Borowitz2009]].
- The CFF infant guideline recommends measuring pancreatic status by fecal elastase or coefficient of fat absorption in all infants with CF, and rechecking it in pancreatic-sufficient infants who lose weight or develop gastrointestinal symptoms [[Borowitz2009]].
- Healthy infants, even premature ones, have fecal elastase in the normal adult range by two weeks of age (CFF infant guideline) [[Borowitz2009]].
- Studies of the PROMISE pediatric cohort used >200 µg/g as the normal range for fecal elastase and <100 µg/g as severe insufficiency [[Green2026PROMISEPeds]].
- The ESPEN-ESPGHAN-ECFS guideline recommends an annual assessment, including fecal pancreatic elastase, for pancreatic-sufficient patients [[Turck2016Nutrition]].

### digestive · Pancreatitis in pancreatic-sufficient people
- Among 277 pancreatic-sufficient people with CF from two population databases, 62 (22%) had pancreatitis [[Ooi2011]].
- Of those with pancreatitis, 70% had genotypes with mild pancreatic insufficiency prevalence (PIP) scores versus 30% with moderate-severe scores (P = .004) [[Ooi2011]].
- Cumulative pancreatitis by age 50 was 50% for mild-PIP genotypes versus 27% for moderate-severe-PIP genotypes (P = .006); hazard ratio 2.4 (95% CI 1.3–4.5) [[Ooi2011]].
- People with pancreatitis were diagnosed with CF later (median 14.9 vs 9.3 years) and had lower sweat chloride (74.5 vs 82.8 mmol/L) than those without [[Ooi2011]].
- Teaching point: pancreatitis needs surviving acinar tissue, so it is a complication of PS, not PI (Established) [[Ooi2011,Singh2017PI]].

### nutrition · Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT): label and guideline doses (quoted, not instructions)
- Creon (pancrelipase) is indicated "for the treatment of exocrine pancreatic insufficiency in adult and pediatric patients"; dosing is based on lipase units [[FDA-Creon]].
- Creon label starting doses for people older than 12 months: 1,000 lipase units/kg/meal for ages >12 months to <4 years; 500 lipase units/kg/meal for ages 4 years and older [[FDA-Creon]].
- Creon label ceiling: do not exceed 2,500 lipase units/kg/meal, 10,000 lipase units/kg/day, or 4,000 lipase units/g fat ingested/day (people >12 months) "without further investigation" [[FDA-Creon]].
- Creon label: snacks take about half the meal dose; daily dosing should reflect about three meals plus two or three snacks [[FDA-Creon]].
- Creon label for infants (birth to 12 months): 3,000 lipase units (one capsule) per 120 mL of formula or per breastfeeding [[FDA-Creon]].
- CFF infant guideline: start PERT at 2,000–5,000 lipase units per feeding (usually described as 120 mL), adjusted up to no more than 2,500 lipase units/kg per feeding, with a maximum of 10,000 lipase units/kg/day [[Borowitz2009]].
- Creon label: swallow capsules whole; if needed, sprinkle contents on acidic soft food with pH ≤4.5 (e.g. applesauce); do not crush or chew, because loss of the enteric coating can irritate the mouth and inactivate the enzyme [[FDA-Creon]].
- Creon label warnings also include hyperuricemia at high doses (enzymes contain purines), a theoretical risk of porcine virus transmission, and hypersensitivity to porcine proteins [[FDA-Creon]].
- Cochrane (2020): 14 trials (641 children and adults) compared PERT formulations, mostly over 4 weeks; evidence was moderate to very low quality, with limited evidence favouring enteric-coated microspheres over non-enteric-coated preparations [[Somaraju2020]].
- The 2020 Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics guideline found not enough evidence to reduce or stop PERT when people start CFTR modulators; if a person stops, it advises monitoring fecal elastase, weight, gastrointestinal symptoms and fat-soluble vitamins [[McDonald2021]].

### nutrition · Fibrosing colonopathy
- US case-control study: 29 children with fibrosing colonopathy needing colectomy (1990–1994; mean age 5.0 years) versus 105 matched controls with CF [[FitzSimmons1997]].
- Mean enzyme dose was 50,046 lipase units/kg/day in cases versus 18,985 in controls [[FitzSimmons1997]].
- Compared with 0–24,000 units/kg/day, adjusted relative risk was 10.9 (95% CI 1.6–71.8) for 24,001–50,000 units/kg/day and 199.5 (95% CI 9.9–4,026.0) for >50,000 units/kg/day [[FitzSimmons1997]].
- Product strength, coating and manufacturer were not associated with risk; the authors supported keeping most daily doses below 10,000 lipase units/kg [[FitzSimmons1997]].
- The Creon label states that doses above 6,000 lipase units/kg/meal have been associated with colonic stricture in children younger than 12 years [[FDA-Creon]].

### digestive · Meconium ileus
- Meconium ileus is often the first sign of CF and occurs in about 20% of people diagnosed with CF [[Sathe2017MI]].
- A review gives meconium ileus at birth in 13% to 17% of all people with CF, with a twin study showing more concordance in identical than non-identical twins [[vanderDoef2011]].
- First-year survival after meconium ileus rose from 55% (born 1958–1972) to 96% (born 1973–1987), as reported in the same review [[vanderDoef2011]].
- History of meconium ileus is a risk factor for later liver disease: incidence rate ratio 5.5 (95% CI 2.7–11) in an Italian prospective cohort [[Colombo2002Liver]]; it was also an independent risk factor in a French cohort of 3,328 people [[Boelle2019]].

### digestive · Distal intestinal obstruction syndrome (DIOS) and constipation
- ESPGHAN CF Working Group definitions: DIOS is "an acute complete or incomplete faecal obstruction in the ileocaecum"; constipation is gradual faecal impaction of the total colon [[Houwen2010]].
- In 8 European CF centres (2001–2005, patients ≤18 years), DIOS incidence was 6.2 (95% CI 4.4–7.9) episodes per 1,000 patient-years [[Houwen2010]].
- Of 39 children with DIOS: 20% relapsed, 92% were pancreatic insufficient, 44% had a history of meconium ileus, and 82% had a severe genotype; conservative treatment worked in 49 of 51 episodes (96%) [[Houwen2010]].
- ECFS guidance gives DIOS prevalence estimates of 5 to 12 episodes per 1,000 patients per year in children, with higher rates in adults; treatment is rehydration plus stool softeners or gut lavage [[Colombo2011DIOS]].
- Lifetime prevalence of DIOS is about 8% in children and 16% in adults with CF; 10% to 20% of people with CF develop at least one DIOS episode early after lung transplant [[vanderDoef2011]].
- International prospective study (10 countries, 102 patients, 112 episodes): incidence similar in children and adults; medical treatment failed only in complete DIOS (11%) [[Munck2016]].
- In that study, factors more common in people with DIOS than in controls were meconium ileus (40% vs 18%), pancreatic insufficiency (92% vs 84%), liver disease (22% vs 12%), diabetes (49% vs 25%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (68% vs 52%) [[Munck2016]].
- Children with a history of meconium ileus needed surgery for DIOS more often (15% vs 2%) [[Munck2016]].
- Constipation was present in 47% of 214 children with CF; low total fat absorption and meconium ileus were independent risk factors, while fibre and fluid intake were not [[vanderDoef2010]].
- Lifetime constipation prevalence is 26–47% in children and 42% in adults with CF [[vanderDoef2011]].

### digestive · Gastro-oesophageal reflux (GERD)
- Gastro-oesophageal reflux is common in children and adults with CF, and pathological GERD is frequent at all ages [[Maqbool2017GERD]].
- A systematic review of pediatric studies found an overall GERD incidence of 46% (range 19–81%; 7 studies, 212 patients) [[Ng2021GERD]].
- In 82 children who had antireflux surgery (all Nissen fundoplication), major complications occurred in 18%; 59% had symptom improvement at a median follow-up of 2 years [[Ng2021GERD]].

### digestive · Gastrointestinal cancer risk and colorectal screening
- US CF registry, 1990–2009 (41,188 people): overall cancer risk in non-transplanted people was similar to background (SIR 1.1, 95% CI 1.0–1.3), but digestive-tract cancer was raised (SIR 3.5, 95% CI 2.6–4.7) [[Maisonneuve2013]].
- After transplant, digestive-tract cancer risk was much higher (SIR 17.3, 95% CI 10.7–26.5), mostly bowel cancers [[Maisonneuve2013]].
- The same study found raised testicular cancer (SIR 1.7) and lymphoid leukemia (SIR 2.0), and lower melanoma (SIR 0.4) [[Maisonneuve2013]].
- Meta-analysis (6 cohorts, 99,925 people): pooled gastrointestinal cancer SIR 8.13 (95% CI 6.48–10.21) versus the general population [[Yamada2018]].
- Pooled SIR was 21.13 (14.82–30.14) after lung transplant versus 4.18 (3.10–5.62) without transplant [[Yamada2018]].
- Site-specific pooled SIRs: small bowel 18.94, biliary tract 17.87, colon 10.91, pancreas 6.18 [[Yamada2018]].
- The CFF colorectal cancer task force states that the colorectal cancer risk in adults with CF is 5–10 times the general population, and 25–30 times after organ transplantation [[Hadjiliadis2018]].
- CFF 2018 recommendations: colonoscopy is the preferred screening method, starting at age 40, with 5-year re-screening and 3-year surveillance intervals and a CF-specific intensive bowel preparation [[Hadjiliadis2018]].
- Transplant recipients with CF should begin colorectal cancer screening at age 30, within 2 years of transplantation [[Hadjiliadis2018]].

### organs · CF-related liver disease (CFLD) and bile ducts
- ECFS 2011: about 5–10% of people with CF develop multilobular cirrhosis during the first decade of life; most then develop portal hypertension (mainly variceal bleeding), and liver failure usually comes later, after childhood [[Debray2011]].
- ECFS 2011 definition: consider liver disease if at least two of these are present: abnormal physical examination, persistently abnormal liver tests, pathological ultrasound; biopsy if in doubt [[Debray2011]].
- Italian prospective cohort (177 people, 14-year median follow-up): liver disease incidence 1.8 per 100 patient-years (95% CI 1.3–2.4), falling sharply after age 10 [[Colombo2002Liver]].
- Risk factors in that cohort: meconium ileus (IRR 5.5), male sex (2.5), severe mutations (2.4); cirrhosis incidence 4.5% within a median 5 years of liver disease diagnosis [[Colombo2002Liver]].
- Among 17 people with cirrhosis in the Italian cohort, 13 developed portal hypertension, 4 oesophageal varices, and 1 needed liver transplantation [[Colombo2002Liver]].
- French cohort (3,328 pancreatic-insufficient people born after 1985): CFLD incidence rose about 1% per year, reaching 32.2% by age 25; severe CFLD rose only after age 5, reaching 10% by age 30 [[Boelle2019]].
- Risk factors for CFLD and severe CFLD in the French cohort were male sex, F508del homozygosity and meconium ileus; earlier ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) use did not change the incidence of severe CFLD [[Boelle2019]].
- Cochrane review of UDCA (4 trials, 137 participants; doses 10–20 mg/kg/day for up to 12 months): no data on death or transplant, low to very low quality evidence, "insufficient evidence to justify its routine use" [[Cheng2017]].
- CFF 2024 hepatobiliary guideline recommends against routine UDCA to prevent advanced liver disease in all people with CF [[Sellers2024]].
- CFF 2024 screening: annual liver tests (total bilirubin, AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, GGT, platelets) from diagnosis; annual abdominal examination for hepatosplenomegaly; abdominal ultrasound at least every 2 years from age 3 (or diagnosis) until late adolescence [[Sellers2024]].
- CFF 2024 definitions: advanced CF liver disease (aCFLD) means nodular liver, advanced fibrosis (F4), multilobular cirrhosis, or non-cirrhotic portal hypertension; CF hepatobiliary involvement (CFHBI) covers milder findings such as hepatomegaly, steatosis, focal biliary cirrhosis, persistent enzyme elevation, cholelithiasis or sclerosing cholangitis [[Sellers2024]].
- CFF 2024: people with CF and advanced liver disease should start CFRD screening at diagnosis of the liver disease, even before age 10, then yearly [[Sellers2024]].
- CFF 2024: children with CF and cirrhosis should have yearly liver cancer (HCC) screening with ultrasound and alpha-fetoprotein [[Sellers2024]].
- CFF 2024 recommends CFTR modulators for people with CFHBI under close monitoring, because lung benefits outweigh liver risk, but recommends against them in decompensated advanced liver disease [[Sellers2024]].
- CFF 2024 notes that modulator trials reported transaminase rises >3× upper limit of normal in about 5% of participants, with few stopping treatment [[Sellers2024]].
- Hepatomegaly is the most common hepatobiliary finding, reported in up to 30% of people with CF in some studies [[Sellers2024]].
- CFF 2024 recommends cholecystectomy for people with CF who have symptomatic gallbladder disease [[Sellers2024]].
- Gallbladder and bile-duct disease (including gallstones) are common complications of CF [[Assis2017]].
- For people with both advanced liver and advanced lung disease, CFF 2024 suggests considering combined lung-liver transplantation [[Sellers2024]].

### organs · CF-related diabetes (CFRD): how common
- University of Minnesota cohort (872 people, to 2008): CFRD was present in 2% of children, 19% of adolescents and 40–50% of adults [[Moran2009Trends]].
- In younger people, CFRD without fasting hyperglycemia predominates; fasting hyperglycemia becomes more common with age [[Moran2009Trends]].
- European CF registry 2008–2015 (insulin use as proxy): CFRD prevalence 0.8% under 10 years, 9.7% at 10–19, 24.1% at 20–29 and 32.7% at ≥30; total 21.6% (as reported in ISPAD 2022) [[Ode2022ISPAD]].
- US CFF registry 2020: about 20% of 20-year-olds, 30% of 30-year-olds and just under 40% of 40-year-olds had CFRD (ISPAD 2022) [[Ode2022ISPAD]].
- ISPAD 2022 notes that both registries may underestimate CFRD, because US screening rates are below 70% in teens and below 40% in adults [[Ode2022ISPAD]].
- Incidence is about 4–9% per year in Denmark and 2.7 cases per 100 person-years at Minnesota; up to 80% of older people with severe genotypes have CFRD [[Ode2022ISPAD]].
- CFRD is more common with female sex, pancreatic insufficiency, severe genotypes and CF liver disease [[Ode2022ISPAD]].
- Insulin secretion defects are present in essentially all people with CF, partly from damage to islets that spreads from exocrine destruction (ISPAD 2022) [[Ode2022ISPAD]].

### organs · CFRD: effect on lungs, weight and survival
- In 152 people with CF followed for 4 years, lung function fell fastest in those with CFRD without fasting hyperglycemia at baseline and slowest in those with normal glucose tolerance [[Milla2000]].
- At baseline in that cohort, 45% had normal glucose tolerance, 38.8% impaired glucose tolerance and 15.8% CFRD without fasting hyperglycemia [[Milla2000]].
- Danish matched study (38 pairs): weight, BMI, FEV1 and FVC began to fall years before diabetes was diagnosed; differences emerged 4 years (weight, BMI), 3 years (FVC) and 1.25 years (FEV1) before diagnosis [[Lanng1992]].
- Minnesota 1987–2002: median survival was 49.5 years in males without diabetes, 47.4 in males with diabetes, 47.0 in females without diabetes and 30.7 in females with diabetes (these are cohort statistics from that era, not predictions) [[Milla2005]].
- From 1992–1997 to 2003–2008, CFRD mortality fell from 6.9 to 3.2 deaths per 100 patient-years in females and from 6.5 to 3.8 in males; the sex difference disappeared [[Moran2009Trends]].
- Of 61 people diagnosed with CFRD in 2003–2008, only 2 died; the authors credit early diagnosis and aggressive treatment [[Moran2009Trends]].

### organs · CFRD: screening, diagnosis and treatment (quoted from guidelines)
- ADA/CFF 2010: annual CFRD screening should begin by age 10 in all people with CF who do not have CFRD, using a 2-hour 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- ADA/CFF 2010: HbA1c is not recommended as a CFRD screening test because it is not sensitive enough [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- ADA/CFF 2010 diagnostic thresholds include 2-hour OGTT glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L), fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), or HbA1c ≥6.5% [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- Fasting glucose alone misses the diagnosis in about half of people with CFRD, those without fasting hyperglycemia [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- During pulmonary exacerbations treated with IV antibiotics or systemic steroids, ADA/CFF 2010 advises screening with fasting and 2-hour post-meal glucose for the first 48 hours [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- ISPAD 2022: yearly OGTT should begin at least by age 10; there is not enough evidence to replace OGTT with continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), or to change screening for people on highly effective modulators [[Ode2022ISPAD]].
- The ESPEN-ESPGHAN-ECFS guideline also states that glucose tolerance testing should start at 10 years of age [[Turck2016Nutrition]].
- ADA/CFF 2010: people with CFRD should be treated with insulin; oral diabetes agents are not as effective and are not recommended outside research trials [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- ADA/CFF 2010: HbA1c is measured quarterly in CFRD, with a goal of <7% for many people, individualized [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- ADA/CFF 2010 and ISPAD 2022: yearly microvascular-complication screening begins 5 years after CFRD diagnosis (or when fasting hyperglycemia first appears, if the date is unknown) [[Moran2010CFRD,Ode2022ISPAD]].
- Gestational diabetes screening by OGTT is recommended at 12–16 and 24–28 weeks for pregnant women with CF not known to have CFRD [[Moran2010CFRD,Ode2022ISPAD]].
- CFRDT randomized trial (100 adults; 61 completers with CFRD without fasting hyperglycemia): insulin aspart reversed a pre-treatment BMI loss of 0.30 units/year to a gain of 0.39 units after 1 year (P = .02); repaglinide gave an early gain that was not sustained; placebo showed no change [[Moran2009CFRDT]].
- In the CFRDT trial, no group showed a difference in rate of lung-function decline or hospitalizations [[Moran2009CFRDT]].
- ISPAD 2022 recommends insulin pumps and CGM for CFRD where appropriate [[Ode2022ISPAD]].
- CFRD is a distinct type of diabetes, different from type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and is linked with worse health outcomes in CF [[Kelly2013]].
- ADA/CFF 2010 and ISPAD 2022: people with CFRD keep the high-calorie, high-salt, high-fat CF diet; calorie restriction is almost never appropriate [[Moran2010CFRD,Ode2022ISPAD]].

### organs · Bone disease
- Meta-analysis of 12 studies (1,055 adults with CF, median age 28.2): pooled osteoporosis prevalence 23.5% (95% CI 16.6–31.0) and osteopenia 38% (95% CI 28.2–48.3) [[Paccou2010]].
- Pooled prevalence of radiological vertebral fractures was 14% (95% CI 7.8–21.7) and of non-vertebral fractures 19.7% (95% CI 6.0–38.8) [[Paccou2010]].
- CFF bone guide: some studies found 50–75% of adults with CF have low bone density; risk factors include poor absorption of fat-soluble vitamins, altered sex hormones, chronic lung infection and inflammatory cytokines, low activity and glucocorticoids [[Aris2005]].
- European (ECFS) bone mineralisation guidelines give Delphi-validated consensus statements on the assessment, prevention and treatment of CF-related skeletal deficits [[SermetGaudelus2011]].
- ESPEN-ESPGHAN-ECFS: bone mineral density examination should be performed from age 8–10 years [[Turck2016Nutrition]].
- CFF vitamin D guideline: yearly screening of vitamin D status, preferably at the end of winter, with serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D of at least 30 ng/mL (75 nmol/L) considered sufficient [[Tangpricha2012]].

### organs · Reproduction (male), joints, kidneys, hearing
- Up to 97–98% of men with CF are infertile because of obstructive azoospermia from congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD); sperm production itself is usually intact [[Kaltsas2025]].
- Sperm retrieval with IVF and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) achieves high success rates for men with CF (narrative review) [[Kaltsas2025]].
- CBAVD also occurs in men without CF: in 102 men with CBAVD and no CF symptoms, 19 had CFTR variants on both copies and 54 on one, and the 5T allele in one copy plus a CF variant in the other was the most common cause [[Chillon1995]].
- Meta-analysis: 78% of men with CBAVD had at least one CFTR variant (46% two, 28% one); 5T allele odds ratio 9.25 (95% CI 7.07–12.11) [[Yu2012CBAVD]].
- CF arthropathy was found in 4.9% of 6,069 people in the German CF registry (2016–2017): 8.4% of adults versus 0.7% of under-18s [[Grehn2021]].
- In adults, arthropathy was associated with female sex (OR 2.10), chronic P. aeruginosa (OR 1.83), CFRD (OR 1.69), pancreatic insufficiency (OR 2.39) and sinusitis/polyps (OR 1.91) [[Grehn2021]].
- Kidney stones affect 3.0% to 6.3% of people with CF versus 1–2% of age-matched controls; proposed mechanisms include hyperoxaluria, hypocitraturia, hyperuricosuria and loss of the oxalate-degrading gut bacterium Oxalobacter formigenes after antibiotics [[Gibney2003]].
- A 2026 US single-centre cohort (332 adults) found a lifetime prevalence of any kidney-stone event of 32% (32% with PI vs 24% without); 51% of those with stones had recurrences [[Ryoo2026]].
- In 63 children with CF who had IV aminoglycosides, 15 (24%) had ototoxicity on extended high-frequency audiometry; 11 of them had received at least 10 courses [[AlMalky2015]].
- Standard pure-tone audiometry missed some cases; risk factors were number of IV aminoglycoside courses, age and lower lung function [[AlMalky2015]].

### organs · Salt loss, dehydration and pseudo-Bartter syndrome
- Pseudo-Bartter syndrome in CF is hypochloremia, hyponatremia, hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis from salt loss in sweat; it can be the first presentation of CF in early childhood [[Mantoo2020]].
- CFF infant guideline: infants with CF need supplemental salt; it recommends 1/8 teaspoon of table salt per day from diagnosis, increasing to 1/4 teaspoon at 6 months [[Borowitz2009]].
- One-eighth teaspoon of table salt contains about 12.5 mEq of sodium; the guideline notes the healthy-infant daily sodium intake (5 mEq at 0–6 months, 16 mEq at 7–12 months) may be insufficient for a baby with CF [[Borowitz2009]].
- Baby foods contain no added salt, which leaves older infants with CF vulnerable to low sodium intake [[Borowitz2009]].
- ESPEN-ESPGHAN-ECFS recommends sodium supplementation, with urinary sodium:creatinine ratio measurement [[Turck2016Nutrition]].

### nutrition · Growth goals and why nutrition matters
- US registry (3,142 children born 1989–1992): weight-for-age above the 10th percentile at age 4 was linked to better lung function from 6 to 18 years [[Yen2013]].
- Children with age-4 weight-for-age above the 50th percentile had fewer pulmonary exacerbations, fewer hospital days and less impaired glucose tolerance or diabetes by 18, and a survival advantage through childhood [[Yen2013]].
- CFF infant guideline: aim for weight-for-length at or above the 50th percentile by 2 years of age ("slightly chubby" in the primary-care summary) [[Borowitz2009]].
- CFF goals (Stallings 2008, as restated in the ADA/CFF CFRD guideline): BMI ≥50th percentile for ages 2–20 years; for adults, BMI ≥22 kg/m² for women and ≥23 kg/m² for men [[Moran2010CFRD,Stallings2008]].
- 2020 Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics guideline: people who kept weight, length, weight-for-length and BMI above the 50th percentile from infancy had better FEV1% predicted 4 to 16 years later, with no extra gain above the 85th percentile [[McDonald2021]].
- The same guideline suggests a 70th percentile cut-off when WHO (rather than CDC) growth charts are used, because WHO charts label fewer children as underweight [[McDonald2021]].
- Children and adolescents with BMI-for-age below the 50th percentile, or worrying trends, should be referred for full nutrition assessment by a registered dietitian [[McDonald2021]].
- ESPEN-ESPGHAN-ECFS: nutritional management should start as early as possible after diagnosis; exclusive breastfeeding is recommended, and energy intake should be adapted to reach normal weight and height for age [[Turck2016Nutrition]].

### nutrition · Energy, fat and the CF diet
- ADA/CFF 2010: people with CF need a very high-calorie diet, usually 120–150% of the recommended daily intake for age, because of higher resting energy expenditure and calorie loss from malabsorption [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- ISPAD 2022 dietary table: calories 120–150% of normal intake for age and sex, fat 40% of total energy, carbohydrate 45–50%, protein 200% of the non-CF reference intake, and unrestricted salt [[Ode2022ISPAD]].
- The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics notes that CFTR modulators may lower energy expenditure, so energy prescriptions may need to be reduced to avoid excess weight gain [[McDonald2021]].
- The same guideline describes nutrition care as historically built on a high-energy, high-fat "legacy diet" [[McDonald2021]].

### nutrition · Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K
- CFF infant guideline: start CF-specific multivitamins with at least the recommended vitamins A, D, E and K shortly after diagnosis [[Borowitz2009]].
- Blood levels of fat-soluble vitamins should be checked about 2 months after starting supplements and yearly thereafter (CFF infant guideline) [[Borowitz2009]].
- CFF consensus doses for infants 0–12 months, as tabulated in the infant guideline: vitamin A 1,500 IU, vitamin E 40–50 IU, vitamin D 400 IU, vitamin K 300–500 µg daily [[Borowitz2009]].
- For ages 1–3 years the same table lists vitamin A 5,000 IU, vitamin E 80–150 IU, vitamin D 400–800 IU and vitamin K 300–500 µg daily [[Borowitz2009]].
- Many newly diagnosed infants have low levels of one or more fat-soluble vitamins; low vitamin E is associated with lower cognitive performance [[Borowitz2009]].
- A randomized trial in 100 pancreatic-insufficient people with CF found a liposomal vitamin preparation raised 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 more than standard preparations over 3 months (+9.7 vs +2.0 ng/mL) (Early evidence) [[Nowak2022]].

### nutrition · Supplements and tube feeding
- ESPEN-ESPGHAN-ECFS: oral nutritional supplements, then polymeric enteral tube feeding, are recommended when growth or nutritional status is impaired [[Turck2016Nutrition]].
- The CFF 2016 enteral tube feeding guideline covers when to consider tube feeding, checking for other causes of poor nutrition, preparing for tube placement, and care afterwards [[Schwarzenberg2016Tube]].
- ESPEN-ESPGHAN-ECFS found not enough evidence to recommend essential fatty acids, growth hormone, appetite stimulants or probiotics routinely [[Turck2016Nutrition]].

### nutrition · Weight, the gut and the pancreas on CFTR modulators
- PROMISE (487 people ≥12 years starting elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor [ETI]): at 6 months, ppFEV1 rose 9.76 points, sweat chloride fell 41.7 mmol/L, and BMI rose significantly [[Nichols2022PROMISE]].
- In 134 adults at one US centre, the rate of BMI change was 1.47 kg/m² per year (95% CI 1.08–1.87) greater after starting ETI; blood pressure also rose [[Petersen2022ETI]].
- UK adults (n = 40): median BMI rose from 23.0 to 24.6 kg/m² after ETI while reported energy intake fell from 2,551 to 2,153 kcal/day, so weight gain may not be explained by eating more [[Caley2023]].
- Overweight and obesity are estimated at 13% to 18% of people with CF; risk is higher in older, male, pancreatic-sufficient people with milder genotypes and better lung function [[McDonald2021]].
- Reviews describe a shift in CF nutrition concern from undernutrition toward overweight and metabolic disease (hypertension, dyslipidemia, steatosis) in the modulator era (Early evidence) [[Harindhanavudhi2026]].
- PROMISE-GI (438 people ≥12 years): after 6 months of ETI, gastrointestinal symptom scores improved only slightly; fecal calprotectin fell by 66.2 µg/g, but fecal elastase and steatocrit did not meaningfully change [[Schwarzenberg2023PROMISEGI]].
- PROMISE pediatric sub-study (ages 6 to <12): no significant change in gastrointestinal symptoms at 12 months; normal calprotectin rose from 22% to 40% at 6 months; no significant change in the share with fecal elastase >200 or <100 µg/g [[Green2026PROMISEPeds]].
- Ivacaftor in children aged 12 to <24 months with gating variants (ARRIVAL): fecal elastase-1 rose and immunoreactive trypsinogen fell at 24 weeks; the authors suggest early ivacaftor may preserve exocrine pancreatic function (Early evidence) [[Rosenfeld2018ARRIVAL]].
- Ivacaftor in children aged 2–5 (KIWI): at 24 weeks, BMI z-score rose by 0.4 and weight z-score by 0.2; 15% had liver tests >8× the upper limit of normal [[Davies2016KIWI]].
- ETI in children ≥2 years (96-week extension): rises in fecal elastase-1 were maintained, with 9 children above the pancreatic-sufficiency threshold (Early evidence) [[Goralski2026]].

### Exam hooks
- About 85% of people with CF are pancreatic insufficient [[Singh2017PI]].
- Each CFTR genotype predicts PI or PS; mild alleles such as R117H give PS [[Kristidis1992]].
- Pancreatitis in CF happens in pancreatic-sufficient people, especially with mild genotypes (HR 2.4) [[Ooi2011]].
- Fecal elastase <200 µg/g supports pancreatic insufficiency; <100 µg/g is severe [[Borowitz2009,Green2026PROMISEPeds]].
- PERT ceiling (Creon label): 2,500 lipase units/kg/meal, 10,000 units/kg/day or 4,000 units/g fat/day [[FDA-Creon]].
- Fibrosing colonopathy is linked to very high enzyme doses (>50,000 units/kg/day: RR 199.5) [[FitzSimmons1997]].
- Meconium ileus affects about 1 in 5 (13–20%) newborns with CF and is often the first sign [[Sathe2017MI,vanderDoef2011]].
- DIOS = obstruction in the ileocaecum; constipation = gradual impaction of the whole colon [[Houwen2010]].
- Multilobular cirrhosis develops in about 5–10% of people with CF, usually in the first decade [[Debray2011]].
- Routine ursodeoxycholic acid is not recommended to prevent advanced CF liver disease [[Sellers2024,Cheng2017]].
- CFRD affects about 2% of children, 19% of adolescents and 40–50% of adults (Minnesota) [[Moran2009Trends]].
- CFRD screening: yearly 2-hour 75-g OGTT from age 10; HbA1c is not a screening test [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- Insulin is the recommended treatment for CFRD [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- Lung function and weight decline for years before CFRD is diagnosed [[Lanng1992]].
- CF colorectal cancer screening: colonoscopy from age 40, or from 30 after transplant [[Hadjiliadis2018]].
- About 97–98% of men with CF have CBAVD [[Kaltsas2025]].
- Osteoporosis affects about 1 in 4 adults with CF (pooled 23.5%) [[Paccou2010]].
- Pseudo-Bartter: hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis from sweat salt loss [[Mantoo2020]].
- CFF goals: BMI ≥50th percentile in children; adults ≥22 (women) and ≥23 (men) kg/m² [[Moran2010CFRD,Stallings2008]].
- Fat-soluble vitamins: A, D, E, K; CF-specific multivitamins start soon after diagnosis [[Borowitz2009]].

### In plain words
- Most people with CF (about 85 in 100) have a pancreas that does not send enough digestive enzymes to the gut, so they take enzyme capsules with meals and snacks [[Singh2017PI]].
- Enzymes help the body take in fat and fat-soluble vitamins. The dose is set by the CF team; more is not always better [[FDA-Creon,FitzSimmons1997]].
- Some babies with CF are born with a bowel blocked by thick first stool (meconium ileus). It is treated soon after birth [[Sathe2017MI]].
- Constipation is very common in CF. A sudden blockage near the end of the small bowel (DIOS) needs quick medical care [[vanderDoef2010,Munck2016]].
- The liver can be affected in CF. Yearly blood tests and regular ultrasounds help the team find problems early [[Sellers2024]].
- Many adults with CF develop a special type of diabetes. A yearly sugar-drink test from age 10 helps catch it early, and insulin treats it [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- People with CF lose more salt in sweat. Babies usually get a little extra salt each day, as their CF team advises [[Borowitz2009]].
- Good growth in early childhood is linked with healthier lungs later on [[Yen2013]].
- Many people with CF need more calories than others their age, but needs are changing for people on newer CFTR modulator medicines [[Moran2010CFRD,McDonald2021]].
- On CFTR modulators, many people gain weight. Some now need help with a healthy weight rather than gaining more [[Petersen2022ETI,Harindhanavudhi2026]].
- Bones can become thinner in CF. Vitamin D, calcium, activity and bone scans are part of care [[Paccou2010,Tangpricha2012]].
- Most men with CF are born without the tubes that carry sperm, but they usually make sperm, and many can become fathers with fertility treatment [[Kaltsas2025]].
- Numbers here describe groups of people, not what will happen to any one person.

## Open questions and controversies
- Energy needs: the brief cites 110–200% of normal energy needs; the guidelines I could read give 120–150% of the age-recommended intake [[Moran2010CFRD,Ode2022ISPAD]], and the 2020 Academy guideline says modulators may lower energy needs [[McDonald2021]]. Status: the 110–200% figure is not used here because I could not read it in its source.
- Ursodeoxycholic acid: ECFS 2011 recommended screening partly to start UDCA early [[Debray2011]]; Cochrane found insufficient evidence for routine use [[Cheng2017]], earlier UDCA did not change severe CFLD in France [[Boelle2019]], and CFF 2024 recommends against routine preventive use [[Sellers2024]]. Status: practice is moving away from routine UDCA.
- CFRD screening in the modulator era: ISPAD 2022 finds inadequate evidence to change OGTT screening for people on highly effective modulators or to replace OGTT with CGM [[Ode2022ISPAD]]. Status: open; research ongoing.
- Can modulators restore the pancreas? In young children, ivacaftor and ETI raised fecal elastase, some above the sufficiency threshold [[Rosenfeld2018ARRIVAL,Goralski2026]]; in people ≥6 years, fecal elastase did not meaningfully change [[Schwarzenberg2023PROMISEGI,Green2026PROMISEPeds]]. Status: (Early evidence) that timing matters; guidelines do not support stopping PERT on modulators [[McDonald2021]].
- Why weight rises on ETI: intake fell while BMI rose in one UK cohort [[Caley2023]]; overnutrition and cardiometabolic risk may increase [[Petersen2022ETI,Harindhanavudhi2026]]. Status: (Hypothesis) that lower energy expenditure and better absorption explain the gain; mechanism unclear.
- Why women with CFRD had worse survival in the 1990s: the Minnesota authors speculated about hormones and inflammation [[Milla2005]]; the sex gap later disappeared with earlier diagnosis and treatment [[Moran2009Trends]]. Status: (Hypothesis) for mechanism; gap closed in that cohort.
- DIOS incidence: estimates differ with definitions; Houwen 2010 found higher pediatric rates than older reports [[Houwen2010]], and the international study found similar rates in children and adults [[Munck2016]], while ECFS 2011 cited higher adult rates [[Colombo2011DIOS]].
- Kidney stones: older reviews give 3.0–6.3% [[Gibney2003]]; a 2026 single-centre cohort counting incidental imaging findings gives 32% lifetime prevalence [[Ryoo2026]]. Status: difference largely reflects method and era.
