## Findings

### screening · History and rationale
- Serum immunoreactive trypsinogen (IRT) was raised in all children with CF in the first few months of life. Crossley, Elliott and Smith (New Zealand, 1979) set up a dried blood-spot IRT assay and proposed it as a newborn screening test [[Crossley1979]].
- CF newborn screening became feasible in 1979 with dried-blood-spot IRT. Infants with a high first IRT had a second IRT measured (the IRT/IRT protocol) [[McGarry2025NBS,Crossley1979]].
- After the CFTR gene and F508del were identified in 1989, some programmes in the early 1990s replaced the second IRT with DNA testing for F508del (the IRT/DNA protocol). This let programmes lower the IRT cutoff, raising sensitivity and speeding up diagnosis. The cost was a bias toward infants of European descent [[McGarry2025NBS,Riordan1989,Kerem1989]].
- The sweat test came first. In 1953 di Sant'Agnese and colleagues reported abnormal sweat electrolytes in CF. In 1959 Gibson and Cooke standardised sweat collection by pilocarpine iontophoresis [[DiSantAgnese1953,Gibson1959]].
- A 2003 CDC and CF Foundation workshop concluded that newborn screening for CF is justified, based on "moderate benefits and low risk of harm" [[Grosse2004MMWR]].
- The same report found that US newborn screening led to diagnosis a median of 1 year earlier than detection from symptoms [[Grosse2004MMWR]].
- Harms it named: anxiety and misunderstanding in families of carrier infants, and possible cross-infection in clinics. It said both can be reduced by genetic counselling and infection control [[Grosse2004MMWR]].
- In 2007 a CF Foundation workshop published guidelines for setting up state CF newborn screening programmes [[Comeau2007]].
- By 2011, CF newborn screening had been enacted in all 50 US states and the District of Columbia [[Rock2011]]. CF newborn screening has been universal in the US since 2010 [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- Newborn bloodspot screening for CF was introduced across the UK in 2007 [[Schluter2020]].
- In Europe in 2016 there were 17 national programmes and 4 countries with regional programmes. Another 25 countries were not screening. IRT/DNA was the most common strategy, and 5 countries used no DNA analysis [[Barben2017Europe]].
- Most European national programmes met the ECFS standards for timeliness. They did less well on sensitivity and specificity [[Barben2017Europe]].

### screening · Evidence for benefit (Wisconsin randomised trial and cohorts)
- The Wisconsin CF Neonatal Screening Project randomised 650,341 newborns (1985–1994) to early diagnosis by screening or to standard diagnosis. Screening used IRT, with DNA testing for F508del added from 1991 [[Farrell2001,Farrell1997]].
- In the 1997 report, the screened group was diagnosed at a mean of 12 weeks of age versus 72 weeks in controls (diagnosis confirmed by sweat test) [[Farrell1997]].
- At diagnosis, the screened group had higher height and weight percentiles. Mean head-circumference percentile was 52nd versus 32nd in controls (P=0.003) [[Farrell1997]].
- Excluding infants with meconium ileus, mean (±SD) age at diagnosis was 13 ± 37 weeks for screened infants and 100 ± 117 weeks for controls [[Farrell2001]].
- Over 13 years, the screened group grew significantly better despite similar nutritional therapy, with far fewer children below the 10th percentile for weight and height. The authors called the trial "unequivocally positive" and found that catch-up growth after a late diagnosis may not be possible [[Farrell2001]].
- In the trial, classical CF (sweat chloride ≥60 mEq/L) had an incidence of 1:4189 [[Farrell2001]].
- Pulmonary outcomes were not better with screening by school age. Screened children had fewer abnormalities on their first chest radiograph, but the groups converged over time. After age 10, screened children had worse chest radiograph scores linked to earlier Pseudomonas aeruginosa acquisition. The authors pointed to respiratory infection and pancreatic status as the dominant factors [[Farrell2003AJRCCM]].
- In screened Wisconsin children, half had a frequent cough by 10.5 months of age. Half had potentially irreversible chest radiograph abnormalities by age 2, and about 85% showed radiographic progression by age 5 [[Farrell2003PP]].
- Cognitive scores were in the normal range overall. Children in the control group who had vitamin E deficiency at diagnosis (α-tocopherol <300 µg/dL) scored significantly lower [[Koscik2004]].
- A Cochrane review found two eligible trials (1,124,483 neonates, 210 with CF). Compared with screened children, controls had odds ratios of 4.12 (95% CI 1.64–10.38) for weight below the 10th percentile and 4.62 (1.69–12.61) for height [[Southern2009Cochrane]].
- In the same review, 88% of screened children and 75% of controls had lung function within normal limits at age 7 [[Southern2009Cochrane]].
- In UK registry data on ΔF508 homozygous children aged 1–10, newborn screening was associated with:
  - a higher height z score
  - a lower chance of height below the 10th percentile
  - fewer long-term therapies, compared with late clinical diagnosis
  - no difference in pulmonary outcome [[Sims2007]]
- In over 3000 UK children born 2000–2015, newborn screening was associated with:
  - a higher average lung function trajectory (+1.56 FEV1 percentage points, 95% CI 0.1–3.02)
  - later onset of chronic P. aeruginosa infection
  - higher weight at age 1 (+0.16 SD)
  - no narrowing of early health inequalities [[Schluter2020]]
- In 2003 registry data, 65.8% of Australian children with CF had been diagnosed after newborn screening, versus 7.2% of US children. Children diagnosed after screening had higher mean FEV1 (+5.3 percentage points, 95% CI 3.6–7.0) and higher BMI [[Martin2012]].
- Screening does not mean no lung disease. In 57 infants diagnosed by screening in Australia (median age 3.6 months), 84.2% had no respiratory symptoms, yet:
  - 21.1% had bacterial infection on bronchoalveolar lavage
  - 80.7% had an abnormal chest CT
  - 18.6% had bronchial dilatation [[Sly2009]]

### screening · Protocols: IRT/IRT, IRT/DNA, IRT/DNA/sequencing
- Every US CF newborn screening protocol starts with IRT on the newborn bloodspot. IRT is usually high in infants with CF, but it can also be high in carriers and for reasons unrelated to CF: birth stress, prematurity, ancestry, ambient temperature, season and kit-to-kit variation [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- In all US programmes, an infant whose IRT is judged high moves on to molecular testing for CFTR variants [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- Under IRT/DNA/SEQ, infants with one CF-causing variant on the panel go on to sequencing of all CFTR exons, flanking introns and selected intronic variants. Most such programmes refer only infants with two CF-causing variants for sweat testing [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- The 2025 CF Foundation guideline makes seven recommendations, each with 100% committee agreement:
  1. A floating IRT cutoff rather than a fixed one.
  2. A very-high-IRT referral strategy when the variant panel does not cover all CFTR2 CF-causing variants or does not reach ≥95% sensitivity in all ancestral groups.
  3. No limiting variant detection to F508del or the ACMG-23 panel.
  4. Screening for all CF-causing variants in CFTR2.
  5. Variant screening at least twice weekly.
  6. A CFTR sequencing tier after IRT and the variant panel.
  7. Notifying both the primary care provider and the CF specialist of abnormal results. [[McGarry2025NBS]]
- US fixed IRT cutoffs range from 50 to 100 ng/mL, and floating cutoffs from the 95th to the 98.8th percentile [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- In Colorado, 7 of 8 cases missed with a fixed cutoff of 60 ng/mL would have been found with a floating 96th-percentile cutoff. A survey of 13 state laboratories found more missed cases with fixed than floating cutoffs (3.48% vs 2.36%) [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- The very-high-IRT ("failsafe") strategy flags infants above about the 99th percentile even when no variant is found. In Victoria, Australia (1991–2003, F508del only), it found 18 of 227 infants with CF (8%). In New York (2002–2005, 32-variant panel) it found 5 of 106 (5%) [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- In New York (2003–2013), 75% of the 33 cases found by very high IRT were in infants who were Black, Hispanic, Asian, American Indian or multiracial [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- Sensitivity of a single-variant tier is low with a limited panel:
  - F508del alone: 56–90%
  - ACMG-23 panel: 47–93%
  - Both are lower still in American Indian, Asian, Black, Hispanic and multiracial infants. [[McGarry2025NBS]]
- Programmes that added a CFTR sequencing tier generally kept sensitivity above 90%, with specificity of 99–100% and positive predictive value of 34–77% [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- California's 3-step model (IRT ≥62 ng/mL, top 1.6%; 28–40 variant panel; then sequencing) screened 2,573,293 newborns in 2007–2012 [[Kharrazi2015]]:
  - 345 CF cases, 533 CRMS cases and 1617 carriers detected
  - 28 CF cases missed
  - sensitivity 92%, positive predictive value 34%
  - CF prevalence 1 in 6899 births
  - median age at referral 34 days
- Modelling in California found that adding third-tier sequencing raised detection in Asian, Black and Hispanic infants from 17–79% (panel alone) to 83–100% [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- Sequencing does not catch every variant. Structural and deep intronic variants are missed unless deletion/duplication testing is done or they are targeted [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- Parents of infants found to carry only one variant after sequencing (presumed carriers) should be told and offered genetic counselling [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- Infants were seen earlier, and delayed evaluation was less likely, when both the primary care provider and the CF specialist were notified than when only the primary care provider was [[McGarry2025NBS]].

### screening · False negatives, equity and delays
- Rock and colleagues listed 14 factors that can cause CF to be missed after newborn screening, across specimen collection, IRT and mutation testing, communication and sweat testing. They advised suspecting CF whenever symptoms fit, whatever the screening result [[Rock2011]].
- In London and South East England, 4 years after screening started:
  - 170 infants were diagnosed by screening (48% already had symptoms)
  - 10 screened children were missed
  - 6 of 34 infants presenting with meconium ileus had a negative screen
  - median age at diagnosis fell to 3 weeks [[Lim2014]]
- In US registry data for 2020, 3.8% of people with CF had a false-negative newborn screen and 11.8% had a delayed diagnosis. Black, Hispanic and mixed-race people were over-represented [[McGarry2023]].
- Detection of at least one CFTR variant across seven panels was 87.5–97.0% in non-Hispanic White people with CF and 41.9–93.1% in Black, Asian and Hispanic people with CF [[McGarry2023]].
- Of 912 infants with CF born in California (2007–2021), 84 had a false-negative screen: 38 because IRT was low and 46 because variant detection was incomplete. Asian infants (OR 6.3) and Black infants (OR 2.5) were more likely than non-Hispanic White infants to have a false negative [[McGarry2024]].
- Low IRT below the floating cutoff has been reported in up to 18% of infants with meconium ileus in an Italian series [[DeCelieGermana2023]].
- In a 2023 US caregiver survey (n=383), 45% reported waiting 8 or more days for the first CF evaluation after a positive screen. 43% were told their child's race or ethnicity predicted the chance of CF [[Heffernan2025]].

### screening · CRMS/CFSPID (positive screen, inconclusive diagnosis)
- A CF Foundation guideline coined the term CFTR-related metabolic syndrome (CRMS) in 2009. It covers infants with high IRT on screening, sweat chloride <60 mmol/L, and up to 2 CFTR variants, at least 1 of which is not clearly CF-causing [[Borowitz2009CRMS]].
- ECFS introduced the term "CF Screen Positive, Inconclusive Diagnosis" (CFSPID) through a Delphi process. It defined two groups [[Munck2015CFSPID]]:
  - group A: normal sweat chloride and two CFTR variants, at least one of unclear consequence
  - group B: intermediate sweat chloride and one or no CFTR variants
- In 2017 the CRMS and CFSPID terms were merged and made equivalent ("CRMS/CFSPID") [[Farrell2017Dx,Ren2017CRMS]].
- Where gene sequencing is not used, there are about 3–5 cases of CF for every case of CRMS/CFSPID. Some regions detect more CRMS/CFSPID than CF [[Ren2017CRMS]].
- Most infants with CRMS/CFSPID never develop CF disease. Between 10% and 20% develop features that raise concern for CF, such as a respiratory culture positive for P. aeruginosa [[Ren2017CRMS]].
- In US registry data for 2010–2012 [[Ren2015Pediatrics]]:
  - 1540 infants met criteria for CF and 309 for CRMS (ratio 5.0:1.0)
  - 40.8% of infants with CRMS had been entered with a clinical diagnosis of CF
  - 11% of infants with CRMS had a positive P. aeruginosa culture in the first year
- In a prospective 8-centre study (82 infants with CFSPID, 80 with CF), 9 of 82 (11%) were diagnosed with CF within 3 years. The authors advise repeat sweat testing at age 2–3 years and extensive genotyping [[Ooi2015]].
- A 2025 meta-analysis cites conversion from CRMS/CFSPID to CF in about 5–11% of children. Reclassifying 537 children against current CFTR2 classifications cut the number who would still be called CRMS/CFSPID [[Wyatt2025]].
- The 2021 ECFS guidance says most infants with CRMS/CFSPID stay healthy and do not convert to CF in childhood. It recommends a detailed assessment in the sixth year of life, including lung function and imaging, and a further appointment as a young adult [[Barben2021CFSPID]].
- CF Foundation 2009 CRMS guidance [[Borowitz2009CRMS]]:
  - a CF specialist assessment by 2 months of age (94% agreement)
  - a repeat sweat test to confirm an intermediate value or show it resolving to <30 or ≥60 mmol/L
  - infants with CRMS "may be treated per CF protocol" if P. aeruginosa grows on an oropharyngeal swab (83% agreement)
- In California, children with one CF-causing and one CFTR2 non-CF-causing variant had lower IRT and sweat chloride, better first-year growth and less persistent P. aeruginosa than children with two CF-causing variants [[Salinas2015]].

### screening · Prenatal and carrier testing (brief)
- ACOG Committee Opinion 691 states that information about carrier screening should be offered to every pregnant woman, ideally before pregnancy. If both partners are carriers, genetic counselling should be offered. Screening can be declined [[ACOG2017]].
- ACMG recommended a 23-variant core panel for pan-ethnic CF carrier screening in 2001, revised in 2004 [[Watson2004,Deignan2020]]. In 2023 ACMG replaced it with a minimum of 100 variants for more equitable detection across US ancestral groups [[Deignan2023,McGarry2025NBS]].
- CF was the first condition for which pan-ethnic carrier screening was recommended. ACMG's 2021 practice resource sets out a consistent approach to carrier screening for all individuals during pregnancy or preconception [[Gregg2021]].
- Over 16 years in Brittany, France, 289 fetuses with echogenic bowel on ultrasound were referred for CFTR analysis. CF was found in 7.6%, and ultrasound identified 10.7% of all CF cases in the region [[Scotet2010]].

### sweattest · Diagnostic criteria (CF Foundation 2017; 2008 for comparison)
- The 2017 CF Foundation guideline came from 32 experts in 9 countries, with ≥80% agreement required for each statement. 27 of 28 statements were approved [[Farrell2017Dx,Farrell2017History]].
- It recommends that CF be diagnosed at all ages by testing CFTR function with a sweat chloride test, and that CFTR2 variant classifications be used to aid diagnosis [[Farrell2017Dx]].
- Even in babies with 2 CF-causing variants found by screening, the diagnosis must be confirmed by showing CFTR dysfunction. Intestinal current measurement may be used when needed. The guideline also gives rules for presumptive diagnosis and for dating the age of diagnosis [[Farrell2017Screened]].
- Sweat chloride bands as used under the 2017 guideline, at all ages, including infants [[Farrell2017Dx,DeCelieGermana2023]]:
  - ≥60 mmol/L: diagnostic of CF
  - 30–59 mmol/L: intermediate
  - <30 mmol/L: negative (CF unlikely)
- Because registries follow many people with confirmed CF whose sweat chloride is 30–40 mmol/L, the 2017 update lowered the lower edge of the intermediate band from 40 to 30 mmol/L [[Sosnay2017Nonscreened]].
- The 2008 guideline used two sets of bands [[Farrell2008Dx]]:
  - infants up to 6 months: ≤29 mmol/L CF unlikely; 30–59 intermediate; ≥60 indicative of CF
  - all ages, the historic bands: ≤39 normal; 40–59 intermediate; ≥60 abnormal (first affirmed in 7200 tests from 1959–1966)
- People who present with symptoms, a family history or a positive screen, and who have intermediate values on two separate occasions, may have CF. Further CFTR testing is then needed [[Sosnay2017Nonscreened,Farrell2008Dx]].
- Two CF-causing variants in trans count as evidence of CFTR dysfunction. Variants with variable or unknown consequence do not, and other CFTR function tests may then help [[Sosnay2017Genetics]].
- Outside newborn screening, diagnosis rests on clinical evidence plus evidence of CFTR dysfunction: sweat chloride, CFTR genetics, or CFTR physiological tests [[Sosnay2017Nonscreened]].
- Rarely, infants with 2 CF-causing variants have a normal sweat chloride. The 2008 guideline states they have CF and should be followed at a CF centre. It also recommends parental DNA testing to show the variants are in trans [[Farrell2008Dx]].

### sweattest · Method and quality
- The Gibson–Cooke method (1959) uses pilocarpine iontophoresis to stimulate sweat, then measures sweat electrolyte concentration [[Gibson1959]].
- Adequate sweat is "at least 75 mg of sweat obtained by the Gibson-Cooke method or at least 15 μL obtained by the Macroduct coil collection method" [[Farrell2008Dx]].
- For an asymptomatic newborn with a positive screen, testing is recommended at ≥2 weeks of age, weight >2 kg and >36 weeks gestation at birth, ideally bilaterally. Symptomatic newborns (e.g. meconium ileus) can be tested from 48 hours if enough sweat is collected [[Farrell2008Dx]].
- A 2023 review summarises CF Foundation practice as sweat testing after a positive screen (or positive prenatal genetic test) by 4 weeks of age and not before 48 hours [[DeCelieGermana2023]].
- Sweat chloride is "the only analyte on which a diagnosis of CF should be based". Sweat conductivity or osmolality "should not be used for diagnosing CF at the present time" [[Farrell2008Dx]].
- The method must detect chloride accurately down to 10 mmol/L without added chloride. Samples from two sites must never be pooled [[Farrell2008Dx]].
- Laboratories should keep the "quantity not sufficient" (QNS) rate below 5% for infants over 3 months [[Farrell2008Dx]].
- In a US survey, the mean (±SD) QNS rate for infants ≤3 months old after a positive screen was 7.2% (±7.6), ranging from 0% to 40% across centres. The collection method did not affect the rate [[LeGrys2010]].
- QNS results are more common in infants younger than 10 days and in premature or low-birthweight infants [[DeCelieGermana2023]].
- Conductivity is a screening test, not a diagnostic test. In 444 infants without CF and 90 with CF, conductivity correlated with chloride (r=0.97). The false-positive rate at a >50 mmol/L cutoff was 16.7%, and the best cutoff was 69 mmol/L [[Domingos2015]].
- An Australasian laboratory survey found wide variation in conductivity cutoffs, sweat-rate criteria and recommended actions [[Greaves2018]].

### sweattest · CFTR function tests beyond the sweat test
- In 1981, nasal potential difference (NPD) in 24 people with CF was more than 3 standard deviations above healthy controls, other disease controls and heterozygotes. Amiloride lowered it more in CF [[Knowles1981]].
- The 2008 guideline describes the CF pattern on NPD as a high baseline potential difference plus a very low response to zero-chloride perfusate with isoproterenol. It notes that diagnostic cutoffs are not consistent across centres [[Farrell2008Dx,Rowe2011]].
- Intestinal current measurement (ICM) on rectal biopsies was validated in 130 subjects. A cumulative chloride secretory response cutoff of 34 µA/cm² separated pancreatic-sufficient CF from controls [[Derichs2010]].
- In 61 people with an unclear diagnosis, ICM classified 6 as pancreatic-sufficient CF and 55 as "CF unlikely" [[Derichs2010]].
- Forskolin-induced swelling of rectal organoids is strongly reduced in CF and tracks the anion currents measured in rectal biopsies. This makes it a candidate functional test (Early evidence) [[Dekkers2013]].
- The CF Foundation's review of 4 decades of diagnosis warns against using NPD and ICM too early, before they are validated and standardised [[Farrell2017History]].

### sweattest · Sweat chloride as a modulator biomarker
- Ivacaftor in people with CF aged 12 or older with a G551D variant: sweat chloride change through week 48 of −48.1 mmol/L versus placebo [[Ramsey2011STRIVE]].
- Elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor (ETI) in F508del/minimal-function genotypes, ages 12 and over: sweat chloride 41.8 mmol/L lower than placebo through 24 weeks [[Middleton2019ETI]].
- ETI in F508del homozygotes: sweat chloride −45.1 mmol/L (95% CI −50.1 to −40.1) versus tezacaftor–ivacaftor at 4 weeks [[Heijerman2019ETI]].
- PROMISE (487 people aged 12 and over starting ETI by prescription): sweat chloride fell by 41.7 mmol/L (95% CI −43.8 to −39.6) at 6 months. Mean values were 88.0 mmol/L at baseline, 49.1 at 1 month and 45.7 at 6 months [[Nichols2022PROMISE]].
- In PROMISE, people switching from ivacaftor to ETI (n=33, 97% F508del/G551D) went from a mean of 52.6 to 26.2 mmol/L at 6 months [[Nichols2022PROMISE]].
- SKYLINE (pooled, through week 24), sweat chloride <60 mmol/L [[Keating2025SKYLINE]]:
  - vanzacaftor–tezacaftor–deutivacaftor: 86%
  - ETI: 77%
  - odds ratio 2.21 (95% CI 1.55–3.15)
- SKYLINE (pooled, through week 24), sweat chloride <30 mmol/L [[Keating2025SKYLINE]]:
  - vanzacaftor–tezacaftor–deutivacaftor: 31%
  - ETI: 23%
  - odds ratio 2.87 (2.00–4.12)
- SKYLINE least-squares mean differences in sweat chloride versus ETI: −8.4 mmol/L in the F508del/minimal-function trial and −2.8 mmol/L in the second trial [[Keating2025SKYLINE]].
- Because modulators lower sweat chloride, a sweat test taken during modulator treatment no longer reflects untreated CFTR function. This has implications for interpreting a diagnostic test in someone already on a modulator (Hypothesis, inferred from the size of the effects above) [[Nichols2022PROMISE,Keating2025SKYLINE]].

### sweattest · Genetic testing and interpretation
- CFTR2 collected genotype and phenotype data on 39,696 people with CF. Of 159 variants with allele frequency ≥0.01%, 127 (80%) met clinical and functional criteria for causing disease. Penetrance in 2,188 fathers classed 12 as neutral, and 20 stayed indeterminate [[Sosnay2013]].
- As of September 2024, CFTR2 had interpreted 1167 CFTR variants and deemed 1085 CF-causing. CF-causing variants are expected to cause CF when present in trans with another CF-causing variant [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- In 2020 ACMG updated its technical standard for CFTR variant testing, noting that expanded panels and sequencing had become feasible [[Deignan2020]].
- Next-generation sequencing results can be filtered by reporting software to a customisable list of hundreds of variants. A laboratory reporting only a pre-set list still counts as running a "variant panel" even if it sequences [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- Variants not yet interpreted by CFTR2 are classified with the ACMG criteria. Pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants prompt the same follow-up as CF-causing variants [[McGarry2025NBS]].

### monitoring · Clinic schedule and cultures
- CF Foundation infant guideline: CF Center visits once a month for the first 6 months, then every 1–2 months for the rest of the first year [[Borowitz2009Infants]].
- The same guideline says that for infants with CF under 2 years old, "oropharyngeal cultures should be performed at least quarterly". The first culture is taken at the first or second visit after diagnosis, and cultures are repeated more often if there are respiratory symptoms [[Borowitz2009Infants]].
- The CF Foundation recommends clinic visits at least 4 times a year, with 4 respiratory cultures and 2 pulmonary function tests. In 2012 only 57.4% of US patients met this [[Wooldridge2015]].
- In one centre, a tracking system raised the share of patients meeting the target (≥4 visits, 4 cultures, 2 PFTs) from 47.5% in 2009 to 71.0% in 2013 [[Wooldridge2015]].
- Another centre raised the share attending clinic 4 or more times a year from 35% (2004) to 90% (2012) [[Berlinski2014]].
- A CF Foundation guideline (2016) covers routine pulmonary surveillance, treatment and nutrition for preschool children aged 2–5. It notes that lung disease often progresses silently at these ages, so sensitive objective tests matter [[Lahiri2016]].
- The ECFS best practice guidelines (2018 revision) set European standards for newborn screening, diagnosis, preventive treatment of lung disease, nutrition and complications [[Castellani2018ECFS]].

### monitoring · Spirometry and FEV1
- GLI 2012 reference equations for ages 3–95 were built from 97,759 records of healthy non-smokers. They gave separate equations for Caucasian, African-American, North East Asian and South East Asian people, plus a composite "other" equation [[Quanjer2012]].
- CF studies once used the Wang equations in childhood and Hankinson in adulthood. Switching between them creates a jump in percent predicted. GLI equations are now used in several national registry reports and also allow z-scores [[Szczesniak2017]].
- GLI Global (2022) is a race-neutral equation. It reweights the original GLI data and needs no race or ethnicity input. It fits similarly to the GLI 2012 "other" equation, with wider limits of normal [[Bowerman2023]].
- The 2023 ATS statement recommends replacing race- and ethnicity-specific equations with race-neutral average equations, and names GLI Global as the recommended equation. It adds a caution about uncertain effects [[Bhakta2023]].
- At one US centre, switching to GLI Global raised the share of Black patients classed as restricted from 26.8% to 37.5% and lowered it in White patients from 22.6% to 18.0%. The share with obstruction did not change significantly [[Moffett2023]].
- ATS and ERS updated spirometry technical standards in 2019, and interpretation standards in 2022 (reference equations, limits of normal, severity) [[Graham2019,Stanojevic2022]].
- FEV1 is the established marker of progression in CF lung disease and has been the key endpoint in pivotal trials. FEV1 decline has been linked to death, exacerbations, hospital stays and P. aeruginosa infection for 50 years [[Szczesniak2017]].
- Mean FEV1 % predicted decline per year in an epidemiologic cohort, with baseline FEV1 in brackets (Established) [[Konstan2007JPediatr]]:
  - ages 6–8: −1.12 (baseline 88.4%)
  - ages 9–12: −2.39 (baseline 85.3%)
  - ages 13–17: −2.34 (baseline 78.4%)
- A high baseline FEV1 was itself a risk factor for faster decline. The authors write that clinicians "should not be reassured by high lung function" [[Konstan2007JPediatr]].
- Severity bands used in CF research, quoted as published: mild >70% FEV1, moderate 40–69%, severe <40% [[Gee2003]].
- In a Toronto cohort of 673 patients (1977–1989), FEV1 below 30% predicted was linked to a 2-year mortality rate above 50%. Each 10% fall in FEV1 doubled the risk of death within 2 years (RR 2.0, 95% CI 1.9–2.2). This is a historic population statistic from before modern treatment [[Kerem1992]].
- After a pulmonary exacerbation treated with IV antibiotics, 25% of 8,479 exacerbations failed to recover to 90% of baseline FEV1 [[Sanders2010]].
- In the Wisconsin cohort, serial spirometry was not reliable with acceptable quality until after age 7. Chest radiograph scoring picked up disease earlier [[Farrell2003PP]].

### monitoring · Lung clearance index (multiple-breath washout)
- Inert-gas washout measures how unevenly ventilation is spread in the lungs. It can be done across a wider age range than spirometry and is more sensitive to early damage. A 2013 ERS/ATS consensus set equipment and test standards [[Robinson2013]].
- At preschool age, 35 of 48 children with CF (73%) had an abnormal LCI but only 5 had an abnormal FEV1. An abnormal preschool LCI predicted any abnormal school-age result with a positive predictive value of 94% [[Aurora2011]].
- In 60 children with CF (mean age 7.8 years), abnormal results were found in [[Owens2011]]:
  - LCI: 84%
  - HRCT: 85%
  - spirometry: 47%
  - CT score correlated with LCI (r=0.77) better than with spirometry (r=−0.43).
- A CF Foundation workshop (2015) judged LCI a valuable trial outcome for preschool children and for older patients with normal FEV1. It found insufficient data to support LCI in routine clinical management [[Subbarao2015]].
- ETI improved median LCI in school-age children (6–11 years) from 3 months onward [[Stahl2024]]:
  - F/MF genotype: −1.0
  - F/F genotype: −0.8
  - MRI global scores improved as well.
- In Danish children aged 6–17, mean LCI improved by 1.7 units 12 months after starting ETI [[Henriksen2025]].
- In F508del homozygotes aged 12 and over, lumacaftor–ivacaftor improved LCI (−1.6) and MRI scores without a significant change in FEV1. LCI and MRI may be more sensitive than FEV1 to the effect of a modulator (Early evidence) [[Graeber2021]].

### monitoring · Chest imaging
- In AREST CF (127 infants diagnosed by screening, CT and bronchoalveolar lavage at 3 months and at 1, 2 and 3 years), bronchiectasis prevalence rose from 29.3% at 3 months to 61.5% at 3 years [[Sly2013NEJM]].
- Risk factors for bronchiectasis in AREST CF [[Sly2013NEJM]]:
  - meconium ileus (OR 3.17)
  - respiratory symptoms (OR 2.27)
  - free neutrophil elastase in lavage fluid (OR 3.02)
  - gas trapping (OR 2.05)
- Expiratory CT scans had poor sensitivity (0.46) for bronchiectasis in young children. Lung volume during CT should be standardised [[Mott2013]].
- Chest MRI scores (morphology and perfusion) respond to CFTR modulators and are used as radiation-free outcomes in research (Early evidence) [[Stahl2024,Graeber2021]].

### monitoring · Annual reviews (other organs)
- CFRD screening (2010 ADA/CF Foundation guideline), quoted as published [[Moran2010CFRD]]:
  - screen with a 2-hour 75 g OGTT
  - "Annual screening for CFRD should begin by age 10 years in all CF patients who do not have CFRD"
  - A1C is not recommended as a screening test
- The same guideline notes that nutrition and lung function begin to decline 6–24 months before CFRD is diagnosed [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- ESPEN-ESPGHAN-ECFS nutrition guidelines [[Turck2016Nutrition]]:
  - glucose tolerance testing from age 10
  - bone mineral density testing from age 8–10
  - annual fecal elastase in pancreatic-sufficient patients
  - regular monitoring of fat-soluble vitamins
- Annual screening for liver disease is recommended. Liver disease is considered when at least two of these are present: abnormal examination, persistently abnormal liver tests, abnormal ultrasound [[Debray2011]].
- About 5–10% of people with CF develop multilobular cirrhosis in the first decade of life [[Debray2011]].
- A CF Foundation consensus guide addresses bone health. Some studies report low bone density in 50–75% of adults with CF [[Aris2005]].

### Exam hooks
- First newborn screen for CF: dried-blood-spot IRT, Crossley 1979 [[Crossley1979]].
- IRT is high in infants with CF but is not specific: carriers, prematurity and birth stress can raise it [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- Protocols: IRT/IRT, then IRT/DNA after the 1989 gene discovery, then IRT/DNA/SEQ (first used in California) [[McGarry2025NBS,Kharrazi2015]].
- Universal CF newborn screening started in the UK in 2007 and in the US in 2010 [[Schluter2020,McGarry2025NBS]].
- The Wisconsin RCT showed better growth and less malnutrition with screening, but no clear lung function benefit [[Farrell2001,Farrell2003AJRCCM]].
- Sweat chloride ≥60 mmol/L is diagnostic, 30–59 is intermediate and <30 is CF unlikely, at all ages (2017) [[Farrell2017Dx,DeCelieGermana2023]].
- The 2017 update lowered the bottom of the intermediate band from 40 to 30 mmol/L [[Sosnay2017Nonscreened]].
- Minimum sweat: ≥75 mg (Gibson–Cooke) or ≥15 µL (Macroduct) [[Farrell2008Dx]].
- Test infants at ≥2 weeks, >2 kg and >36 weeks gestation. Symptomatic newborns from 48 hours [[Farrell2008Dx]].
- Diagnosis is made on sweat chloride, never on conductivity [[Farrell2008Dx]].
- Two CF-causing variants still need CFTR dysfunction shown by a sweat test to confirm the diagnosis [[Farrell2017Screened]].
- CRMS = CFSPID: positive screen with inconclusive sweat and genetic results. 10–20% develop features of concern for CF [[Ren2017CRMS]].
- NPD in CF: high baseline potential difference, a large amiloride response and little chloride response [[Knowles1981,Farrell2008Dx]].
- FEV1 <30% predicted was linked to >50% 2-year mortality (Kerem 1992, historic) [[Kerem1992]].
- LCI picks up early disease when FEV1 is normal [[Aurora2011,Owens2011]].
- Annual OGTT from age 10 for CFRD; A1C not used for screening [[Moran2010CFRD]].
- CF Foundation target: ≥4 visits a year, 4 respiratory cultures and 2 PFTs [[Wooldridge2015]].
- 25% of IV-treated exacerbations do not recover to baseline FEV1 [[Sanders2010]].
- Bronchiectasis is present in 29.3% of screened infants at 3 months and 61.5% by age 3 [[Sly2013NEJM]].

### In plain words
- Every newborn in the US and UK has a few drops of blood tested for CF in the first days of life [[McGarry2025NBS,Schluter2020]].
- A "positive" newborn screen means the baby needs more testing. It does not mean the baby has CF. Many babies with a positive screen are healthy carriers [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- Being a carrier is common. It is nobody's fault and does not make a person sick [[McGarry2025NBS]].
- Finding CF early helps babies grow better, because nutrition care can start right away [[Farrell2001]].
- The sweat test is painless. A small electric current carries a medicine into the skin of the arm to make it sweat, and the sweat is collected and its salt (chloride) is measured [[Gibson1959,Farrell2008Dx]].
- Sometimes not enough sweat is collected, especially in very young or small babies, and the test has to be repeated [[LeGrys2010]].
- A newborn screen can miss CF. If a child has symptoms, CF should still be checked, even after a normal screen [[Rock2011]].
- Some babies get an "inconclusive" result (CRMS/CFSPID). Most stay healthy, but they are followed for a few years to be safe [[Barben2021CFSPID]].
- Clinic visits about every three months let the care team check breathing, growth and germs in the airways early [[Wooldridge2015]].
- Spirometry measures how much air you can blow out in one second (FEV1). Numbers are compared with healthy people of the same age, sex and height [[Quanjer2012,Bowerman2023]].
- CFTR modulator medicines lower sweat salt, a sign the CFTR channel is working better [[Nichols2022PROMISE]].
- Survival numbers from registries describe groups of people in the past. They cannot predict what will happen to any one person [[Kerem1992]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Lung benefit of screening: the Wisconsin RCT found no difference in pulmonary function by school age [[Farrell2003AJRCCM]]. Registry studies find modestly higher FEV1 after screening (+1.56 points UK; +5.3 points US/Australia) [[Schluter2020,Martin2012]]. Status: the growth benefit is established; the lung benefit is probable but confounded by changes in care.
- Floating versus fixed IRT cutoff, and whether very-high-IRT referral is still needed: the CF Foundation favours a floating cutoff and very-high-IRT referral only when the panel is limited [[McGarry2025NBS]]. Some studies found little effect of threshold changes on sensitivity [[McGarry2025NBS]]. Status: consensus opinion, low-quality evidence.
- Should screening aim to find CRMS/CFSPID? Some programmes favour fewer CRMS/CFSPID labels, given low conversion rates and family distress. Others want to find every child at risk [[Wyatt2025,Barben2021CFSPID]]. Status: unresolved, programme-dependent.
- Long-term outcomes of CRMS/CFSPID beyond early childhood are unknown, including later CFTR-related disorders [[Ren2017CRMS,Barben2021CFSPID]].
- Equity: limited variant panels miss more Black, Hispanic, Asian and multiracial infants [[McGarry2023,McGarry2024]]. Recommended fixes are all CFTR2 CF-causing variants plus a sequencing tier [[McGarry2025NBS]]. Status: implementation varies by state.
- Race-neutral spirometry: the ATS recommends GLI Global [[Bhakta2023]], but it changes how impairment is classified by group [[Moffett2023]]. Its effect on CF registry trends and trial eligibility is not yet known (Hypothesis).
- Sweat chloride in the modulator era: SKYLINE treats <30 mmol/L as a treatment target [[Keating2025SKYLINE]]. How best to interpret a diagnostic sweat test in someone already on a modulator has not been settled in the guidelines I read.
- LCI in routine care: the 2015 CF Foundation workshop found insufficient evidence [[Subbarao2015]]. It is increasingly used to measure modulator response in children with normal FEV1 [[Stahl2024,Henriksen2025]]. Status: research use established; clinical role evolving.
- Pushing NPD, ICM and organoid assays forward for unclear diagnoses versus waiting for standardisation [[Derichs2010,Dekkers2013,Farrell2017History]].
