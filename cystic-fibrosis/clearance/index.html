Part 4 · Treatment

Airway clearance & inhaled therapies

Thick mucus traps germs in the lungs, so much of daily CF care is about thinning it, cutting it and moving it out. Scrub one airway lining through five treatments and see what the trial behind each one found.

Study material, not a care planThe order in the animation is a teaching order. Trial results and label wording are quoted as published. Which treatments a person uses, and in what order, is planned with their CF team.

Step 1 · before the mist

Bronchodilator

In the Elkins 2006 hypertonic saline trial, every dose was preceded by a bronchodilator 116. The Cayston (aztreonam lysine) label states a bronchodilator before each dose 133, and the TOBI label lists bronchospasm among its warnings 139. Taken as a long-term treatment in its own right, the CFF 2013 chronic-medications guideline graded inhaled β2-agonists I: insufficient evidence 300.

In plain wordsIn the trials and labels here, the bronchodilator is the warm-up before some inhaled treatments, not a treatment for the mucus itself.
Exam hookCFF 2013: inhaled β2-agonists and inhaled anticholinergics as chronic therapy are grade I (insufficient evidence) 300.

How the CFF graded chronic lung medicines (2013)

The CF Foundation's chronic-medications guideline used the US Preventive Services Task Force scheme: A strongly recommend, B recommend, C consider for selected patients, D recommend against, I insufficient evidence. Its lung-disease bands by FEV1 % predicted were normal >90%, mild 70–89%, moderate 40–69% and severe <40% 300. The guideline predates elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor (ETI).

Airway clearance techniques compared: what the Cochrane reviews concluded

Airway clearance means daily breathing techniques, devices or exercise that help move mucus up and out 152. The CFF 2009 guideline rated the evidence for airway clearance as fair, with moderate benefit 152. Head-to-head, the reviews keep landing in the same place: little evidence that any one technique beats another 501.

Exercise

An adjunct, and good for health

The CFF 2009 guideline recommends aerobic exercise as an adjunct to airway clearance and for overall health 152. Across 15 training studies (487 people), the evidence of benefit to aerobic capacity, lung function and quality of life was very low to low quality, and the authors found "no reason to actively discourage" exercise 354.

Early evidence
Choosing a technique

The one you will keep doing

The Cochrane overview advises people with CF to choose by comfort, convenience, flexibility, practicality and cost 501. The CFF guideline says the prescription should be individualised 152.

Established
Open question

Can exercise replace airway clearance?

Guidelines endorse exercise as an adjunct 152, but the evidence on substitution is very low certainty, and the review names it a top research priority for people with CF 204.

Hypothesis

SIMPLIFY: stopping hypertonic saline or dornase alfa on ETI

Once ETI improves CFTR function, are the daily mucus inhalers still needed? SIMPLIFY ran two parallel open-label, randomised non-inferiority trials at 80 US clinics. People already on ETI who used hypertonic saline (≥3%) or dornase alfa were randomised to stop or continue it for 6 weeks. Entry needed ages 12–17 with ppFEV1 ≥70%, or 18 and over with ppFEV1 ≥60%; mean baseline ppFEV1 was 96.9%, and the non-inferiority margin was −3 points 277. Pick a trial.

Hypertonic saline trial: the whole confidence interval sits to the right of the −3 point margin, so stopping was non-inferior to continuing over 6 weeks in this population 277.

Lung clearance index also did not change meaningfully (hypertonic saline trial difference 0.06, 95% CI −0.31 to 0.44), and there were no treatment-related serious adverse events 277.

Limits, as the trial reported them: 6 weeks is short, the trial was not powered for exacerbations, and respiratory adverse events were slightly more common in those who stopped, mainly in the small adult subgroup with ppFEV1 60 to <70% Early evidence for lower lung function 277. Any change is planned with the CF team.

Other mucus and anti-inflammatory treatments

Mucoactive

Inhaled dry-powder mannitol

Mannitol 400 mg twice daily against a 50 mg control for 26 weeks raised FEV1 by a mean 105 mL; the relative gain over control was 3.75% (P = 0.029). Exacerbations were fewer but not significantly (HR 0.74) 5. A second trial (324 people) found +92.9 mL over control, with or without rhDNase, and 35.4% fewer people with an exacerbation (P = 0.045). Cough, haemoptysis and throat pain were common side effects 30.

Anti-inflammatory

Azithromycin

Saiman 2003 (185 people with Pseudomonas for ≥1 year) gave 250 mg (<40 kg) or 500 mg (≥40 kg) three days a week for 168 days. FEV1 rose 0.094 L more than placebo and exacerbation risk fell (HR 0.65); nausea, diarrhoea and wheeze were more frequent 391. The Cochrane review found a slight FEV1 gain up to 6 months (high certainty) and a longer time to exacerbation (HR 0.61) 437. NTM must be considered before and during long-term azithromycin 149.

Anti-inflammatory

High-dose ibuprofen

In a 4-year trial (85 people aged 5–39, FEV1 ≥60%), ibuprofen titrated to a peak plasma level of 50–100 μg/mL slowed the yearly FEV1 decline: −2.17% against −3.60% per year (P = 0.02) 242.

Recommended against

Routine corticosteroids

The CFF recommends against routine inhaled corticosteroids and chronic oral corticosteroids (grade D) in people aged 6 and over without asthma or ABPA 300.
