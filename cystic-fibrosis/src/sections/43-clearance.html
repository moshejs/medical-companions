How the CFF graded chronic lung medicines (2013)

The CF Foundation's chronic-medications guideline used the US Preventive Services Task Force scheme: A strongly recommend, B recommend, C consider for selected patients, D recommend against, I insufficient evidence. Its lung-disease bands by FEV1 % predicted were normal >90%, mild 70–89%, moderate 40–69% and severe <40% [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]]. The guideline predates elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor (ETI).

Airway clearance techniques compared: what the Cochrane reviews concluded

Airway clearance means daily breathing techniques, devices or exercise that help move mucus up and out [[Flume2009ACT]]. The CFF 2009 guideline rated the evidence for airway clearance as fair, with moderate benefit [[Flume2009ACT]]. Head-to-head, the reviews keep landing in the same place: little evidence that any one technique beats another [[Wilson2019ACT]].

Exercise

An adjunct, and good for health

The CFF 2009 guideline recommends aerobic exercise as an adjunct to airway clearance and for overall health [[Flume2009ACT]]. Across 15 training studies (487 people), the evidence of benefit to aerobic capacity, lung function and quality of life was very low to low quality, and the authors found "no reason to actively discourage" exercise [[Radtke2017Exercise]].

Early evidence
Choosing a technique

The one you will keep doing

The Cochrane overview advises people with CF to choose by comfort, convenience, flexibility, practicality and cost [[Wilson2019ACT]]. The CFF guideline says the prescription should be individualised [[Flume2009ACT]].

Established
Open question

Can exercise replace airway clearance?

Guidelines endorse exercise as an adjunct [[Flume2009ACT]], but the evidence on substitution is very low certainty, and the review names it a top research priority for people with CF [[Heinz2022Exercise]].

Hypothesis

SIMPLIFY: stopping hypertonic saline or dornase alfa on ETI

Once ETI improves CFTR function, are the daily mucus inhalers still needed? SIMPLIFY ran two parallel open-label, randomised non-inferiority trials at 80 US clinics. People already on ETI who used hypertonic saline (≥3%) or dornase alfa were randomised to stop or continue it for 6 weeks. Entry needed ages 12–17 with ppFEV1 ≥70%, or 18 and over with ppFEV1 ≥60%; mean baseline ppFEV1 was 96.9%, and the non-inferiority margin was −3 points [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]]. Pick a trial.

Lung clearance index also did not change meaningfully (hypertonic saline trial difference 0.06, 95% CI −0.31 to 0.44), and there were no treatment-related serious adverse events [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]].

Limits, as the trial reported them: 6 weeks is short, the trial was not powered for exacerbations, and respiratory adverse events were slightly more common in those who stopped, mainly in the small adult subgroup with ppFEV1 60 to <70% Early evidence for lower lung function [[MayerHamblett2023SIMPLIFY]]. Any change is planned with the CF team.

Other mucus and anti-inflammatory treatments

Mucoactive

Inhaled dry-powder mannitol

Mannitol 400 mg twice daily against a 50 mg control for 26 weeks raised FEV1 by a mean 105 mL; the relative gain over control was 3.75% (P = 0.029). Exacerbations were fewer but not significantly (HR 0.74) [[Aitken2012Mannitol]]. A second trial (324 people) found +92.9 mL over control, with or without rhDNase, and 35.4% fewer people with an exacerbation (P = 0.045). Cough, haemoptysis and throat pain were common side effects [[Bilton2011Mannitol]].

Anti-inflammatory

Azithromycin

Saiman 2003 (185 people with Pseudomonas for ≥1 year) gave 250 mg (<40 kg) or 500 mg (≥40 kg) three days a week for 168 days. FEV1 rose 0.094 L more than placebo and exacerbation risk fell (HR 0.65); nausea, diarrhoea and wheeze were more frequent [[Saiman2003Azithro]]. The Cochrane review found a slight FEV1 gain up to 6 months (high certainty) and a longer time to exacerbation (HR 0.61) [[Southern2024Macrolide]]. NTM must be considered before and during long-term azithromycin [[Floto2016NTM]].

Anti-inflammatory

High-dose ibuprofen

In a 4-year trial (85 people aged 5–39, FEV1 ≥60%), ibuprofen titrated to a peak plasma level of 50–100 μg/mL slowed the yearly FEV1 decline: −2.17% against −3.60% per year (P = 0.02) [[Konstan1995Ibuprofen]].

Recommended against

Routine corticosteroids

The CFF recommends against routine inhaled corticosteroids and chronic oral corticosteroids (grade D) in people aged 6 and over without asthma or ABPA [[Mogayzel2013Pulm]].
