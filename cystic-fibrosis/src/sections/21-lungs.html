Part 2 · How CF affects the body

Lungs: mucus, infection & bronchiectasis

Lung disease causes most of the illness and deaths in CF. It starts with mucus that does not clear, which invites infection and a fierce neutrophil response, and over years that response widens and scars the airways (bronchiectasis). Scrub through a lifetime as the pre-modulator studies recorded it, then step round the cycle that drives it.

Lung disease in CF works through bronchiectasis, small-airway obstruction and progressive loss of breathing capacity [[Elborn2016,Ratjen2015]]. Failure of CFTR causes mucus retention and chronic infection, which drive airway inflammation that harms the lungs [[Elborn2016]]. The vicious cycle: obstruction, infection, inflammation

Each turn of the cycle feeds the next: mucus that does not clear shelters bacteria, bacteria draw in neutrophils, and neutrophil products damage the airway wall, which then clears mucus even less well. Step round it.

The Australian Respiratory Early Surveillance Team for CF (AREST CF) followed babies diagnosed by newborn screening with chest CT and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) from 3 months of age. The airway changes start long before most children have symptoms.

The Leeds criteria call P. aeruginosa infection chronic when more than 50% of the previous 12 months' cultures are positive, and intermittent when 50% or fewer are [[Lee2003]]. The range of organisms is widening, which makes trends hard to read [[LiPuma2010,Gibson2003]].

Pulmonary exacerbations

An exacerbation is a spell when breathing symptoms get worse, often treated with antibiotics. Exacerbations are among the most important events in the course of CF [[Goss2007]]. The "Fuchs criteria", from the 1994 dornase alfa trial, define one as antibiotic treatment plus at least 4 of 12 respiratory signs and symptoms; a later analysis found that more than a third of respiratory events met 4 or more criteria without investigators calling them exacerbations [[VanDevanter2021]]. A prospective score from symptoms and chest findings predicted exacerbations with 86% sensitivity and 86% specificity [[Rosenfeld2001]].

How often

27%

of people on placebo had at least one exacerbation needing parenteral antibiotics over 24 weeks in the dornase alfa trial (968 people), against 22% and 19% on once- and twice-daily dornase [[Fuchs1994Dornase]].

Recovery

1 in 4

of 8,479 IV-treated exacerbations (US registry, 2003–2006) did not get back to 90% of the pre-exacerbation best FEV1 within 3 months. Female sex, pancreatic insufficiency, undernutrition, persistent P. aeruginosa, B. cepacia complex or MRSA, ABPA and a bigger initial FEV1 drop made this more likely [[Sanders2010]].

Long-term impact

HR 4.05

for death or lung transplant over 3 years in 446 Ontario adults with more than 2 exacerbations a year versus fewer than 1 (95% CI 1.15–14.28); HR 1.55 for a 5% FEV1 decline [[deBoer2011]].

How many people: complications at a glance

Bleeding, air leaks, fungus and sinuses

Massive haemoptysis

In US registry data for 1990–1999 (28,858 people), massive haemoptysis (coughing up a large amount of blood) had an average annual incidence of 0.87% and occurred in 4.1% overall. Mean age was 24.2 years and nearly 60% had FEV1 below 40% predicted. It was linked to S. aureus (OR 1.3) and diabetes (OR 1.1), and followed by higher 2-year mortality [[Flume2005Hemoptysis]].

Pneumothorax

Spontaneous pneumothorax (air leaking around a lung) had an annual incidence of 0.64% and occurred in 3.4% overall; mean age 21.9 years, and nearly 75% had FEV1 below 40%. Risk was higher with P. aeruginosa (OR 2.3), B. cepacia (OR 1.8), FEV1 below 30% (OR 1.5), ABPA (OR 1.5) and prior massive haemoptysis (OR 1.4) [[Flume2005Pneumothorax]].

How these are managed

CF Foundation recommendations on haemoptysis and pneumothorax were reached by Delphi expert consensus, because the data were too thin for evidence-based ones [[Flume2010]]. Both were followed by higher 2-year mortality in the registry analyses [[Flume2005Hemoptysis,Flume2005Pneumothorax]].

ABPA

Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis is an allergic reaction to the Aspergillus mould. In the European registry (12,447 people) prevalence was 7.8%, low under 6 years and about 10% after; it rose with worse lung function and was linked to pneumothorax and massive haemoptysis [[Mastella2000]]. The US reported only 2%, probably under-diagnosis [[Geller1999]]. Its features overlap with CF lung disease itself, so a consensus conference set CF-specific criteria [[Stevens2003]].

Sinuses and nasal polyps

Chronic rhinosinusitis is highly prevalent in CF; CF polyps are more neutrophilic, with fewer eosinophils, than other polyps [[Hamilos2016]]. On endoscopy, 37% of 211 adults had nasal polyps [[Hadfield2000]]. One year of ETI cut the median Lund-Mackay sinus CT score from 5.8 to 3.3 [[Sheikh2023]].

Neutrophil elastase

Across 269 people aged 9–54, sputum elastase correlated with lower FEV1 (r = −0.35), and each 1-log rise went with a further 2.9% FEV1 decline over time [[MayerHamblett2007]]. In 35 children followed for 3 years, it was the most informative single biomarker of faster decline [[Sagel2012]].

Population statistics, not predictions. In the registry cohort on ETI, annual death (0.47%) and lung transplant (0.16%) rates were well below the 2019 registry rates (1.65% and 1.08%) [[Bower2023]]. Pre-modulator decline rates of 1–2.4 FEV1 points a year should not be applied to people on these medicines, and the long-term effect on established bronchiectasis is unknown [[Konstan2007,Bower2023,Nichols2023]]. Even on modulators, germs already in the lungs often stay, so regular check-ups still matter [[Nichols2023]].
