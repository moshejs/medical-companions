The first hour: bundle & antibiotics

Sepsis is treated as an emergency because, on average, earlier antibiotics go with better survival, most clearly in septic shock. Watch the Hour-1 bundle unfold on a clock, see what the timing studies found, and follow the trials that retired early goal-directed therapy.

Sepsis is recognised

SSC 2021: "Sepsis and septic shock are medical emergencies, and we recommend that treatment and resuscitation begin immediately" (best practice statement). The SSC 2018 bundle update introduced the "Hour-1 bundle", whose five elements are meant to be started within the first hour. The 2018 paper is paywalled, so its wording here comes from a verbatim listing in an open-access compliance study. 133,261,507

In plain wordsSepsis is an emergency. Once a hospital team suspects it, they start several things at once, as quickly as they can.
Exam hookHour-1 bundle: lactate (remeasure if >2), blood cultures before antibiotics, broad-spectrum antibiotics, 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate ≥4, vasopressors for MAP ≥65. 261,507

What SSC 2021 says about timing, as published

The 2018 Hour-1 bundle paper is paywalled, so the five elements above are quoted from a verbatim listing in an open-access study. The 2021 guideline then separated people with possible shock from those with possible sepsis without shock. These are guideline statements quoted as published, not instructions. 133,261,507

  • "Sepsis and septic shock are medical emergencies, and we recommend that treatment and resuscitation begin immediately" (best practice statement). 133
  • "For adults with possible septic shock or a high likelihood for sepsis, we recommend administering antimicrobials immediately, ideally within 1 h of recognition" (strong; low-quality evidence for septic shock, very low for sepsis without shock). 133
  • "For adults with possible sepsis without shock, we suggest a time-limited course of rapid investigation and if concern for infection persists, the administration of antimicrobials within 3 h from the time when sepsis was first recognised" (weak, very low quality). 133
  • For adults with a low likelihood of infection and no shock, "we suggest deferring antimicrobials while continuing to closely monitor the patient" (weak, very low quality). 133
  • For sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock, "we suggest that at least 30 mL/kg of intravenous (IV) crystalloid fluid should be given within the first 3 h of resuscitation" (weak, low quality); the guideline notes this fixed volume rests on observational evidence. 133
  • An initial MAP target of 65 mm Hg over higher targets in septic shock on vasopressors (strong, moderate quality); starting vasopressors peripherally rather than waiting for central access, for a short time and in a vein at or above the antecubital fossa (weak, very low quality). 133
  • ICU admission within 6 h for adults with sepsis or septic shock who need ICU care (weak, low quality), and against qSOFA "compared to SIRS, NEWS, or MEWS as a single screening tool" (strong, moderate quality). 133

Average associations, not predictions

Beyond 3 hours

Kumar 2006 reported an adjusted odds ratio for death of 1.119 for each hour of delay in effective antimicrobials after hypotension began in septic shock; in New York, each hour to antibiotics carried an OR of 1.04; at Kaiser Permanente, 1.09 per hour from registration; in Utah, 10% higher odds of death at 1 year per hour from arrival. 240,266,349,435

The effect was strongest in shock: Liu 2017's absolute increase per hour was 0.3% in sepsis, 0.4% in severe sepsis and 1.8% in shock, and Im 2022 found each 1-h delay within 3 h carried a 35% higher mortality risk in septic shock, with no such trend without shock. A meta-analysis found no significant difference at the 1-h or 3-h thresholds. 210,266,459

In plain wordsIn studies, each hour of delay before antibiotics was linked to worse outcomes on average, especially in septic shock. These are population figures, not predictions for one person. 210,266

Early goal-directed therapy: one trial, then three

Mortality as published; the outcome window differs by trial (in-hospital, 60-day or 90-day), so compare bars within a trial, not across trials. 29,309,367,368,395

2001 · one centre

Rivers: a big effect

Rivers 2001 randomised 263 ED patients with severe sepsis or septic shock at one centre to 6 hours of early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) or standard therapy. In-hospital mortality was 30.5% with EGDT and 46.5% with standard therapy (P = 0.009). 395

In plain wordsAn early single-hospital trial suggested that a strict treatment protocol in the first hours saved many lives.

SEP-1 and mandated sepsis care

US hospitals have reported the Medicare SEP-1 measure since October 2015. A case passes only if every qualifying element is done: lactate, blood cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics, 30 mL/kg crystalloid, vasopressors and reassessment. 389,481

Observational

Compliant care, lower mortality

In Medicare data from 3,241 hospitals, SEP-1-compliant care was associated with lower 30-day mortality: 21.81% vs 27.48% in the standard match and 22.22% vs 26.28% in the stringent match. 481

Chart review

No difference after adjustment

In 851 cases at 7 US hospitals, 67% failed SEP-1. Crude mortality was higher in failures (18.4% vs 11.0%) but not after adjusting for illness severity (OR 1.36, 0.85–2.18). The commonest reason for failing was a missed 3- or 6-hour lactate (40%); antibiotic delays over 3 h were linked to death (OR 1.94) but caused only 15.1% of failures. 389

Position paper

Limit it to septic shock?

IDSA's 2021 position paper, with five endorsing societies, recommends limiting SEP-1 to septic shock, warning that it may drive antibiotic overuse and that its "time zero" definition is not evidence-based. 393

State mandate

New York's regulations

After New York's 2013 sepsis regulations, 3-hour bundle compliance rose from 53.4% to 64.7% and risk-adjusted mortality fell from 28.8% to 24.4% (91,357 hospitalisations). Against 4 control states, adjusted mortality was 3.2% (1.0–5.4%) lower than expected by the 10th quarter. 219,262

Open questions

Unresolved

One hour for everyone?

Cohorts show the strongest timing effect in shock and weak or no effect without it; a meta-analysis found no significant benefit at the 1-h or 3-h thresholds; IDSA argues mandated timing should be limited to septic shock. SSC 2021 now separates the two groups. 133,210,266,393,459

Established

Is "7.6% per hour" causal?

The association is consistent in large cohorts, but the causal size is uncertain. Kumar's figure comes from a retrospective cohort open to confounding by indication; Puskarich found no hourly effect from triage in a protocolised cohort, and giving antibiotics in the ambulance did not change mortality in a randomised trial. 10,240,377

Conflicting

Does SEP-1 save lives?

The evidence is observational only, and it conflicts: Medicare data favour compliance, a multicentre chart review found no difference after severity adjustment, and the New York data suggest a population-level benefit. 219,262,389,481

Sepsis Companion is an educational synthesis of the published literature on sepsis (the body's harmful response to an infection), septic shock and the other types of shock, made for health-science, medical and nursing courses and for people who survive sepsis, their families and caregivers.

It is not medical advice. It cannot diagnose, and nothing here should start, stop or change a medicine or a care plan without your own care team. The qSOFA, SOFA, NEWS2, Phoenix and shock-index calculators are for learning, never for self-triage or for deciding whether to seek care. Antibiotic and vasopressor doses, fluid volumes, MAP and lactate targets and guideline statements are quoted as published in guidelines and labels (Surviving Sepsis Campaign, NICE, Sepsis-3), never as instructions, and mortality figures are population statistics. Sepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?
