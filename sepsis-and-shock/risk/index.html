Part 2 · Who is at risk & recognising it

Who is at risk

Any infection can lead to sepsis 447. Some people carry a higher chance: babies and young children, older adults, people with long-term illness, cancer or weakened immunity, people who are pregnant or have just given birth, and people in recent contact with healthcare. See how the numbers shift with age, and what the studies mean by "higher risk".

Call nowSepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?

No one is to blameA risk factor is a pattern seen across large groups of people, not a judgement about anyone. Most risk factors here, such as age, a long-term illness, cancer treatment or giving birth, are not choices, and a person with none of them can still get sepsis. Knowing who is at higher risk simply helps families and care teams think of sepsis sooner.

Age: the youngest and the oldest

Worldwide, sepsis incidence peaks in early childhood and again among older adults 399. Scrub through four views of the same pattern, each from a named study population.

Icons are rounded to whole people. Each view comes from a different study population and year, named in the note; mortality and incidence figures are population statistics, not predictions for any one person.

View 1 · the US population

12 in 100 people are 65 or older

In the US analysis by Martin and colleagues, adults aged 65 or older made up 12% of the population 279. Keep that number in mind for the next view.

In plain wordsOut of every 100 people in the US population studied, about 12 were aged 65 or over 279.
Exam hookOlder adults: 12% of the population but 64.9% of sepsis cases 279.

Age also shapes outcome. In US data from 1995, mortality from severe sepsis rose from 10% in children to 38.4% in people over 85 19, and age independently predicted death (OR 2.26) 279. Babies and children have their own signs and scores: see Sepsis in babies and children.

Long-term conditions and frailty

In the community-based REGARDS cohort (30,239 US adults followed over time), several conditions present at the start predicted later sepsis, and risk rose with the number of chronic conditions 506. Choose a bar to see what its number means.

Chronic lung disease: adjusted hazard ratio 2.43, the strongest single condition in REGARDS. People who had it at the start went on to develop sepsis at about 2.4 times the rate of comparable people without it, after adjusting for other factors 506. A hazard ratio compares rates between groups; it does not tell any one person their own chance.

Adjusted hazard ratios from REGARDS: chronic conditions 506, frailty 271, steroids (risk of infection) 92.

Cancer

A history of cancer

People with a history of cancer had sepsis incidence of 1,465 per 100,000 a year, a relative risk of 9.77 compared with people without cancer, and cancer independently predicted death from sepsis (adjusted OR 1.98) 105.

Chemotherapy

Febrile neutropenia

In adults hospitalised with it, in-hospital mortality was 2.6% with no major comorbidity, 10.3% with one and at least 21.4% with more than one 239. ASCO/IDSA endorse giving the first antibiotic dose within 1 hour of triage (quoted as published) 466.

Liver

Cirrhosis

In cirrhosis (advanced scarring of the liver), infection raised mortality about four-fold (pooled OR 3.75); about 30% died within a month of infection and another 30% by a year 30. Among adults with sepsis, cirrhosis went with higher 30-day mortality (OR 3.59) 134.

Frailty

More than one thing at once

Frailty (at least two of weakness, exhaustion and low physical activity) was present in 19.9% of REGARDS participants and went with higher sepsis risk (adjusted HR 1.44) and higher 30-day case fatality (adjusted OR 1.62) 271. In intensive care, cancer, advanced heart failure and cirrhosis were among the independent risk factors for in-hospital death 406.

Weakened immunity

Medicines

Steroids

Baseline oral or injected steroid use (2.24% of REGARDS) went with a higher risk of infection (adjusted HR 2.10) and, once infected, higher odds of sepsis (adjusted OR 2.11) 92. Among adults with sepsis, immunosuppression went with higher 30-day mortality (OR 2.52) 134.

No working spleen

After splenectomy

In 19,680 people after removal of the spleen (median follow-up 6.9 years), infection occurred in 3.2% and death in 1.4%. The highest rates were in thalassaemia major (8.2% infection, 5.1% death) and sickle-cell anaemia (7.3%, 4.8%) 58.

Vague signs

It may not look like the list

In a Thai emergency cohort, patients with none of the "SEPSIS" warning-sign symptoms more often had underlying immunosuppression (24.6% vs 8.3%), which suggests immunosuppressed people may present vaguely 511. Warning-sign lists are reminders, not tests: see Recognising sepsis.

Established

Preventing infection in neutropenia

In afebrile neutropenic patients after chemotherapy, antibiotic prophylaxis reduced all-cause mortality (RR 0.66) across 109 trials, mostly in blood cancers 160. More in Preventing sepsis.

Pregnancy and after birth

Sepsis caused 10.7% of maternal deaths worldwide in 2003–2009, about 261,000 deaths, the third direct cause after haemorrhage and hypertensive disorders 415. In the WHO GLOSS study (713 facilities, 52 countries), 70.4 hospitalised women per 1,000 live births had a maternal infection 170. In the UK, severe maternal sepsis affected 4.7 per 10,000 maternities; 19.5% developed septic shock, and the authors estimate about 50 women with life-threatening sepsis for each maternal sepsis death 6.

Healthcare contact, surgery and hospital-onset sepsis

Most starts at home

But often after care

In a CDC-led review of 1,078 adults, 90.3% had community-onset sepsis, yet 60.7% had recent healthcare exposure, including 24.1% with an outpatient visit in the prior 7 days. 41.5% had received treatment such as antimicrobials, chemotherapy, wound care, dialysis or surgery in the prior 30 days 134.

Frequent care

Chronic conditions and recent care

In a CDC chart review in 4 New York hospitals (246 adults, 79 children), 72% had a healthcare factor in the prior 30 days or a chronic condition needing frequent care, and pneumonia was the commonest infection leading to sepsis 331.

In hospital

Hospital-onset sepsis

Sepsis that began in hospital made up 12.1% of cases in 136 US hospitals (about 1 in 200 hospitalisations). These patients had more comorbidities and higher SOFA scores, and in-hospital mortality was 33% vs 17% for community-onset sepsis (adjusted OR 2.1) 390.

Unequal risk

US Black patients had a 67% higher age- and sex-standardised severe-sepsis rate than white patients (9.4 vs 5.6 per 1,000) and 80% higher standardised mortality 283. The gap came from both a higher infection rate and a higher risk of organ dysfunction once infected, which shrank after adjustment for comorbidity, poverty and hospital 283. In 6 US states, Black residents still had higher incidence after adjustment for regional poverty (adjusted rate ratio 1.44) 45. From 1979 to 2000, sepsis was more common in men (RR 1.28) and in non-white people (RR 1.90) 278.

Higher neighbourhood socioeconomic status went with fewer serious-infection hospitalisations (11.7 vs 15.6 per 1,000 person-years) but not with sepsis among people already in hospital with infection, so the gap seems to work mainly through infection risk 122. The community-based REGARDS cohort found the opposite direction for race, a reminder that findings depend on study design and case definition 283,506. Tobacco use, alcohol use, lower education and lower income were also associated with sepsis in REGARDS 506.

Hypothesis

Why the gaps exist

Higher infection rates, higher organ-dysfunction risk, the hospital where care happens and neighbourhood infection risk have all been shown to contribute 45,122,283. That structural causes drive much of this, rather than anything about individuals, is likely but still a hypothesis.

Worldwide

Where you live

In 2017, 85.0% of sepsis cases (41.5 million) occurred in countries with a low, low-middle or middle Socio-demographic Index, and the burden was highest in sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania and South, East and Southeast Asia 399.

These figures describe groups of people and cannot predict what will happen to any one person. Outcomes have improved: in Australian and New Zealand intensive care units, severe-sepsis mortality fell from 35.0% in 2000 to 18.4% in 2012, and was below 5% in patients without comorbidities or older age 222. How to spot sepsis continues in Recognising sepsis, and how to lower risk in Preventing sepsis.

Sepsis Companion is an educational synthesis of the published literature on sepsis (the body's harmful response to an infection), septic shock and the other types of shock, made for health-science, medical and nursing courses and for people who survive sepsis, their families and caregivers.

It is not medical advice. It cannot diagnose, and nothing here should start, stop or change a medicine or a care plan without your own care team. The qSOFA, SOFA, NEWS2, Phoenix and shock-index calculators are for learning, never for self-triage or for deciding whether to seek care. Antibiotic and vasopressor doses, fluid volumes, MAP and lactate targets and guideline statements are quoted as published in guidelines and labels (Surviving Sepsis Campaign, NICE, Sepsis-3), never as instructions, and mortality figures are population statistics. Sepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?
