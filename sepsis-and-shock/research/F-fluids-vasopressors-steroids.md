# F · Fluids, vasopressors, steroids and MAP targets

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: fluids, pressors.

This pass covers resuscitation in sepsis and septic shock: how much fluid and which fluid, how fluid responsiveness is judged, fluid overload, the vasopressors (noradrenaline/norepinephrine, vasopressin, adrenaline/epinephrine, angiotensin II), inotropes, mean arterial pressure (MAP) targets and corticosteroids. Trial numbers come from PubMed/Europe PMC abstracts. Guideline statements and label text are quoted as published. They describe what guidelines and labels say. They are not instructions for treating anyone.

## Findings

### fluids · Definitions that frame resuscitation
- Sepsis-3 says patients with septic shock can be clinically identified by "a vasopressor requirement to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 65 mm Hg or greater and serum lactate level greater than 2 mmol/L (>18 mg/dL) in the absence of hypovolemia". This combination is associated with hospital mortality rates greater than 40% (a population statistic) [[Singer2016Sepsis3,ShankarHari2016Shock]].
- The Sepsis-3 shock definition requires fluids first ("in the absence of hypovolemia"). That is why fluid resuscitation and vasopressors are taught together [[Singer2016Sepsis3]].

### fluids · How much fluid first: guideline statements as published
- SSC 2021, recommendation 5: "For patients with sepsis induced hypoperfusion or septic shock we suggest that at least 30 mL/kg of intravenous (IV) crystalloid fluid should be given within the first 3 h of resuscitation". Weak recommendation, low-quality evidence [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021 explains that the fixed 30 mL/kg figure (ideal body weight in the 2016 version) "was based on observational evidence". No prospective trials compare different initial volumes [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2026 keeps the statement for adults with sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock: "we suggest administering at least 30 mL/kg of IV crystalloid in the first 3 hr" (conditional, low certainty) [[Prescott2026SSC]].
- SSC 2026 strongly recommends crystalloids as first-line resuscitation fluid (strong, moderate certainty). SSC 2021 said the same: strong recommendation, moderate quality [[Prescott2026SSC,Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021 opens resuscitation with a best-practice statement: sepsis and septic shock are medical emergencies, and treatment and resuscitation should "begin immediately" [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021, recommendation 45: "There is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation on the use of restrictive versus liberal fluid strategies in the first 24 h of resuscitation". The remark adds that fluid "should be given only if patients present with signs of hypoperfusion" [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2026, for adults who have had 30 mL/kg and still have hypoperfusion: "we suggest using either a liberal or a restrictive fluid resuscitation strategy" (conditional, low certainty) [[Prescott2026SSC]].
- SSC 2021 notes the fluid volumes in the major resuscitation trials. Rivers's protocol group received over 13 L. The usual-care groups of ProCESS, ARISE and ProMISe received about 7–8 L over the first 72 h [[Evans2021SSC]].

### fluids · Restrictive versus liberal: the trials
- CLASSIC (2022) enrolled 1554 ICU patients with septic shock who had already received at least 1 L. The restrictive group received a median of 1798 mL of IV fluid in ICU, versus 3811 mL with standard care. 90-day mortality was 42.3% (323/764) versus 42.1% (329/781): adjusted difference 0.1 percentage points (95% CI −4.7 to 4.9; P=0.96) [[Meyhoff2022CLASSIC]].
- In CLASSIC, serious adverse events in ICU occurred in 29.4% (restrictive) versus 30.8% (standard) [[Meyhoff2022CLASSIC]].
- At 1 year in CLASSIC, mortality was 51.3% (385/746, restrictive) versus 49.9% (383/767, standard), a difference of 1.5 percentage points (99% CI −4.8 to 7.8). Quality of life and cognition were similar [[Kjaer2023CLASSIC1y]].
- CLOVERS (2023) enrolled 1563 US patients with sepsis-induced hypotension after 1–3 L of fluid. The restrictive arm prioritised vasopressors and received less fluid over 24 h (difference of medians −2134 mL; 95% CI −2318 to −1949). Death before discharge home by day 90 was 14.0% versus 14.9% (difference −0.9 points; 95% CI −4.4 to 2.6; P=0.61) [[Shapiro2023CLOVERS]].
- ARISE FLUIDS (NEJM 2026) randomised 1000 adults with septic shock in the emergency department after an initial 1–2 L. One arm got restricted fluid plus early vasopressors, the other more fluid and later vasopressors. The vasopressor group received a median of 1108 mL less fluid in 24 h (95% CI −1395 to −850). Median days alive and out of hospital at day 90 were 76 in both groups (difference 0.0; 95% CI −2.7 to 2.7) [[Peake2026ARISEFLUIDS]].
- In ARISE FLUIDS, pulmonary oedema occurred in 0.6% of the vasopressor group versus 5.0% of the fluids group (P<0.001) [[Peake2026ARISEFLUIDS]].
- FACTT (2006) enrolled 1000 patients with acute lung injury.
  - Conservative versus liberal fluid strategy gave 60-day mortality of 25.5% versus 28.4% (P=0.30).
  - Mean 7-day cumulative fluid balance was −136 mL versus +6992 mL.
  - Ventilator-free days were 14.6 versus 12.1 (P<0.001) [[Wiedemann2006FACTT]].
- In FACTT, the conservative strategy did not increase shock or the use of dialysis: 10% versus 14% (P=0.06) [[Wiedemann2006FACTT]].
- Early norepinephrine (CENSER, 310 adults, Thailand, phase II) shortened median time to norepinephrine from 192 to 93 min. Shock control by 6 h was 76.1% versus 48.4% (P<0.001). 28-day mortality was 15.5% versus 21.9% (P=0.15, not significant). Cardiogenic pulmonary oedema was 14.4% versus 27.7% [[Permpikul2019CENSER]].
- Synthesis (Established): three large adult trials of fluid restriction after initial resuscitation (CLASSIC, CLOVERS, ARISE FLUIDS) showed no mortality difference either way. Less fluid with earlier vasopressors appears safe, and in ARISE FLUIDS it came with less pulmonary oedema [[Meyhoff2022CLASSIC,Shapiro2023CLOVERS,Peake2026ARISEFLUIDS]].

### fluids · When fluid boluses caused harm: low-resource settings
- FEAST (2011) randomised 3141 African children with severe febrile illness and impaired perfusion (stratum A) to a 20–40 mL/kg bolus of 5% albumin, a saline bolus, or no bolus. 48-hour mortality was 10.6% (albumin), 10.5% (saline) and 7.3% (no bolus). Relative risk for any bolus versus control was 1.45 (95% CI 1.13 to 1.86; P=0.003) [[Maitland2011FEAST]].
- At 4 weeks in FEAST, mortality was 12.2%, 12.0% and 8.7% (P=0.004 for bolus vs control). The effect was consistent across subgroups for shock severity, malaria, coma, sepsis, acidosis and severe anaemia [[Maitland2011FEAST]].
- In a Zambian trial of 212 adults with sepsis and hypotension (89.5% HIV-positive), an early resuscitation protocol increased in-hospital mortality: 48.1% versus 33.0% with usual care (RR 1.46; 95% CI 1.04 to 2.05). The protocol group received a median of 3.5 L versus 2.0 L in the first 6 h [[Andrews2017Zambia]].
- Teaching point (Established): bolus volumes that seem safe in well-resourced ICUs increased deaths in children in African hospitals without intensive care. Fluid harm depends on context [[Maitland2011FEAST,Andrews2017Zambia]].

### fluids · Fluid overload and cumulative balance (observational)
- In VASST patients with septic shock, average fluid balance was +4.2 L at enrolment (about 12 h after presentation) and +11 L cumulatively by day 4. A more positive balance at 12 h and at day 4 was associated with higher 28-day mortality [[Boyd2011VASST]].
- In VASST, patients with central venous pressure (CVP) above 12 mm Hg at 12 h had the highest mortality, and those below 8 mm Hg the lowest [[Boyd2011VASST]].
- In 173 ICU patients with sepsis (34% died), daily fluid balance was more than twice as large in non-survivors: 29 versus 13 mL/kg. Adjusted hazard ratio was 1.014 per mL/kg increase [[Acheampong2015]].
- A meta-analysis of 31 observational studies (31,076 ICU patients) found:
  - Fluid overload after 3 days of ICU stay: adjusted relative risk of death 8.83 (95% CI 4.03 to 19.33).
  - Cumulative fluid balance in patients with sepsis: adjusted relative risk of death 1.66 (95% CI 1.39 to 1.98).
  - Mortality rose by a factor of 1.19 per litre of positive balance [[Messmer2020CCM]].
- In 23,513 US patients with severe sepsis or septic shock (2013 Premier database), mean day-1 ICU fluid was 4.4 L.
  - Between 1 and 4.99 L, mortality fell slightly with each litre (−0.7% per litre).
  - Above 5 L, mortality rose by 2.3% for each additional litre (association, not causation) [[Marik2017ICM]].
- A review frames fluid as a drug with "four D's" (drug, dosing, duration, de-escalation). It describes four phases of septic-shock fluid therapy: resuscitation, optimisation, stabilisation and evacuation (de-resuscitation) (Hypothesis-level framework) [[Malbrain2018AIC]].
- Caveat (Established): observational links between positive balance and death are confounded by illness severity. The randomised restrictive-fluid trials did not show a mortality benefit [[Messmer2020CCM,Meyhoff2022CLASSIC,Shapiro2023CLOVERS]].

### fluids · Which crystalloid: balanced solutions versus 0.9% saline
- SSC 2021, recommendation 33: "we suggest using balanced crystalloids instead of normal saline for resuscitation". Weak recommendation, low quality of evidence [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2026: "we suggest using balanced crystalloids over 0.9% saline" for adults undergoing initial resuscitation (conditional, moderate certainty). A remark suggests 0.9% saline for sepsis with traumatic brain injury [[Prescott2026SSC]].
- SMART (2018) was a cluster-crossover trial of 15,802 critically ill adults. Major adverse kidney events within 30 days (death, new renal replacement therapy (RRT) or persistent renal dysfunction) occurred in 14.3% with balanced crystalloids versus 15.4% with saline: marginal OR 0.91 (95% CI 0.84 to 0.99; P=0.04) [[Semler2018SMART]].
- In SMART, 30-day in-hospital mortality was 10.3% versus 11.1% (P=0.06). New RRT was 2.5% versus 2.9% [[Semler2018SMART]].
- In the 1641 SMART patients with sepsis (secondary analysis), 30-day in-hospital mortality was 26.3% with balanced crystalloids versus 31.2% with saline (aOR 0.74; 95% CI 0.59 to 0.93) [[Brown2019SMARTsepsis]].
- SALT-ED (13,347 non-critically ill emergency department patients) found no difference in hospital-free days (median 25 each). Major adverse kidney events were 4.7% versus 5.6% favouring balanced crystalloids (aOR 0.82) [[Self2018SALTED]].
- BaSICS (2021) enrolled 11,052 ICU patients in Brazil. 90-day mortality was 26.4% with a balanced solution versus 27.2% with saline (adjusted HR 0.97; 95% CI 0.90 to 1.05; P=0.47) [[Zampieri2021BaSICSfluid]].
- PLUS (2022) enrolled 5037 ICU patients in Australia and New Zealand. 90-day mortality was 21.8% with Plasma-Lyte 148 versus 22.0% with saline (difference −0.15 points; 95% CI −3.60 to 3.30). New RRT was 12.7% versus 12.9% [[Finfer2022PLUS]].
- A 2022 meta-analysis pooled 13 RCTs (35,884 participants). In low-risk-of-bias trials, the 90-day mortality RR for balanced versus saline was 0.96 (95% CI 0.91 to 1.01). The posterior probability that balanced crystalloids reduce mortality was 89.5% [[Hammond2022NEJMEvid]].
- An individual-patient meta-analysis (BEST-Living, 6 trials, 34,685 participants) found:
  - In-hospital mortality 16.8% (balanced) versus 17.3% (saline): OR 0.962 (95% CrI 0.909 to 1.019).
  - In traumatic brain injury, 19.1% versus 14.7% (OR 1.424), favouring saline [[Zampieri2024BESTLiving]].
- A before-and-after study in one Melbourne ICU (760 vs 773 patients) restricted chloride-rich fluids. Injury and failure classes of acute kidney injury (AKI) fell from 14% to 8.4% (P<0.001) [[Yunos2012JAMA]].
- In children, PRoMPT BOLUS (NEJM 2026, 8482 analysed, 47 emergency departments) found no difference. Major adverse kidney events at 30 days were 3.4% (balanced) versus 3.0% (saline): RR 1.10 (95% CI 0.88 to 1.40). Hyperchloraemia was less common with balanced fluid (31.4% vs 49.0%) [[Balamuth2026PRoMPT]].

### fluids · Colloids: albumin, starches, gelatin
- SAFE (2004) randomised 6997 ICU patients to 4% albumin or saline. 28-day deaths were 726 versus 729 (RR 0.99; 95% CI 0.91 to 1.09; P=0.87). The two fluids gave equivalent outcomes [[Finfer2004SAFE]].
- In the 1218 SAFE patients with severe sepsis (prespecified subgroup), the unadjusted RR of death with albumin was 0.87 (95% CI 0.74 to 1.02). The adjusted OR was 0.71 (95% CI 0.52 to 0.97). RRT use was 18.7% versus 18.2% [[Finfer2011SAFEsepsis]].
- ALBIOS (2014) randomised 1818 patients with severe sepsis to 20% albumin (target serum albumin ≥30 g/L) plus crystalloid, or crystalloid alone.
  - 28-day mortality was 31.8% versus 32.0% (RR 1.00).
  - 90-day mortality was 41.1% versus 43.6% (RR 0.94; 95% CI 0.85 to 1.05).
  - The albumin group had higher MAP and lower net fluid balance [[Caironi2014ALBIOS]].
- A German open-label trial (JAMA Netw Open 2026, 440 patients with septic shock, stopped early for slow enrolment) targeted serum albumin ≥3.0 g/dL with 20% albumin. 90-day mortality was 43.3% versus 45.9% (RR 0.94; 95% CI 0.76 to 1.17). The authors call the result inconclusive [[Sakr2026JAMANetwOpen]].
- SSC 2021, recommendation 34, suggested "using albumin in patients who received large volumes of crystalloids over using crystalloids alone" (weak, moderate quality) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2026 reverses that direction: "we suggest using crystalloids alone over crystalloids with supplemental albumin for fluid resuscitation" (conditional, moderate certainty) [[Prescott2026SSC]].
- 6S (2012) randomised 798 patients with severe sepsis to 6% HES 130/0.42 or Ringer's acetate.
  - 90-day mortality was 51% versus 43% (RR 1.17; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.36).
  - RRT was 22% versus 16% (RR 1.35) [[Perner2012SixS]].
- CHEST (2012) randomised 7000 ICU patients to 6% HES 130/0.4 or saline. 90-day mortality was 18.0% versus 17.0% (RR 1.06; 95% CI 0.96 to 1.18). RRT was 7.0% versus 5.8% (RR 1.21; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.45). Adverse events were 5.3% versus 2.8% [[Myburgh2012CHEST]].
- VISEP (2008) was stopped early for safety. 10% pentastarch (HES 200/0.5) was associated with higher rates of acute renal failure and RRT than Ringer's lactate, and the toxicity "increased with accumulating doses" [[Brunkhorst2008VISEP]].
- CRISTAL (2013) randomised 2857 ICU patients with hypovolaemic shock to colloids or crystalloids. 28-day mortality was 25.4% versus 27.0% (RR 0.96; P=0.26). 90-day mortality was 30.7% versus 34.2% (RR 0.92; P=0.03, a secondary outcome) [[Annane2013CRISTAL]].
- Cochrane 2018 (69 studies, 30,020 participants) found:
  - Starches probably make little or no difference to mortality but slightly increase RRT (RR 1.30; 95% CI 1.14 to 1.48) and blood transfusion (RR 1.19).
  - Albumin or fresh frozen plasma versus crystalloids: mortality RR 0.98 at end of follow-up [[Lewis2018Cochrane]].
- Cochrane 2018 found low-certainty evidence that gelatins may make little or no difference to mortality versus crystalloids (RR 0.89; 95% CI 0.74 to 1.08) [[Lewis2018Cochrane]].
- SSC 2021 and SSC 2026 both "recommend against using starches for resuscitation" (strong, high certainty) and "suggest against using gelatin" (weak/conditional, moderate certainty) [[Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]].

### fluids · Is the patient fluid-responsive? Dynamic measures and perfusion targets
- SSC 2021, recommendation 6, suggests "using dynamic measures to guide fluid resuscitation, over physical examination or static parameters alone" (weak, very low quality). Dynamic parameters "include response to a passive leg raise or a fluid bolus, using stroke volume (SV), stroke volume variation (SVV), pulse pressure variation (PPV), or echocardiography" [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2026 keeps the suggestion to use dynamic measures over physical examination or static measures alone (conditional, low certainty) [[Prescott2026SSC]].
- About half of unstable patients respond to fluid. 56% of patients responded to a fluid challenge in a 24-study review of CVP [[Marik2008CVP]], and the mean prevalence was 50% (95% CI 42% to 56%) across 50 studies [[Bentzer2016JAMA]].
- CVP correlates poorly with blood volume (pooled r 0.16) and does not predict fluid responsiveness: area under the ROC curve 0.56 (95% CI 0.51 to 0.61). The authors conclude CVP "should not be used to make clinical decisions regarding fluid management" [[Marik2008CVP]].
- Passive leg raising (PLR) with cardiac output measurement (21 studies, 995 fluid challenges):
  - Pooled sensitivity 0.85 and specificity 0.91; area under the curve 0.95.
  - Best threshold: a PLR-induced rise in cardiac output of ≥10 ± 2%.
  - Judged by pulse pressure instead, sensitivity was only 0.56 [[Monnet2016PLR]].
- In unstable adults, a rise in cardiac output with PLR gave a positive likelihood ratio of 11 (95% CI 7.6 to 17). No rise gave a negative LR of 0.13. Physical examination findings were not predictive [[Bentzer2016JAMA]].
- Pulse pressure variation in mechanically ventilated patients (29 studies, 685 patients):
  - Area under the curve 0.94, versus 0.55 for CVP.
  - Mean threshold 12.5 ± 1.6%.
  - Sensitivity 0.89, specificity 0.88.
  - Valid only during controlled ventilation without spontaneous breathing [[Marik2009PPV]].
- FRESH (2020, 124 analysed) used PLR-guided resuscitation in septic shock. Fluid balance at 72 h was lower (0.65 vs 2.02 L; −1.37 L). Fewer patients needed RRT (5.1% vs 17.5%) or mechanical ventilation (17.7% vs 34.1%) [[Douglas2020FRESH]].
- SSC 2021, recommendation 8, suggests "using capillary refill time to guide resuscitation as an adjunct to other measures of perfusion" (weak, low quality). Recommendation 7 suggests guiding resuscitation to decrease serum lactate when lactate is elevated [[Evans2021SSC]].
- ANDROMEDA-SHOCK (2019, 424 patients) compared resuscitation targeting capillary refill time (CRT) with targeting lactate. 28-day mortality was 34.9% versus 43.4% (HR 0.75; 95% CI 0.55 to 1.02; P=0.06, not significant). 72-h SOFA was lower with CRT (5.6 vs 6.6) [[Hernandez2019ANDROMEDA]].
- ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 (JAMA 2025, 1467 patients, 86 centres) tested a personalised CRT-targeted protocol (pulse pressure, diastolic pressure, fluid responsiveness, echocardiography). It beat usual care on a hierarchical composite: win ratio 1.16 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.33), driven mainly by shorter vital support [[Hernandez2025ANDROMEDA2]].
- The ESICM shock consensus (2014) made 44 statements, including individualising blood pressure targets and assessing and predicting fluid responsiveness [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

### fluids · Protocolised early goal-directed therapy (EGDT): historical arc
- Rivers (2001, 263 emergency department patients) reported in-hospital mortality of 30.5% with 6 h of EGDT versus 46.5% with standard therapy (P=0.009) [[Rivers2001EGDT]].
- The PRISM patient-level meta-analysis of ProCESS, ARISE and ProMISe (3723 patients, 138 hospitals) found 90-day mortality of 24.9% with EGDT versus 25.4% with usual care (aOR 0.97; 95% CI 0.82 to 1.14). EGDT used more ICU care and cost more [[PRISM2017]].
- ProCESS found no improvement from protocol-based resuscitation for septic shock diagnosed in the emergency department [[ProCESS2014]].
- BaSICS also tested bolus speed. A slower 333 mL/h versus 999 mL/h gave 90-day mortality of 26.6% versus 27.0% (HR 1.03; 95% CI 0.96 to 1.11) [[Zampieri2021BaSICSrate]].

### pressors · Noradrenaline (norepinephrine): first-line
- SSC 2021, recommendation 37: "we recommend using norepinephrine as the first-line agent over other vasopressors". Strong recommendation; evidence quality is high versus dopamine, moderate versus vasopressin, low versus epinephrine and selepressin, very low versus angiotensin II [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2026 splits this. It recommends norepinephrine first-line over dopamine, epinephrine or selepressin (strong). It suggests norepinephrine over vasopressin or angiotensin II (conditional; low and very low certainty) [[Prescott2026SSC]].
- SSC 2021 remark: where norepinephrine is unavailable, "epinephrine or dopamine can be used as an alternative", with special attention to arrhythmia risk [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SOAP II (2010, 1679 patients with shock) found:
  - 28-day mortality 52.5% with dopamine versus 48.5% with norepinephrine (OR 1.17; 95% CI 0.97 to 1.42; P=0.10).
  - Arrhythmic events 24.1% versus 12.4% (P<0.001).
  - Dopamine was linked to excess deaths in the 280 patients with cardiogenic shock [[DeBacker2010SOAPII]].
- In a meta-analysis of 32 trials (3544 patients), norepinephrine versus dopamine gave all-cause mortality RR 0.89 (95% CI 0.81 to 0.98), with fewer arrhythmias. No other vasopressor comparison showed a mortality difference [[Avni2015PLoSOne]].
- "Renal-dose" dopamine (2 µg/kg/min) did not protect the kidneys in 328 critically ill patients. Peak creatinine was 245 versus 249 µmol/L, and RRT 35 versus 40 patients [[Bellomo2000ANZICS]].
- Levophed label: "LEVOPHED is indicated to raise blood pressure in adult patients with severe, acute hypotension." Its mechanism is α-adrenergic vasoconstriction with β-adrenergic inotropy [[FDA-Levophed]].
- Levophed label dosing as published: "After an initial dosage of 8 to 12 mcg per minute via intravenous infusion, assess patient response and adjust dosage to maintain desired hemodynamic effect… Typical maintenance intravenous dosage is 2 to 4 mcg per minute." The label also says to address hypovolaemia before starting [[FDA-Levophed]].
- Levophed label warnings: tissue ischaemia, local necrosis with extravasation, hypotension after abrupt discontinuation, arrhythmias, and sulfite allergy. For extravasation the label describes infiltration with "5 to 10 mg of phentolamine mesylate in 10 to 15 mL of 0.9% Sodium Chloride" [[FDA-Levophed]].

### pressors · Peripheral vasopressor start
- SSC 2021, recommendation 44 (kept in 2026): "we suggest starting vasopressors peripherally to restore MAP rather than delaying initiation until a central venous access is secured" (weak, very low quality). The 2021 remark: give them "only for a short period of time and in a vein in or proximal to the antecubital fossa" [[Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]].
- In one medical ICU (734 patients, 783 peripheral infusions, mean 49 h), extravasation occurred in 2% (19 patients) with no tissue injury after treatment. 13% eventually needed central access [[CardenasGarcia2015JHM]].
- A 2020 systematic review (7 studies, 1382 patients) found extravasation in 3.4% (95% CI 2.5% to 4.7%) of peripheral infusions, with no reported tissue necrosis or limb ischaemia [[Tian2020EMA]].
- In 635 patients given peripheral norepinephrine under a protocol, 51.6% of first exposures never needed a central line. Extravasation occurred in 35 patients (75.8 events/1000 infusion-days), and no patient needed surgery [[Yerke2024Chest]].
- Of 204 published local tissue injury events from peripheral vasopressors, 85.3% occurred at sites distal to the antecubital or popliteal fossa [[Loubani2015JCC]].

### pressors · Vasopressin
- SSC 2021, recommendation 38: "For adults with septic shock on norepinephrine with inadequate MAP levels, we suggest adding vasopressin instead of escalating the dose of norepinephrine" (weak, moderate quality). Remark: "vasopressin is usually started when the dose of norepinephrine is in the range of 0.25–0.5 μg/kg/min" [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2026 suggests adding vasopressin for adults with septic shock on escalating norepinephrine doses (conditional, moderate certainty) [[Prescott2026SSC]].
- VASST (2008) randomised 778 patients on at least 5 µg/min norepinephrine to low-dose vasopressin (0.01–0.03 U/min) or norepinephrine. 28-day mortality was 35.4% versus 39.3% (P=0.26), and 90-day mortality 43.9% versus 49.6% (P=0.11) [[Russell2008VASST]].
- In VASST's prespecified less-severe stratum, 28-day mortality was 26.5% with vasopressin versus 35.7% with norepinephrine (P=0.05). The heterogeneity test was not significant (P=0.10) [[Russell2008VASST]].
- In a post hoc VASST analysis, patients also given corticosteroids had lower mortality with vasopressin than norepinephrine (35.9% vs 44.7%). Corticosteroids raised plasma vasopressin levels by 33% at 6 h and 67% at 24 h (Early evidence) [[Russell2009VASSTsteroid]].
- VANISH (2016, 409 patients, 2×2 factorial with hydrocortisone) compared early vasopressin (up to 0.06 U/min) with norepinephrine. Survivors who never developed kidney failure were 57.0% versus 59.2%. RRT was 25.4% versus 35.3% (difference −9.9%; 95% CI −19.3% to −0.6%) [[Gordon2016VANISH]].
- An individual-patient meta-analysis (4 trials, 1453 patients) found no effect of vasopressin on 28-day mortality (RR 0.98; 95% CI 0.86 to 1.12). It caused more digital ischaemia (+1.7%) and fewer arrhythmias (−2.8%). An RRT reduction (RR 0.86) was not robust [[Nagendran2019IPD]].
- VASSPR (JAMA 2026) was a cluster-crossover trial of 3959 patients in 13 hospitals. A lower vasopressin start threshold (0.1 µg/kg/min norepinephrine-equivalent) versus a higher one (0.4) gave 28-day mortality of 31.8% versus 33.0% (adjusted marginal OR 0.98; 95% CI 0.87 to 1.06) [[Peltan2026VASSPR]].
- In an observational cohort of 1610 patients with septic shock, odds of in-hospital death rose 20.7% for every 10 µg/min of norepinephrine-equivalent dose at vasopressin start, up to 60 µg/min (aOR 1.21) (association only) [[Sacha2022CCM]].
- Vasostrict label indication: "to increase blood pressure in adults with vasodilatory shock who remain hypotensive despite fluids and catecholamines" [[FDA-Vasostrict]].
- Vasostrict label dosing as published for septic shock: "start with a dose of 0.01 units/minute. If the target blood pressure response is not achieved, titrate up by 0.005 units/minute at 10- to 15-minute intervals. The maximum dose… for septic shock 0.07 units/minute." It is tapered once target pressure has held for 8 hours without catecholamines [[FDA-Vasostrict]].
- Vasostrict label mechanism: V1-receptor vasoconstriction on vascular smooth muscle, with V2-mediated antidiuresis. Literature-reported adverse reactions include mesenteric ischaemia, distal limb ischaemia, atrial fibrillation and bradycardia [[FDA-Vasostrict]].
- SSC 2021 and 2026 "suggest against using terlipressin" (weak/conditional, low certainty) [[Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]].

### pressors · Adrenaline (epinephrine), angiotensin II and other add-ons
- SSC 2021, recommendation 39: for inadequate MAP "despite norepinephrine and vasopressin, we suggest adding epinephrine" (weak, low quality). SSC 2026 keeps this (conditional, very low certainty) [[Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]].
- CATS (2007, 330 patients with septic shock) compared epinephrine alone with norepinephrine plus dobutamine. 28-day mortality was 40% versus 34% (RR 0.86; 95% CI 0.65 to 1.14), and serious adverse events were similar [[Annane2007CATS]].
- CAT (2008, 280 ICU patients) found no difference in time to MAP goal with epinephrine versus norepinephrine. Epinephrine caused transient metabolic effects, and 12.9% of patients were withdrawn because of them [[Myburgh2008CAT]].
- ATHOS-3 (2017) gave angiotensin II or placebo to 321 patients with vasodilatory shock on more than 0.2 µg/kg/min norepinephrine. The MAP response at 3 h was 69.9% versus 23.4% (OR 7.95). 28-day mortality was 46% versus 54% (HR 0.78; 95% CI 0.57 to 1.07; P=0.12) [[Khanna2017ATHOS3]].
- Giapreza label: "GIAPREZA increases blood pressure in adults with septic or other distributive shock." Dosing as published: start "at 20 nanograms (ng)/kg/min", titrate "as frequently as every 5 minutes by increments of up to 15 ng/kg/min". The maximum is 80 ng/kg/min in the first 3 hours and 40 ng/kg/min for maintenance [[FDA-Giapreza]].
- Giapreza label warning: thromboembolic events were 12.9% with Giapreza versus 5.1% with placebo in ATHOS-3, mostly deep vein thrombosis (4.3% vs 0%). The label says to use concurrent VTE prophylaxis [[FDA-Giapreza]].
- At study entry in ATHOS-3 (per the label), 97% of patients were on norepinephrine, 67% on vasopressin, and 47% on three or more vasopressors [[FDA-Giapreza]].
- TRICYCLE (phase II, 2026, 79 patients) started angiotensin II, vasopressin and norepinephrine early together. Plasma renin fell faster and tachyarrhythmias were less common (15.6% vs 68.6%). 28-day mortality was similar (36.8% vs 46.3%; not powered) (Early evidence) [[Kalamar2026TRICYCLE]].
- Methylene blue started within 24 h (single-centre RCT, 91 patients) shortened time to vasopressor discontinuation (69 vs 94 h). Mortality was similar (Early evidence) [[IbarraEstrada2023MB]].

### pressors · Inotropes for septic cardiomyopathy
- SSC 2021, recommendation 41: for septic shock with cardiac dysfunction and persistent hypoperfusion "despite adequate volume status and arterial blood pressure, we suggest either adding dobutamine to norepinephrine or using epinephrine alone" (weak, low quality) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2026 suggests using inotropes over none for septic shock with cardiac dysfunction and persistent hypoperfusion (conditional, very low certainty), added to vasopressors rather than replacing them. It also suggests dobutamine with norepinephrine, or epinephrine alone [[Prescott2026SSC]].
- SSC 2021 and 2026 suggest against levosimendan [[Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]].
- LeoPARDS (2016, 516 adults with sepsis) found no reduction in mean daily SOFA with levosimendan versus placebo (6.68 vs 6.06). Weaning from ventilation was less likely (HR 0.77), and supraventricular tachyarrhythmia was more common (3.1% vs 0.4%) [[Gordon2016LeoPARDS]].

### pressors · MAP targets
- SSC 2021, recommendation 9 (kept in 2026): "For adults with septic shock on vasopressors, we recommend an initial target mean arterial pressure (MAP) of 65 mm Hg over higher MAP targets" (strong, moderate quality) [[Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]].
- SSC 2026 adds a statement for adults aged 65 or older with septic shock: "we suggest an initial MAP range of 60-65 mm Hg over higher ranges" (conditional, low certainty) [[Prescott2026SSC]].
- SSC 2021 explains that organ perfusion falls below a MAP threshold "usually understood to be approximately 60 mm Hg" [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SEPSISPAM (2014) randomised 776 patients with septic shock to a MAP target of 80–85 or 65–70 mm Hg.
  - 28-day mortality was 36.6% versus 34.0% (HR 1.07; 95% CI 0.84 to 1.38).
  - 90-day mortality was 43.8% versus 42.3%.
  - New atrial fibrillation was more common with the high target [[Asfar2014SEPSISPAM]].
- In SEPSISPAM patients with chronic hypertension, the higher target reduced RRT use, without a mortality difference [[Asfar2014SEPSISPAM]].
- The 65 trial (2020) randomised 2600 patients aged 65 or older with vasodilatory hypotension to permissive hypotension (MAP 60–65 mm Hg) or usual care.
  - Vasopressor exposure fell: median 33 versus 38 h; 17.7 versus 26.4 mg norepinephrine-equivalent.
  - 90-day mortality was 41.0% versus 43.8% (absolute difference −2.85%; 95% CI −6.75 to 1.05; P=0.15) [[Lamontagne2020SIXTYFIVE]].
- In the 65 trial, the prespecified adjusted OR for 90-day mortality was 0.82 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.98). Serious adverse events were 6.2% versus 5.8% [[Lamontagne2020SIXTYFIVE]].
- An individual-patient meta-analysis of SEPSISPAM, OVATION-pilot and 65 (3352 patients) gave a 90-day mortality RR of 0.93 for lower versus higher MAP targets (95% CrI 0.76 to 1.07), with posterior probability of benefit 87% (low certainty). No subgroup benefited from higher targets [[Angriman2025NEJMEvid]].

### pressors · Corticosteroids
- SSC 2021, recommendation 58: "For adults with septic shock and an ongoing requirement for vasopressor therapy we suggest using IV corticosteroids" (weak, moderate quality) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021 remark, quoted as published: "The typical corticosteroid used in adults with septic shock is IV hydrocortisone at a dose of 200 mg/day given as 50 mg intravenously every 6 h or as a continuous infusion. It is suggested that this is commenced at a dose of norepinephrine or epinephrine ≥ 0.25 mcg/kg/min at least 4 h after initiation" [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2026 still suggests IV corticosteroids for adults with septic shock (conditional, low certainty) [[Prescott2026SSC]].
- The SCCM/ESICM 2024 corticosteroid focused update made a conditional recommendation to give corticosteroids in septic shock. It recommended against high-dose, short-duration corticosteroids for septic shock [[Chaudhuri2024SCCM]].
- Annane 2002 (300 patients) gave hydrocortisone 50 mg IV every 6 h plus fludrocortisone 50 µg daily for 7 days. Among corticotropin non-responders, 28-day mortality was 53% versus 63% with placebo (HR 0.67; 95% CI 0.47 to 0.95) [[Annane2002JAMA]].
- CORTICUS (2008, 499 patients) gave hydrocortisone 50 mg IV every 6 h for 5 days, then a taper. 28-day mortality was 34.3% versus 31.5% (P=0.51), and 39.2% versus 36.1% in non-responders. Shock reversed faster, with more superinfections [[Sprung2008CORTICUS]].
- ADRENAL (2018, 3800 ventilated patients with septic shock) gave hydrocortisone 200 mg/day by continuous infusion.
  - 90-day mortality was 27.9% versus 28.8% (OR 0.95; 95% CI 0.82 to 1.10; P=0.50).
  - Median shock duration was 3 versus 4 days (HR 1.32).
  - Fewer patients were transfused (37.0% vs 41.7%) [[Venkatesh2018ADRENAL]].
- APROCCHSS (2018, 1241 patients) tested hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone. 90-day mortality was 43.0% versus 49.1% with placebo (RR 0.88; 95% CI 0.78 to 0.99; P=0.03). Vasopressor-free days to day 28 were 17 versus 15. Hyperglycaemia was more common [[Annane2018APROCCHSS]].
- In APROCCHSS, mortality was also lower at ICU discharge (35.4% vs 41.0%), hospital discharge (39.0% vs 45.3%) and day 180 (46.6% vs 52.5%), but not at day 28 (33.7% vs 38.9%; P=0.06) [[Annane2018APROCCHSS]].
- Cochrane 2019 (61 trials, 12,192 participants) found:
  - Corticosteroids probably slightly reduce 28-day mortality (RR 0.91; 95% CI 0.84 to 0.99; moderate certainty).
  - They increase muscle weakness (RR 1.21) and hypernatraemia.
  - They probably do not increase superinfection (RR 1.06) [[Annane2019Cochrane]].
- A 2018 meta-analysis (42 RCTs, 10,194 patients) found:
  - Short-term mortality RR 0.93 (95% CI 0.84 to 1.03; low certainty).
  - Higher shock reversal at day 7 (RR 1.26; high certainty).
  - More hypernatraemia (RR 1.64) and hyperglycaemia (RR 1.16) [[Rochwerg2018CCM]].
- A 2024 dose-response meta-analysis (45 RCTs, 9563 patients) found short-term mortality RR 0.93 (95% CI 0.88 to 0.99) and shock reversal RR 1.24 at day 7. Optimal dosing was about 260 mg/day hydrocortisone or equivalent (Early evidence) [[Pitre2024CCE]].
- The Solu-Cortef label lists adrenocortical insufficiency among its endocrine indications but gives no septic-shock-specific dose. Septic-shock regimens come from trials and guidelines, not the label [[FDA-SoluCortef]].

### Exam hooks
- Initial fluid in SSC 2021 and 2026: at least 30 mL/kg IV crystalloid within the first 3 h for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock. Weak/conditional, low certainty [[Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]].
- SMART: major adverse kidney events 14.3% (balanced) versus 15.4% (saline), OR 0.91 [[Semler2018SMART]].
- BaSICS and PLUS found no mortality difference between balanced solutions and saline [[Zampieri2021BaSICSfluid,Finfer2022PLUS]].
- Starches: 6S showed more deaths (51% vs 43%) and RRT (22% vs 16%). SSC strongly recommends against them [[Perner2012SixS,Evans2021SSC]].
- SAFE: 4% albumin equals saline for 28-day mortality (RR 0.99) [[Finfer2004SAFE]].
- FEAST: fluid boluses raised 48-h mortality in African children (10.6% and 10.5% vs 7.3%) [[Maitland2011FEAST]].
- CLASSIC, CLOVERS and ARISE FLUIDS: restrictive or early-vasopressor strategies did not change mortality [[Meyhoff2022CLASSIC,Shapiro2023CLOVERS,Peake2026ARISEFLUIDS]].
- CVP does not predict fluid responsiveness (AUC 0.56). PLR with cardiac output does (AUC 0.95) [[Marik2008CVP,Monnet2016PLR]].
- Norepinephrine is first-line. Dopamine causes more arrhythmias (24.1% vs 12.4% in SOAP II) [[DeBacker2010SOAPII,Evans2021SSC]].
- Vasopressin is the second agent. SSC 2021 says it is usually started at norepinephrine 0.25–0.5 µg/kg/min [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Vasostrict label septic-shock range: 0.01 to 0.07 units/minute [[FDA-Vasostrict]].
- Angiotensin II (ATHOS-3) raised MAP response (69.9% vs 23.4%) but carries a thrombosis warning (12.9% vs 5.1%) [[Khanna2017ATHOS3,FDA-Giapreza]].
- The initial MAP target is 65 mm Hg. SEPSISPAM's 80–85 target gave no survival gain but more atrial fibrillation [[Asfar2014SEPSISPAM,Evans2021SSC]].
- 65 trial: permissive MAP 60–65 mm Hg in people aged 65 or older gave 41.0% versus 43.8% 90-day mortality (not significant) [[Lamontagne2020SIXTYFIVE]].
- ADRENAL: hydrocortisone 200 mg/day gave faster shock reversal (3 vs 4 days) with no change in 90-day mortality (27.9% vs 28.8%) [[Venkatesh2018ADRENAL]].
- APROCCHSS: hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone gave 90-day mortality of 43.0% versus 49.1% [[Annane2018APROCCHSS]].
- The SSC steroid threshold is norepinephrine or epinephrine ≥0.25 µg/kg/min for at least 4 h, as published [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Peripheral vasopressors: SSC suggests starting peripherally rather than delaying for a central line. Extravasation was about 2–3.4% in cohorts [[Evans2021SSC,Tian2020EMA,CardenasGarcia2015JHM]].

### In plain words
- In sepsis, blood vessels become leaky and floppy, so blood pressure can drop and organs get too little blood. Doctors first give fluid through a drip to fill the vessels back up [[Singer2016Sepsis3,Evans2021SSC]].
- Guidelines suggest a first amount of fluid based on body weight, given over the first few hours. After that, doctors check whether more fluid is actually helping before giving more [[Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]].
- Too much fluid can also cause harm, such as fluid in the lungs. Big studies found that giving less fluid and starting blood-pressure medicine earlier was about as safe as giving more fluid [[Meyhoff2022CLASSIC,Shapiro2023CLOVERS,Peake2026ARISEFLUIDS]].
- A simple test is lifting the legs for a minute. If the heart pumps more blood, the person will probably benefit from more fluid [[Monnet2016PLR,Bentzer2016JAMA]].
- Most hospitals use salt-water drips. "Balanced" drips are a little closer to the body's own fluid, and guidelines lean toward them [[Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]].
- Some older drip types, called starches, harmed the kidneys and are no longer recommended for sepsis [[Perner2012SixS,Myburgh2012CHEST]].
- If fluid alone does not raise blood pressure enough, doctors use medicines that tighten blood vessels. The usual first choice is noradrenaline (norepinephrine), and others can be added [[Evans2021SSC,FDA-Levophed]].
- These medicines are usually given in intensive care with close monitoring. Sometimes they are started in an arm vein so treatment is not delayed [[Evans2021SSC,Tian2020EMA]].
- Doctors usually aim for an average blood pressure (the "MAP") of about 65. For older adults, slightly lower may be fine [[Evans2021SSC,Lamontagne2020SIXTYFIVE,Prescott2026SSC]].
- Some people in septic shock also get a steroid called hydrocortisone. It can help blood pressure recover sooner, though its effect on survival is small and still debated [[Venkatesh2018ADRENAL,Annane2018APROCCHSS]].
- Numbers in these studies describe large groups of people, not what will happen to any one person.
- Sepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?

## Open questions and controversies
- How much fluid first? SSC 2021/2026 keep "at least 30 mL/kg" in 3 h as a conditional, low-certainty suggestion based on observational data [[Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]]. Restrictive-fluid trials (CLASSIC, CLOVERS, ARISE FLUIDS) found no mortality difference [[Meyhoff2022CLASSIC,Shapiro2023CLOVERS,Peake2026ARISEFLUIDS]]. FEAST and the Zambian trial showed harm from boluses in low-resource settings [[Maitland2011FEAST,Andrews2017Zambia]]. Emergency physicians have published a formal dissent from the 2026 SSC guidelines [[Spiegel2026AJEM]]. Status: unresolved. Individualised, responsiveness-guided fluid is the direction of travel.
- Albumin: SSC 2021 suggested it after large crystalloid volumes [[Evans2021SSC]]. SSC 2026 suggests crystalloids alone [[Prescott2026SSC]]. ALBIOS and the German 2026 trial were neutral [[Caironi2014ALBIOS,Sakr2026JAMANetwOpen]]. The SAFE sepsis subgroup hinted at benefit [[Finfer2011SAFEsepsis]]. Status: no proven benefit.
- Balanced versus saline: SMART showed a small kidney benefit [[Semler2018SMART]]. BaSICS and PLUS were neutral [[Zampieri2021BaSICSfluid,Finfer2022PLUS]]. Pooled data give about a 90% probability of a small mortality benefit [[Hammond2022NEJMEvid,Zampieri2024BESTLiving]], but saline looks better in traumatic brain injury [[Zampieri2024BESTLiving]]. Status: guideline suggests balanced, with a traumatic brain injury exception.
- When to add vasopressin: SSC 2021's practice remark is 0.25–0.5 µg/kg/min norepinephrine [[Evans2021SSC]]. VASSPR found no mortality difference between 0.1 and 0.4 thresholds [[Peltan2026VASSPR]]. Observational data link later starts with higher mortality [[Sacha2022CCM]]. Status: open.
- Early multimodal vasopressors (angiotensin II, vasopressin and norepinephrine together) and methylene blue: phase II signals only [[Kalamar2026TRICYCLE,IbarraEstrada2023MB]]. Status: Early evidence.
- MAP target: 65 mm Hg is standard [[Evans2021SSC]]. Lower targets in older adults appear at least as safe [[Lamontagne2020SIXTYFIVE,Angriman2025NEJMEvid]]. People with chronic hypertension may need less RRT at higher targets [[Asfar2014SEPSISPAM]]. Status: individualisation debated.
- Steroids: hydrocortisone alone (ADRENAL) did not change mortality [[Venkatesh2018ADRENAL]]. Hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone (APROCCHSS) did [[Annane2018APROCCHSS]]. Meta-analyses show a small short-term mortality benefit and consistent faster shock reversal [[Annane2019Cochrane,Rochwerg2018CCM,Pitre2024CCE]]. Whether fludrocortisone matters, and the best dose, timing and threshold, remain unresolved [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Perfusion targets: CRT-targeted resuscitation was borderline in ANDROMEDA-SHOCK [[Hernandez2019ANDROMEDA]]. A personalised CRT protocol won on a composite outcome in ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 [[Hernandez2025ANDROMEDA2]]. Status: Early evidence that personalised haemodynamics shorten organ support.
