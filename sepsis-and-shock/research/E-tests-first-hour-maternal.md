# E · Tests, biomarkers, the first hour & maternal sepsis

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: tests, firsthour, maternal.

This pass covers the tests used when sepsis (the body's harmful response to an infection) is suspected: lactate, blood and source cultures, procalcitonin, CRP, white count and rapid molecular diagnostics. It also covers the "first hour" (the Surviving Sepsis Campaign Hour-1 bundle, SSC 2021 timing statements, the antibiotic-timing cohorts, the SEP-1 debate and the early goal-directed therapy trials) and maternal sepsis (definitions, burden, early warning tools, group A streptococcus, guidelines). Trial and cohort numbers come from PubMed or Europe PMC abstracts. SSC 2021 wording comes from the open-access full text.

Access notes:
- SSC 2021 recommendation wording was read from the Intensive Care Med version's open-access full text (PMC8486643). That text is identical in substance to the Crit Care Med co-publication.
- The 2018 Hour-1 bundle paper (Levy, Evans & Rhodes) is paywalled in both journals. Its five bundle elements are quoted from a verbatim listing in an open-access compliance study [[Wang2021Hour1]], and the SSC 2021 text confirms the bundle exists and includes lactate [[Evans2021SSC]].
- MBRRACE-UK reports and RCOG Green-top Guidelines 64a/64b (2012) are not indexed in PubMed, so I could not validate them and I cite no numbers from them. UK maternal sepsis data come from the CMACE 2006–08 enquiry (indexed in BJOG) and the UKOSS 2011–12 case-control study.
- The NICE NG51 guideline is not cited: the only PubMed-indexed copy is a 2018 journal reprint, and I took no wording from it.
- Doses, fluid volumes, MAP and lactate targets and guideline statements below are quoted as published. They are not instructions. Mortality figures are population statistics from the studies named. They do not predict what will happen to any one person.

Call-now box for every page in these modules: "Sepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?" Any lactate, qSOFA, SOFA, NEWS2, shock-index or obstetric-score calculators are teaching tools only. They must never be used for self-triage or to decide whether to seek care.

## Findings

### tests · Lactate: what it is and what the thresholds mean
- Sepsis-3 identifies septic shock (the most severe form of sepsis, with very low blood pressure and cell-level stress) by two criteria: a vasopressor requirement to keep mean arterial pressure (MAP) at 65 mm Hg or more, and a serum lactate above 2 mmol/L (>18 mg/dL) without hypovolaemia. That combination carried hospital mortality above 40% [[Singer2016Sepsis3]].
- Sepsis-3 defines organ dysfunction as a rise in SOFA score of 2 points or more, which was associated with in-hospital mortality above 10% [[Singer2016Sepsis3]].
- The septic shock task force's systematic review found 44 studies (166,479 patients) that used different cut-offs for blood pressure, fluids, vasopressors, lactate and base deficit. Pooled crude septic shock mortality was 46.5% (95% CI 42.7–50.3%) [[ShankarHari2016Shock]].
- SSC 2021: "For adults suspected of having sepsis, we suggest measuring blood lactate" (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021 rationale: lactate "alone is neither sensitive nor specific enough to rule-in or rule-out the diagnosis on its own". It is used as an adjunct that shifts the pre-test probability of sepsis [[Evans2021SSC]].
- In an urban US ED cohort of 1,278 admitted patients with infection, in-hospital mortality rose with venous lactate. It was 4.9% (43/877) at 0–2.5 mmol/L, 9.0% (24/267) at 2.5–4.0 mmol/L and 28.4% (38/134) at 4.0 mmol/L or more [[Shapiro2005Lactate]].
- In the same cohort, a lactate of 4.0 mmol/L or more was 36% sensitive (95% CI 27–45%) and 92% specific (90–93%) for any in-hospital death. For death within 3 days it was 55% sensitive and 91% specific [[Shapiro2005Lactate]].
- In 830 ED adults with severe sepsis (median lactate 2.9 mmol/L; 28-day mortality 22.9%), intermediate and high lactate were each linked to mortality independent of shock. In the non-shock group the ORs were 2.05 (intermediate) and 4.87 (high); in the shock group they were 3.27 and 4.87 [[Mikkelsen2009Lactate]].
- In the SSC database (28,150 patients, 218 sites, 2005–2010), mortality was 44.5% with lactate above 4 mmol/L plus hypotension, against 29% in patients meeting neither criterion. On multivariable analysis only lactate above 4 mmol/L (with or without hypotension) was significantly associated with mortality [[Casserly2015Lactate]].
- A high lactate can have causes other than infection. SSC 2021 says lactate "should be interpreted considering the clinical context and other causes of elevated lactate" [[Evans2021SSC]].

### tests · Lactate-guided and perfusion-guided resuscitation
- SSC 2021 suggests guiding resuscitation to decrease serum lactate when it is raised, rather than not using lactate (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021 suggests using capillary refill time (how fast colour returns after pressing a fingertip) to guide resuscitation in septic shock "as an adjunct to other measures of perfusion" (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Jansen 2010 (LACTATE trial) enrolled 348 ICU patients with lactate of 3.0 mEq/L or more. The lactate group aimed to lower lactate by 20% or more every 2 hours for 8 hours. Hospital mortality was 33.9% (58/171) in the lactate group and 43.5% (77/177) in controls (P = 0.067). The adjusted hazard ratio was 0.61 (95% CI 0.43–0.87) [[Jansen2010Lactate]].
- In LACTATE, the lactate-guided group received more fluids and vasodilators, but lactate levels did not differ between groups [[Jansen2010Lactate]].
- Jones 2010 randomised 300 ED patients to a lactate clearance target of at least 10% or a ScvO2 target of at least 70%. In-hospital mortality was 17% (25/150) with lactate clearance and 23% (34/150) with ScvO2, which met noninferiority [[Jones2010LactateClearance]].
- ANDROMEDA-SHOCK (2019) randomised 424 adults with septic shock at 28 ICUs in 5 countries to an 8-hour protocol targeting either normal capillary refill time or lactate (normalising, or falling more than 20% per 2 h) [[Hernandez2019ANDROMEDA]].
- ANDROMEDA-SHOCK 28-day mortality was 34.9% (74/212) with capillary refill targeting and 43.4% (92/212) with lactate targeting. The HR was 0.75 (95% CI 0.55–1.02; P = .06), so the difference was not significant [[Hernandez2019ANDROMEDA]].
- Capillary refill targeting gave less organ dysfunction at 72 h (mean SOFA 5.6 vs 6.6; difference −1.00, 95% CI −1.97 to −0.02) [[Hernandez2019ANDROMEDA]].
- ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 (JAMA 2025) randomised 1,501 patients within 4 hours of septic shock at 86 centres in 19 countries. It compared a personalised capillary refill time protocol (n = 720 analysed) with usual care (n = 747) [[Hernandez2025ANDROMEDA2]].
- ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2's hierarchical primary outcome favoured the personalised protocol (win ratio 1.16, 95% CI 1.02–1.33; P = .04), mainly through shorter vital support. Wins for death were 19.1% vs 17.8%, and wins for duration of vital support were 26.4% vs 21.1% (Early evidence) [[Hernandez2025ANDROMEDA2]].

### tests · Blood cultures and source cultures
- SSC 2021 restates the 2016 best-practice statement: routine microbiologic cultures, including blood, should be taken before antimicrobials "if it results in no substantial delay in the start of antimicrobials (i.e. < 45 min)" [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SMFM 2023 (pregnancy) recommends cultures, including blood, before antimicrobials "as long as there are no substantial delays in timely administration of antibiotics" (Best Practice) [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].
- In 325 ED adults with severe sepsis (SBP <90 mm Hg or lactate ≥4 mmol/L), blood cultures drawn before antimicrobials were positive in 31.4% (102/325). Cultures drawn a median 70 minutes after antimicrobials were positive in 19.4% (63/325) [[Cheng2019Cultures]].
- Cultures drawn after antimicrobials were only 52.9% sensitive (95% CI 42.8–62.9%). Adding other cultures found a pathogen in 67.6% of those with positive pre-antimicrobial blood cultures [[Cheng2019Cultures]].
- In adult inpatients with 3 or more cultures in 24 h (629 single-organism episodes), the first culture detected 73.1% of bloodstream infections. Two cultures detected 89.7%, three detected 98.2% and four detected 99.8% [[Lee2007Cultures]].
- The same study concluded that two blood cultures in 24 h detect about 90% of bloodstream infections, and that up to four may be needed for a detection rate above 99% [[Lee2007Cultures]].
- The pre-test probability of bacteraemia ranges from about 2% in cellulitis to 69% in septic shock (meta-analysis of 35 studies; 4,566 bacteraemic episodes) [[Coburn2012Bacteremia]].
- Fever alone does not predict bacteraemia well (likelihood ratio 1.9 for ≥38 °C), and neither does isolated leukocytosis (LR <1.7). Shaking chills are more informative (LR 4.7) [[Coburn2012Bacteremia]].
- Contaminated blood cultures (false positives) range from 1% to more than 10% of all blood culture results. Older guidance set ≤3% as an acceptable contamination rate, though the evidence behind that target is debated [[Sautter2024Contamination]].
- ASM's 2024 evidence-based guideline found that chlorhexidine skin preparation, diversion devices, sterile technique, phlebotomy teams and education each lowered contamination. Together these practices cut contamination by about 40–60% [[Sautter2024Contamination]].
- False-positive cultures can lead to unnecessary antibiotics, higher costs and delay in finding the true cause of illness [[Sautter2024Contamination,Doern2019Contamination]].
- The IDSA/ASM 2024 microbiology utilisation guide lists the best specimens, collection volumes and transport conditions by body system (blood, respiratory, urine, CSF, intra-abdominal, skin and soft tissue) for source cultures [[Miller2024IDSAMicro]].
- SSC 2021 recommends "rapidly identifying or excluding a specific anatomical diagnosis of infection that requires emergent source control" (Best Practice Statement). Its examples include intra-abdominal abscess, GI perforation, ischaemic bowel, cholangitis, obstructed pyelonephritis and necrotising soft tissue infection, which in practice usually means imaging [[Evans2021SSC]].

### tests · Procalcitonin, CRP and white count
- Procalcitonin (PCT, a blood protein that rises mainly in bacterial infection) is "undetectable in healthy states, but rises rapidly in response to pro-inflammatory stimuli, especially bacterial infections" (SSC 2021 rationale) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021: "we suggest against using procalcitonin plus clinical evaluation to decide when to start antimicrobials, as compared to clinical evaluation alone" (weak recommendation, very low quality of evidence) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021: where adequate source control is in place and the right duration is unclear, "we suggest using procalcitonin AND clinical evaluation to decide when to discontinue antimicrobials over clinical evaluation alone" (weak, low quality) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- A diagnostic meta-analysis (30 studies, 3,244 critically ill patients) found PCT separated sepsis from non-infectious SIRS with sensitivity 0.77 (95% CI 0.72–0.81), specificity 0.79 (0.74–0.84) and AUROC 0.85, with high heterogeneity (I² = 96%) [[Wacker2013PCT]].
- Compared with CRP for telling bacterial infection from non-infective inflammation in hospitalised patients, PCT had higher sensitivity (88% vs 75%) and higher specificity (81% vs 67%) [[Simon2004PCTCRP]].
- PRORATA (2010; 621 ICU patients analysed) used PCT to start and stop antibiotics. Day-28 mortality was 21.2% with PCT and 20.4% with control, which met noninferiority. PCT patients had 14.3 vs 11.6 antibiotic-free days by day 28 (difference 2.7 days, 95% CI 1.4–4.1) [[Bouadma2010PRORATA]].
- In SAPS (Netherlands, 15 hospitals, 1,546 ICU patients), the PCT stop rule was a fall of 80% or more from peak, or a level of 0.5 μg/L or lower. Median antibiotic duration was 5 days with PCT and 7 days with standard care [[deJong2016SAPS]].
- SAPS 28-day mortality (intention to treat) was 20% (149/761) with PCT guidance and 25% (196/785) with standard care (absolute difference 5.4%, 95% CI 1.2–9.5). Per-protocol 1-year mortality was 36% vs 43% [[deJong2016SAPS]].
- ProACT (2018; 1,656 ED patients with suspected lower respiratory infection at 14 US hospitals) found no fall in antibiotic-days with PCT results and guidance: 4.2 vs 4.3 days (difference −0.05, 95% CI −0.6 to 0.5) [[Huang2018ProACT]].
- A patient-level meta-analysis (11 trials, 4,482 ICU patients with infection or sepsis) found lower 30-day mortality with PCT guidance (21.1% vs 23.7%; adjusted OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.8–0.99) and shorter antibiotic courses (9.3 vs 10.4 days) [[Wirz2018PCTICU]].
- A patient-level meta-analysis of 26 trials (6,708 patients with acute respiratory infection) found lower 30-day mortality with PCT guidance (9% vs 10%; adjusted OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.70–0.99). It also found 2.4 fewer antibiotic days (5.7 vs 8.1) and fewer antibiotic side effects (16% vs 22%) [[Schuetz2018PCTMeta]].
- The US FDA approved procalcitonin for guiding antibiotic therapy in acute respiratory infections in February 2017 [[Schuetz2018PCTMeta]].
- ADAPT-Sepsis (JAMA 2025; 2,760 adults in 41 UK ICUs) found daily PCT-guided stopping shortened antibiotics (mean 9.8 vs 10.7 days; difference 0.88 days, 95% CI 0.19–1.58). Mortality was noninferior (28-day 20.9% vs 19.4%) [[Dark2025ADAPT]].
- In ADAPT-Sepsis, a daily CRP-guided protocol did not shorten antibiotic duration (difference 0.09 days, 95% CI −0.60 to 0.79), and its mortality result was inconclusive [[Dark2025ADAPT]].
- A white blood cell count alone is a weak test for bloodstream infection: isolated leukocytosis has a likelihood ratio below 1.7 for bacteraemia [[Coburn2012Bacteremia]].

### tests · Rapid molecular diagnostics
- In a randomised trial of 617 patients with positive blood culture bottles, rapid multiplex PCR identified the organism 1.3 hours after Gram stain, against 22.3 hours with standard processing [[Banerjee2015rmPCR]].
- In the same trial, rapid PCR reduced treatment of contaminants (25% control vs 11% rmPCR vs 8% with stewardship). Mortality, length of stay and cost did not differ [[Banerjee2015rmPCR]].
- A meta-analysis of 31 studies (5,920 patients with bloodstream infection) found lower mortality with molecular rapid diagnostic testing (OR 0.66, 95% CI 0.54–0.80; NNT 20). The benefit was significant only with an antimicrobial stewardship programme (OR 0.64), not without one (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.46–1.12) [[Timbrook2017mRDT]].
- Molecular rapid testing shortened time to effective therapy by 5.03 hours (weighted mean) and length of stay by 2.48 days [[Timbrook2017mRDT]].
- T2 magnetic resonance testing for candidaemia directly from whole blood (1,801 patients) had 91.1% sensitivity and 99.4% specificity per assay. Mean time to species identification was 4.4 hours, compared with 2–5 days for culture [[Mylonakis2015T2]].

### firsthour · The Hour-1 bundle and SSC 2021 timing statements
- The SSC 2018 bundle update introduced the "Hour-1 bundle", whose elements are meant to be started within the first hour [[Levy2018Bundle,Wang2021Hour1]].
- The five Hour-1 elements, as quoted verbatim: "measure lactate level and remeasure lactate level if initial lactate is >2 mmol/L, obtain blood cultures before administering antibiotics, administer broad-spectrum antibiotics, begin rapid administration of 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate ≥4 mmol/L, apply vasopressor if hypotension is present during or after fluid resuscitation to maintain a mean arterial pressure ≥65 mmHg" [[Wang2021Hour1,Levy2018Bundle]].
- SSC 2021: "Sepsis and septic shock are medical emergencies, and we recommend that treatment and resuscitation begin immediately" (Best Practice Statement) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021: "For adults with possible septic shock or a high likelihood for sepsis, we recommend administering antimicrobials immediately, ideally within 1 h of recognition" (strong recommendation; low-quality evidence for septic shock, very low for sepsis without shock) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021: "For adults with possible sepsis without shock, we suggest a time-limited course of rapid investigation and if concern for infection persists, the administration of antimicrobials within 3 h from the time when sepsis was first recognised" (weak, very low quality) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021: for adults with a low likelihood of infection and no shock, "we suggest deferring antimicrobials while continuing to closely monitor the patient" (weak, very low quality) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021: for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock, "we suggest that at least 30 mL/kg of intravenous (IV) crystalloid fluid should be given within the first 3 h of resuscitation" (weak, low quality). The guideline notes this fixed volume rests on observational evidence [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021 recommends an initial MAP target of 65 mm Hg over higher targets for adults with septic shock on vasopressors (strong, moderate quality) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021 suggests starting vasopressors peripherally rather than waiting for central venous access, for a short time only and in a vein at or above the antecubital fossa (weak, very low quality) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021 suggests ICU admission within 6 h for adults with sepsis or septic shock who need ICU care (weak, low quality) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021 recommends against qSOFA "compared to SIRS, NEWS, or MEWS as a single screening tool for sepsis or septic shock" (strong, moderate quality) [[Evans2021SSC]].

### firsthour · Antibiotic timing: the evidence
- Kumar 2006 was a retrospective cohort of 2,731 adults with septic shock at 14 ICUs in Canada and the US (1989–2004). Among the 2,154 treated after hypotension began, each hour of delay in effective antimicrobials had an adjusted OR for death of 1.119 (95% CI 1.103–1.136) [[Kumar2006Delay]].
- Kumar 2006: effective antimicrobials within the first hour of documented hypotension were associated with 79.9% survival. "Each hour of delay in antimicrobial administration over the ensuing 6 hrs was associated with an average decrease in survival of 7.6%" (as published) [[Kumar2006Delay]].
- In Kumar 2006, only 50% of patients with septic shock received effective antimicrobials within 6 hours of documented hypotension. Median time to effective therapy was 6 hours [[Kumar2006Delay]].
- Kumar 2009 (5,715 patients with septic shock): survival was 52.0% after appropriate and 10.3% after inappropriate initial antimicrobials (OR 9.45). Inappropriate initial therapy occurred in about 20% of patients [[Kumar2009Inappropriate]].
- In the New York State mandate cohort (49,331 patients, 149 hospitals; Seymour 2017), each hour to completion of the 3-hour bundle had an OR for risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality of 1.04 (95% CI 1.02–1.05). Each hour to antibiotics had an OR of 1.04 (1.03–1.06) [[Seymour2017Timing]].
- In the same New York cohort, time to completing the initial IV fluid bolus was not associated with mortality (OR 1.01 per hour, 95% CI 0.99–1.02) [[Seymour2017Timing]].
- Median times in the New York cohort were 1.30 h to bundle completion, 0.95 h to antibiotics and 2.56 h to fluid bolus completion. 82.5% had the 3-hour bundle completed within 3 hours [[Seymour2017Timing]].
- Liu 2017 (35,000 sepsis inpatients at 21 Kaiser Permanente EDs): each hour of antibiotic delay after registration had an adjusted OR of 1.09 (95% CI 1.05–1.13). Median time to antibiotics was 2.1 h [[Liu2017Antibiotics]].
- Liu 2017: the absolute mortality increase per hour of delay was 0.3% for sepsis, 0.4% for severe sepsis and 1.8% for shock [[Liu2017Antibiotics]].
- Peltan 2019 (10,811 ED patients with sepsis at 4 Utah hospitals): each extra hour from arrival to antibiotics was associated with 10% higher odds of 1-year mortality (95% CI 5–14%). Median door-to-antibiotic time was 166 min, and 1-year mortality was 19% [[Peltan2019Door]].
- In Peltan 2019, 1-year mortality was higher when door-to-antibiotic time was over 3 h than at 3 h or less (adjusted OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.13–1.43). It was not significantly higher for over 1 h vs 1 h or less (OR 1.26, 95% CI 0.98–1.62) [[Peltan2019Door]].
- Ferrer 2014 (SSC database; 17,990 patients given antibiotics after sepsis was identified) found adjusted hospital mortality "increased steadily after 1 hour". Overall in-hospital mortality was 29.7% [[Ferrer2014Antibiotics]].
- Im 2022 (Korean ED cohort, 3,035 patients) found antibiotics within 1 h were associated with lower in-hospital mortality in septic shock (adjusted OR 0.66, 95% CI 0.44–0.99) but not in sepsis without shock (OR 0.85, 0.64–1.15) [[Im2022Antibiotics]].
- In Im 2022, among patients with septic shock treated within 3 h, each 1-h delay carried a 35% higher mortality risk. No such trend was seen without shock [[Im2022Antibiotics]].
- Gaieski 2010 (261 ED patients on EGDT) found appropriate antibiotics within 1 h of triage were associated with lower mortality: 19.5% vs 33.2% (OR 0.30, 95% CI 0.11–0.83) [[Gaieski2010Antibiotics]].
- Puskarich 2011 (291 ED patients with septic shock on a quantitative resuscitation protocol) found no rise in mortality with each hour of delay after triage. Antibiotics given only after shock was recognised were associated with higher mortality (OR 2.4) [[Puskarich2011Antibiotics]].
- Sterling 2015 meta-analysis (11 studies) found no significant mortality difference for antibiotics more than 3 h after ED triage (pooled OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.92–1.46) or more than 1 h after shock recognition (OR 1.46, 0.89–2.40) [[Sterling2015Meta]].
- The PHANTASi trial (Netherlands; 2,672 patients) gave ceftriaxone 2,000 mg in the ambulance a median 26 min before ED arrival. 28-day mortality was 8% in both arms (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.74–1.24) [[Alam2018PHANTASi]].

### firsthour · SEP-1 and mandated sepsis care
- The US Medicare SEP-1 measure has been reported by hospitals since October 2015. Compliance requires every qualifying element: lactate measurement, blood cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics, 30 mL/kg crystalloid, vasopressors and reassessment [[Townsend2022SEP1,Rhee2018SEP1]].
- Townsend 2022 analysed Medicare data from 3,241 hospitals. SEP-1-compliant care was associated with lower 30-day mortality: 21.81% vs 27.48% in the standard match (ARR 5.67%) and 22.22% vs 26.28% in the stringent match (ARR 4.06%) (observational) [[Townsend2022SEP1]].
- Rhee 2018 (851 cases at 7 US hospitals) found 67% failed SEP-1. Crude mortality was higher in failures (18.4% vs 11.0%), but not after adjustment for illness severity (adjusted OR 1.36, 95% CI 0.85–2.18) [[Rhee2018SEP1]].
- In Rhee 2018, the most common reason for failing SEP-1 was a missed 3- or 6-hour lactate measurement (40% of failures). Antibiotic delays over 3 h were linked to death (adjusted OR 1.94) but caused only 15.1% of failures [[Rhee2018SEP1]].
- IDSA's 2021 position paper (with five endorsing societies) recommends limiting SEP-1 to septic shock. It warns that the measure may drive antibiotic overuse and that its "time zero" definition is not evidence-based [[Rhee2021IDSA]].
- New York's 2013 sepsis regulations: in 91,357 hospitalisations, 3-hour bundle compliance rose from 53.4% to 64.7% and risk-adjusted mortality fell from 28.8% to 24.4% [[Levy2018NewYork]].
- A comparative interrupted time series (1,012,410 sepsis admissions; New York vs 4 control states) found adjusted absolute mortality 3.2% (95% CI 1.0–5.4%) lower than expected in New York by the 10th quarter after the regulations [[Kahn2019NYMandate]].

### firsthour · Early goal-directed therapy: from Rivers to PRISM
- Rivers 2001 (263 ED patients with severe sepsis or septic shock at one centre) compared 6 hours of early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) with standard therapy. In-hospital mortality was 30.5% with EGDT and 46.5% with standard therapy (P = 0.009) [[Rivers2001EGDT]].
- ProCESS 2014 (1,341 patients, 31 US EDs) found 60-day in-hospital mortality of 21.0% with protocol EGDT, 18.2% with protocol standard therapy and 18.9% with usual care (RR protocol vs usual 1.04, 95% CI 0.82–1.31) [[ProCESS2014]].
- ARISE 2014 (1,600 patients, 51 centres, mainly Australia/New Zealand) found 90-day mortality of 18.6% with EGDT and 18.8% with usual care (difference −0.3 percentage points, 95% CI −4.1 to 3.6) [[ARISE2014]].
- ProMISe 2015 (1,260 patients, 56 English hospitals) found 90-day mortality of 29.5% with EGDT and 29.2% with usual care (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.85–1.20). EGDT raised costs, and the probability that it was cost-effective was below 20% [[Mouncey2015ProMISe]].
- PRISM 2017 pooled individual data from 3,723 patients at 138 hospitals in ProCESS, ARISE and ProMISe. 90-day mortality was 24.9% with EGDT and 25.4% with usual care (adjusted OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.82–1.14). EGDT increased ICU use and costs [[PRISM2017]].
- PRISM found no benefit of EGDT even in subgroups with worse shock (higher lactate, or hypotension plus hyperlactataemia) [[PRISM2017]].

### maternal · Definitions and burden
- The WHO 2017 consensus definition: "Maternal sepsis is a life-threatening condition defined as organ dysfunction resulting from infection during pregnancy, child-birth, post-abortion, or post-partum period" [[Bonet2017Maternal]].
- WHO's systematic analysis of 60,799 maternal deaths (2003–09) attributed 10.7% (95% UI 5.9–18.6%) of maternal deaths worldwide to sepsis, behind haemorrhage (27.1%) and hypertensive disorders (14.0%) [[Say2014Causes]].
- The GLOSS 1-week inception cohort (2017; 713 facilities in 52 countries; 2,850 women) found 70.4 (95% CI 67.7–73.1) hospitalised women per 1,000 live births had a maternal infection [[GLOSS2020]].
- In GLOSS, 10.9 (9.8–12.0) women per 1,000 live births had infection-related severe maternal outcomes (death or near-miss). Ratios were highest in low- and middle-income countries [[GLOSS2020]].
- In GLOSS, 6.8% of women with severe maternal outcomes died in hospital, and infection-related deaths made up more than half of in-hospital maternal deaths [[GLOSS2020]].
- In GLOSS, only 63.9% of women had a complete set of vital signs recorded, and 70.2% received antimicrobials on the day infection was suspected or diagnosed [[GLOSS2020]].
- In GLOSS's pregnancy-related infections (1,456 women), genital tract (51.2%) and urinary tract (36.5%) were the commonest sources. E. coli caused 87.3% of identified urinary infections, and 13.1% of women did not get antibiotics on the day infection was suspected [[RibeiroDoValle2024GLOSS]].
- UK CMACE enquiry 2006–08: sepsis became "the most common cause of Direct maternal death". The sepsis mortality rate rose from 0.85 to 1.13 per 100,000 maternities, particularly from community-acquired group A streptococcal disease [[Cantwell2011CMACE]].
- The same UK enquiry found substandard care in 70% of Direct deaths and 55% of Indirect deaths [[Cantwell2011CMACE]].
- The UKOSS case-control study (2011–12; 365 cases) found severe maternal sepsis in 4.7 (95% CI 4.2–5.2) per 10,000 maternities. 19.5% developed septic shock, and 1.4% (5 women) died [[Acosta2014UK]].
- Acosta 2014 estimated that for each maternal sepsis death about 50 women have life-threatening sepsis morbidity. Genital tract infection (31.0%) and E. coli (21.1%) were the commonest source and organism [[Acosta2014UK]].
- Risk factors for severe maternal sepsis in Acosta 2014 were febrile illness or antibiotics in the previous 2 weeks (aOR 12.07), caesarean after labour onset (aOR 8.06), pre-labour caesarean (aOR 3.83) and operative vaginal delivery (aOR 2.49). Median time from delivery to sepsis was 3 days [[Acosta2014UK]].
- In US delivery hospitalisations (Nationwide Inpatient Sample 1998–2008; about 45 million deliveries), sepsis complicated 1 in 3,333 deliveries, severe sepsis 1 in 10,823 and sepsis-related death 1 in 105,263. Severe sepsis and sepsis deaths were increasing [[Bauer2013Delivery]].
- In Michigan (1999–2006), sepsis caused 15% (22/151) of pregnancy-related deaths. Delays in appropriate antibiotics occurred in 73% (11/15) of reviewed cases, and delays in escalation of care in 53% [[Bauer2015Michigan]].
- A 2025 review says maternal sepsis rates are increasing and that sepsis now ranks as the second leading cause of preventable maternal mortality [[Yahya2025Maternal]].

### maternal · Group A streptococcus
- In US surveillance (2007–09), postpartum women had invasive group A streptococcus (GAS) at 0.56 per 1,000 woman-years, against 0.019 in non-pregnant women. The authors describe this as a roughly 20-fold higher incidence of GAS and GBS [[Deutscher2011Strep]].
- In UKOSS, GAS infection was associated with progression to septic shock (aOR 4.84, 95% CI 2.17–10.78), as was multiple pregnancy (aOR 5.75) [[Acosta2014UK]].
- For 50% of women with GAS infection in UKOSS, less than 2 hours passed between the first sign of a systemic inflammatory response and a diagnosis of severe sepsis. For 75%, it was less than 9 hours [[Acosta2014UK]].
- Acosta 2014 concludes that signs of severe sepsis in peripartum women, "particularly with confirmed or suspected group A streptococcal infection, should be regarded as an obstetric emergency" [[Acosta2014UK]].
- GAS puerperal sepsis remains an important cause of maternal and infant death worldwide, including in countries with modern antibiotics and intensive care [[Hamilton2013GAS]].

### maternal · Why pregnancy can hide sepsis
- In normal pregnancy, systemic vascular resistance falls by 25–30%, cardiac output rises by about 40%, and maternal heart rate rises by 10–20 bpm [[SomaPillay2016]].
- Blood pressure falls in the first and second trimesters and returns to non-pregnant levels in the third trimester [[SomaPillay2016]].
- Minute ventilation rises by 40–50%, mainly through larger breaths, giving a mild compensated respiratory alkalosis (arterial pH about 7.44) and serum bicarbonate of 18–22 mmol/L [[SomaPillay2016]].
- Plasma volume rises by about 50% by 34 weeks, so haemoglobin falls. In 5–10% of women, platelets fall to 100–150 × 10⁹/L by term with no disease present [[SomaPillay2016]].
- These normal changes overlap with early sepsis signs (fast heart rate, lower blood pressure, faster breathing), which is why obstetric-specific thresholds and scores were developed [[Albright2014SOS,SomaPillay2016]].
- SMFM 2023 recommends that clinicians consider sepsis in pregnant or postpartum patients with otherwise unexplained end-organ damage and a suspected or confirmed infection "regardless of the presence of fever" (GRADE 1C) [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].

### maternal · Obstetric early warning tools
- MEOWS (modified early obstetric warning system) was recommended for all obstetric inpatients by the UK 2003–05 enquiry. In 676 consecutive admissions it was 89% sensitive, 79% specific, with PPV 39% and NPV 98% for morbidity [[Singh2012MEOWS]].
- The US National Partnership for Maternal Safety proposed the Maternal Early Warning Criteria: abnormal parameters that should prompt urgent bedside evaluation by a clinician able to escalate care [[Mhyre2014MEWC]].
- The Sepsis in Obstetrics Score (S.O.S.; maximum 28) was derived in 850 ED patients. A score of 6 or more had sensitivity 88.9%, specificity 95.2%, PPV 16.7% and NPV 99.9% for ICU admission (AUC 0.97) [[Albright2014SOS]].
- In prospective validation (425 pregnant or postpartum women with suspected infection), S.O.S. AUC was 0.85 (95% CI 0.76–0.95). A score of 6 or more had sensitivity 64%, specificity 88% and NPV 98.6% [[Albright2017SOS]].
- In that validation, women with a score of 6 or more had antibiotics started at 3.2 h on average (3.7 h in lower scorers), and only 5.6% received them within 1 hour [[Albright2017SOS]].
- Among 364 women with chorioamnionitis, six published obstetric early warning scores had sensitivities of 40–100% and specificities of 4–97% for severe sepsis. All PPVs were below 15% [[Edwards2015MOEWS]].
- A GLOSS secondary analysis tested 21 early warning systems in 2,560 women from 46 countries. Obstetric- and sepsis-specific scores did better than general ones, but "no score demonstrated enough diagnostic accuracy to be used alone to identify sepsis" [[Chimwaza2025EWS]].
- In that analysis, the NICE risk stratification tool, modified shock index, maternity SIRS and Early Maternal Infection Prompts had high sensitivity (88.1–97.5%). qSOFA in pregnancy and obstetrically modified SOFA had high specificity (90.4–100%) [[Chimwaza2025EWS]].
- The SOMANZ 2023 position statement abandoned qSOFA for diagnosing sepsis in pregnancy because of poor performance in practice. It calls for a single national maternity early warning chart in Australia, as New Zealand already has [[Bowyer2025SOMANZ]].
- SMFM 2023 recommends that institutions develop their own maternal sepsis detection protocols, "avoiding the use of a single screening tool alone" (GRADE 1B) [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].

### maternal · Maternal sepsis bundles and guideline statements (as published)
- SMFM 2023: sepsis and septic shock in pregnancy are medical emergencies, and treatment and resuscitation should begin immediately (Best Practice) [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].
- SMFM 2023 recommends obtaining a serum lactate in pregnant or postpartum patients with suspected sepsis (GRADE 1B) [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].
- SMFM 2023: in pregnant or postpartum patients with septic shock or a high likelihood of sepsis, it recommends empiric broad-spectrum antimicrobials, "ideally within 1 hour of recognition" (GRADE 1C) [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].
- SMFM 2023 recommends early IV administration (within the first 3 hours) of 1 to 2 L of balanced crystalloid in sepsis with hypotension or suspected hypoperfusion (GRADE 1C). This is a smaller fixed volume than the SSC adult 30 mL/kg suggestion [[SMFM2023Sepsis,Evans2021SSC]].
- SMFM 2023 recommends norepinephrine as the first-line vasopressor in pregnancy and postpartum septic shock (GRADE 1C), and pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis in septic shock (GRADE 1B) [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].
- SMFM 2023: if a uterine source is suspected or confirmed, it recommends "prompt delivery or evacuation of uterine contents to achieve source control, regardless of gestational age" (GRADE 1C) [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].
- SMFM 2023 recommends ongoing comprehensive support for pregnant and postpartum sepsis survivors and their families (Best Practice) [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].
- SOMANZ 2017 was the first Australian/New Zealand guideline for sepsis in pregnancy and the postpartum period. It covers bacterial, viral and non-infective causes and obstetric considerations for anaesthesia and intensive care [[Bowyer2017SOMANZ]].
- Fetal considerations: in the S.O.S. derivation cohort, a score of 6 or more was independently associated with fetal tachycardia as well as positive blood cultures and ICU or telemetry admission [[Albright2014SOS]].

### Exam hooks
- Sepsis-3 septic shock = vasopressors needed for MAP ≥65 mm Hg AND lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluids; hospital mortality >40% [[Singer2016Sepsis3]].
- The Hour-1 bundle has five elements: lactate (remeasure if >2), blood cultures before antibiotics, broad-spectrum antibiotics, 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate ≥4, and vasopressors for MAP ≥65 [[Wang2021Hour1,Levy2018Bundle]].
- SSC 2021: antibiotics within 1 h for possible septic shock or high likelihood of sepsis. For possible sepsis without shock, rapid assessment and antibiotics within 3 h if concern persists [[Evans2021SSC]].
- SSC 2021 suggests against PCT to start antibiotics but suggests PCT plus clinical evaluation to stop them [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Kumar 2006: about 7.6% average fall in survival for each hour of delay over the first 6 h of hypotension, from a retrospective cohort [[Kumar2006Delay]].
- Seymour 2017 (New York): OR 1.04 per hour for bundle completion and for antibiotics, but no association for time to fluid bolus [[Seymour2017Timing]].
- Rivers 2001 EGDT cut mortality from 46.5% to 30.5%. ProCESS, ARISE and ProMISe (and the PRISM meta-analysis) found no benefit over usual care [[Rivers2001EGDT,PRISM2017]].
- Blood cultures drawn after antibiotics are positive about 40% less often (31.4% → 19.4%) [[Cheng2019Cultures]].
- Two blood culture sets detect about 90% of bloodstream infections; three detect about 98% [[Lee2007Cultures]].
- An acceptable blood culture contamination rate has traditionally been set at ≤3% [[Sautter2024Contamination]].
- ANDROMEDA-SHOCK: capillary refill vs lactate targeting, 28-day mortality 34.9% vs 43.4%, not significant (P = .06) [[Hernandez2019ANDROMEDA]].
- SSC 2021 recommends against qSOFA as a single screening tool [[Evans2021SSC]].
- WHO 2017 maternal sepsis = organ dysfunction from infection during pregnancy, childbirth, post-abortion or postpartum [[Bonet2017Maternal]].
- GLOSS: 70.4 maternal infections per 1,000 live births among hospitalised women [[GLOSS2020]].
- Sepsis became the leading cause of Direct maternal death in the UK in 2006–08, driven by group A streptococcus [[Cantwell2011CMACE]].
- Postpartum women have about 20-fold higher incidence of invasive GAS than non-pregnant women [[Deutscher2011Strep]].
- In pregnancy, heart rate rises 10–20 bpm and minute ventilation 40–50%, which can mask early sepsis [[SomaPillay2016]].
- SMFM 2023: if the uterus is the source, deliver or evacuate the uterus regardless of gestational age [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].

### In plain words
- Sepsis is the body's harmful reaction to an infection. It can damage organs and needs emergency care [[Singer2016Sepsis3]].
- Lactate is a chemical in the blood. A high level can mean the body's tissues are not getting enough oxygen, and higher levels go with more serious illness [[Shapiro2005Lactate]].
- Doctors take blood cultures (samples to grow germs) before antibiotics if they can do it quickly, because antibiotics make germs harder to find [[Cheng2019Cultures]].
- Antibiotics should not wait for culture results. When shock is possible, guidelines say to give them within an hour [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Procalcitonin is a blood test that helps doctors decide when it may be safe to stop antibiotics [[deJong2016SAPS,Evans2021SSC]].
- Newer gene-based tests can name the germ in hours instead of days [[Banerjee2015rmPCR]].
- In studies, each hour of delay before antibiotics was linked to worse outcomes on average, especially in septic shock. These are population figures, not predictions for one person [[Liu2017Antibiotics,Im2022Antibiotics]].
- Pregnancy changes heart rate, breathing and blood pressure, so the warning signs of sepsis can be easier to miss [[SomaPillay2016]].
- After giving birth, a person has a much higher risk of a serious strep infection. It can get worse within hours [[Deutscher2011Strep,Acosta2014UK]].
- Feeling very unwell with a fever, or without one, after giving birth or a miscarriage needs urgent medical help [[SMFM2023Sepsis]].
- Warning scores help staff notice when someone is getting sicker. No single score is perfect, and they are not for checking yourself at home [[Chimwaza2025EWS]].
- If someone with an infection becomes very unwell, call your emergency number and ask: could this be sepsis?

## Open questions and controversies
- One hour for everyone? SSC 2021 asks for antibiotics within 1 h for possible shock or high likelihood of sepsis [[Evans2021SSC]]. Cohorts show the strongest timing effect in shock and weak or no effect without shock [[Im2022Antibiotics,Liu2017Antibiotics]]. A meta-analysis found no significant benefit at the 1-h or 3-h thresholds [[Sterling2015Meta]], and IDSA argues mandated timing should be limited to septic shock [[Rhee2021IDSA]]. Status: unresolved. SSC 2021 now separates shock from possible sepsis without shock.
- Is the Kumar "7.6% per hour" figure causal? It comes from a retrospective cohort with confounding by indication [[Kumar2006Delay]]. Puskarich found no hourly effect from triage in a protocolised cohort [[Puskarich2011Antibiotics]], and the prehospital antibiotic RCT showed no mortality difference [[Alam2018PHANTASi]]. Status: association is consistent in large cohorts, causal size uncertain.
- Does SEP-1 save lives? Medicare data show lower mortality with compliant care [[Townsend2022SEP1]]. Multicentre chart review found no difference after severity adjustment [[Rhee2018SEP1]]. New York mandate data suggest population-level benefit [[Levy2018NewYork,Kahn2019NYMandate]]. Status: observational evidence only, and it conflicts.
- A fixed 30 mL/kg? SSC 2021 calls it a weak suggestion based on observational data [[Evans2021SSC]]. Time to fluid bolus was not linked to mortality in New York [[Seymour2017Timing]]. SMFM proposes 1–2 L in pregnancy [[SMFM2023Sepsis]]. Status: debated. Fluid choice and volume are covered by pass F.
- Lactate or capillary refill as the resuscitation target? ANDROMEDA-SHOCK did not reach significance on mortality [[Hernandez2019ANDROMEDA]]. ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 showed a win-ratio benefit for a personalised capillary refill protocol, driven by less vital support rather than fewer deaths [[Hernandez2025ANDROMEDA2]]. Status: Early evidence favours capillary-refill-based personalised resuscitation as an adjunct.
- PCT for stopping antibiotics: mortality benefit or just safety? SAPS and the ICU meta-analysis reported lower mortality [[deJong2016SAPS,Wirz2018PCTICU]]. PRORATA and ADAPT-Sepsis showed noninferior mortality with shorter courses [[Bouadma2010PRORATA,Dark2025ADAPT]], and ProACT showed no reduction in antibiotics in US EDs [[Huang2018ProACT]]. Status: PCT safely shortens courses in ICUs. A mortality benefit is uncertain.
- Which obstetric warning score? Scores vary widely in thresholds and accuracy [[Edwards2015MOEWS,Chimwaza2025EWS]]. SOMANZ dropped qSOFA [[Bowyer2025SOMANZ]], and SMFM advises against relying on any single tool [[SMFM2023Sepsis]]. Status: no validated universal tool.
- Rapid molecular diagnostics: does faster identification save lives? The meta-analysis shows lower mortality only with stewardship [[Timbrook2017mRDT]]. The RCT showed better antibiotic use but no mortality difference [[Banerjee2015rmPCR]]. Status: Early evidence. The benefit depends on the stewardship programme around the test.
