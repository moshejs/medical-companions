## Findings

### after · What "after sepsis" looks like: the big picture
- Each year more than 19 million people worldwide develop sepsis and about 14 million survive to hospital discharge (Prescott & Angus review estimate) [[Prescott2018Recovery]].
- Among people discharged after sepsis, about half recover, one-third die during the following year, and one-sixth have severe persistent impairments (review estimate; population statistics) [[Prescott2018Recovery]].
- Impairments after sepsis include an average of 1 to 2 new functional limitations, for example being unable to bathe or dress without help [[Prescott2018Recovery]].
- The review's recommended post-discharge focus has three parts: (1) find new physical, mental and cognitive problems and refer for treatment, (2) review and adjust long-term medicines, (3) look for treatable conditions that often lead to readmission (infection, heart failure, kidney failure, aspiration). It adds that for people whose health was poor or falling before sepsis, focusing on palliation of symptoms may be appropriate [[Prescott2018Recovery]].
- "Post-sepsis syndrome" describes a cluster of long-term effects after sepsis: neurocognitive impairment, functional disability, psychological problems and worsening medical conditions, with higher risk of death and poorer quality of life (Established as a clinical concept; its mechanisms are still being worked out) [[Mostel2019PSS]].
- Post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) was agreed at a 2010 SCCM stakeholders' conference as the term for new or worsening problems in physical, cognitive or mental health that arise after critical illness and persist beyond the hospital stay. The term applies to the survivor or to a family member [[Needham2012PICS]].
- A systematic review found ongoing excess deaths up to 2 years and beyond after the usual 28-day endpoint, plus consistently impaired quality of life in survivors. Its authors warn that 28-day mortality alone can mislead [[Winters2010LongTerm]].

### after · Late deaths
- In US Health and Retirement Study participants aged ≥65 (960 sepsis admissions, 1998–2010), sepsis was linked to a 22.1% (95% CI 17.5–26.7%) absolute increase in late mortality (31 days to 2 years) compared with matched adults not in hospital. The increase was 10.4% (5.4–15.4%) compared with non-sepsis infection admissions and 16.2% (10.2–22.2%) compared with sterile inflammatory admissions [[Prescott2016LateMortality]].
- The same study concluded that more than one in five people who survive sepsis have a late death not explained by their health before sepsis [[Prescott2016LateMortality]].
- Among 94,748 adult sepsis survivors discharged from 192 English critical care units (2009–2014), 15% had died by 1 year after discharge, and 6% to 8% died per year over the following 5 years [[ShankarHari2019LateMortality]].
- In that cohort, age, sex, ethnicity, severe comorbidities, dependency, non-surgical status and site of infection were independently associated with long-term mortality [[ShankarHari2019LateMortality]].
- In the German SMOOTH trial cohort of ICU sepsis survivors (mean age 61.6), overall mortality was 13.7% at 6 months and 18.2% at 12 months after ICU discharge [[Schmidt2016SMOOTH]].

### after · Readmission
- In a meta-analysis of 56 non-randomised studies, mean rehospitalisation after sepsis was 9.3% (8.3–10.3%) by 7 days, 21.4% (17.6–25.4%) by 30 days and 39.0% (22.0–59.4%) by 365 days. Infection was the most common readmission diagnosis [[ShankarHari2020Rehospitalisation]].
- Risk factors for rehospitalisation in that meta-analysis included older age, male sex, comorbidities, non-elective admission, a hospital stay before the sepsis admission, and infection type and illness severity (certainty: moderate for rates, low for risk factors) [[ShankarHari2020Rehospitalisation]].
- Among 2,617 US older adults surviving severe sepsis, 42.7% were rehospitalised within 90 days [[Prescott2015Readmission]].
- Readmission for sepsis was 6.4% after severe sepsis vs 2.8% in matched survivors of other acute medical conditions, and acute renal failure was 3.3% vs 1.2% (both p<0.001). Heart failure readmissions were 5.5% vs 7.8%, so they were common but not higher than in controls [[Prescott2015Readmission]].
- Infection-related readmissions occurred in 11.9% (10.6–13.1%) of severe sepsis survivors vs 8.0% (7.0–9.1%) of matched controls [[Prescott2015Readmission,Prescott2018Recovery]].
- Using an expanded definition, 41.6% of 90-day readmissions after severe sepsis were for ambulatory care-sensitive conditions (potentially treatable outside hospital), vs 37.1% after other conditions [[Prescott2015Readmission]].
- The Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 rationale notes that almost 40% of sepsis survivors are rehospitalised within 3 months, often for preventable conditions [[Evans2021SSC]].

### after · Heart and circulation
- A meta-analysis of 27 observational studies found that, compared with non-sepsis controls, sepsis survivors had higher long-term risk of myocardial infarction (aHR 1.77, 95% CI 1.26–2.48), stroke (aHR 1.67, 1.37–2.05) and heart failure (aHR 1.65, 1.46–1.86). Certainty was low to very low (Early evidence) [[Kosyakovsky2021CV]].
- The pooled cumulative incidence of myocardial infarction, stroke and heart failure in sepsis survivors ranged from 3% to 9% at the longest follow-up, and the excess hazard may persist for at least 5 years [[Kosyakovsky2021CV]].
- The Prescott & Angus review cites adjusted hazard ratios of 1.1–1.4 for new cardiovascular events after sepsis [[Prescott2018Recovery]].

### after · Thinking and memory
- In older Americans (mean age 76.9), the prevalence of moderate to severe cognitive impairment rose 10.6 percentage points after severe sepsis (OR 3.34, 95% CI 1.53–7.25). After non-sepsis hospitalisations it did not change (OR 1.15, 0.80–1.67) [[Iwashyna2010Cognitive]].
- Survivors with no limitations before sepsis developed a mean of 1.57 (0.99–2.15) new functional limitations, vs 0.48 (0.39–0.57) after non-sepsis hospitalisation [[Iwashyna2010Cognitive]].
- The review summarises this as a 3-fold rise in moderate to severe cognitive impairment, from 6.1% before to 16.7% after hospitalisation [[Prescott2018Recovery]].
- In BRAIN-ICU (821 adults with respiratory failure or shock), 40% had global cognition scores 1.5 SD below population means at 3 months (similar to moderate traumatic brain injury) and 26% were 2 SD below (similar to mild Alzheimer's disease). At 12 months the figures were 34% and 24% [[Pandharipande2013BRAINICU]].
- In BRAIN-ICU, delirium developed in 74% during the hospital stay, and longer delirium was independently associated with worse global cognition at 3 and 12 months [[Pandharipande2013BRAINICU]].

### after · Mood, trauma and sleep
- Among sepsis survivors, the review reports anxiety in 32%, depression in 29% and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in 44% [[Prescott2018Recovery]].
- Across ICU survivors, clinically important PTSD symptoms had a pooled prevalence of 25% (18–34%) at 1–6 months and 17% (10–26%) at 7–12 months, using Impact of Event Scale ≥35. With the lower threshold of ≥20, the figures were 44% and 34% [[Parker2015PTSD]].
- PTSD symptoms after ICU were more common with benzodiazepine use and with early memories of frightening ICU experiences [[Parker2015PTSD]].
- Depressive symptoms (HADS-D ≥8) affected 29% of ICU survivors at 2–3 months, 34% at 6 months and 29% at 12–14 months, with little change over the first year [[Rabiee2016Depression]].
- Anxiety symptoms (HADS-A ≥8) affected 32% at 2–3 months, 40% at 6 months and 34% at 12–14 months [[Nikayin2016Anxiety]].
- Abnormal sleep after critical illness was reported in 50–66.7% within 1 month, 34–64.3% at >1–3 months, 22–57% at >3–6 months and 10–61% beyond 6 months. Most studies showed improvement over time [[Altman2017Sleep]].

### after · Muscles, strength and physical function
- ICU-acquired weakness (nerve and/or muscle damage from critical illness) affects limb and breathing muscles. It increases in-ICU, in-hospital and long-term mortality and reduces long-term physical function and quality of life [[Vanhorebeek2020ICUAW]].
- Risk factors include illness severity, organ failure, drugs that harm muscle and nerve, and immobility. Measures shown in RCTs to help prevent it are avoiding hyperglycaemia, not using early parenteral nutrition, and minimising sedation. Evidence for early mobilisation and electrical stimulation is inconsistent [[Vanhorebeek2020ICUAW]].
- Among ARDS survivors 5 years after ICU, the median 6-minute walk distance was 436 m (76% of predicted) and the SF-36 physical score was 41 (age/sex norm 50), so physical function had not returned to normal (ARDS cohort, not sepsis-specific) [[Herridge2011ARDS]].
- In an observational study of about 30,000 sepsis survivors cited by the review, referral to rehabilitation within 90 days was associated with lower 10-year mortality (aHR 0.94, 0.92–0.97) [[Prescott2018Recovery]].

### after · Work and money
- After critical illness, pooled return to employment among previously employed survivors was 33% (21–48%) at 3 months, 55% (45–64%) at 6 months and 56% (45–66%) at 12 months. Returning to work was associated with better quality of life and fewer depressive symptoms [[McPeake2019RTW]].
- Caregivers also reported changes such as reduced working hours and lost earnings [[McPeake2019RTW]].
- In Norway, among 12,260 working-age adults (18–60) employed before a sepsis hospitalisation (2010–2021), 58.6% were working at 6 months, 67.5% at 1 year and 63.4% at 2 years [[Skei2023RTW]].
- Sustainable return to work was less likely after ICU-treated sepsis than non-ICU sepsis (HR 0.56, 0.52–0.61), and the proportion returning to work did not improve over the study decade [[Skei2023RTW]].

### after · Families (PICS-F) and bereavement
- Post-intensive care syndrome-family (PICS-F) covers anxiety, acute stress, post-traumatic stress, depression and complicated grief in relatives. It occurs in parents of newborns and children as well as in families of adults, and may last more than 4 years [[Davidson2012PICSF]].
- 90 days after a relative's ICU stay, 33.1% of family members had post-traumatic stress symptoms consistent with moderate to major PTSD risk. The rate was 48.4% when information felt incomplete, 50% when the relative died in ICU, and 81.8% among those who shared end-of-life decisions [[Azoulay2005Family]].
- In a French RCT among families of patients dying in ICU, a proactive end-of-life conference plus a bereavement brochure lowered PTSD-related symptoms at 90 days (45% vs 69%), anxiety (45% vs 67%) and depression (29% vs 56%) [[Lautrette2007Brochure]].
- How staff communicate with families and include them in care and decisions may affect long-term family outcomes. Proposed supports are visits after discharge, support groups and clinics [[Davidson2012PICSF]].

### after · What helps recovery: trials and guidance
- SMOOTH (Germany, 291 ICU sepsis survivors): a 12-month primary-care intervention with nurse case management did not improve the primary outcome, mental health-related quality of life at 6 months (treatment effect 2.15 SF-36 MCS points, 95% CI −1.79 to 6.09; p=0.28) [[Schmidt2016SMOOTH]].
- IMPACTS (3 North Carolina hospitals, 691 high-risk patients): a 30-day nurse-navigator Sepsis Transition And Recovery programme lowered the composite of 30-day death or readmission from 33.3% to 28.7% (adjusted OR 0.80, 0.64–0.98). Deaths were 9.5% vs 12.0% and rehospitalisations 20.3% vs 24.6% [[Taylor2022IMPACTS]].
- A Cochrane review of ICU follow-up services (5 studies, 1,707 participants) found that they probably make little or no difference to 12-month mortality (RR 0.96, 0.76–1.22; moderate certainty) and may make little or no difference to quality of life or PTSD (low certainty) [[SchofieldRobinson2018Cochrane]].
- ICU diaries given to patients 1 month after ICU discharge reduced new PTSD at 3 months from 13% to 5% (p=0.02; 352 randomised) [[Jones2010Diaries]].
- Clinician teams setting up ICU follow-up clinics and peer support groups found enablers (motivated leaders, sharing survivors' experiences with administrators) and barriers (patient and family access, funding, low awareness) [[Haines2019Thrive]].

### after · Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 statements on recovery (quoted as published)
- Rec 74 (best practice statement): "we recommend discussing goals of care and prognosis with patients and families over no such discussion." Rec 75 (weak, low-quality evidence): addressing goals of care early (within 72 h) over late [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Rec 79 (weak, very low-quality evidence): "For adult survivors of sepsis or septic shock and their families, we suggest referral to peer support groups over no such referral" [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Rec 82 (best practice statement): screening patients and families for economic and social support (housing, nutritional, financial and spiritual) and referring where available [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Rec 83 (weak, very low-quality evidence): offering written and verbal sepsis education (diagnosis, treatment, and post-ICU/post-sepsis syndrome) before hospital discharge and in follow-up [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Rec 84 (best practice statement): giving patients and families the opportunity for shared decision-making in post-ICU and discharge planning [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Recs 85–87: a critical care transition programme on transfer to the ward (weak suggestion); medicine reconciliation at both ICU and hospital discharge (best practice statement); and including information about the ICU stay, sepsis, treatments and common impairments after sepsis in the written and verbal discharge summary (best practice statement) [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Recs 88–89: discharge plans for people with new impairments should include follow-up with clinicians able to manage long-term sequelae (best practice statement). There is insufficient evidence to recommend early over routine post-discharge follow-up [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Recs 91–93: assessment and follow-up for physical, cognitive and emotional problems after discharge (best practice statement); referral to a post-critical illness follow-up programme if available (weak); and for survivors ventilated >48 h or in ICU >72 h, referral to a post-hospital rehabilitation programme (weak, very low-quality evidence) [[Evans2021SSC]].

### children · Long-term outcomes after childhood sepsis
- In the Life After Pediatric Sepsis Evaluation (389 children with community-acquired septic shock, 12 US PICUs), 8%, 11%, 12% and 13% had died by 1, 3, 6 and 12 months after PICU admission [[Zimmerman2020LAPSE]].
- Among surviving children in LAPSE, 50%, 37%, 30% and 35% had not regained their baseline health-related quality of life at 1, 3, 6 and 12 months. In-hospital mortality was 9% [[Zimmerman2020LAPSE]].
- Among 4,528 US children surviving severe sepsis, 18.8% were rehospitalised within 30 days vs 17.1% of matched children after other acute conditions. The commonest reasons were chemotherapy, device complications and sepsis, and only 11.5% were for ambulatory care-sensitive conditions [[Carlton2019PedReadmit]].
- In MOSAIC (245 UK children surviving serogroup B meningococcal disease vs 328 controls), 36% had one or more deficits in physical, cognitive or psychological functioning vs 15% of controls (matched OR 2.7, 1.8–4.1). Major disabling deficits were found in 9% vs 2% (OR 5.0) [[Viner2012MOSAIC]].
- MOSAIC found disabling amputations in 3 of 239 children (1%) after MenB disease vs none of 322 controls. Bilateral hearing loss ≥40 dB was 5% vs <1%, psychological disorders 26% vs 10%, and mean full-scale IQ 99.5 vs 107.2 [[Viner2012MOSAIC]].
- The MOSAIC authors suggest screening all MenB survivors for psychological disorders and cognitive deficits as well as hearing loss [[Viner2012MOSAIC]].
- Under the 2024 Phoenix criteria, children with sepsis had in-hospital mortality of 7.1% in higher-resource settings and 28.5% in lower-resource settings. For septic shock the figures were 10.8% and 33.5% [[Schlapbach2024Phoenix]].

### prevention · Recognising meningococcal disease early (children)
- In 448 UK children with meningococcal disease, most had only non-specific symptoms in the first 4–6 h but were close to death by 24 h [[Thompson2006Meningococcal]].
- The classic signs (haemorrhagic rash, meningism, impaired consciousness) appeared late, at a median onset of 13–22 h [[Thompson2006Meningococcal]].
- 72% of the children had early sepsis signs (leg pains, cold hands and feet, abnormal skin colour) first appearing at a median of 8 h, well before the median hospital admission time of 19 h [[Thompson2006Meningococcal]].
- Only 51% of the children were sent to hospital after the first consultation. The authors urge parents and clinicians to look for the early signs rather than wait for the rash [[Thompson2006Meningococcal]].

### prevention · Pneumococcal vaccines
- CAPiTA (84,496 adults ≥65, Netherlands): PCV13 vs placebo, per-protocol first episodes. Efficacy was 45.6% (95.2% CI 21.8–62.5) against vaccine-type community-acquired pneumonia and 75.0% (41.4–90.8) against vaccine-type invasive pneumococcal disease, over a mean follow-up of 3.97 years [[Bonten2015CAPiTA]].
- CAPiTA showed no efficacy against community-acquired pneumonia from any cause (5.1%, 95% CI −5.1 to 14.2) [[Bonten2015CAPiTA]].
- After PCV7 was introduced for US children in 2000, invasive pneumococcal disease in children <2 years fell 69%, from 188.0 to 59.0 per 100,000 (baseline 1998–99 vs 2001) [[Whitney2003PCV7]].
- Unvaccinated adults also benefited (herd effect). In 2001, rates were 32% lower for ages 20–39, 8% lower for 40–64 and 18% lower for ≥65 (60.1 to 49.5 per 100,000) [[Whitney2003PCV7]].
- In RCTs, pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPV) protected adults against invasive pneumococcal disease (OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.14–0.45). It did not protect against all-cause pneumonia in high-income countries or substantially reduce all-cause mortality (OR 0.90, 0.74–1.09) [[Moberley2013Cochrane]].
- ACIP (2021, published 2022) recommended either PCV20 alone or PCV15 followed by PPSV23 for all adults ≥65 and for adults 19–64 with certain risk conditions who have not had a PCV [[Kobayashi2022ACIP]].
- On 27 June 2024, ACIP added a single dose of PCV21 as an option for adults ≥19 for whom a PCV is recommended (FDA approval 17 June 2024). Indications were unchanged [[Kobayashi2024PCV21]].

### prevention · Hib and meningococcal vaccines
- After Hib conjugate vaccines were licensed in the US (1987 for ≥18 months; 1990 for infants), Hib disease in children <5 fell 71%, from 37 to 11 per 100,000 between 1989 and 1991. H. influenzae meningitis fell 82% between 1985 and 1991 [[Adams1993Hib]].
- England's MenC conjugate programme: vaccine efficacy in vaccinated children was 94% (95% CI 86–97%) in 2001–02. Among unvaccinated children in the catch-up age groups, MenC attack rates fell 67% (52–77%), a herd immunity effect [[Ramsay2003MenC]].
- In 2015 the UK introduced an adolescent MenACWY conjugate programme for 13–18-year-olds, in response to the rapid spread of a hypervirulent MenW clone (ST-11 complex) [[Campbell2015MenW]].
- In September 2015 the UK became the first country to put 4CMenB (Bexsero) into its national infant schedule (2 and 4 months). Two-dose vaccine effectiveness was 82.9% (95% CI 24.1–95.2) against all MenB cases, and cases in eligible infants halved (37 vs an average of 74; IRR 0.50) [[Parikh2016MenB]].
- Over the first 3 years in England, MenB cases in vaccine-eligible cohorts were 63 observed vs 253 expected (IRR 0.25, 0.19–0.36), with a 75% reduction in fully eligible age groups and an estimated 277 cases prevented [[Ladhani2020MenB]].

### prevention · Influenza, COVID-19 and RSV
- ACIP recommends routine annual influenza vaccination for everyone ≥6 months without contraindications. Adults ≥65 should preferentially receive high-dose, recombinant or adjuvanted vaccine (2024–25 season) [[Grohskopf2024ACIP]].
- In the pivotal BNT162b2 trial (43,548 participants ≥16), two doses were 95% effective against laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 (95% credible interval 90.3–97.6). Of 10 severe cases after dose 1, 9 were in the placebo group [[Polack2020BNT162b2]].
- RSVPreF3 OA (adjuvanted, single dose, adults ≥60; 24,966 participants) had 82.6% efficacy against RSV lower respiratory tract disease and 94.1% against severe disease over one season [[Papi2023RSV]].
- Bivalent RSVpreF (adults ≥60; 34,284 participants) had 66.7% efficacy against RSV lower respiratory illness with ≥2 signs and 85.7% with ≥3 signs [[Walsh2023RSV]].
- MATISSE: RSVpreF given at 24–36 weeks of pregnancy was 81.8% effective against medically attended severe RSV lower respiratory illness in infants within 90 days (99.5% CI 40.6–96.3), and 69.4% within 180 days [[Kampmann2023MATISSE]].
- MELODY: one dose of nirsevimab (long-acting monoclonal antibody) in healthy infants born ≥35 weeks was 74.5% effective (49.6–87.1) against medically attended RSV lower respiratory infection through 150 days [[Hammitt2022MELODY]].
- HARMONIE (8,058 infants, France/Germany/UK, pragmatic): nirsevimab reduced RSV lower respiratory infection hospitalisation from 1.5% to 0.3% (efficacy 83.2%, 67.8–92.0) [[Drysdale2023HARMONIE]].

### maternal · Preventing infection around birth
- Without intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis, about 50% of GBS-colonised women pass the bacteria to their newborns, and 1–2% of those newborns develop early-onset GBS disease [[ACOG2020GBS]].
- ACOG (2020) recommends universal GBS screening between 36 0/7 and 37 6/7 weeks. All women with a positive culture should receive intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis unless a prelabour caesarean is done with intact membranes (quoted as published) [[ACOG2020GBS]].
- ACOG notes that prophylaxis for 4 or more hours is more effective than shorter courses, but obstetric interventions should not be delayed only to reach 4 hours [[ACOG2020GBS]].
- As US intrapartum prophylaxis expanded, early-onset GBS disease fell 65%, from 1.7 per 1,000 live births in 1993 to 0.6 per 1,000 in 1998. An estimated 3,900 early-onset infections and 200 newborn deaths were prevented in 1998 [[Schrag2000GBS]].
- A-PLUS (29,278 women in labour, 8 countries): a single oral 2-g dose of azithromycin lowered maternal sepsis or death from 2.4% to 1.6% (RR 0.67, 0.56–0.79). It did not change stillbirth, neonatal death or neonatal sepsis (10.5% vs 10.3%) [[Tita2023APLUS]].
- C/SOAP (2,013 women having non-elective caesarean): adding azithromycin to standard prophylaxis lowered endometritis, wound or other infection from 12.0% to 6.1% (RR 0.51, 0.38–0.68) [[Tita2016CSOAP]].
- ANODE (3,427 UK women after operative vaginal birth): one IV dose of amoxicillin-clavulanate lowered confirmed or suspected infection from 19% to 11% (RR 0.58, 0.49–0.69) [[Knight2019ANODE]].
- The WHO GLOSS 1-week study (713 facilities, 52 countries) found 70.4 hospitalised maternal infections per 1,000 live births and 10.9 infection-related severe maternal outcomes per 1,000 live births. The highest ratios were in low- and middle-income countries [[GLOSS2020]].

### prevention · Infection prevention in hospitals
- In a Geneva teaching hospital (1994–1997), a hospital-wide campaign centred on bedside alcohol hand rub raised hand-hygiene compliance from 48% to 66%. Prevalence of healthcare-associated infection fell from 16.9% to 9.9% (1994–1998) [[Pittet2000HandHygiene]].
- Over the same period, MRSA transmission fell from 2.16 to 0.93 episodes per 10,000 patient-days, and hand-rub use rose from 3.5 to 15.4 L per 1,000 patient-days [[Pittet2000HandHygiene]].
- WHO's "My five moments for hand hygiene" sets out when health workers must clean their hands during care to interrupt transmission. It was developed with a user-centred design approach [[Sax2007FiveMoments]].
- In 103 Michigan ICUs, an evidence-based central-line intervention cut the mean catheter-related bloodstream infection rate from 7.7 to 1.4 per 1,000 catheter-days at 16–18 months (incidence-rate ratio 0.34, 0.23–0.50; up to a 66% reduction) [[Pronovost2006CLABSI]].
- A US national programme to prevent catheter-associated urinary tract infection (926 units) lowered rates in non-ICU wards from 2.28 to 1.54 per 1,000 catheter-days (IRR 0.68). Catheter use fell from 20.1% to 18.8%. ICU rates were largely unchanged [[Saint2016CAUTI]].

### prevention · People at higher risk: neutropenia and no working spleen
- In afebrile neutropenic patients after chemotherapy, antibiotic prophylaxis reduced all-cause mortality (RR 0.66, 0.55–0.79; NNT 34) and infection-related death (RR 0.61) compared with placebo or no treatment, across 109 trials (Established, mostly in blood cancers) [[GafterGvili2012Prophylaxis]].
- In that review, quinolone prophylaxis had fewer side effects leading to discontinuation than trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole [[GafterGvili2012Prophylaxis]].
- ASCO/IDSA (2018) endorse giving people with febrile neutropenia the first dose of empirical antibacterial therapy within 1 hour of triage (quoted as published, not as instructions) [[Taplitz2018FN]].
- The MASCC risk index scores 7 factors at fever onset (burden of illness, no hypotension, no COPD, solid tumour or no prior fungal infection, outpatient status, no dehydration, age <60). A score ≥21 identified low-risk febrile neutropenia with a positive predictive value of 91%, specificity 68% and sensitivity 71% [[Klastersky2000MASCC]].
- In a literature review of 19,680 people after splenectomy (median follow-up 6.9 years), infection occurred in 3.2% and death in 1.4%. The highest risk was in thalassaemia major (infection 8.2%, death 5.1%) and sickle-cell anaemia (7.3%, 4.8%) [[Bisharat2001Splenectomy]].

### prevention · Urinary infection in older adults
- Symptomatic UTI in older women is diagnosed clinically when, without another cause, any 2 of the following are present: fever, worsened urgency or frequency, acute dysuria, suprapubic tenderness, or costovertebral angle pain or tenderness. The diagnosis also needs laboratory evidence [[Mody2014UTI]].
- Asymptomatic bacteriuria in older women is transient, often resolves without treatment, is not linked to morbidity or mortality, and should not be treated [[Mody2014UTI]].
- IDSA (2019) recommends screening for and treating asymptomatic bacteriuria only in pregnancy or before invasive urologic procedures, not in older adults or people with catheters. The guideline notes that unnecessary treatment drives antimicrobial resistance [[Nicolle2019ASB]].

### prevention · Public awareness and equity
- In a 2009 international telephone survey of 6,021 people, on average 88% in Italy, Spain, the UK, France and the US had never heard the term "sepsis" (Germany: 53% knew the word) [[Rubulotta2009Awareness]].
- In an Australian poll of 3,352 parents (2023), 61.6% had heard the term sepsis. Only 27.8% of "sepsis-aware" parents thought they could recognise it in their child, and 37.3% would consider calling an ambulance [[Peters2023Awareness]].
- After WHO's "STOP SEPSIS!" maternal awareness campaign for health workers in 46 countries, the share who had never heard of maternal sepsis fell by 63.4% (pre/post OR 0.35, 0.18–0.68) (Early evidence: before-and-after surveys) [[Brizuela2020STOPSEPSIS]].
- GBD 2017 estimated 48.9 million sepsis cases and 11.0 million sepsis-related deaths worldwide in 2017 (19.7% of all deaths). The highest burden was in sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania and South, East and Southeast Asia [[Rudd2020GBD]].
- Age-standardised sepsis incidence fell 37.0% and mortality 52.8% from 1990 to 2017 [[Rudd2020GBD]].

### Exam hooks
- PICS = new or worsening physical, cognitive or mental health problems after critical illness that persist beyond hospital, and PICS-F applies to family members [[Needham2012PICS]].
- After sepsis discharge: about half recover, one-third die within a year, one-sixth have severe persistent impairment [[Prescott2018Recovery]].
- Iwashyna 2010: severe sepsis raised moderate to severe cognitive impairment (OR 3.34) and added about 1.5 new functional limitations [[Iwashyna2010Cognitive]].
- About 40% of sepsis survivors are readmitted within 90 days, and infection is the commonest readmission diagnosis [[Prescott2015Readmission,ShankarHari2020Rehospitalisation]].
- More than 1 in 5 older sepsis survivors has a late death not explained by pre-sepsis health (22.1% absolute excess) [[Prescott2016LateMortality]].
- Sepsis survivors have higher long-term risk of MI (aHR 1.77), stroke (1.67) and heart failure (1.65) [[Kosyakovsky2021CV]].
- SMOOTH was negative on its primary outcome (mental HRQoL at 6 months) [[Schmidt2016SMOOTH]].
- ICU diaries reduced new PTSD from 13% to 5% [[Jones2010Diaries]].
- SSC 2021 suggests a post-hospital rehabilitation programme for survivors ventilated >48 h or in ICU >72 h [[Evans2021SSC]].
- Meningococcal disease: leg pain, cold hands and feet and abnormal skin colour appear at a median of about 8 h, while the rash appears late (13–22 h) [[Thompson2006Meningococcal]].
- CAPiTA: PCV13 prevented vaccine-type pneumonia (45.6%) and IPD (75.0%) in adults ≥65, but not all-cause pneumonia [[Bonten2015CAPiTA]].
- PCV7 in infants cut IPD in under-2s by 69% and lowered adult rates through herd protection [[Whitney2003PCV7]].
- UK 4CMenB: 2-dose effectiveness 82.9%, and MenB cases in eligible infants fell by 75% over 3 years [[Parikh2016MenB,Ladhani2020MenB]].
- Intrapartum GBS prophylaxis: early-onset GBS fell from 1.7 to 0.6 per 1,000 live births (1993–1998) [[Schrag2000GBS]].
- A-PLUS: intrapartum oral azithromycin lowered maternal sepsis or death (RR 0.67) but not neonatal sepsis [[Tita2023APLUS]].
- Pronovost checklist bundle: CLABSI fell from 7.7 to 1.4 per 1,000 catheter-days [[Pronovost2006CLABSI]].
- MASCC ≥21 = low-risk febrile neutropenia [[Klastersky2000MASCC]].
- Do not treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in older adults [[Nicolle2019ASB,Mody2014UTI]].

### In plain words
- Many people get better after sepsis, but recovery can take months, and it is common to feel tired, weak, forgetful or low for a while [[Prescott2018Recovery]].
- These after-effects have a name, post-sepsis syndrome, and having them is not a sign of weakness or failure [[Mostel2019PSS]].
- About 4 in 10 people go back into hospital within 3 months, often because of another infection. Knowing the warning signs and getting help early matters [[Prescott2015Readmission,Evans2021SSC]].
- Bad dreams, flashbacks, worry and low mood are common after intensive care and can be treated. Tell your doctor or nurse [[Parker2015PTSD,Rabiee2016Depression]].
- Families feel it too. Many relatives have stress, anxiety or sadness after a loved one's time in intensive care, and support is available [[Azoulay2005Family,Davidson2012PICSF]].
- A diary of the intensive care stay, written by staff and family, can help people make sense of what happened [[Jones2010Diaries]].
- Going back to work often takes time, and about half of people are working again by 6 months to a year [[McPeake2019RTW,Skei2023RTW]].
- Before you leave hospital, you can ask for written information about sepsis, your treatment, and what problems to look out for [[Evans2021SSC]].
- In children, early signs of meningococcal sepsis can be leg pain, cold hands and feet, and pale or blotchy skin, often hours before any rash [[Thompson2006Meningococcal]].
- Vaccines against pneumococcus, Hib, meningococcus, flu, COVID-19 and RSV prevent many of the infections that can lead to sepsis [[Whitney2003PCV7,Adams1993Hib,Ladhani2020MenB,Papi2023RSV]].
- Clean hands, in hospital and at home, stop germs from spreading [[Pittet2000HandHygiene]].
- People having chemotherapy, and people without a working spleen, are at higher risk. They should know who to call straight away if they get a fever [[Taplitz2018FN,Bisharat2001Splenectomy]].

## Open questions and controversies
- Do post-sepsis follow-up programmes work? SMOOTH (primary-care case management) was negative on mental HRQoL [[Schmidt2016SMOOTH]], and the Cochrane review found little or no effect of ICU follow-up services on mortality or quality of life [[SchofieldRobinson2018Cochrane]]. The 30-day nurse-navigator programme in IMPACTS reduced the composite of death or readmission [[Taylor2022IMPACTS]]. SSC 2021 gives only weak, very-low-certainty suggestions for follow-up and rehabilitation programmes [[Evans2021SSC]]. Status: unresolved, and the "dose" and target group are unclear.
- Is late harm caused by sepsis itself or by poor health before it? Matched analyses attribute a 10–22% absolute late-mortality excess to sepsis [[Prescott2016LateMortality]]. Cardiovascular associations are of low to very low certainty because of residual confounding [[Kosyakovsky2021CV]]. Status: causal mechanisms not established (Hypothesis: persistent inflammation, immune dysfunction, residual organ damage).
- Heart failure after sepsis: the review lists heart failure among common readmissions (5.5%) [[Prescott2018Recovery]], but in matched comparison it was not more frequent than after other acute admissions (5.5% vs 7.8%) [[Prescott2015Readmission]]. Readmission for sepsis (6.4% vs 2.8%) and acute renal failure (3.3% vs 1.2%) were the clear excesses. Status: the wording matters for teaching.
- PTSD prevalence depends on the threshold. After ICU it is 25% vs 44% at 1–6 months with IES ≥35 vs ≥20 [[Parker2015PTSD]], and the review quotes 44% for sepsis survivors [[Prescott2018Recovery]]. Status: the builders should show the threshold beside any PTSD figure.
- 4CMenB effectiveness: early two-dose effectiveness was 82.9% with wide CIs [[Parikh2016MenB]], while 3-year adjusted effectiveness estimates were 52.7–59.1% with CIs crossing zero, even though cases fell 75% [[Ladhani2020MenB]]. Status: the population impact is clear, and the individual-level estimate is imprecise.
- Pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine prevents invasive disease, but RCTs show no clear effect on all-cause pneumonia in high-income settings or on mortality [[Moberley2013Cochrane]]. Similarly, PCV13 did not reduce all-cause pneumonia in CAPiTA [[Bonten2015CAPiTA]]. Status: settled for IPD, uncertain for broader outcomes.
- Intrapartum azithromycin reduces maternal sepsis [[Tita2023APLUS]], but there are stewardship concerns about mass antibiotic use (Hypothesis: resistance selection). Status: guideline uptake varies by setting.
- Do public awareness campaigns save lives? The evidence is mostly surveys of knowledge [[Rubulotta2009Awareness,Peters2023Awareness]] and before-and-after campaign recognition [[Brizuela2020STOPSEPSIS]]. No outcome trials were found in this pass. Status: Early evidence.
