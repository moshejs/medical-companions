# A · Epidemiology, burden, risk factors & history

Pass date: 2026-11-04. Modules served: what, risk, history, home.

This pass covers how common sepsis (the body's harmful response to an infection) is worldwide and in high-income countries, who gets it, how often it kills, what it costs, which infections and germs cause it, the risk factors, public-facing warning-sign messages, and the history of the idea from ancient Greece to the 2024 Phoenix paediatric criteria. Every mortality, incidence and cost figure below is a population statistic from a named study population. None of them predicts what will happen to one person.

Access notes:
- Numbers come from PubMed/Europe PMC abstracts. Where the abstract lacked a figure, I read the open full text through Europe PMC or PMC: Rudd 2020 (PMC6970225), Vincent 2022 (PMC9686931), Stang 2022 (PMC9209376), Prescott 2023 (PMC10877561) and Slade 2003 (PMC154124).
- HCUP cost figures were read from AHRQ's own web editions of Statistical Briefs #204 and #261. The PubMed Bookshelf records were validated, but the NCBI Bookshelf page served a CAPTCHA, which I did not attempt.

## Findings

### what · Global burden (GBD 2017)
- In 2017 there were an estimated 48.9 million incident cases of sepsis worldwide (95% uncertainty interval [UI] 38.9–62.9 million) and 11.0 million sepsis-related deaths (10.1–12.0 million), representing 19.7% (18.2–21.4%) of all global deaths. (Established) [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Global incident cases fell from 60.2 million (47.2–79.7) in 1990 to 48.9 million in 2017, a decrease of 18.8% [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Sepsis-related deaths fell from 15.7 million (14.7–16.7) in 1990 to 11.0 million in 2017, a decrease of 29.7% (22.1–36.6%) [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Age-standardised sepsis incidence fell from 1074.7 to 677.5 cases per 100,000 between 1990 and 2017 (−37.0%; 95% UI 11.8–54.5). Age-standardised mortality fell by 52.8% (47.7–57.5%), to 148.1 deaths per 100,000 in 2017 [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- The age-standardised share of all global deaths related to sepsis declined from 29.1% (27.2–31.4%) in 1990 to 20.1% (18.5–21.8%) in 2017 [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- In 2017, 20.3 million incident cases (14.0–29.7) occurred in children younger than 5 years, 4.9 million in those aged 5–19 years and 23.7 million in adults aged 20 years or older. The authors note that more than half of all cases occurred among children, many of them neonates [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Sepsis-related deaths in 2017 were 2.9 million (2.6–3.2) in children under 5, 454,000 at ages 5–19, and 7.7 million (6.9–8.5) in adults aged 20 or older [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Incidence peaked in early childhood, with a second peak among older adults [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Burden was highest in sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania, south Asia, east Asia and southeast Asia. In 2017, 85.0% (82.2–87.4%) of incident cases (41.5 million) occurred in countries with a low, low-middle or middle Socio-demographic Index [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Age-standardised incidence in 2017 was higher in females (716.5 vs 642.8 per 100,000), but age-standardised sepsis-related mortality was higher in males (164.2 vs 134.1 per 100,000) [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Of the 2017 cases, 33.1 million (67.4%) occurred in people with an underlying infectious cause and 15.8 million (32.6%) in people with underlying injuries or non-communicable diseases. Of the deaths, 5.11 million (46.4%) occurred in people with a non-infectious underlying cause of death [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Lower respiratory infection was the most common underlying cause of sepsis-related death in every year from 1990 to 2017: 1.8 million (1.3–2.1) such deaths in 2017. Road injury was the leading injury-related cause and maternal disorders the leading non-communicable cause of sepsis cases (5.7 million cases) [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Among children under 5 in 2017, the commonest causes of sepsis were diarrhoeal diseases (5.9 million cases; 27.9%), neonatal disorders (5.1 million; 25.7%) and lower respiratory infections (3.3 million; 16.5%) [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- The GBD estimate is about twice an earlier extrapolation from high-income-country hospital data, which suggested 31.5 million sepsis cases, 19.4 million severe sepsis cases and potentially 5.3 million deaths a year. That extrapolation was described as tentative because there were no population-level data from lower-income countries [[Fleischmann2016AJRCCM,Rudd2020GBD]]
- In GBD 2019, an estimated 7.7 million deaths (5.7–10.2) were associated with 33 bacterial pathogens. These made up 13.6% of all global deaths and 56.2% (52.1–60.1%) of all sepsis-related deaths [[Ikuta2022Pathogens]]
- Five pathogens (*Staphylococcus aureus*, *Escherichia coli*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*) caused 54.9% of the deaths among the bacteria studied. Infection-related deaths in 2019 totalled an estimated 13.7 million [[Ikuta2022Pathogens]]
- An estimated 4.95 million deaths (3.62–6.57) were associated with bacterial antimicrobial resistance in 2019, including 1.27 million (0.911–1.71) attributable to it. The attributable death rate was highest in western sub-Saharan Africa (27.3 per 100,000) and lowest in Australasia (6.5 per 100,000) [[Murray2022GRAM]]

### what · Hospital-treated sepsis in high-income settings
- A 2020 meta-analysis pooled 189 hospital-treated sepsis cases per 100,000 person-years (95% CI 133–267), with 26.7% (22.9–30.7%) dying. ICU-treated sepsis was 58 per 100,000 person-years (42–81), with 41.9% (36.2–47.7%) dying before hospital discharge. These were mostly administrative-data studies [[FleischmannStruzek2020ICM]]
- The same meta-analysis found about 50% lower hospital-treated incidence than the GBD/IHME model. Incidence was 46% higher in studies after 2008 than over the whole time frame [[FleischmannStruzek2020ICM]]
- An earlier meta-analysis of 7 high-income countries found 288 hospital-treated sepsis cases and 148 severe-sepsis cases per 100,000 person-years. In the last decade studied, the figures were 437 and 270 per 100,000, with hospital mortality of 17% (sepsis) and 26% (severe sepsis) [[Fleischmann2016AJRCCM]]
- In a Swedish population study that reviewed every adult started on intravenous antibiotics, annual incidence was 780 per 100,000 by Sepsis-3 and 687 per 100,000 by the 1991/2001 severe-sepsis definition. This is higher than most previous estimates [[Mellhammar2016Sweden]]
- In US EHR data from 409 hospitals (2014), sepsis was present in 6.0% of adult admissions (173,690 of 2,901,019). Of these patients, 15.0% died in hospital and 6.2% were discharged to hospice [[Rhee2017JAMA]]
- From 2009 to 2014, US sepsis incidence by clinical criteria was stable (+0.6%/year; 95% CI −2.3 to 3.5%). By claims it rose 10.3%/year. Claims also showed a steeper fall in mortality (−7.0%/year vs −3.3%/year clinically) [[Rhee2017JAMA]]
- Clinical EHR criteria were more sensitive than claims for sepsis (69.7% vs 32.3%), with similar positive predictive value (70.4% vs 75.2%) [[Rhee2017JAMA]]
- A CDC-authored viewpoint states that in the US, sepsis contributes to about 1.7 million adult hospitalisations and more than a third of all hospital deaths [[Prescott2023CoreElements]]
- In a record review of 568 adults who died in hospital or went to hospice at 6 US hospitals, sepsis was present in 52.8% (95% CI 48.6–57.0%) and was the immediate cause of death in 34.9% [[Rhee2019Mortality]]
- In that cohort, the commonest underlying causes of death in people with sepsis were solid cancer (21.0%), chronic heart disease (15.3%), haematologic cancer (10.3%), dementia (9.7%) and chronic lung disease (9.0%). Hospice-qualifying conditions were present on admission in 40.3% [[Rhee2019Mortality]]
- Suboptimal care (most often delayed antibiotics) was found in 22.7% of sepsis-associated deaths. Reviewers judged only 3.7% definitely or moderately likely preventable, and another 8.3% possibly preventable [[Rhee2019Mortality]]
- A landmark US analysis of 1995 discharge data estimated 751,000 severe-sepsis cases a year (3.0 per 1,000 population; 2.26 per 100 discharges). Mortality was 28.6% (215,000 deaths), "as many deaths annually as those from acute myocardial infarction" [[Angus2001CCM]]
- In that analysis, 51.1% of cases received intensive care. The projected incidence increase was 1.5% a year [[Angus2001CCM]]
- From 1979 to 2000, US sepsis cases rose from about 164,000 (82.7 per 100,000) to nearly 660,000 (240.4 per 100,000), an annualised increase of 8.7% [[Martin2003NEJM]]
- Over the same period, in-hospital mortality fell from 27.8% (1979–1984) to 17.9% (1995–2000), yet total deaths kept rising [[Martin2003NEJM]]
- Four published US claims methods applied to the same 2004–2009 data gave annual severe-sepsis incidence ranging 3.5-fold, from 894,013 (300 per 100,000) to 3,110,630 (1,031 per 100,000). In-hospital mortality ranged from 14.7% to 29.9% [[Gaieski2013Benchmark]]
- In Australia and New Zealand ICUs (101,064 patients), absolute severe-sepsis mortality fell from 35.0% in 2000 to 18.4% in 2012, a relative risk reduction of 47.5%. In patients without comorbidities or older age, mortality was below 5% [[Kaukonen2014JAMA]]
- Across 36 multicentre trials, usual-care 28-day severe-sepsis mortality fell from 46.9% (1991–1995) to 29% (2006–2009), about 3.0% a year. This trend matched administrative data [[Stevenson2014Trends]]
- Hospital-onset sepsis made up 12.1% of sepsis cases (0.5% of all hospitalisations) in 136 US hospitals. In-hospital mortality was 33% vs 17% for community-onset sepsis (adjusted OR 2.1; 95% CI 2.0–2.2) [[Rhee2019HospOnset]]
- In a US multicentre cohort of 6,404 sepsis encounters, 35.9% were hospital-onset [[Baghdadi2020HospOnset]]
- In a CDC-led review of 1,078 adults with sepsis, 90.3% had community-onset sepsis (diagnosed within 3 days of admission). 60.7% had healthcare exposures before admission, and 41.5% had received treatment such as antimicrobials, chemotherapy, wound care, dialysis or surgery in the prior 30 days [[Fay2020Exposures]]
- In a CDC chart review in 4 New York hospitals (246 adults, 79 children), 72% of patients had a healthcare factor in the prior 30 days or a chronic condition needing frequent care. Pneumonia was the most common infection leading to sepsis, and 25% of patients died [[Novosad2016MMWR]]

### what · Sepsis in intensive care units
- In the 2002 European SOAP study (198 ICUs, 24 countries), 37.4% of 3,147 adult ICU patients had sepsis and 24.7% had it on admission. Sites were lung (68%) and abdomen (22%), and cultures were positive in 60% [[Vincent2006SOAP]]
- The commonest SOAP organisms were *S. aureus* (30%, including 14% methicillin-resistant), *Pseudomonas* species (14%) and *E. coli* (13%) [[Vincent2006SOAP]]
- In the 2012 ICON audit (10,069 ICU patients, worldwide), sepsis occurred during the ICU stay in 29.5%. ICU mortality was 25.8% and hospital mortality 35.3%, ranging from 19.3% hospital mortality in Oceania to 47.2% in Africa [[Sakr2018ICON]]
- In the 2017 EPIC III point-prevalence study (15,202 ICU patients, 88 countries), 54% had suspected or proven infection and 70% received at least one antibiotic. In-hospital mortality among infected patients was 30% [[Vincent2020EPICIII]]
- Among EPIC III patients with a positive culture, gram-negative organisms were found in 67%, gram-positive in 37% and fungi in 16%. ICU-acquired infection carried higher adjusted mortality than community-acquired infection (OR 1.32; 95% CI 1.10–1.60) [[Vincent2020EPICIII]]
- In England, Wales and Northern Ireland critical care (ICNARC, 1996–2004), 27.0% of admissions had severe sepsis in the first 24 hours. Hospital mortality for these admissions fell from 48.3% to 44.7%, while estimated deaths rose from 9,000 to 14,000 a year [[Harrison2006ICNARC]]
- ICNARC treated incidence of severe sepsis in critical care rose from 46 to 66 per 100,000 population (1996 to 2003) [[Harrison2006ICNARC]]
- A UK emergency-medicine study stated, in its background, that severe sepsis "is likely to account for around 37,000 deaths annually in the UK". This is an estimate quoted by the authors, not measured in the study [[Daniels2011SepsisSix]]

### what · Septic shock outcomes (population statistics)
- In the Sepsis-3 systematic review (44 studies; 166,479 patients), crude septic-shock mortality was 46.5% (95% CI 42.7–50.3%) [[ShankarHari2016Shock]]
- People needing vasopressors to keep mean arterial pressure at or above 65 mm Hg, with lactate above 2 mmol/L after fluids, had 42.3% hospital mortality (95% CI 41.2–43.3%). This combination became the Sepsis-3 septic-shock criterion, "associated with hospital mortality rates greater than 40%" [[ShankarHari2016Shock,Singer2016Sepsis3]]
- A 2019 meta-analysis (Europe and North America, 71 studies) found septic shock in 10.4% of ICU admissions. Hospital mortality was 39.0% (95% CI 34.4–43.9%) and 28-/30-day mortality 36.7% [[Vincent2019Shock]]
- A 2020 meta-analysis (170 studies, 2009–2019) found 30-day septic-shock mortality of 34.7% and 90-day mortality of 38.5%. For sepsis, 30-day mortality was 24.4% and 90-day 32.2%. Each 1-point higher average SOFA score was associated with 1.8–3.3% higher mortality [[Bauer2020Mortality]]
- In US Premier data (2.57 million sepsis cases, 2010–2016), mortality was 5.6% for sepsis without organ dysfunction, 14.9% for severe sepsis and 34.2% for septic shock [[Paoli2018Costs]]
- In Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries, 6-month mortality after a sepsis admission was about 60% for septic shock, 36% for severe sepsis, 31% for organism-specified sepsis and 27% for unspecified sepsis [[Buchman2020Medicare]]

### what · Children and newborns
- A 2018 review estimated 48 sepsis cases (95% CI 27–86) and 22 severe-sepsis cases per 100,000 child-years, and 2,202 neonatal sepsis cases per 100,000 live births. Extrapolated globally, that is about 3.0 million neonatal and 1.2 million paediatric cases a year [[FleischmannStruzek2018Paeds]]
- A 2021 neonatal meta-analysis estimated 2,824 cases per 100,000 live births (95% CI 1,892–4,194), with 17.6% dying. Incidence was 3,930 per 100,000 in 2009–2018, based on 4 low- and middle-income-country studies [[Fleischmann2021Neonatal]]
- In the SPROUT point-prevalence study (128 PICUs, 26 countries), 8.2% of PICU patients had severe sepsis. Hospital mortality was 25% and did not differ by age or between developed and resource-limited countries [[Weiss2015SPROUT]]
- In SPROUT, the commonest infection sites were respiratory (40%) and bloodstream (19%). 67% of patients had multiorgan dysfunction at recognition, and 17% of survivors developed at least moderate disability [[Weiss2015SPROUT]]
- In 7 US states, paediatric severe-sepsis prevalence rose from 0.56 to 0.89 per 1,000 children (1995–2005). Newborn prevalence more than doubled, from 4.5 to 9.7 per 1,000 births, and case fatality fell from 10.3% to 8.9% [[Hartman2013Paeds]]
- Under the Phoenix criteria, children with sepsis (Phoenix Sepsis Score ≥2) had in-hospital mortality of 7.1% in higher-resource and 28.5% in lower-resource settings. For septic shock the figures were 10.8% and 33.5% [[Schlapbach2024Phoenix]]

### what · Sources of infection, organisms and culture-negative sepsis
- Pneumonia was the most common infection leading to sepsis in the CDC New York review. The commonest blood-culture organisms were *E. coli* in adults, *Klebsiella* in children aged 1 year or older, and *Enterococcus* in infants under 1 year. No pathogen was isolated in 33% of patients [[Novosad2016MMWR]]
- In the CDC multistate cohort, a sepsis-associated pathogen was found in 56.9% of patients. The commonest were *E. coli*, *S. aureus*, *K. pneumoniae* and *Clostridioides difficile* [[Fay2020Exposures]]
- Respiratory sources were more common in men than women (36% vs 29%), and genitourinary sources more common in women (35% vs 27%) (US discharge data 1979–2003) [[Esper2006Disparities]]
- After 1987, gram-positive bacteria became the predominant pathogens in US sepsis. Fungal sepsis rose 207% between 1979 and 2000 [[Martin2003NEJM]]
- Lung is the most frequent ICU sepsis source (68% in SOAP), followed by abdomen (22%) [[Vincent2006SOAP]]
- In a Singapore medical ICU (1,001 severe-sepsis patients), 41.5% were culture-negative. Culture-negative patients were less ill and had lower hospital mortality (35.9% vs 44.0%) [[Phua2013CultureNeg]]
- In US national data (6.8 million severe-sepsis admissions, 2000–2010), 47.1% were culture-negative, and the proportion rose from 33.9% to 43.5%. In this dataset, culture-negative status was linked with higher mortality (34.6% vs 22.7%; OR 1.75). This is the opposite direction to the Singapore cohort [[Gupta2016CultureNeg,Phua2013CultureNeg]]

### what · Costs
- Septicaemia was the most expensive condition treated in US hospitals in 2013: $23.7 billion, 6.2% of aggregate costs, across 1.30 million stays [[Torio2016HCUP]]
- In 2017 it was again the most expensive condition: $38.2 billion, 8.8% of aggregate hospital costs, across 2.09 million stays (5.8% of all stays) [[Liang2020HCUP]]
- In 1995, average US severe-sepsis cost per case was $22,100, with $16.7 billion in annual total costs nationally [[Angus2001CCM]]
- Average cost per case rose with severity: $16,324 for sepsis without organ dysfunction, $24,638 for severe sepsis and $38,298 for septic shock. Cases not present at admission cost $51,022 vs $18,023 for those present on admission (Premier 2010–2016) [[Paoli2018Costs]]
- Medicare inpatient plus skilled-nursing costs for sepsis rose from an estimated $27.7 billion (2012) to $41.5 billion (2018). Fee-for-service sepsis admissions rose from 811,644 to 1,136,889 [[Buchman2020Medicare]]
- US paediatric severe-sepsis hospitalisations in 2005 numbered 75,255, at a cost of $4.8 billion [[Hartman2013Paeds]]

### risk · Age
- Incidence of severe sepsis rose more than 100-fold with age, from 0.2 per 1,000 in children to 26.2 per 1,000 in people over 85. Mortality rose from 10% in children to 38.4% in people over 85 (US, 1995) [[Angus2001CCM]]
- Adults aged 65 or older were 12% of the US population but 64.9% of sepsis cases, a relative risk of 13.1 (95% CI 12.6–13.6) vs younger adults. Age independently predicted death (OR 2.26) [[Martin2006Age]]
- Globally, incidence peaks in early childhood and again in older adults [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Neonates carry the highest population incidence of any age group, about 2,824 per 100,000 live births [[Fleischmann2021Neonatal]]

### risk · Chronic disease and frailty
- In the community-based REGARDS cohort (30,239 adults), baseline conditions predicting later sepsis included chronic lung disease (adjusted HR 2.43), peripheral artery disease (2.16), chronic kidney disease (1.99), myocardial infarction (1.79), diabetes (1.78), stroke (1.67) and deep vein thrombosis (1.63) [[Wang2012Chronic]]
- Sepsis risk in REGARDS increased with the number of chronic conditions. Tobacco use, alcohol use, lower education and lower income were also associated [[Wang2012Chronic]]
- Frailty (at least 2 of weakness, exhaustion and low physical activity) was present in 19.9% of REGARDS participants. It was associated with higher sepsis risk (adjusted HR 1.44; 95% CI 1.26–1.64) and higher 30-day sepsis case fatality (adjusted OR 1.62) [[Mahalingam2019Frailty]]
- People with a history of cancer had sepsis incidence of 1,465 per 100,000 a year, a relative risk of 9.77 vs people without cancer. Cancer independently predicted death from sepsis (adjusted OR 1.98) [[Danai2006Cancer]]
- In adults with cancer hospitalised for febrile neutropenia (low white cells with fever after chemotherapy), in-hospital mortality was 9.5% overall. It was 2.6% with no major comorbidity, 10.3% with one and at least 21.4% with more than one [[Kuderer2006FN]]
- In cirrhosis (advanced liver scarring), infections increased mortality about 4-fold (pooled OR 3.75). About 30% of patients died within 1 month of infection and another 30% by 1 year [[Arvaniti2010Gastro]]
- Among adults with sepsis, cirrhosis (OR 3.59) and immunosuppression (OR 2.52) were associated with higher 30-day mortality [[Fay2020Exposures]]
- In the ICON audit, cancer, advanced heart failure and cirrhosis were among the independent risk factors for in-hospital death in ICU sepsis [[Sakr2018ICON]]

### risk · Immunosuppression, asplenia and medicines
- Baseline oral or injected steroid use (2.24% of REGARDS) was associated with a higher risk of infection (adjusted HR 2.10) and with higher odds of sepsis once infected (adjusted OR 2.11; 95% CI 1.33–3.36) [[Chaudhary2017Steroids]]
- After splenectomy (removal of the spleen; 19,680 patients, median follow-up 6.9 years), infection incidence was 3.2% and mortality 1.4%. Rates were highest in thalassaemia major (8.2% infection, 5.1% death) and sickle-cell anaemia (7.3%, 4.8%) [[Bisharat2001Splenectomy]]
- In a Thai emergency department cohort, patients with no "SEPSIS"-mnemonic symptoms more often had underlying immunosuppression (24.6% vs 8.3%). This suggests immunosuppressed people may present vaguely [[Wattanapaiboon2020SEPSIS]]

### risk · Pregnancy and the postpartum period
- Sepsis caused 10.7% (95% UI 5.9–18.6%) of maternal deaths worldwide in 2003–2009, about 261,000 deaths. It was the third direct cause after haemorrhage and hypertensive disorders [[Say2014Causes]]
- In the WHO GLOSS study (713 facilities, 52 countries), 70.4 hospitalised women per 1,000 live births had a maternal infection. 10.9 per 1,000 had infection-related severe maternal outcomes, highest in low- and middle-income countries [[GLOSS2020]]
- UK severe maternal sepsis incidence was 4.7 per 10,000 maternities (2011–2012). 19.5% developed septic shock and 1.4% died. The authors estimate about 50 women with life-threatening sepsis morbidity for each maternal sepsis death [[Acosta2014UK]]
- UK maternal sepsis risk factors included febrile illness or antibiotics in the prior 2 weeks (aOR 12.07), caesarean after labour onset (aOR 8.06), pre-labour caesarean (aOR 3.83), operative vaginal delivery (aOR 2.49), black or other minority ethnicity (aOR 1.82) and primiparity (aOR 1.60) [[Acosta2014UK]]
- In that study, group A streptococcal infection was associated with progression to septic shock (aOR 4.84). For half of the women with it, there was less than 2 hours between the first systemic sign and a diagnosis of severe sepsis [[Acosta2014UK]]

### risk · Healthcare exposure, devices and hospital-onset sepsis
- Most sepsis begins outside hospital. Even so, 60.7% of adults had recent healthcare exposures, including 24.1% with an outpatient visit in the prior 7 days [[Fay2020Exposures]]
- Hospital-onset sepsis affected about 1 in 200 hospitalisations. These patients had more comorbidities, higher SOFA scores and twice the adjusted mortality of community-onset sepsis [[Rhee2019HospOnset]]
- Sepsis not present at admission was nearly three times as costly per case ($51,022 vs $18,023) [[Paoli2018Costs]]

### risk · Race, ethnicity and social deprivation
- US Black patients had a 67% higher age- and sex-standardised severe-sepsis rate than white patients (9.4 vs 5.6 per 1,000) and 80% higher standardised mortality (1.8 vs 1.0 per 1,000) [[Mayr2010Race]]
- That gap was explained by both a higher infection rate (incidence rate ratio 1.39) and a higher risk of acute organ dysfunction once infected (OR 1.29, attenuated to 1.14 after adjustment for comorbidity, poverty and hospital) [[Mayr2010Race]]
- In 6 US states, standardised severe-sepsis incidence was 6.08 per 1,000 in Black, 4.06 in Hispanic and 3.58 in white residents. After adjustment for regional poverty, Black residents still had higher incidence (adjusted rate ratio 1.44) [[Barnato2008Race]]
- From 1979 to 2000, sepsis was more common in men (RR 1.28) and in non-white people (RR 1.90). Mortality was highest among Black men [[Martin2003NEJM]]
- Higher neighbourhood socioeconomic status was linked with fewer serious-infection hospitalisations (11.7 vs 15.6 per 1,000 person-years; adjusted HR 0.84) but not with sepsis among those already hospitalised with infection. This suggests the disparity works mainly through infection risk [[Donnelly2018nSES]]
- REGARDS found white race associated with incident community sepsis (HR 1.39). This contrasts with hospital-discharge studies, and shows that findings depend on study design and case definition [[Wang2012Chronic,Mayr2010Race]]

### home · Warning signs messaging and public awareness
- The public "SEPSIS" mnemonic, as published: S = Slurred speech or confusion; E = Extreme shivering or muscle pain, fever; P = Passing no urine all day; S = Severe breathlessness; I = It feels like you are going to die; S = Skin mottled or discoloured. It was developed by the World Sepsis Day committee to raise awareness and "has not been validated" [[Wattanapaiboon2020SEPSIS]]
- In 437 Thai emergency patients with sepsis, mnemonic symptoms were reported as follows: S (confusion) 22.2%, E 54.9%, P 4.1%, S (breathlessness) 41.4%, I 1.1% and S (skin) 1.1%. 14.9% had none of them, so a negative mnemonic does not rule sepsis out [[Wattanapaiboon2020SEPSIS]]
- In that cohort, vague presenting symptoms were independently associated with in-hospital death (adjusted OR 2.17) [[Wattanapaiboon2020SEPSIS]]
- Sepsis Alliance's "TIME" message: T = Temperature higher or lower; I = Infection, may have signs and symptoms of an infection; M = Mental decline, confused, sleepy, difficult to rouse; E = Extremely ill, severe pain or discomfort, shortness of breath (unverified: charity web page) [[SepsisAlliance2026TIME]]
- In a 2009 telephone survey of 6,021 people, a mean of 88% in Italy, Spain, the UK, France and the US had never heard the word "sepsis" (53% knew it in Germany). Of those who knew the word, 58% did not know it is a leading cause of death [[Rubulotta2009Awareness]]
- In a 2022 Canadian survey (3,200 adults), 61% had heard of sepsis. Knowledge of symptoms (31.5%) and risk factors (16.5%) was low, and only 25% recognised vaccination as prevention [[ParsonsLeigh2022Canada]]
- A CDC-led study concluded that infection prevention (vaccination, reducing transmission in healthcare, managing chronic disease) is likely to have a substantial impact on reducing sepsis [[Novosad2016MMWR]]

### history · From putrefaction to germs
- The word "sepsis" comes from the Greek σήψη (putrefaction). It was used by Homer more than 2,500 years ago, and by Hippocrates, Aristotle, Plutarch and Galen, meaning decay or decomposition [[Vincent2022Concept]]
- Historical reviews trace sepsis thinking from Hippocrates and Galen to Lister, Fleming and Semmelweis [[Funk2009History]]
- Alexander Gordon (Aberdeen, 1795) and Oliver Wendell Holmes (Boston, 1843) suspected that puerperal (childbed) sepsis was carried by birth attendants, before Semmelweis [[Stang2022Semmelweis]]
- In the Vienna General Hospital, Clinic 1 was staffed by medical students and doctors, and Clinic 2 (from 1840) by midwives. Clinic 1's puerperal-sepsis mortality ratio was 11.3% (2,117 of 18,793) from July 1840 to October 1846 [[Stang2022Semmelweis]]
- Ignaz Semmelweis introduced chlorine handwashing in mid-May 1847. Clinic 1 mortality was 18.3% (57 of 312) in April–May 1847, 12.2% the next month, and 2.0% (122 of 6,189) from June 1847 to February 1849 [[Stang2022Semmelweis]]
- Semmelweis attributed the deaths to "cadaverous particles" from autopsies. He delayed publishing his main work until 1861, and his ideas were poorly received in Vienna [[Stang2022Semmelweis]]
- Louis Pasteur cultured *Streptococcus pyogenes* from the blood of a woman with puerperal sepsis about 30 years after Semmelweis's work. This was germ theory linking a specific microbe to sepsis [[Stang2022Semmelweis]]
- Joseph Lister published "On the Antiseptic Principle in the Practice of Surgery" in the BMJ in 1867 [[Lister1867BMJ]]
- Alexander Fleming's report on the antibacterial action of a *Penicillium* mould was published in 1929 in the British Journal of Experimental Pathology, as the validated reprint record shows. The discovery itself is commonly dated to 1928 (unverified) [[Fleming1929Penicillium]]
- Chain, Florey and colleagues reported "Penicillin as a chemotherapeutic agent" in 1940, opening the antibiotic era [[Chain1940Penicillin]]

### history · Modern definitions and campaigns
- Roger Bone introduced the term "sepsis syndrome" in the late 1980s (clinical signs of infection plus poor perfusion or dysfunction of at least one organ) [[Vincent2022Concept]]
- The ACCP/SCCM consensus conference (Northbrook, August 1991; published 1992) defined SIRS (systemic inflammatory response syndrome), sepsis, severe sepsis, septic shock and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (Sepsis-1) [[Bone1992SIRS]]
- SIRS criteria used 2 or more of 4 simple variables (temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, white cell count). Because most infected patients meet them, sepsis and infection became confused [[Vincent2022Concept]]
- The SOFA (Sepsis-related Organ Failure Assessment) score was published in 1996 by an ESICM working group [[Vincent1996SOFA]]
- The 2001 SCCM/ESICM/ACCP/ATS/SIS International Sepsis Definitions Conference (29 participants, published 2003; "Sepsis-2") expanded the list of signs and symptoms but found no evidence to change the 1992 definitions [[Levy2003SCCM]]
- The Surviving Sepsis Campaign issued its Barcelona Declaration at the ESICM congress in October 2002, a six-point plan aiming to cut the relative mortality of sepsis by 25% over 5 years [[Slade2003SSC]]
- Rivers' 2001 single-centre emergency-department trial of early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) reported in-hospital mortality of 30.5% vs 46.5% with standard therapy (263 patients). It shaped the "early, protocolised" era [[Rivers2001EGDT]]
- The first Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines were developed in 2003 by experts from 11 organisations and published in 2004 [[Dellinger2004SSC]]
- The 2005 International Pediatric Sepsis Consensus Conference adapted the adult SIRS-based definitions for children [[Goldstein2005Paeds]]
- In the Surviving Sepsis Campaign bundle programme (15,022 patients, 165 sites, 2005–2008), unadjusted hospital mortality fell from 37% to 30.8% over 2 years. The adjusted absolute drop was 5.4% [[Levy2010SSC]]
- The 2017 PRISM individual-patient meta-analysis of ProCESS, ARISE and ProMISe (3,723 patients) found no benefit of EGDT over usual care: 90-day mortality 24.9% vs 25.4% [[PRISM2017]]
- Under "Rory's Regulations", New York State Article 28 hospitals had to implement sepsis protocols and report patient-level data, beginning in 2013 [[Prescott2018NYReporting,Kahn2019NYMandate]]
- Rory Staunton, a 12-year-old in New York whose 2012 death from sepsis prompted the regulations, is the namesake. These biographical details come from public reports and are not in a validated abstract (unverified)
- In New York's mandated reporting (91,357 hospitalisations, 2014–2016), 3-hour bundle compliance rose from 53.4% to 64.7%, and risk-adjusted mortality fell from 28.8% to 24.4% [[Levy2018NewYork]]
- Compared with 4 control states, New York's regulations were associated with adjusted absolute mortality 3.2% (95% CI 1.0–5.4%) lower than expected by the 10th quarter after implementation [[Kahn2019NYMandate]]
- In New York data, each hour of delay to completing the 3-hour bundle (OR 1.04 per hour) or to antibiotics (OR 1.04 per hour) was associated with higher risk-adjusted mortality. Delay to the fluid bolus was not (OR 1.01) [[Seymour2017Timing]]
- In 2006, each hour of delay in effective antimicrobials after hypotension in septic shock was associated with an average 7.6% decrease in survival over the first 6 hours. This fuelled the time-to-antibiotic emphasis [[Kumar2006Delay]]
- CMS's SEP-1 (Severe Sepsis/Septic Shock Early Management Bundle) reporting began in October 2015. In its first national data, mean hospital bundle compliance was 48.9% (SD 19.4%) [[Barbash2019SEP1,Barbash2021SEP1]]
- In 11 hospitals, SEP-1 increased 3-hour lactate measurement by 23.7 percentage points but did not change mortality (absolute change 0.1 points; 95% CI −0.9 to 1.1) [[Barbash2021SEP1]]
- In 7 hospitals, failing SEP-1 was not associated with mortality after risk adjustment (aOR 1.36; 95% CI 0.85–2.18). Antibiotic delays of more than 3 hours were associated with mortality (aOR 1.94) [[Rhee2018SEP1]]
- Sepsis-3 (2016) defined sepsis as "life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection", operationalised as an increase in SOFA of 2 points or more. It dropped "severe sepsis" as redundant and introduced qSOFA (respiratory rate ≥22/min, altered mentation, systolic BP ≤100 mm Hg) [[Singer2016Sepsis3]]
- In the Sepsis-3 derivation data, qSOFA predicted in-hospital mortality outside the ICU (AUROC 0.81) better than SOFA. Inside the ICU, SOFA (0.74) outperformed qSOFA (0.66) and SIRS (0.64) [[Seymour2016qSOFA]]
- The World Health Assembly made sepsis a global health priority in 2017, adopting a resolution on improving the prevention, diagnosis and management of sepsis, including survivors' access to rehabilitation. The resolution number WHA70.7 and the date of May 2017 are unverified [[Reinhart2017WHA,Cecconi2018Lancet,Prescott2023CoreElements]]
- Following the resolution, WHO published a *Global report on the epidemiology and burden of sepsis* in 2020. It is cited here for its existence only (unverified: not indexed) [[WHO2020SepsisReport]]
- The Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines were updated in 2021, co-published in Intensive Care Medicine and Critical Care Medicine [[Evans2021SSC]]
- In 2023, the CDC released the Hospital Sepsis Program Core Elements, with 7 elements: hospital leadership commitment, accountability, multiprofessional expertise, action, tracking, reporting and education [[Prescott2023CoreElements]]
- The Core Elements were modelled on CDC's antibiotic stewardship core elements. US hospitals achieving all 7 stewardship elements rose from 40.9% in 2014 to 94.9% in 2021 [[Prescott2023CoreElements]]
- In 2024, NICE published updated guidance NG51, *Suspected sepsis: recognition, diagnosis and early management* [[NICE2024NG51]]
- The 2024 Phoenix criteria (35 experts, 6 continents, more than 3 million EHR encounters) define paediatric sepsis as a Phoenix Sepsis Score of at least 2 in a child with suspected infection. This replaced the 2005 SIRS-based criteria [[Schlapbach2024Phoenix]]

### Exam hooks
- GBD 2017: about 48.9 million sepsis cases and 11.0 million sepsis-related deaths, 19.7% of all global deaths [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- More than half of global sepsis cases in 2017 were in children (20.3 million under age 5) [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- The highest burden is in sub-Saharan Africa and South/East/Southeast Asia, and 85% of cases are in low-to-middle SDI countries [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Sepsis is present in about 6% of US adult admissions (EHR surveillance) [[Rhee2017JAMA]]
- Sepsis is present in about half of hospitalisations ending in death (52.8%) and is the immediate cause in about a third (34.9%) [[Rhee2019Mortality]]
- Claims data overstate rising incidence and falling mortality compared with clinical EHR data [[Rhee2017JAMA,Gaieski2013Benchmark]]
- Septicaemia is the most expensive US hospital condition: $38.2 billion (8.8% of costs) in 2017 [[Liang2020HCUP]]
- The Sepsis-3 septic-shock criterion (vasopressor for MAP ≥65 mm Hg plus lactate >2 mmol/L after fluids) carries hospital mortality above 40% [[Singer2016Sepsis3,ShankarHari2016Shock]]
- Adults aged 65 or older make up 12% of the population but about 65% of sepsis cases (RR 13.1) [[Martin2006Age]]
- Incidence rises more than 100-fold from childhood to age 85 or older [[Angus2001CCM]]
- Sepsis was the third leading direct cause of maternal death (10.7%) [[Say2014Causes]]
- Hospital-onset sepsis is about 1 in 8 sepsis cases with twice the adjusted mortality [[Rhee2019HospOnset]]
- About 40–47% of severe sepsis cases are culture-negative [[Phua2013CultureNeg,Gupta2016CultureNeg]]
- Semmelweis's chlorine handwashing (May 1847) lowered Clinic 1 puerperal mortality from 18.3% to about 2% [[Stang2022Semmelweis]]
- Definitions timeline: Bone 1991/1992 SIRS (Sepsis-1), Levy 2001/2003 (Sepsis-2), Singer 2016 (Sepsis-3), Schlapbach 2024 (Phoenix, children) [[Bone1992SIRS,Levy2003SCCM,Singer2016Sepsis3,Schlapbach2024Phoenix]]
- Rivers 2001 EGDT (30.5% vs 46.5%) was not confirmed by ProCESS, ARISE and ProMISe (PRISM: 24.9% vs 25.4%) [[Rivers2001EGDT,PRISM2017]]
- The Barcelona Declaration (October 2002) launched the Surviving Sepsis Campaign, aiming for a 25% relative mortality reduction in 5 years [[Slade2003SSC]]
- The 7 CDC Hospital Sepsis Program Core Elements (2023) are leadership, accountability, multiprofessional expertise, action, tracking, reporting and education [[Prescott2023CoreElements]]

### In plain words
- Sepsis is when the body's fight against an infection starts to harm its own organs. Any infection can lead to it [[Singer2016Sepsis3]]
- Sepsis is common. Worldwide, about 49 million people got sepsis in 2017, and it was linked to about 1 in 5 deaths [[Rudd2020GBD]]
- Babies, young children and older adults are most likely to get sepsis [[Rudd2020GBD,Angus2001CCM]]
- Most sepsis starts at home, not in hospital, often from a chest, urine, skin or gut infection [[Fay2020Exposures,Novosad2016MMWR]]
- People with long-term illnesses like lung, kidney or heart disease, diabetes or cancer, or people who are frail, have a higher chance of sepsis [[Wang2012Chronic,Mahalingam2019Frailty,Danai2006Cancer]]
- Medicines that weaken the immune system (like steroids or chemotherapy), or having no spleen, also raise the risk [[Chaudhary2017Steroids,Kuderer2006FN,Bisharat2001Splenectomy]]
- Pregnancy and the weeks after giving birth are a time to watch closely for signs of infection [[Acosta2014UK,Say2014Causes]]
- Survival figures describe large groups of people, not you or your loved one. Many people survive sepsis, and survival has improved over time [[Kaukonen2014JAMA,Stevenson2014Trends]]
- Many people have never heard the word "sepsis". Knowing the word and asking "Could this be sepsis?" can help [[Rubulotta2009Awareness,ParsonsLeigh2022Canada]]
- Warning-sign lists like "SEPSIS" are reminders, not tests. Someone can have sepsis without matching the list [[Wattanapaiboon2020SEPSIS]]
- Washing hands, vaccines and good care of long-term conditions help prevent the infections that lead to sepsis [[Novosad2016MMWR,Stang2022Semmelweis]]
- Call-now box for builders (required on recognition pages): "Sepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?"

## Open questions and controversies
- How many people get sepsis worldwide? The GBD model gives 48.9 million cases a year [[Rudd2020GBD]]. The hospital-treated meta-analysis gives about 50% lower incidence than IHME, and population data are missing for most low- and middle-income countries [[FleischmannStruzek2020ICM]]. Status: unresolved, because surveillance is lacking.
- Is sepsis incidence really rising? US claims show about 10% a year growth, while clinical EHR criteria show stable incidence in 2009–2014 [[Rhee2017JAMA]]. Methods alone vary incidence 3.5-fold [[Gaieski2013Benchmark]]. Status: the apparent rise is at least partly coding and awareness.
- How many sepsis deaths are preventable? Sepsis is the immediate cause in about a third of hospital deaths, but only 3.7% were judged definitely or moderately preventable, and another 8.3% possibly preventable [[Rhee2019Mortality]]. Status: this is debated, and it shapes how mortality-based quality measures should be read.
- Do mandates and SEP-1 save lives? New York's regulations were associated with lower mortality than control states [[Kahn2019NYMandate,Levy2018NewYork]]. SEP-1 changed processes (lactate testing) without a mortality change [[Barbash2021SEP1]], and failing SEP-1 did not predict death after adjustment [[Rhee2018SEP1]]. Status: mixed evidence.
- Is culture-negative sepsis milder or deadlier? It had lower mortality in one ICU cohort [[Phua2013CultureNeg]] and higher in US administrative data [[Gupta2016CultureNeg]]. Status: depends on case definition and data source.
- Why do racial disparities exist? Higher infection rates and organ-dysfunction risk have both been shown [[Mayr2010Race]], along with hospital-of-care effects [[Barnato2008Race]] and neighbourhood infection risk [[Donnelly2018nSES]]. Community-cohort data differ [[Wang2012Chronic]]. Status: multifactorial, with structural causes likely (Hypothesis).
- Are public mnemonics accurate? The "SEPSIS" mnemonic is unvalidated, and two items were reported by only 1.1% of patients [[Wattanapaiboon2020SEPSIS]]. Status: messages may need local adaptation. They should never be used to rule sepsis out.
- How far have mortality trends really fallen? Some studies show real declines [[Kaukonen2014JAMA,Stevenson2014Trends]]. Septic-shock mortality plateaued after 2011 in a meta-analysis [[Bauer2020Mortality]]. Status: improvement is likely real but uneven.

## Provenance
- **How sources were validated.** All PMIDs, titles, author lists, journals, volumes, pages, PMCIDs and DOIs were taken from the Europe PMC REST API (core records, `EXT_ID:<PMID> AND SRC:MED`). Every number comes from the abstract in that record or, where stated in the access notes, from the open full text through Europe PMC or PMC. NCBI E-utilities was rate-limiting at the time (HTTP 429), so Europe PMC is the validating service named on each line.
- **Keys shared with other passes.** Keys already used by passes E–H for the same works were reused with matching text: Rudd2020GBD, Singer2016Sepsis3, ShankarHari2016Shock, Schlapbach2024Phoenix, Sakr2018ICON, Vincent2020EPICIII, Murray2022GRAM, Levy2018NewYork, Kahn2019NYMandate, Rhee2018SEP1, Rubulotta2009Awareness, Seymour2017Timing, Rivers2001EGDT, PRISM2017, Acosta2014UK, GLOSS2020, Kumar2006Delay, Say2014Causes, Bisharat2001Splenectomy and Evans2021SSC. Arvaniti2010Gastro reuses the cirrhosis companion's key.
- **Primary historical papers.** Lister 1867, Fleming 1929 (2001 WHO Bulletin reprint) and Chain 1940 (2005 reprint) were validated as records and are cited only for their titles and years. No abstracts exist for these.
- **Unverified items.** The WHO 2020 global report (existence and ISBN only), the Sepsis Alliance TIME wording (charity web page), the resolution number WHA70.7 with its May 2017 date, Rory Staunton's biographical details, and Fleming's 1928 discovery date.
- **Dropped or not used.**
  - The UK Sepsis Trust figures and its own page for the SEPSIS mnemonic (site blocked).
  - CDC MMWR full-text percentages by infection site (Access Denied).
  - Liu 2014 JAMA "hospital deaths" research letter (no abstract to read).
  - Best and Neuhauser 2004 and Majno 1991 (no readable text).
  - NICE NG51 risk-factor list (site blocked).
  - Canonical works not cited because they belong to other passes: Venkatesh2018ADRENAL, Annane2018APROCCHSS, Prescott2018Recovery and RCP2017NEWS2. Vincent1996SOFA is cited for its year of publication only (the record has no abstract).
- **What the HCUP figures rest on.** The sepsis cost figures were read on AHRQ's hcup-us.ahrq.gov pages for Statistical Briefs #204 and #261. The PubMed Bookshelf records were validated. The NCBI Bookshelf page presented a CAPTCHA, which I did not attempt to bypass.
