Part 1 · Understanding sepsis & shock

What sepsis is

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection: the infection starts it, but the body's own reaction is what injures the organs. Septic shock is a more severe part of sepsis, identified by low blood pressure that needs a vasopressor and a raised lactate despite fluids. 447 Scrub from an infection to septic shock, then see how many people it touches worldwide.

Phase 1 · infection

An infection starts

Germs multiply at one site, here a chest infection. The local immune response is working as it should, and this is not yet sepsis: Sepsis-3 needs organ dysfunction caused by the response. 447 Lower respiratory infection is the most common underlying cause of sepsis-related death worldwide. 399

In plain wordsAn infection, such as a chest or urine infection, is common and usually stays where it started.
Exam hookInfection alone is not sepsis. Sepsis = infection + organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response. 447

Scene 1 · cases

48.9 million cases in 2017

An estimated 48.9 million incident cases of sepsis worldwide in 2017 (95% UI 38.9–62.9 million): 20.3 million in children under 5, 4.9 million at ages 5–19 and 23.7 million in adults aged 20 or older. Incidence peaks in early childhood, with a second peak in older adults. 399

In plain wordsMore than half of all people who got sepsis in 2017 were children, many of them newborn babies.
Exam hookGBD 2017: 48.9 million cases; > half in children; 20.3 million under 5. 399

These are model estimates, and other methods give lower figures. A 2020 meta-analysis of mostly administrative hospital data pooled 189 hospital-treated sepsis cases per 100,000 person-years (95% CI 133–267), about 50% lower than the GBD model, with 26.7% dying; ICU-treated sepsis was 58 per 100,000, with 41.9% dying before discharge. 153 Population data are missing for most low- and middle-income countries, so the true global number is still debated. 150,153

Where every adult started on intravenous antibiotics was reviewed (Sweden), incidence was 780 per 100,000 a year by Sepsis-3, higher than most earlier estimates. 290 A CDC-authored viewpoint states that sepsis contributes to about 1.7 million adult hospitalisations in the US each year and more than a third of all hospital deaths. 365 In a record review of 568 adults who died in hospital or went to hospice, sepsis was present in 52.8% and was the immediate cause of death in 34.9%. 391

Which infections, which germs

Underlying cause

The chest leads

Lower respiratory infection was the most common underlying cause of sepsis-related death in every year from 1990 to 2017 (1.8 million deaths in 2017). In children under 5 the commonest causes of sepsis were diarrhoeal diseases (27.9%), neonatal disorders (25.7%) and lower respiratory infections (16.5%). 399

Not only infections

Injury and chronic disease

A third of 2017 cases (15.8 million; 32.6%) occurred in people whose underlying cause was an injury or a non-communicable disease. Maternal disorders were the leading non-communicable cause (5.7 million cases), road injury the leading injury. 399

Where it starts

Usually outside hospital

In a CDC-led review of 1,078 adults, 90.3% had community-onset sepsis, although 60.7% had healthcare contact beforehand. 134 Hospital-onset sepsis was 12.1% of cases in 136 US hospitals, with 33% in-hospital mortality vs 17% for community-onset. 390 In ICUs the lung (68%) and abdomen (22%) are the main sources. 496

Bacteria

Five pathogens, half the deaths

In GBD 2019, 7.7 million deaths were associated with 33 bacterial pathogens, 56.2% of all sepsis-related deaths. Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa caused 54.9% of them. 209 An estimated 1.27 million deaths in 2019 were attributable to bacterial antimicrobial resistance. 310

Culture-negative

Often no germ is found

41.5% of severe-sepsis patients in a Singapore ICU and 47.1% of US severe-sepsis admissions (2000–2010) were culture-negative. Their outcome differed by study: lower mortality in Singapore (35.9% vs 44.0%), higher in the US data (34.6% vs 22.7%). 180,355

Intensive care

A large share of the ICU

Sepsis affected 37.4% of adult ICU patients in 24 European countries (SOAP, 2002) and occurred in 29.5% during their ICU stay worldwide (ICON, 2012), with hospital mortality ranging from 19.3% in Oceania to 47.2% in Africa. 406,496 In 2017, 54% of 15,202 ICU patients had suspected or proven infection. 500

Mortality has fallen. In Australian and New Zealand ICUs, severe-sepsis mortality fell from 35.0% in 2000 to 18.4% in 2012, and in patients without other illnesses or older age it was below 5%. 222 In the usual-care arms of 36 trials, 28-day mortality fell from 46.9% (1991–1995) to 29% (2006–2009). 460 Globally, age-standardised sepsis mortality fell by 52.8% between 1990 and 2017. 399

Sepsis is also costly. Septicaemia was the most expensive condition treated in US hospitals in 2017: $38.2 billion, 8.8% of aggregate hospital costs. 264 Average cost per case rose with severity, from $16,324 (sepsis without organ dysfunction) to $38,298 (septic shock). 337

Open questions

Controversy

How many people really get sepsis?

The GBD model gives 48.9 million cases a year, about twice an earlier extrapolation of 31.5 million from high-income hospital data. A hospital-treated meta-analysis gives about 50% lower incidence than the model. Status: unresolved, because surveillance is lacking in most of the world. 150,153,399

Controversy

Is sepsis becoming more common?

US claims data showed incidence rising 10.3% a year from 2009 to 2014; clinical criteria in health records showed it stable (+0.6% a year). Four claims methods applied to the same data varied 3.5-fold. The apparent rise is at least partly coding and awareness. 162,388

Controversy

How many deaths are preventable?

Reviewers found suboptimal care (most often delayed antibiotics) in 22.7% of sepsis-associated hospital deaths, but judged only 3.7% definitely or moderately likely preventable and another 8.3% possibly preventable. Many people who died had serious illnesses such as cancer, heart disease or dementia. 391

In plain wordsSepsis is when the body's fight against an infection starts to harm its own organs. Any infection can lead to it, often a chest, urine, skin or gut infection that starts at home. It is common: worldwide, about 49 million people got sepsis in 2017, and it was linked to about 1 in 5 deaths. Babies, young children and older adults are most often affected. Survival has improved over time, and many people recover. 134,222,399,447
