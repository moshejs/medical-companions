Part 5 · Special situations & recovery

Sepsis in pregnancy and after birth

Pregnancy changes the heart rate, breathing and blood pressure, so the early signs of sepsis can hide in plain sight. See why, how often maternal sepsis happens worldwide, how fast group A streptococcus can move, and which trials have prevented infection around birth.

Call nowSepsis is a medical emergency. If someone who is pregnant, has recently given birth or has had a miscarriage or abortion becomes very unwell, with or without a fever (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?

The WHO 2017 consensus definition: "Maternal sepsis is a life-threatening condition defined as organ dysfunction resulting from infection during pregnancy, child-birth, post-abortion, or post-partum period". 61

Why pregnancy can hide sepsis

Step 1 · a new baseline

Pregnancy changes the numbers

In normal pregnancy, systemic vascular resistance falls by 25–30%, cardiac output rises by about 40% and the heart rate rises by 10–20 beats per minute. Blood pressure falls in the first and second trimesters and returns to non-pregnant levels in the third. Minute ventilation rises by 40–50%, mainly through larger breaths, giving a mild compensated respiratory alkalosis. 454

In plain wordsDuring pregnancy the heart beats faster, more blood is pumped and breathing gets deeper. This is normal and healthy.
Exam hookPregnancy: heart rate +10–20 bpm, cardiac output +40%, SVR −25–30%, minute ventilation +40–50%. 454

SMFM 2023 recommends that clinicians consider sepsis in pregnant or postpartum patients with otherwise unexplained end-organ damage and a suspected or confirmed infection "regardless of the presence of fever" (GRADE 1C). 452

How common it is

Population figures from the studies named; they say nothing about any one pregnancy. 6,171,416

Worldwide · 2003–09

A leading cause of maternal death

WHO's systematic analysis of 60,799 maternal deaths attributed 10.7% (95% UI 5.9–18.6%) of maternal deaths worldwide to sepsis, about 261,000 deaths, behind haemorrhage (27.1%) and hypertensive disorders (14.0%). 416

In plain wordsAround the world, sepsis is one of the three main direct causes of death in pregnancy and after birth. These are population figures, not a prediction for any one pregnancy.
UK

The leading direct cause, 2006–08

In the UK confidential enquiry for 2006–08, sepsis became "the most common cause of Direct maternal death". The sepsis mortality rate rose from 0.85 to 1.13 per 100,000 maternities, particularly from community-acquired group A streptococcal disease, and care was substandard in 70% of Direct deaths. 80

US

Rare, but rising

Among about 45 million US delivery hospitalisations (1998–2008), sepsis complicated 1 in 3,333 deliveries, severe sepsis 1 in 10,823 and sepsis-related death 1 in 105,263, and severe sepsis and deaths were increasing. In Michigan, sepsis caused 15% of pregnancy-related deaths, with delays in antibiotics in 73% of reviewed cases. 46,47

Worldwide

Gaps in basic care

In GLOSS, only 63.9% of women had a complete set of vital signs recorded, and 70.2% received antimicrobials on the day infection was suspected or diagnosed. Infection-related deaths made up more than half of in-hospital maternal deaths. 171

Global

Among all sepsis

In the Global Burden of Disease 2017 analysis, maternal disorders were the leading non-communicable cause of sepsis cases (5.7 million cases). A 2025 review says maternal sepsis rates are increasing. 400,527

Where infections start, and who is at higher risk

In GLOSS's pregnancy-related infections (1,456 women), the genital tract (51.2%) and urinary tract (36.5%) were the commonest sources. E. coli caused 87.3% of identified urinary infections, and 13.1% of women did not get antibiotics on the day infection was suspected. 395

In the UK case-control study (2011–12; 365 cases), severe maternal sepsis occurred in 4.7 per 10,000 maternities; 19.5% developed septic shock and 1.4% (5 women) died. Genital tract infection (31.0%) and E. coli (21.1%) were the commonest source and organism, and the median time from delivery to sepsis was 3 days. 6

Group A streptococcus

Incidence

About 20-fold higher after birth

In US surveillance (2007–09), postpartum women had invasive group A streptococcus (GAS) at 0.56 per 1,000 woman-years, against 0.019 in non-pregnant women; the authors describe a roughly 20-fold higher incidence of GAS and GBS. 117

Speed

Hours, not days

In UKOSS, GAS infection was associated with progression to septic shock (aOR 4.84, 2.17–10.78), and for half of women with GAS less than 2 hours passed between the first sign of a systemic inflammatory response and severe sepsis. The authors conclude that such signs, "particularly with confirmed or suspected group A streptococcal infection, should be regarded as an obstetric emergency". 6

Still today

Not only a historical disease

GAS puerperal sepsis remains an important cause of maternal and infant death worldwide, including in countries with modern antibiotics and intensive care. 185

In that GLOSS analysis, the NICE risk stratification tool, the modified shock index, maternity SIRS and Early Maternal Infection Prompts were highly sensitive (88.1–97.5%), while qSOFA in pregnancy and obstetrically modified SOFA were highly specific (90.4–100%). The SOMANZ 2023 position statement abandoned qSOFA for diagnosing sepsis in pregnancy, and SMFM 2023 recommends that institutions develop their own detection protocols, "avoiding the use of a single screening tool alone" (GRADE 1B). 67,96,452

Treatment statements, as published

These are guideline statements quoted from SMFM 2023, not instructions. 452

  • Sepsis and septic shock in pregnancy are medical emergencies, and treatment and resuscitation should begin immediately (best practice). 452
  • Obtain a serum lactate in pregnant or postpartum patients with suspected sepsis (GRADE 1B), and cultures, including blood, before antimicrobials "as long as there are no substantial delays in timely administration of antibiotics" (best practice). 452
  • Empiric broad-spectrum antimicrobials in septic shock or a high likelihood of sepsis, "ideally within 1 hour of recognition" (GRADE 1C). 452
  • Early IV balanced crystalloid, 1 to 2 L within the first 3 hours, in sepsis with hypotension or suspected hypoperfusion (GRADE 1C): a smaller fixed volume than the SSC adult suggestion of 30 mL/kg. 134,452
  • Norepinephrine (noradrenaline) as the first-line vasopressor (GRADE 1C), and pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis in septic shock (GRADE 1B). 452
  • If a uterine source is suspected or confirmed, "prompt delivery or evacuation of uterine contents to achieve source control, regardless of gestational age" (GRADE 1C). 452

In the S.O.S. derivation cohort, a score of 6 or more was also independently associated with fetal tachycardia. SOMANZ 2017 was the first Australian and New Zealand guideline for sepsis in pregnancy and after birth, covering obstetric considerations for anaesthesia and intensive care. 11,66

Preventing infection around birth: the trials

ANODE · randomised trial

Confirmed or suspected infection: 19 vs 11 per 100

3,427 UK women after operative (assisted) vaginal birth. A single intravenous dose of amoxicillin-clavulanate after operative vaginal birth lowered confirmed or suspected infection from 19% to 11%. RR 0.58 (0.49–0.69). Interventions and doses are quoted as published, not as advice. 230

In plain wordsTrials like this test whether one antibiotic dose at the right moment prevents infection in large groups of women. Whether it applies to you is a question for your maternity team.
Established

Group B streptococcus

Without intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis, about 50% of GBS-colonised women pass the bacteria to their newborns, and 1–2% of those newborns develop early-onset GBS disease. ACOG (2020) recommends universal GBS screening at 36 0/7 to 37 6/7 weeks and intrapartum prophylaxis for a positive culture unless a prelabour caesarean is done with intact membranes, as published. 5

Established

What prophylaxis achieved

As US intrapartum prophylaxis expanded, early-onset GBS disease fell 65%, from 1.7 to 0.6 per 1,000 live births (1993–1998), preventing an estimated 3,900 early-onset infections and 200 newborn deaths in 1998. ACOG notes 4 or more hours of prophylaxis is more effective, but obstetric care should not be delayed only to reach 4 hours. 5,422

Early evidence

Awareness among health workers

After WHO's "STOP SEPSIS!" maternal awareness campaign for health workers in 46 countries, the share who had never heard of maternal sepsis fell by 63.4% (before-and-after surveys). 72

After maternal sepsisSMFM 2023 recommends ongoing comprehensive support for pregnant and postpartum sepsis survivors and their families (best practice). Recovery can take time, and many people find it helps to talk it through with their care team. 452

Open questions

Unresolved

Which warning score?

Scores vary widely in thresholds and accuracy, SOMANZ dropped qSOFA, and SMFM advises against relying on any single tool: there is no validated universal tool. 67,96,128,452

Debated

How much fluid?

SMFM proposes 1–2 L in pregnancy, while SSC 2021's 30 mL/kg for adults is a weak suggestion on observational evidence. 134,452

Sepsis Companion is an educational synthesis of the published literature on sepsis (the body's harmful response to an infection), septic shock and the other types of shock, made for health-science, medical and nursing courses and for people who survive sepsis, their families and caregivers.

It is not medical advice. It cannot diagnose, and nothing here should start, stop or change a medicine or a care plan without your own care team.
