Preventing sepsis

Every case of sepsis starts with an infection, so each infection prevented is a case of sepsis that cannot happen. Add the barriers one at a time (clean hands, safer lines and catheters, vaccines, care around birth, and knowing the signs) and see the published before-and-after numbers for each.

Infection comes first

GBD 2017 estimated 48.9 million sepsis cases and 11.0 million sepsis-related deaths, 19.7% of all deaths worldwide; age-standardised incidence fell 37.0% and mortality 52.8% from 1990 to 2017 (drawn as an index, 1990 = 100). 400 Sepsis is a dysregulated host response to infection, so every barrier that stops an infection, or stops it spreading, removes a possible case. 448

Why prevention matters

The Global Burden of Disease study estimated 48.9 million sepsis cases and 11.0 million sepsis-related deaths worldwide in 2017, 19.7% of all deaths, with the highest burden in sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania and South, East and Southeast Asia. Age-standardised incidence fell 37.0% and mortality 52.8% between 1990 and 2017. 400 Because sepsis is the body's harmful response to an infection, preventing infections, and stopping them spreading, prevents sepsis. 448

Clean hands

In a Geneva teaching hospital, a hospital-wide campaign centred on alcohol hand rub at the bedside raised hand hygiene compliance from 48% to 66% (1994–1997). Healthcare-associated infection fell from 16.9% to 9.9% of patients, MRSA transmission from 2.16 to 0.93 episodes per 10,000 patient-days, and hand rub use rose from 3.5 to 15.4 L per 1,000 patient-days. 358 WHO's "My five moments for hand hygiene" sets out when health workers clean their hands during care, to interrupt transmission. It was developed with a user-centred design approach. 415

Safer lines and catheters

Devices that enter the body can carry germs in. In 103 Michigan ICUs, an evidence-based central-line intervention cut catheter-related bloodstream infections from a mean of 7.7 to 1.4 per 1,000 catheter-days at 16–18 months (incidence-rate ratio 0.34). 370 A US national programme for urinary catheters (926 units) lowered catheter-associated urinary infection on non-ICU wards from 2.28 to 1.54 per 1,000 catheter-days and catheter use from 20.1% to 18.8%; ICU rates were largely unchanged. 405 Nursing care of lines and catheters is covered in Nursing care.

Around birth

As US intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis for group B streptococcus expanded, early-onset GBS disease in newborns fell 65%, from 1.7 to 0.6 per 1,000 live births (1993–1998). 422 In A-PLUS, a single oral dose of azithromycin in labour lowered maternal sepsis or death from 2.4% to 1.6%, without changing neonatal sepsis. 479 One dose of antibiotic after operative vaginal birth lowered infection from 19% to 11% (ANODE). 230 More in Sepsis in pregnancy and after birth.

People at higher risk

Chemotherapy

Neutropenia

Established In afebrile neutropenic patients after chemotherapy, mostly with blood cancers, antibiotic prophylaxis reduced all-cause mortality (RR 0.66; NNT 34) across 109 trials. 161 ASCO/IDSA (2018) endorse giving people with febrile neutropenia the first dose of empirical antibacterial therapy within 1 hour of triage, quoted as published. 467 The MASCC risk index (a score of 21 or more) identifies lower-risk febrile neutropenia. 229

No working spleen

Asplenia

In a review of 19,680 people after splenectomy, infection occurred in 3.2% and death in 1.4% over a median 6.9 years, highest in thalassaemia major (8.2%) and sickle-cell anaemia (7.3%). 58 People having chemotherapy, and people without a working spleen, should know who to call straight away if they get a fever. 58,467

Older adults

Urine tests without symptoms

Asymptomatic bacteriuria in older women is transient, often clears by itself, is not linked to illness or death, and should not be treated. 302 IDSA (2019) recommends screening for and treating it only in pregnancy or before invasive urological procedures; unnecessary treatment drives antimicrobial resistance. 330 Diagnosing a symptomatic UTI needs symptoms such as fever, new urgency, painful urination or flank pain, plus laboratory evidence. 302

Knowing the signs: meningococcal disease in children

In 448 UK children with meningococcal disease, most had only non-specific symptoms for the first 4–6 hours, but many were close to death by 24 hours. The classic signs (a haemorrhagic rash, neck stiffness, impaired consciousness) appeared late, at a median of 13–22 hours. Earlier sepsis signs (leg pains, cold hands and feet, abnormal skin colour) appeared in 72%, at a median of 8 hours. Only 51% were sent to hospital after the first consultation. 474 Drag through the first day:

Awareness and equity

In a 2009 telephone survey of 6,021 people, on average 88% in Italy, Spain, the UK, France and the US had never heard the word "sepsis" (in Germany, 53% knew it). 399 In a 2023 Australian poll of 3,352 parents, 61.6% had heard of sepsis, but only 27.8% of those thought they could recognise it in their child, and 37.3% would consider calling an ambulance. 355 After WHO's "STOP SEPSIS!" maternal sepsis campaign for health workers in 46 countries, the share who had never heard of maternal sepsis fell by 63.4%. 72

Early evidence

Do awareness campaigns save lives?

The evidence so far is mostly surveys of knowledge and before-and-after recognition of campaigns; no outcome trials were found. 72,355,399

In plain words

Ask: could this be sepsis?

Knowing the word helps. If someone with an infection gets much worse, asking "could this be sepsis?" puts the question on the table. See Recognising sepsis.
