Nursing care

Nurses take the observations, raise the alarm and keep the sepsis bundle moving. Follow one illustrative ward afternoon through the observe, score, escalate, act and reassess loop, then try the urine-output chart and the exam-style scenarios.

Call nowSepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?

SSC 2021 recommends that hospitals run a sepsis performance improvement programme, including screening of acutely ill, high-risk patients and standard operating procedures (strong recommendation); in a meta-analysis of 50 observational studies such programmes were linked to lower mortality (OR 0.66). 133 Nurse-driven protocols are one way to run them: in one emergency department a Detect, Act, Reassess, Titrate (DART) protocol with a checklist raised compliance with all 3-hour bundle measures from 30% to 80% (single-centre quality project). 305

A nurse's sepsis checklist

What the sources in this companion put at the bedside, in order. Each item is quoted from the named source as published; local protocols and prescribers decide.

  1. Screen and scoreUse the hospital's screening tool for acutely ill, high-risk patients, knowing its limits: SIRS, qSOFA, SOFA, NEWS and MEWS vary widely in accuracy. 133
  2. Escalate earlyRapid response teams in adults cut cardiac arrests outside the ICU by 33.8% (RR 0.66), without a clear fall in hospital mortality. 88
  3. Cultures before antibioticsIf it causes no substantial delay (under 45 minutes) (SSC 2021). Two sets detect about 90% of bloodstream infections. 133,252
  4. Paired cultures if a line is suspectedFrom the catheter and a peripheral vein, before antibiotics start and/or the catheter is removed (IDSA 2009, A-II). 291
  5. Antibiotics on timeIdeally within 1 h for possible septic shock or a high likelihood of sepsis; within 3 h for possible sepsis without shock if concern persists (SSC 2021). 133
  6. Lactate, fluids and pressureThe bundle and its targets are in the first hour, fluids and vasopressors.
  7. Vasopressor line checksPeripheral infusion "only for a short period of time and in a vein in or proximal to the antecubital fossa" (SSC 2021). 133
  8. Hourly urine, daily balanceUrine below 0.5 mL/kg/hour for 6 hours meets the KDIGO definition of AKI. 225
  9. Devices out when not neededAsk every day whether each catheter can come out. 53
  10. Delirium, skin and mobilityCAM-ICU screening, pressure-area care and early mobility (below). 129,244,426

Cultures and antibiotics: getting the timing right

Blood cultures taken after antibiotics find the germ much less often. In 325 emergency patients with severe sepsis, cultures before antimicrobials were positive in 31.4%; cultures a median 70 minutes after were positive in 19.4%, a sensitivity of only 52.9%. 94 Contaminated cultures range from 1% to more than 10% of results, and older guidance set ≤3% as acceptable, though the evidence behind that target is debated. 412 Under New York's mandated protocols, 82.5% of patients completed the 3-hour bundle within 3 hours, and each hour to antibiotics was linked to higher risk-adjusted mortality (OR 1.04 per hour). 435 A nurse-driven rapid response protocol halved the median time to the first antibiotic dose, from 269 to 135 minutes (32 patients, one hospital). 429

Fluid balance and urine output

KDIGO urine-output stages: stage 1 is below 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6–12 hours, stage 2 below 0.5 mL/kg/h for 12 hours or more, and stage 3 below 0.3 mL/kg/h for 24 hours or more, or no urine for 12 hours or more. 225 In ARDS, keeping fluid balance near even over 7 days (−136 mL vs +6,992 mL) shortened ventilation and ICU stay without more shock or dialysis, which is why daily balance is charted carefully. 518 Try the teaching chart.

Vasopressor lines and extravasation

Where

Peripheral start, close to the elbow

SSC 2021 suggests starting vasopressors peripherally rather than delaying until a central line is placed (weak), "only for a short period of time and in a vein in or proximal to the antecubital fossa". 133 Of 325 published extravasation or tissue-injury events, 318 came from peripheral lines, and 85.3% of peripheral injuries involved a site below the antecubital or popliteal fossa. 269

Early evidence

How often it goes wrong

In one medical ICU, 734 patients received vasopressors peripherally 783 times for 49 ± 22 hours: extravasation occurred in 19 (2%), with no tissue injury after local phentolamine and nitroglycerin paste, and 13% later needed a central line. 81 A meta-analysis of 11 adult studies (16,055 patients) found a pooled 1.8% rate of local adverse events, and 3.3% in children; evidence is mostly low quality. 335

Label wording

Noradrenaline (norepinephrine) label

The Levophed label says to "Infuse LEVOPHED into a large vein", to check blood pressure every two minutes until the desired effect, then every five minutes, to reduce the infusion gradually because abrupt stopping may cause marked hypotension, and to avoid leg veins in older people or those with occlusive vascular disease. For extravasation, the area "should be infiltrated as soon as possible with 10 mL to 15 mL of saline solution containing from 5 mg to 10 mg of an adrenergic blocking agent" (quoted as published). 137

Delirium, skin, diaries and families

Established

Delirium: screen every day

CAM-ICU, used by study nurses, had sensitivities of 100% and 93% and specificities of 98% and 100% against expert diagnosis, took about 2 minutes, and found delirium in 39.5% of observations where the patient looked alert. 129 Among ventilated patients, delirium was linked to higher 6-month mortality (34% vs 15%; adjusted HR 3.2). 130 In BRAIN-ICU, 40% had cognition at 3 months similar to moderate traumatic brain injury, and 34% still did at 12 months. 336

Skin

Pressure injuries

In DecubICUs (13,254 patients, 90 countries), pressure injury prevalence was 26.6% and ICU-acquired prevalence 16.2%, most often at the sacrum (37%) and heels (19.5%). ICU-acquired injury was linked to a Braden score below 19, a stay over 3 days, ventilation and kidney replacement therapy, and the odds of death rose with stage (stage III or worse, OR 2.8). 244

Early evidence

ICU diaries

A diary given 1 month after discharge (352 patients) cut new PTSD at 3 months from 13% to 5%. 217 A larger trial (657 patients, 35 French ICUs), with diaries written by staff and family, found no significant reduction in PTSD symptoms (29.9% vs 34.3%). 164 Status: unresolved; diaries may still help survivors make sense of gaps in memory. Families

Talking with families

SSC 2021 recommends discussing goals of care and prognosis with patients and families, early rather than late, and suggests written and verbal sepsis education before discharge. 133 For families of patients dying in the ICU, a proactive conference where families spent more of the time talking (14 vs 5 minutes), plus a bereavement brochure, lowered PTSD-related symptoms (45% vs 69%). 251

In plain words

For patients and familiesNurses check breathing, pulse, blood pressure, temperature and alertness regularly, and add them into a score that helps them notice when someone is getting sicker. 492 They measure urine every hour in intensive care, because passing very little urine can be an early sign the kidneys are struggling. 225 Medicines that raise blood pressure can damage the skin if they leak from the vein, so the drip site is checked often. 137 Many people in intensive care become confused for a while (delirium); nurses check for it every day with a short test, and getting up and moving early helps recovery. 129,426 If you are worried that someone is getting worse, tell the nurse straight away and ask: could this be sepsis?

Sepsis Companion is an educational synthesis of the published literature on sepsis (the body's harmful response to an infection), septic shock and the other types of shock, made for health-science, medical and nursing courses and for people who survive sepsis, their families and caregivers.

It is not medical advice. It cannot diagnose, and nothing here should start, stop or change a medicine or a care plan without your own care team. The qSOFA, SOFA, NEWS2, Phoenix and shock-index calculators are for learning, never for self-triage or for deciding whether to seek care. Antibiotic and vasopressor doses, fluid volumes, MAP and lactate targets and guideline statements are quoted as published in guidelines and labels (Surviving Sepsis Campaign, NICE, Sepsis-3), never as instructions, and mortality figures are population statistics.
