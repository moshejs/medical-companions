Part 2 · Who is at risk & recognising it

Recognising sepsis

Sepsis often starts with signs that look like any other infection. The clues are a person who is suddenly much more unwell than expected, and observations (breathing, pulse, blood pressure, temperature, alertness) that keep getting worse. This module shows the warning signs in plain words, then how hospitals turn the numbers into a score such as NEWS2.

Where it starts

It starts with an infection

Sepsis begins with an infection somewhere in the body: here, the lungs, because pneumonia was the commonest infection behind sepsis in a CDC review of 325 patients. Early on, it can look like an ordinary illness. Vague presenting symptoms were independently linked with in-hospital death in a Thai cohort (adjusted OR 2.17). 331,511

Why sepsis is easy to miss

Vague at first

No single sign is reliable

In the Thai cohort, 14.9% of people with sepsis had none of the SEPSIS signs, so a "negative" mnemonic does not rule sepsis out. Vague presenting symptoms were independently linked with in-hospital death (adjusted OR 2.17). 511

Who it happens to

Often people already in contact with care

A CDC review of 246 adults and 79 children with severe sepsis or septic shock in four New York hospitals found that 72% had a health-care factor in the previous 30 days, or a chronic condition needing frequent care. Pneumonia was the commonest infection behind it. 331

Children

Early signs come before the rash

In 448 UK children with meningococcal disease, leg pain, cold hands and feet and abnormal skin colour first appeared at a median of 8 hours, well before the classic rash and the median hospital admission at 19 hours. 473

Awareness

Many parents would not spot it

In an Australian poll of 3,352 parents (2023), 61.6% had heard the word sepsis. Only 27.8% of those parents thought they could recognise it in their child. 354

Watching the numbers: how a score rises

In hospital, nurses record six observations again and again: breathing rate, oxygen saturation (and whether oxygen is being given), blood pressure, pulse, level of consciousness and temperature. NEWS2, the National Early Warning Score used across the UK NHS, gives each one 0 to 3 points and adds them up. Scale 2 for oxygen saturation gives 3 points at 83% or less, 2 at 84–85%, 1 at 86–87%, 0 at 88–92% (or 93% or more on air), then 1, 2 and 3 points at 93–94%, 95–96% and 97% or more on oxygen. "V, P or U" means the person responds only to voice, only to pain, or not at all. 492

How the scores compare

Several bedside tools compete for the same job: spotting the infected patient who is about to get much worse. qSOFA (3 items) and SIRS (4 items) come from the sepsis definitions, explained in . The Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 guideline puts it plainly: "We recommend against using qSOFA compared to SIRS, NEWS, or MEWS as a single screening tool for sepsis or septic shock" (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence). It adds that "neither SIRS nor qSOFA are ideal screening tools". 133

Screening systems, alerts and the evidence

Established

Screening as part of a programme

SSC 2021 recommends that hospitals and health systems use a performance improvement programme including "sepsis screening for acutely ill, high-risk patients" (strong recommendation). A meta-analysis of 50 observational studies linked such programmes with lower mortality (OR 0.66); a pooled analysis of three randomised trials of active screening showed no clear mortality benefit (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.51–1.58). 133

Early evidence

NEWS across a whole region

In the West of England (2015–2019), using NEWS across community, primary and secondary care was followed by falling suspected-sepsis mortality not seen in the rest of England. This was a quality-improvement study, not a randomised trial. 371

Established

Not every alert works

A widely used proprietary sepsis model, validated externally in 38,455 hospitalisations, had an AUROC of 0.63. It missed 67% of sepsis cases while alerting on 18% of all patients. 523

Early evidence

Alerts that are acted on

With the TREWS machine-learning alert (590,736 patients, 5 hospitals), confirmation of the alert by a clinician within 3 hours was associated with an 18.7% relative reduction in in-hospital mortality. The study was a prospective cohort, not randomised. 8

Early evidence

NEWS2 plus a blood test

The PRONTO trial added procalcitonin to NEWS2 in emergency departments in England and Wales. It did not change intravenous antibiotic starts at 3 hours (48.4% vs 48.2%); 28-day mortality was lower with the procalcitonin-guided algorithm (13.6% vs 16.6%), an effect the authors say needs further research. 479

Shock index

Pulse divided by blood pressure

Shock index is heart rate ÷ systolic blood pressure. In 2,524 emergency patients with suspected infection, those with a shock index of 0.7 or more were about three times as likely to have a lactate of 4.0 mmol/L or more (15.8% vs 4.9%); 1.0 or more was the most specific cut-off. A single-centre pilot. 54

In hospital: NEWS2 in the UK guidance

NICE's 2024 update of its sepsis guidance brought NEWS2 in for assessing people with suspected sepsis who are aged 16 or over, are not pregnant or recently pregnant, and are in an acute hospital, an acute mental health setting or an ambulance. 168 The score is a trend tool: Churpek and colleagues found that patients who went on to die or need intensive care first met 2 or more SIRS criteria a median of 17 hours before that outcome, and 2 or more qSOFA criteria only 5 hours before. Repeated observations are what reveal the slope. 96 A lactate blood test often sits beside the score: SSC 2021 suggests measuring blood lactate in adults suspected of having sepsis (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence), while noting that lactate "alone is neither sensitive nor specific enough to rule-in or rule-out the diagnosis".
