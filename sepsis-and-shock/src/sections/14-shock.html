Part 1 · Understanding sepsis & shock

Shock and haemodynamics

Shock means the organs are not getting enough oxygen from the blood, and the blood pressure can still look normal at first. Think of the circulation as a pump, a set of pipes and a tank of blood: scrub through the four ways it can fail, then build a blood pressure yourself from heart rate, stroke volume and vessel tone.

Call nowSepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?

What shock is, and what it is not

The European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) task force defines circulatory shock as "a life-threatening, generalized form of acute circulatory failure associated with inadequate oxygen utilization by the cells". The cells run short of oxygen (dysoxia), and blood lactate rises [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

Shock does not require low blood pressure. Hypotension (a systolic pressure below 90 mmHg, a mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg, or a fall of 40 mmHg or more from baseline) is common, but ESICM states it "should not be required to define shock" [[Cecconi2014ESICM]]. Lactate is "typically >2 mEq/L (or mmol/L) in shock states", and in septic shock levels above 1.5 mmol/L are associated with higher mortality [[Cecconi2014ESICM]]. Lactate and how it is measured are covered in Lactate, cultures and tests.

Pump, pipes and tank: the four ways the circulation fails

Weil and Shubin proposed a reclassification of shock "with special reference to distributive defects" in 1971, the origin of the four-class scheme still taught Established [[Weil1971]]. ESICM describes three low-flow states (hypovolaemic, cardiogenic and obstructive) and one hyperkinetic state (distributive), and notes that shock can be a combination [[Cecconi2014ESICM]]. How often each type is seen

Build a blood pressure

Cardiac output (the blood the heart pumps in a minute) is stroke volume times heart rate [[Vincent2008CO]]. In sepsis it is usually normal or high even when the heart muscle is depressed, because a fast heart rate and low vascular tone reduce afterload; Vincent compares this to a tired cyclist keeping up speed on a smooth downhill road [[Vincent2008CO]]. Fluids raise stroke volume through the Frank–Starling mechanism, "like adding a tailwind to the cyclist" [[Vincent2008CO]].

Try "Low tone" and then "Low tone + fast heart": a faster heart lifts output and claws back some pressure, but not all of it. That is the pattern of high-output septic shock, where the pressure can stay low despite a normal or high cardiac output [[Vincent2008CO,Cecconi2014ESICM]]. In septic shock, some people have a left ventricular ejection fraction close to 40% despite a cardiac index above 3 L/min/m² [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

Three windows on perfusion

ESICM names three bedside "windows" for checking whether tissues are getting enough blood: the skin, the kidneys and the brain [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

Skin

Cutaneous perfusion

Signs that suggest impaired microcirculation include mottled skin, acrocyanosis (bluish fingers and toes), slow capillary refill and a larger temperature gap between the core and the toes [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

Kidneys

Urine output

Falling urine output is the kidney window [[Cecconi2014ESICM]]. Kidney support in intensive care is covered in Organ support in intensive care.

Brain

Mental status

New confusion or unusual sleepiness is the brain window [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

In the blood

Venous oxygen and CO2

A low central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO2) suggests oxygen transport is inadequate, but normal or high values cannot rule out local hypoperfusion. A venous-to-arterial CO2 gap above 6 mmHg suggests too little tissue blood flow even when ScvO2 is above 70% [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

In plain wordsWarning signs that organs are short of blood include cold or blotchy (mottled) skin, passing little urine, and confusion or unusual sleepiness [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

Bedside numbers: mean arterial pressure and the shock index

The traditional estimate of mean arterial pressure (MAP) is the diastolic pressure plus one third of the pulse pressure. In 1878 people, a factor of 0.412 correlated better with target-organ damage than the traditional one third [[Papaioannou2016]]. The shock index, introduced by Allgöwer and Burri in 1967, is heart rate divided by systolic pressure [[Allgower1967]].

Teaching toolThis shows how the formulas work. It is not for self-triage, for diagnosis or for deciding whether to seek care: heart rate and pressure often look normal in early, compensated shock [[Koch2019SI]]. If you are worried about someone, call your local emergency number.

Bands as published: usual normal range 0.5–0.7, some evidence accepts up to 0.9, and values approaching 1.0 indicate worsening haemodynamics [[Koch2019SI,Rady1994]]. Above 1.0 "has been widely found to predict increased risk of mortality" [[Koch2019SI]]. Trauma groups: Mutschler 2013 [[Mutschler2013SI]].

How intensive care teams assess a circulation in shock

ESICM 2014

Fluid challenge

ESICM states that a cardiac output rise of at least 10–15% defines a positive response to fluid. It recommends a fluid challenge when fluids are given, unless hypovolaemia is obvious, and prefers dynamic over static predictors of fluid responsiveness [[Cecconi2014ESICM]]. More in Fluids.

ESICM 2014

A pressure target

ESICM recommends initially targeting a MAP of 65 mmHg or more and individualising the target, and suggests tolerating lower pressure in uncontrolled bleeding without severe head injury. These are quoted as published, not as instructions [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

Measuring flow

Cardiac output and echo

ESICM does not recommend routine cardiac output measurement when shock responds to initial therapy, but recommends it when shock does not respond, and recommends against routine pulmonary artery catheters. It calls echocardiography the best bedside method for repeatedly assessing cardiac function [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

RUSH

Ultrasound in shock

The RUSH exam (Rapid Ultrasound in SHock) is a stepwise bedside ultrasound algorithm that checks the pump, the tank and the pipes to identify the type of shock [[Perera2010RUSH]]. ESICM adds that echocardiography helps choose treatment and judge the response [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

History

Early goal-directed therapy

In Rivers 2001 (263 adults), a protocol adjusting preload, afterload and contractility "to balance oxygen delivery with oxygen demand" reported in-hospital mortality of 30.5% vs 46.5% [[Rivers2001EGDT]]. The later PRISM meta-analysis of three trials (3723 patients) found no benefit over usual care: 90-day mortality 24.9% vs 25.4% [[PRISM2017]].

Sepsis-3

Septic shock, defined

Sepsis-3 identifies septic shock by "a vasopressor requirement to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 65 mm Hg or greater and serum lactate level greater than 2 mmol/L (>18 mg/dL) in the absence of hypovolemia" [[Singer2016Sepsis3]]. In the derivation data, that combination carried a hospital mortality of 42.3% [[ShankarHari2016Shock]]. See Definitions & scores.

Mortality figures on this page describe large groups of people and cannot predict what will happen to any one person. Septic shock case-fatality is reported "in the range of 40–50 %, reaching as high as 80 %" [[Cecconi2014ESICM]], yet many people survive, and the heart muscle that weakens in septic shock often recovers over days [[Parker1984]].
