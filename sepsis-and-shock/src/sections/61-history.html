History & people

From an ancient Greek word for rot to the 2025 update of the SOFA score: handwashing in a Vienna maternity clinic, carbolic acid in surgery, penicillin, the first agreed definitions, a trial that seemed to change everything and then did not, a drug that came and went, a World Health Assembly resolution and new criteria for children. Three stretches are compressed: antiquity to 1790, 1790 to 1950, and 1950 to 1985. Pasteur's work is placed about 30 years after Semmelweis's, as the source dates it. [[Stang2022Semmelweis]] Fleming's report was published in 1929. [[Fleming1929Penicillium]]

Washing hands in Vienna, 1847

At the Vienna General Hospital, Clinic 1 was staffed by medical students and doctors who also did autopsies, and Clinic 2 by midwives. Ignaz Semmelweis blamed "cadaverous particles" carried on hands, and in mid-May 1847 brought in chlorine handwashing. [[Stang2022Semmelweis]] Choose a period to see how many of every 100 women who gave birth in Clinic 1 died of puerperal (childbed) sepsis. Alexander Gordon in Aberdeen (1795) and Oliver Wendell Holmes in Boston (1843) had already suspected that birth attendants carried the disease. [[Stang2022Semmelweis]]

Mortality ratios as reported from the clinic's records; historical figures for one hospital, not a prediction for anyone. [[Stang2022Semmelweis]] Semmelweis delayed publishing his main work until 1861, and his ideas were poorly received in Vienna. [[Stang2022Semmelweis]]

When a promising result did not hold

Two stories from the 2000s shaped how sepsis research is done. A single-centre trial of early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) reported a large fall in deaths, and three later multicentre trials did not confirm it. [[Rivers2001EGDT,PRISM2017]] Drotrecogin alfa (activated) was approved after one trial, PROWESS, that was stopped early for efficacy; the manufacturer withdrew it worldwide in 2011 after PROWESS-SHOCK failed. [[MedLett2011Xigris,Angus2012Drotrecogin]] More than 100 randomised trials have tested ways to modulate the septic response; "with one short-lived exception" (drotrecogin alfa), none produced a new treatment. How the definitions changed

YearNameWhat it said
1991 · 1992Sepsis-1 (ACCP/SCCM)Defined SIRS, sepsis, severe sepsis, septic shock and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. SIRS meant 2 or more of 4 simple signs: temperature, heart rate, breathing rate and white cell count. [[Bone1992SIRS]] Because most infected patients meet them, sepsis and infection became confused. [[Vincent2022Concept]]
2001 · 2003Sepsis-2An international conference of 29 participants expanded the list of signs and symptoms but found no evidence to change the 1992 definitions. [[Levy2003Sepsis2]]
2005Paediatric consensusAdapted the adult SIRS-based definitions for children. [[Goldstein2005Paeds]]
2016Sepsis-3Sepsis became "life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection", shown by a SOFA rise of 2 points or more; "severe sepsis" was dropped and qSOFA introduced. [[Singer2016Sepsis3]]
2024Phoenix (children)Sepsis in a child with suspected infection is a Phoenix Sepsis Score of at least 2, built by 35 experts from 6 continents with more than 3 million electronic health record encounters. [[Schlapbach2024Phoenix]]
2025SOFA-2A revised organ score from 3.34 million ICU encounters; Sepsis-3 is still defined with the original SOFA. [[Ranzani2025SOFA2,Lobo2025SOFA2]]

People behind the ideas

Antiquity

Homer, Hippocrates, Aristotle, Galen

"Sepsis" comes from the Greek word for putrefaction. Homer used it more than 2,500 years ago, and Hippocrates, Aristotle, Plutarch and Galen used it to mean decay. [[Vincent2022Concept]]

1847 · 1861

Ignaz Semmelweis

Brought in chlorine handwashing in Vienna's Clinic 1 in May 1847; deaths from childbed fever fell to 2.0% over the next 20 months. He published his main work only in 1861. [[Stang2022Semmelweis]]

1867

Joseph Lister

Published "On the Antiseptic Principle in the Practice of Surgery" in the BMJ. [[Lister1867BMJ]] Historical reviews trace sepsis thinking through Lister, Semmelweis and Fleming. [[Funk2009History]]

1870s

Louis Pasteur

About 30 years after Semmelweis, he grew Streptococcus pyogenes from the blood of a woman with puerperal sepsis: germ theory linking a named microbe to sepsis. [[Stang2022Semmelweis]]

1929 · 1940

Alexander Fleming, Ernst Chain, Howard Florey

Fleming reported the antibacterial action of a Penicillium mould; Chain, Florey and colleagues showed penicillin working as a medicine, opening the antibiotic era. [[Fleming1929Penicillium,Chain1940Penicillin]]

Late 1980s · 1992

Roger Bone

Introduced the term "sepsis syndrome", then led the consensus conference that defined SIRS and Sepsis-1. [[Vincent2022Concept,Bone1992SIRS]]

1996

Jean-Louis Vincent and the ESICM group

Published the SOFA (Sepsis-related Organ Failure Assessment) score, which scores six organ systems. [[Vincent1996SOFA]] It became the yardstick for organ dysfunction in Sepsis-3. [[Singer2016Sepsis3]]

2001

Emanuel Rivers

His emergency-department trial of early goal-directed therapy shaped the "early, protocolised" era; later trials found no benefit over usual care. [[Rivers2001EGDT,PRISM2017]]

2016

Mervyn Singer, Christopher Seymour and the Sepsis-3 task force

Redefined sepsis around organ dysfunction. Seymour's analysis showed qSOFA predicting death outside the ICU (AUROC 0.81) and SOFA doing better inside it. [[Singer2016Sepsis3,Seymour2016qSOFA]]

2017

Konrad Reinhart and colleagues

Described the World Health Assembly resolution that made sepsis a global health priority, covering prevention, diagnosis, management and survivors' access to rehabilitation. [[Reinhart2017WHA]]

2024

Luregn Schlapbach and the Phoenix task force

Replaced the 2005 SIRS-based criteria for children with the data-driven Phoenix score. In-hospital mortality with Phoenix sepsis was 7.1% in higher-resource and 28.5% in lower-resource settings (population figures). [[Schlapbach2024Phoenix]] See Sepsis in babies and children.
