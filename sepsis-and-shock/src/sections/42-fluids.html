Part 4 · Treatment

Fluids

In septic shock the vessels leak and relax, so fluid through a drip is usually the first treatment. But fluid only helps while the heart can use it, and some of it leaks out. Scrub the Frank–Starling curve, watch fluid leave a leaky vessel, and compare the big trials on how much fluid, and which fluid, to give.

Call nowSepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?

Sepsis-3 identifies septic shock by "a vasopressor requirement to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 65 mm Hg or greater and serum lactate level greater than 2 mmol/L (>18 mg/dL) in the absence of hypovolemia" [[Singer2016Sepsis3]]. The words "in the absence of hypovolemia" mean fluid comes first, which is why fluids and vasopressors are taught together [[Singer2016Sepsis3]]. That combination went with hospital mortality above 40%, a figure for large groups of patients, not a prediction for one person [[Singer2016Sepsis3,ShankarHari2016Shock]]. This page describes what guidelines and trials say. It is not a set of instructions.

Will more fluid help? The Frank–Starling curve

Cardiac output is stroke volume times heart rate, and it depends on heart rate, contractility, preload and afterload. Within limits, the more the heart muscle is stretched before it contracts (its preload), the harder it contracts: the Frank–Starling relationship [[Vincent2008CO]]. Fluid raises stroke volume only on the steep part of that curve. Scrub through two patients, one on the steep part and one on the flat part, and a passive leg raise that tests which is which.

Schematic curve, not patient data. The curve shape and the dots are illustrative. The 10% line is the published PLR threshold (a rise in cardiac output of ≥10 ± 2%) [[Monnet2016PLR]]; the ESICM consensus defines a positive response to fluid as a cardiac output rise of at least 10–15% [[Cecconi2014ESICM]]. The bars in step 5 are pooled areas under the ROC curve [[Marik2008CVP,Marik2009PPV,Monnet2016PLR]].

How much fluid first: the guideline wording

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) guidelines grade every statement. "Weak" or "conditional" means the panel thought most people would want the option but that reasonable choices differ. The certainty rating says how sure the panel was about the evidence. The best-known number in sepsis care, 30 mL/kg, is a weak suggestion on low-quality evidence.

Where the fluid goes: leaky vessels and a rising balance

The inside of every blood vessel is lined by the glycocalyx, a gel-like layer about 0.5–5.0 μm thick that helps control permeability and vessel tone [[Uchimido2019]]. Sepsis strips it away, so fluid given into the vein does not all stay there. Scrub through a vessel and a fluid-balance chart built from the VASST cohort.

Less fluid, earlier vasopressors: the restrictive trials

CLASSIC · 2022

Restriction in the ICU

1554 ICU patients with septic shock who had already had at least 1 L. The restrictive group received a median of 1798 mL of IV fluid in ICU versus 3811 mL. 90-day mortality was 42.3% versus 42.1% (adjusted difference 0.1 points; 95% CI −4.7 to 4.9). Serious adverse events: 29.4% versus 30.8% [[Meyhoff2022CLASSIC]]. At 1 year: 51.3% versus 49.9%, with similar quality of life and cognition [[Kjaer2023CLASSIC1y]].

CLOVERS · 2023

Vasopressors first, in the US

1563 patients with sepsis-induced hypotension after 1–3 L. The restrictive arm prioritised vasopressors and got less fluid over 24 h (difference of medians −2134 mL). Death before discharge home by day 90: 14.0% versus 14.9% (difference −0.9 points; 95% CI −4.4 to 2.6) [[Shapiro2023CLOVERS]].

ARISE FLUIDS · 2026

In the emergency department

1000 adults with septic shock after an initial 1–2 L. The early-vasopressor group got a median of 1108 mL less fluid in 24 h. Days alive and out of hospital at day 90 were 76 in both groups. Pulmonary oedema: 0.6% versus 5.0% [[Peake2026ARISEFLUIDS]].

FACTT · 2006

Lungs: a conservative strategy

1000 patients with acute lung injury. Mean 7-day cumulative balance was −136 mL (conservative) versus +6992 mL (liberal). 60-day mortality 25.5% versus 28.4% (P=0.30); ventilator-free days 14.6 versus 12.1. Shock or dialysis did not rise (10% vs 14%) [[Wiedemann2006FACTT]].

CENSER · 2019

Early noradrenaline

310 adults in Thailand (phase II). Median time to norepinephrine fell from 192 to 93 min. Shock control by 6 h: 76.1% versus 48.4%. 28-day mortality 15.5% versus 21.9% (P=0.15, not significant). Cardiogenic pulmonary oedema: 14.4% versus 27.7% [[Permpikul2019CENSER]].

Established

What the three big trials add up to

CLASSIC, CLOVERS and ARISE FLUIDS showed no mortality difference either way. Less fluid with earlier vasopressors appears safe, and in ARISE FLUIDS it came with less pulmonary oedema [[Meyhoff2022CLASSIC,Shapiro2023CLOVERS,Peake2026ARISEFLUIDS]]. See Vasopressors & steroids.

Which fluid

Balanced or saline

A small kidney signal

SMART (15,802 critically ill adults): major adverse kidney events within 30 days in 14.3% with balanced crystalloids versus 15.4% with saline (OR 0.91; 95% CI 0.84 to 0.99) [[Semler2018SMART]]. In its 1641 patients with sepsis, 30-day in-hospital mortality was 26.3% versus 31.2% (secondary analysis) [[Brown2019SMARTsepsis]]. BaSICS (11,052) and PLUS (5037) found no mortality difference [[Zampieri2021BaSICSfluid,Finfer2022PLUS]].

Early evidence

Pooling the crystalloid trials

A 2022 meta-analysis (13 RCTs, 35,884 people) gave a posterior probability of 89.5% that balanced crystalloids reduce mortality [[Hammond2022NEJMEvid]]. The BEST-Living individual-patient analysis (34,685) found in-hospital mortality of 16.8% versus 17.3%, but in traumatic brain injury 19.1% versus 14.7%, favouring saline [[Zampieri2024BESTLiving]]. A Melbourne before-and-after study restricting chloride-rich fluid saw kidney injury fall from 14% to 8.4% [[Yunos2012JAMA]].

Children

PRoMPT BOLUS

8482 children with septic shock in 47 emergency departments: major adverse kidney events at 30 days were 3.4% with balanced fluid versus 3.0% with saline (RR 1.10; 95% CI 0.88 to 1.40). Hyperchloraemia was less common with balanced fluid (31.4% vs 49.0%) [[Balamuth2026PRoMPT]]. See Sepsis in babies and children.

Albumin

Safe, but no proven benefit

SAFE (6997 ICU patients): 4% albumin and saline gave equivalent 28-day outcomes (RR 0.99) [[Finfer2004SAFE]]. Its severe-sepsis subgroup hinted at benefit (adjusted OR 0.71) [[Finfer2011SAFEsepsis]]. ALBIOS (1818, 20% albumin): 90-day mortality 41.1% versus 43.6% (RR 0.94; 95% CI 0.85 to 1.05) [[Caironi2014ALBIOS]]. A German 2026 trial (440, stopped early) was inconclusive [[Sakr2026JAMANetwOpen]].

Starches

Harm to the kidneys

6S (798 with severe sepsis): 90-day mortality 51% with HES 130/0.42 versus 43% with Ringer's acetate (RR 1.17), and renal replacement therapy 22% versus 16% [[Perner2012SixS]]. CHEST (7000): RRT 7.0% versus 5.8% [[Myburgh2012CHEST]]. VISEP was stopped early, with toxicity that "increased with accumulating doses" [[Brunkhorst2008VISEP]]. Cochrane: starches slightly increase RRT (RR 1.30) [[Lewis2018Cochrane]].

Gelatin and other colloids

Little or no difference

Cochrane 2018 (69 studies, 30,020 people) found gelatins may make little or no difference to mortality versus crystalloids (RR 0.89, low certainty), and albumin or plasma none either (RR 0.98) [[Lewis2018Cochrane]]. CRISTAL (2857 with hypovolaemic shock) found no 28-day difference with colloids (25.4% vs 27.0%) [[Annane2013CRISTAL]].

Early goal-directed therapy: how a protocol rose and fell

2001

Rivers

In 263 emergency patients, 6 hours of protocolised early goal-directed therapy gave in-hospital mortality of 30.5% versus 46.5% (P=0.009) [[Rivers2001EGDT]]. Its protocol group received over 13 L of fluid [[Evans2021SSC]].

2014–2017

ProCESS, ARISE, ProMISe and PRISM

ProCESS found no improvement from protocol-based resuscitation [[ProCESS2014]]. Pooled in PRISM (3723 patients, 138 hospitals): 90-day mortality 24.9% versus 25.4% with usual care, with more ICU use and cost [[PRISM2017]]. Usual-care groups received about 7–8 L over 72 h [[Evans2021SSC]].

2021

Bolus speed

BaSICS also tested how fast boluses run: 333 versus 999 mL/h gave 90-day mortality of 26.6% versus 27.0% (HR 1.03) [[Zampieri2021BaSICSrate]].

When fluid harms, and what is still argued

Established

Context matters: FEAST and Zambia

In 3141 African children with severe febrile illness, boluses raised 48-hour mortality (10.6% albumin, 10.5% saline, 7.3% no bolus; RR 1.45), consistently across subgroups [[Maitland2011FEAST]]. In 212 Zambian adults with sepsis and hypotension, an early resuscitation protocol (median 3.5 L vs 2.0 L in 6 h) raised in-hospital mortality to 48.1% from 33.0% [[Andrews2017Zambia]]. Bolus volumes that seem safe in well-resourced ICUs increased deaths in hospitals without intensive care.

Established caveat

Overload: a link, not proof

Observational studies tie positive balance to death: across 31 studies, fluid overload after 3 days had an adjusted RR of 8.83, and mortality rose 1.19-fold per litre [[Messmer2020CCM]]; above 5 L on day 1, mortality rose 2.3% per extra litre [[Marik2017ICM]]; non-survivors had over twice the daily balance (29 vs 13 mL/kg) [[Acheampong2015]]. Sicker patients get more fluid, so these links are confounded, and the randomised restrictive trials found no mortality benefit [[Messmer2020CCM,Meyhoff2022CLASSIC,Shapiro2023CLOVERS]].

Hypothesis

Fluid as a drug

A review frames fluid as a drug with "four D's" (drug, dosing, duration, de-escalation) and four phases: resuscitation, optimisation, stabilisation and evacuation [[Malbrain2018AIC]]. Over-aggressive fluid may worsen glycocalyx damage, while plasma and albumin may lessen it; these effects are not yet well understood [[Uchimido2019]].

Early evidence

Personalised resuscitation

Individualised, responsiveness-guided fluid is the direction of travel. ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 suggests personalised haemodynamic targets shorten organ support [[Hernandez2025ANDROMEDA2]]. The ESICM consensus already calls for individualising blood pressure targets and predicting fluid responsiveness [[Cecconi2014ESICM]].

In plain wordsIn sepsis, blood vessels become leaky and floppy, so blood pressure can drop and organs get too little blood. Doctors first give fluid through a drip, then check whether more fluid is actually helping before giving more [[Singer2016Sepsis3,Evans2021SSC,Prescott2026SSC]]. A simple test is lifting the legs for a minute: if the heart pumps more blood, more fluid will probably help [[Monnet2016PLR,Bentzer2016JAMA]]. Too much fluid can also cause harm, such as fluid in the lungs, and big studies found that giving less fluid and starting blood-pressure medicine earlier was about as safe [[Meyhoff2022CLASSIC,Shapiro2023CLOVERS,Peake2026ARISEFLUIDS]].

The percentages on this page describe large groups of people in trials, not what will happen to any one person. When fluid alone does not raise blood pressure enough, the next step is a vasopressor: see Vasopressors & steroids.
