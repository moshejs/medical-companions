After sepsis: recovery & families

Leaving hospital is not the end of sepsis for many people. Follow the year after discharge across body, thinking and mood, see how often people go back into hospital or back to work, and find what is known to help survivors, families and the bereaved.

For survivors and familiesMany people get better after sepsis, but recovery can take months, and it is common to feel tired, weak, forgetful or low for a while. These after-effects have a name, post-sepsis syndrome, and having them is not a sign of weakness or failure. [[Prescott2018Recovery,Mostel2019PSS]] The figures on this page come from large groups of people in studies. They describe populations and cannot predict what will happen to you or to someone you love.

Post-sepsis syndrome and PICS

"Post-sepsis syndrome" describes a cluster of long-term effects after sepsis: problems with thinking, functional disability, psychological problems and worsening of other medical conditions, with a higher risk of death and a poorer quality of life. It is an established clinical concept, though its mechanisms are still being worked out. [[Mostel2019PSS]] A wider term, post-intensive care syndrome (PICS), was agreed at a 2010 Society of Critical Care Medicine conference for new or worsening problems in physical, cognitive or mental health that arise after critical illness and persist beyond the hospital stay. The term applies to the survivor or to a family member (PICS-F). [[Needham2012PICS]]

Each year more than 19 million people worldwide develop sepsis and about 14 million survive to leave hospital. Among people discharged after sepsis, a review estimated that about half recover, one third die during the following year and one sixth have severe persistent impairments. [[Prescott2018Recovery]] A systematic review found excess deaths continuing up to 2 years and beyond, and warned that 28-day mortality alone can mislead. [[Winters2010LongTerm]]

Body

Weakness and daily tasks

ICU-acquired weakness is damage to nerves and muscles, in the limbs and the breathing muscles, caused by critical illness. It reduces long-term physical function and quality of life. [[Vanhorebeek2020ICUAW]] Survivors of sepsis gain on average 1 to 2 new functional limitations, such as being unable to bathe or dress without help. [[Prescott2018Recovery]] In older Americans with no limitations beforehand, the mean was 1.57 new limitations after severe sepsis vs 0.48 after other hospital stays. [[Iwashyna2010Cognitive]]

Thinking

Memory and concentration

Moderate to severe cognitive impairment rose about 3-fold after severe sepsis in older adults, from 6.1% to 16.7%, while it did not change after other hospital stays. [[Prescott2018Recovery,Iwashyna2010Cognitive]] After respiratory failure or shock, 40% of adults scored like people with moderate traumatic brain injury at 3 months, and 26% like people with mild Alzheimer's disease. Longer delirium in hospital went with worse scores. [[Pandharipande2013BRAINICU]]

Mind and sleep

Worry, low mood, nightmares

The review reports anxiety in 32%, depression in 29% and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in 44% of sepsis survivors. [[Prescott2018Recovery]] PTSD figures depend on the threshold used: across ICU survivors they were 25% at 1–6 months with a stricter cut-off (Impact of Event Scale ≥35) and 44% with a looser one (≥20). [[Parker2015PTSD]] Abnormal sleep is common, and most studies show it improves over time. [[Altman2017Sleep]] These problems can be treated: tell your doctor or nurse.

Family

PICS-F

Relatives can develop anxiety, acute stress, post-traumatic stress, depression and complicated grief. This happens to parents of newborns and children as well as to families of adults, and may last more than 4 years. [[Davidson2012PICSF]] See "For families, and for the bereaved" below.

Back into hospital, heart health and late deaths

In a meta-analysis of 56 studies, rehospitalisation after sepsis averaged 9.3% by 7 days, 21.4% by 30 days and 39.0% by 1 year, and infection was the most common reason. [[ShankarHari2020Rehospitalisation]] Among 2,617 older US adults surviving severe sepsis, 42.7% were readmitted within 90 days. Compared with matched survivors of other illnesses, the clear excesses were readmission for sepsis (6.4% vs 2.8%) and acute kidney failure (3.3% vs 1.2%); heart failure readmissions were common (5.5%) but not more frequent than in the comparison group (7.8%). [[Prescott2015Readmission]] Many readmissions are for conditions that might be treated outside hospital: 41.6% by an expanded definition. [[Prescott2015Readmission]]

The review's suggested focus after discharge has three parts: find new physical, mental and cognitive problems and refer for treatment; review and adjust long-term medicines; and look for treatable conditions that often lead to readmission, such as infection, heart failure, kidney failure and aspiration. For people whose health was poor or falling before sepsis, it notes that focusing on relief of symptoms may be appropriate. [[Prescott2018Recovery]]

Going back to work

After critical illness, about a third of previously employed survivors were back at work at 3 months (33%) and just over half at 6 and 12 months (55% and 56%). Going back to work went with better quality of life and fewer depressive symptoms, and caregivers too reported reduced hours and lost earnings. [[McPeake2019RTW]] In a Norwegian national registry of 12,260 working-age adults employed before a sepsis admission, 58.6% were working at 6 months, 67.5% at 1 year and 63.4% at 2 years. Sustained return was less likely after ICU-treated sepsis (HR 0.56), and it did not improve over the decade studied. [[Skei2023RTW]] Screening survivors and families for financial and social needs is a best practice statement in the Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 guideline. [[Evans2021SSC]]

What the Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 says

Quoted or summarised as published, with the strength of each statement. These describe what the guideline asks of health services; they are not instructions to readers. [[Evans2021SSC]]

  • Goals of care (best practice statement): "we recommend discussing goals of care and prognosis with patients and families over no such discussion", and (weak) addressing them early, within 72 h. [[Evans2021SSC]]
  • Peer support (weak, very low quality): "For adult survivors of sepsis or septic shock and their families, we suggest referral to peer support groups over no such referral". [[Evans2021SSC]]
  • Social and economic needs (best practice statement): screen patients and families for housing, nutritional, financial and spiritual support, and refer where available. [[Evans2021SSC]]
  • Information: written and verbal sepsis education, including post-ICU and post-sepsis syndrome, before discharge and at follow-up (weak); shared decision-making in discharge planning, medicine reconciliation at ICU and hospital discharge, and a discharge summary that covers the ICU stay, sepsis, treatments and common impairments (best practice statements). [[Evans2021SSC]]
  • Follow-up and rehabilitation: assessment and follow-up for physical, cognitive and emotional problems (best practice statement); referral to a post-critical illness follow-up programme if available (weak); and for survivors ventilated for more than 48 h or in ICU for more than 72 h, referral to a post-hospital rehabilitation programme (weak, very low quality). There is insufficient evidence to recommend early over routine follow-up. [[Evans2021SSC]]

For families, and for the bereaved

You matter tooFamilies feel it too. Many relatives have stress, anxiety or sadness after a loved one's time in intensive care, whether the person survived or died, and support is available. [[Azoulay2005Family,Davidson2012PICSF]] Feeling this way is a common response to a frightening time, not a failing.

Ninety days after a relative's ICU stay, 33.1% of family members had post-traumatic stress symptoms at a level linked with moderate to major PTSD risk. The share was higher when information had felt incomplete (48.4%), when the relative died in ICU (50%) and among relatives who had shared end-of-life decisions (81.8%). [[Azoulay2005Family]] How staff communicate with families and include them in care and decisions may affect how families fare later; proposed supports include visits after discharge, support groups and clinics. [[Davidson2012PICSF]]

For families of patients dying in ICU, a French randomised trial of a planned end-of-life conference with the care team plus a bereavement brochure found fewer PTSD-related symptoms at 90 days (45% vs 69%), less anxiety (45% vs 67%) and less depression (29% vs 56%). [[Lautrette2007Brochure]] The Surviving Sepsis Campaign suggests peer support groups for survivors and their families. [[Evans2021SSC]] Sepsis charities such as the UK Sepsis Trust and Sepsis Alliance offer information and support for survivors and the bereaved.

If someone you love is in ICU

Things families have found helpful

  • Ask the team for regular updates, and say if information feels incomplete. [[Azoulay2005Family]]
  • Ask whether the unit keeps an ICU diary you can write in. [[Jones2010Diaries]]
  • Ask about goals of care and what the team expects, and who can help with decisions. [[Evans2021SSC]]
After a death

Grief after sepsis

Complicated grief is recognised as part of PICS-F. You can ask for a meeting with the team to talk through what happened, and ask about bereavement support. In the French trial, a planned conference and a bereavement brochure lowered later distress. [[Davidson2012PICSF,Lautrette2007Brochure]]

Children

After childhood sepsis

Children recover over months too, and parents can develop PICS-F. Outcomes in children are covered in Sepsis in babies and children. [[Zimmerman2020LAPSE,Davidson2012PICSF]]

Call nowSepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis? After sepsis, new infections are the most common reason for going back into hospital. [[ShankarHari2020Rehospitalisation]]
