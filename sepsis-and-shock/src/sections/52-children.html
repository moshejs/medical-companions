Sepsis in babies and children

Children are not small adults. Their normal pulse and breathing rates change with age, newborns have their own patterns of infection, and since 2024 children have their own sepsis definition, the Phoenix criteria. This module covers the signs parents notice, newborn sepsis, the Phoenix score, and why fluid boluses can do harm in some settings.

Call nowSepsis is a medical emergency. If a child or baby with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?

How big the problem is

Worldwide

About half of all sepsis is in children

"Half of the 50 million people affected by sepsis globally every year are neonates and children younger than 19 years." The Global Burden of Disease study counted 20.3 million cases in children under 5 in 2017, and 2.9 million sepsis-related deaths at that age. [[Watson2024Burden,Rudd2020GBD]]

Newborns

The highest rate of any age

A 2021 meta-analysis (2,797,879 live births, 14 countries) estimated neonatal sepsis at 2,824 per 100,000 live births, with 17.6% dying; early-onset sepsis had higher incidence and mortality than late-onset. [[Fleischmann2021Neonatal]]

Children

Rates in older children

A 2018 review estimated 48 sepsis cases per 100,000 child-years, with mortality of 1–5% for sepsis and 9–20% for severe sepsis. Extrapolated worldwide, about 1.2 million paediatric and 3.0 million neonatal cases a year. [[FleischmannStruzek2018Paeds]]

Causes

What tips children into sepsis

Among children under 5 in 2017, the commonest underlying causes were diarrhoeal diseases (27.9% of cases), neonatal disorders (25.7%) and lower respiratory infections (16.5%). [[Rudd2020GBD]]

Mortality figures here and below describe large groups of children in the studies named. They cannot predict what will happen to any one child.

You know your child bestIf your child seems much more unwell than with a usual illness, or is getting worse fast, seek help urgently and say what has changed. Do not wait for a rash: in the meningococcal study, leg pain, cold hands and feet and abnormal skin colour came hours before it. [[Thompson2006Meningococcal]] In an Australian poll, only 27.8% of sepsis-aware parents thought they could recognise sepsis in their child, so it is normal to feel unsure; asking "could this be sepsis?" is always reasonable. [[Peters2023Awareness]]

Newborns: early-onset and late-onset sepsis

Neonatal sepsis may come from infection in the womb, from the mother's bacteria during birth, or from the hospital or community afterwards. How it shows depends on the timing of exposure, the amount of bacteria, the baby's immune status and the germ. [[Shane2017Neonatal]]

Early-onset sepsis as defined in NICHD surveillance (pathogen in blood or CSF within 72 hours of birth). [[Stoll2020EOS,Stoll2002LOS]]

Early onset

Who it affects

In 217,480 newborns at 18 NICHD centres (2015–2017), early-onset sepsis was 1.08 per 1,000 live births and 18.47 per 1,000 at 22–28 weeks' gestation. 93.6% showed signs of illness within 72 hours. 29.0% of infected preterm infants died; no term infants died. [[Stoll2020EOS]]

Late onset

Very small babies in hospital

Late-onset sepsis occurs "after 3 days of age". Among 6,215 very low birth weight infants, 21% had culture-proven late-onset sepsis, 48% of it coagulase-negative staphylococci. Mortality was 18% in infected and 7% in uninfected infants. [[Stoll2002LOS]]

Group B strep

Screening and antibiotics in labour

About half of colonised mothers pass group B streptococcus (GBS) to their babies; without antibiotics in labour, 1–2% of those babies develop early-onset GBS disease. ACOG recommends universal GBS screening at 36 0/7–37 6/7 weeks. The AAP calls antibiotic prophylaxis in labour "the only currently available effective strategy" against early-onset GBS disease; there is no effective prevention of late-onset disease. [[ACOG2020GBS,Puopolo2019GBS]]

Changing germs

E. coli and GBS

In US surveillance (2005–2014), early-onset GBS fell from 0.27 to 0.22 per 1,000 live births while E. coli stayed stable (0.21 to 0.18); 11% of cases died. In the NICHD cohort, 53.3% of GBS cases were in babies whose mothers had screened negative. [[Schrag2016EOS,Stoll2020EOS]]

Established

The Kaiser early-onset sepsis calculator

Built from 608,014 births, the calculator combines maternal risk (intrapartum temperature, duration of membrane rupture, antibiotics given more than 4 hours before delivery) with the baby's condition. At Kaiser Permanente, blood cultures fell from 14.5% to 4.9% of newborns and early antibiotics from 5.0% to 2.6%, with no change in adverse outcomes. Across 13 studies, antibiotic use was 56% of the previous level; safety evidence was "limited". [[Puopolo2011EOS,Escobar2014EOS,Kuzniewicz2017EOS,Achten2019EOS]]

Tests

No single test decides

The AAP notes that laboratory tests "alone are neither sensitive nor specific enough to guide EOS management decisions" in babies born at 35 weeks or later. [[Puopolo2018AAP]]

From IPSCC 2005 to the Phoenix criteria 2024

The 2005 International Pediatric Sepsis Consensus Conference adapted the adult SIRS-based definitions for children, with age-specific cut-offs for vital signs and lab tests. Paediatric SIRS required that the temperature or white-count criterion be met. These were expert-opinion definitions, and the Phoenix task force later described the SIRS criteria as having "poor predictive properties". [[Goldstein2005Paeds,Schlapbach2024Phoenix]] Organ-dysfunction scores did better: in 2,594 Australian and New Zealand PICU admissions with infection, adjusted AUROC was 0.829 for age-adapted SOFA against 0.710 for SIRS, and a paediatric SOFA (pSOFA) reached AUC 0.94 in 8,711 PICU encounters. [[Schlapbach2018SOFAchildren,Matics2017pSOFA]]

The SCCM Pediatric Sepsis Definition Task Force (35 experts, 6 continents) then built the Phoenix criteria from an international survey, a systematic review, a modified Delphi process and a new score derived from more than 3 million electronic health record encounters in 10 health systems in the USA, Colombia, Bangladesh, China and Kenya. [[Schlapbach2024Phoenix,SanchezPinto2024Phoenix]]

Established

How well it performs

Across development and external validation (AUROC 0.71–0.92), Phoenix had higher positive predictive value, and higher or similar sensitivity, compared with the 2005 IPSCC criteria. In a US nine-centre PICU dataset (25,680 encounters), 28% of Phoenix-positive children were IPSCC-negative, and they had higher mortality than IPSCC-positive, Phoenix-negative children (4.7% vs 1.7%). [[SanchezPinto2024Phoenix,SanchezPinto2025Validation]]

Early evidence

Open questions

Phoenix was built on retrospective health-record data and needs lab tests that are not always available. Prospective validation is ongoing. [[SanchezPinto2024Phoenix,Schlapbach2024Phoenix]]

Guidance

Paediatric Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2020

The panel (49 experts) suggests "implementing systematic screening for timely recognition of septic shock and other sepsis-associated organ dysfunction" in acutely unwell children (weak recommendation, very low quality evidence). As published, it recommends antimicrobials "within 1 h of recognition" in septic shock (strong) and "within 3 h of recognition" in sepsis-associated organ dysfunction without shock (weak). [[Weiss2020SSCchildren]]

Early evidence

Paediatric early warning scores

In the EPOCH cluster trial (21 hospitals, 144,539 discharges), a bedside paediatric early warning system did not change all-cause mortality (adjusted OR 1.01) but reduced significant deterioration events (0.50 vs 0.84 per 1,000 patient-days). PEWS have "a limited evidence base". [[Parshuram2018EPOCH,Chapman2019PEWS]]

Fluids: why more is not always better (FEAST)

For years, rapid fluid boluses were standard for children in shock. The FEAST trial tested this in 3,141 African children with severe febrile illness and impaired perfusion, in hospitals without intensive care: a 20–40 mL/kg bolus of 5% albumin, a saline bolus, or no bolus. Boluses increased deaths. [[Maitland2011FEAST]]

FEAST, stratum A. Relative risk of death at 48 hours for any bolus versus no bolus 1.45 (95% CI 1.13–1.86). [[Maitland2011FEAST]]

Established

Consistent across groups

The harm was consistent across subgroups for shock severity, malaria, coma, sepsis, acidosis and severe anaemia. Bolus volumes that seem safe in well-resourced intensive care units increased deaths in children in African hospitals without intensive care: fluid harm depends on context. [[Maitland2011FEAST,Andrews2017Zambia]]

Adults too

A similar signal in Zambia

In 212 Zambian adults with sepsis and hypotension, an early resuscitation protocol with more fluid (median 3.5 L vs 2.0 L in 6 hours) increased in-hospital mortality, 48.1% versus 33.0%. [[Andrews2017Zambia]] See .

Which fluid

Balanced fluid or saline

In children, the PRoMPT BOLUS trial (8,482 analysed, 47 emergency departments) found no difference in major adverse kidney events at 30 days (3.4% balanced vs 3.0% saline); hyperchloraemia was less common with balanced fluid. [[Balamuth2026PRoMPT]]

After childhood sepsis

In the Life After Pediatric Sepsis Evaluation (389 children with community-acquired septic shock in 12 US PICUs), 50% of survivors had not regained their baseline health-related quality of life at 1 month, and 35% at 12 months.
