Part 4 · Treatment

The first hour: bundle & antibiotics

Sepsis is treated as an emergency because, on average, earlier antibiotics go with better survival, most clearly in septic shock. Watch the Hour-1 bundle unfold on a clock, see what the timing studies found, and follow the trials that retired early goal-directed therapy.

Call nowSepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?

What SSC 2021 says about timing, as published

The 2018 Hour-1 bundle paper is paywalled, so the five elements above are quoted from a verbatim listing in an open-access study. The 2021 guideline then separated people with possible shock from those with possible sepsis without shock. These are guideline statements quoted as published, not instructions. [[Levy2018Bundle,Wang2021Hour1,Evans2021SSC]]

  • "Sepsis and septic shock are medical emergencies, and we recommend that treatment and resuscitation begin immediately" (best practice statement). [[Evans2021SSC]]
  • "For adults with possible septic shock or a high likelihood for sepsis, we recommend administering antimicrobials immediately, ideally within 1 h of recognition" (strong; low-quality evidence for septic shock, very low for sepsis without shock). [[Evans2021SSC]]
  • "For adults with possible sepsis without shock, we suggest a time-limited course of rapid investigation and if concern for infection persists, the administration of antimicrobials within 3 h from the time when sepsis was first recognised" (weak, very low quality). [[Evans2021SSC]]
  • For adults with a low likelihood of infection and no shock, "we suggest deferring antimicrobials while continuing to closely monitor the patient" (weak, very low quality). [[Evans2021SSC]]
  • For sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock, "we suggest that at least 30 mL/kg of intravenous (IV) crystalloid fluid should be given within the first 3 h of resuscitation" (weak, low quality); the guideline notes this fixed volume rests on observational evidence. [[Evans2021SSC]]
  • An initial MAP target of 65 mm Hg over higher targets in septic shock on vasopressors (strong, moderate quality); starting vasopressors peripherally rather than waiting for central access, for a short time and in a vein at or above the antecubital fossa (weak, very low quality). [[Evans2021SSC]]
  • ICU admission within 6 h for adults with sepsis or septic shock who need ICU care (weak, low quality), and against qSOFA "compared to SIRS, NEWS, or MEWS as a single screening tool" (strong, moderate quality). [[Evans2021SSC]]

Time to antibiotics: what the studies found

About these numbersEvery figure below is an average association in a large group of patients, mostly from observational studies. It cannot say what would happen to any one person, and a delay is rarely anyone's single fault: sepsis is often hard to recognise early.

Each line extends one study's published per-hour odds ratio, assuming it stays constant; the studies measured time from different starting points and in different groups. Not a prediction for anyone. [[Kumar2006Delay,Peltan2019Door,Liu2017Antibiotics,Seymour2017Timing]]

"Each hour of delay in antimicrobial administration over the ensuing 6 hrs was associated with an average decrease in survival of 7.6%" (as published). Only 50% got effective therapy within 6 h; median 6 h. [[Kumar2006Delay]]

SEP-1 and mandated sepsis care

US hospitals have reported the Medicare SEP-1 measure since October 2015. A case passes only if every qualifying element is done: lactate, blood cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics, 30 mL/kg crystalloid, vasopressors and reassessment. [[Townsend2022SEP1,Rhee2018SEP1]]

Observational

Compliant care, lower mortality

In Medicare data from 3,241 hospitals, SEP-1-compliant care was associated with lower 30-day mortality: 21.81% vs 27.48% in the standard match and 22.22% vs 26.28% in the stringent match. [[Townsend2022SEP1]]

Chart review

No difference after adjustment

In 851 cases at 7 US hospitals, 67% failed SEP-1. Crude mortality was higher in failures (18.4% vs 11.0%) but not after adjusting for illness severity (OR 1.36, 0.85–2.18). The commonest reason for failing was a missed 3- or 6-hour lactate (40%); antibiotic delays over 3 h were linked to death (OR 1.94) but caused only 15.1% of failures. [[Rhee2018SEP1]]

Position paper

Limit it to septic shock?

IDSA's 2021 position paper, with five endorsing societies, recommends limiting SEP-1 to septic shock, warning that it may drive antibiotic overuse and that its "time zero" definition is not evidence-based. [[Rhee2021IDSA]]

State mandate

New York's regulations

After New York's 2013 sepsis regulations, 3-hour bundle compliance rose from 53.4% to 64.7% and risk-adjusted mortality fell from 28.8% to 24.4% (91,357 hospitalisations). Against 4 control states, adjusted mortality was 3.2% (1.0–5.4%) lower than expected by the 10th quarter. [[Levy2018NewYork,Kahn2019NYMandate]]

Open questions

Unresolved

One hour for everyone?

Cohorts show the strongest timing effect in shock and weak or no effect without it; a meta-analysis found no significant benefit at the 1-h or 3-h thresholds; IDSA argues mandated timing should be limited to septic shock. SSC 2021 now separates the two groups. [[Im2022Antibiotics,Liu2017Antibiotics,Sterling2015Meta,Rhee2021IDSA,Evans2021SSC]]

Established

Is "7.6% per hour" causal?

The association is consistent in large cohorts, but the causal size is uncertain. Kumar's figure comes from a retrospective cohort open to confounding by indication; Puskarich found no hourly effect from triage in a protocolised cohort, and giving antibiotics in the ambulance did not change mortality in a randomised trial. [[Kumar2006Delay,Puskarich2011Antibiotics,Alam2018PHANTASi]]

Conflicting

Does SEP-1 save lives?

The evidence is observational only, and it conflicts: Medicare data favour compliance, a multicentre chart review found no difference after severity adjustment, and the New York data suggest a population-level benefit. [[Townsend2022SEP1,Rhee2018SEP1,Levy2018NewYork,Kahn2019NYMandate]]
