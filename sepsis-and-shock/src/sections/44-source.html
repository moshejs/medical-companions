Part 4 · Treatment

Source control & antimicrobial stewardship

Antibiotics alone cannot clear a pocket of pus, a blocked infected kidney or an infected line. Follow one illustrative patient from the first hour to the last dose: find the source, take cultures, start broad antibiotics, drain the source, then narrow and stop as soon as it is safe.

Call nowSepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis?

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) 2021 asks teams to rapidly identify or exclude an anatomical source of infection that needs emergent source control, and to carry out any needed intervention "as soon as medically and logistically practical" (Best Practice Statement). [[Evans2021SSC]] It also suggests daily assessment for de-escalation, shorter rather than longer courses once the source is controlled, and procalcitonin plus clinical judgement to decide when to stop. [[Evans2021SSC]]

Illustrative patient, schematic. The organism, the bacteria count and the procalcitonin curve are invented to show the sequence. Timings and rules are quoted as published: antimicrobials ideally within 1 h for possible septic shock (SSC 2021) [[Evans2021SSC]]; the SAPS procalcitonin stop rule of a fall of 80% or more from peak, or 0.5 μg/L or lower [[deJong2016SAPS]]; 7 days noninferior to 14 for bloodstream infection in BALANCE [[Daneman2025BALANCE]]. They are teaching facts, not instructions.

Where sepsis starts

Which source is commonest depends on who is counted. In the European SOAP study of adult ICU patients, the lungs were the source in 68% and the abdomen in 22%. [[Vincent2006SOAP]] In BALANCE, a trial of bloodstream infections, the urinary tract led (42.2%), followed by the abdomen, lungs, lines and skin. [[Daneman2025BALANCE]] In US discharge data, respiratory sources were commoner in men (36% vs 29%) and genitourinary sources in women (35% vs 27%). [[Esper2006Disparities]] Pick a source to see what "source control" means there.

Percentages are from different populations and are not directly comparable: SOAP counted ICU patients with sepsis [[Vincent2006SOAP]]; BALANCE counted adults with bloodstream infection and excluded S. aureus [[Daneman2025BALANCE]]. In a Spanish cohort of 3,663 ICU patients, 32% needed source control, mostly for abdominal, urinary and soft-tissue infections. [[Martinez2017Source]]

Source control: how fast?

SSC 2021 found that smaller studies suggest source control within 6–12 hours is advantageous, with survival generally lower beyond that point, and it prefers the least invasive option that works (a needle drain before an open operation, for example). Surgery is considered when other approaches are inadequate or cannot be done in time, or when the diagnosis stays uncertain after imaging. [[Evans2021SSC]] All the timing evidence is observational. Lines

Infected intravascular devices

SSC 2021 recommends prompt removal of intravascular access devices that are a possible source, after other vascular access has been established (Best Practice Statement). [[Evans2021SSC]] IDSA 2009 lists when long-term catheters should come out: severe sepsis, suppurative thrombophlebitis, endocarditis, bacteraemia persisting beyond 72 h of active therapy, or S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, fungi or mycobacteria (A-II). Short-term catheters should be removed for gram-negative bacilli, S. aureus, enterococci, fungi and mycobacteria (A-II). [[Mermel2009IDSA]]

Bile ducts

Acute cholangitis

Tokyo Guidelines 2018: mild cases usually settle with antibiotics; moderate cases are indicated for early endoscopic or percutaneous biliary drainage; severe cases need drainage "as soon as possible" once breathing and circulation have been stabilised. [[Miura2018TG18]]

Early evidence

Why the timing debate is open

The 6-hour signal comes from MEDUSA, a perforation series and the soft-tissue meta-analysis [[Bloos2014Source,Azuhata2014Perforation,Nawijn2020NSTI]], while the Spanish cohort found no link beyond 12 hours [[Martinez2017Source]]. There is no randomised trial, so SSC settles on "as soon as medically and logistically practical". [[Evans2021SSC]]

The first antibiotic: fast, and broad enough

SSC 2021 recommends antimicrobials immediately, ideally within 1 hour of recognition, for adults with possible septic shock or a high likelihood of sepsis (strong recommendation). For possible sepsis without shock it suggests a time-limited course of rapid investigation and, if concern persists, antimicrobials within 3 hours (weak recommendation). [[Evans2021SSC]] In 2,154 people with septic shock, effective antimicrobials in the first hour of hypotension went with 79.9% survival to discharge, and each hour of delay over the next 6 hours with an average fall of 7.6%. [[Kumar2006Delay]] More on timing is in the first hour.

Breadth is a balance. In 17,430 adults with culture-positive community-onset sepsis, 67.0% got cover for resistant organisms, but those organisms were uncommon (MRSA 11.7%, ceftriaxone-resistant gram-negatives 13.1%, VRE 2.1%, ESBL 0.8%). Both inadequate (OR 1.19) and unnecessarily broad (OR 1.22) empirical therapy were linked to higher in-hospital mortality. [[Rhee2020Broad]] Early evidence

How long? Shorter courses and procalcitonin

Once the source is controlled, trial after trial has found shorter courses as good as longer ones. SSC 2021 suggests shorter rather than longer therapy with adequate source control, and procalcitonin plus clinical evaluation to decide when to stop where the right duration is unclear (both weak recommendations). It suggests against using procalcitonin to decide when to start. [[Evans2021SSC]] Established

Early evidence

De-escalation: safe, useful, or both?

SSC 2021 suggests daily assessment for de-escalation over fixed durations without daily review (weak). [[Evans2021SSC]] A systematic review found a pooled mortality RR of 0.68 with de-escalation, but warns this may only reflect that it happens in patients who are already improving. [[Tabah2016Deescalation]] The only randomised trial (116 patients) found longer ICU stay and more superinfections (27% vs 11%) with de-escalation, and similar mortality. [[Leone2014Deescalation]] Status: unresolved.

Stewardship goals

What a stewardship team checks

A meta-analysis of 145 studies linked guideline-adherent empirical therapy with a 35% relative reduction in mortality (RR 0.65) and de-escalation with 56% (RR 0.44), though evidence quality was generally low. [[Schuts2016Stewardship]] The WHO AWaRe classification sorts antibiotics into Access, Watch and Reserve groups as a simple "traffic light"; in a 56-country survey of children, Access use ranged from 7.8% to 61.2% of prescriptions. [[Hsia2019AWaRe]]

Dosing: how the drug is given

Beta-lactams such as piperacillin-tazobactam and meropenem can be given as a short bolus or as a slow (prolonged or continuous) infusion. SSC 2021 suggests prolonged infusion for maintenance, after an initial bolus, over conventional bolus infusion (weak, moderate quality), and asks for dosing by pharmacokinetic principles: augmented renal clearance, AKI, low albumin, kidney replacement therapy and ECMO all change antibiotic levels. [[Evans2021SSC]]

Early evidence

BLING III (2024)

7,031 critically ill adults on piperacillin-tazobactam or meropenem in 104 ICUs. 90-day mortality was 24.9% with continuous infusion vs 26.8% with intermittent infusion (OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.81–1.01; P = .08), just missing significance. Clinical cure was higher (55.7% vs 50.0%). [[Dulhunty2024BLING3]]

Established

The 2024 meta-analysis

18 trials, 9,108 adults with sepsis: prolonged infusion was linked to lower 90-day mortality (RR 0.86, 95% credible interval 0.72–0.98; high certainty), a 99.1% probability of benefit, lower ICU mortality (RR 0.84) and more clinical cure (RR 1.16). [[AbdulAziz2024Infusion]]

Early evidence

Measuring drug levels (TARGET)

In 249 patients on continuous piperacillin-tazobactam, daily level-guided dosing reached target concentrations more often (37.3% vs 14.6%) but did not lower mean SOFA score (7.9 vs 8.2) or 28-day mortality significantly (21.6% vs 25.8%). [[Hagel2022TARGET]] SSC notes that drug-level monitoring is not widely available. [[Evans2021SSC]]

Resistance: why stewardship matters to everyone

Global burden

More than a million deaths a year

An estimated 4.95 million deaths in 2019 were associated with bacterial antimicrobial resistance, 1.27 million directly attributable to it. Six pathogens led (E. coli, S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, S. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa), and the attributable death rate was highest in western sub-Saharan Africa. [[Murray2022GRAM]]

Forecast

Towards 2050

The GBD 2021 analysis counted 1.14 million attributable deaths in 2021 and forecasts 1.91 million attributable and 8.22 million associated deaths in 2050. AMR deaths fell by more than half in children under 5 since 1990 but rose by over 80% in adults aged 70 and over; MRSA-attributable deaths rose from 57,200 to 130,000. A "better care" scenario could avert 92.0 million deaths from 2025 to 2050. [[Naghavi2024GRAM]]

Side effect

C. difficile

IDSA/SHEA describe C. difficile as the most commonly identified cause of healthcare-associated infection in US adults. [[McDonald2018CDI]] In a community meta-analysis the odds were highest after clindamycin (OR 16.80), then cephalosporins, monobactams and carbapenems (5.68) and fluoroquinolones (5.50), against penicillins (2.71), macrolides (2.65) and tetracyclines (0.92, no effect). [[Brown2013CDI]]

In plain words

For patients and familiesSepsis often has a "source", such as an abscess (a pocket of pus), a blocked infected kidney or bile duct, an infected line or dead tissue. Doctors drain or remove it as soon as it is safe. [[Evans2021SSC]] With some fast-spreading infections under the skin, an operation within hours can make a big difference. [[Nawijn2020NSTI]] Antibiotics start fast; later the team switches to the narrowest drug that works and stops as soon as it is safe, because shorter courses often work just as well with fewer side effects. [[Evans2021SSC,Daneman2025BALANCE,Sawyer2015STOPIT]] The percentages on this page come from studies of large groups and cannot say what will happen to one person.
