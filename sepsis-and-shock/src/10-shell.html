Sepsis and shock, explained

Sepsis is the body's harmful, out-of-control response to an infection: the infection starts it, but the body's own reaction injures its organs, and it can become life-threatening. It is common, with an estimated 48.9 million cases worldwide in 2017, and it was linked to about 1 in 5 deaths. [[Singer2016Sepsis3,Rudd2020GBD]]

Every animation can be driven from the keyboard: Tab to its timeline, then arrow keys to scrub and Enter to play or pause.

Call nowSepsis is a medical emergency. If someone with an infection becomes very unwell (confused or very drowsy, breathing very fast, cold, mottled or bluish skin, passing little or no urine), call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department now and ask: could this be sepsis? Do not use this guide to decide.

For students

Every module has an Exam hook box and a citation trail. The qSOFA, SOFA, NEWS2, Phoenix and shock-index calculators are teaching aids, with criteria quoted exactly as published. Nursing students will find escalation, bundles and monitoring in their own module. Finish with flashcards, memory aids and a quiz.

For patients, families and caregivers

Look for the In plain words boxes. Many people survive sepsis, and the recovery modules describe, with sources, what life after sepsis can involve and where families can find support. Mortality figures are population statistics, not predictions for one person. Bring questions to your own care team: the app reports what guidelines and trials say, not what anyone should take or do.
